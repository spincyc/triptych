§The Code, Canon by Canon

A canon lawyer arrives with a citation, not with a date. So the canon is the entrance here: type c. 1095 and this page shows that canon, where it sits in the Code, every act that touched it, and the text on both sides of each change. The acts are the second axis — open one and see everything it reached, from a single canon to the whole of a Book.

The structure of the Code

Books, Parts, Titles and Chapters, as the record places each canon — which is how the Code is used and how it is cited. Nothing here is worked out from the shape of an identifier; where the record places a canon nowhere, it stands apart and says so.

The acts, and what each one reached

Every act this record carries, oldest first, with its extent beside it. Pascite gregem Dei rewrote the whole of a Book and a later rescript may touch a single paragraph; the counts say which is which before you open anything.

The bodies of law, and no map between them

Where this record carries more than one Code, they are separate lines and they number independently. This page joins no canon of one to a canon of another: any such correspondence is a scholarly claim, and it is shown with its source or it is not shown.

What this record claims

Written by the record itself, not by this page. Silence in it is silence because nothing was established, never because a canon was checked and found unchanged. 







  

    The words are often not here, and that is a stated position.
    The 1917 Code is in the public domain and its text may be shown. The Latin of
    the 1983 Code is a work of the Holy See later than 1929 and is protected, as
    are the standard English translations, and this project holds no licence for
    either. So for many canons this page carries the citation, the place in the
    structure, the acts that touched it and the dates — and, in place of the
    words, the reason they are withheld. A canon whose words nobody here has read
    is a different thing again and is said differently: an unread canon is not a
    withheld one, and neither is a canon that did not change.
  

  

    A station is normally the act — a constitution, a motu proprio, a decree, a
    rescript — and never a printing, because two printings of one canon differ
    constantly and no act stands behind those differences. Where a witness
    survives and no act has been located, the station is printed rather
    than promulgated, and that is a weaker claim about evidence, not a
    smaller change. What an act changed is computed once, by the generator that
    writes this record, and read here; this page diffs nothing itself.
  

  

    A citation is a link. Opening a canon writes it into the
    address bar — #canon=1095, with #unit= beside it
    for the exact row — so the URL you copy is the citation you would have
    written anyway.
  

  

    Everything here is closed until you open it, and that is what it costs to
    read. The page arrives with the acts and their counts and no canon at all;
    the index of every canon arrives the first time you look one up; a canon's
    history arrives when you open it, and the law as it stood arrives only if you
    ask. The consequence is worth knowing: your browser's own find-in-page
    reaches only what you have opened.
