\section{What the Words Will Bear}

Six words carry the description. Each is examined here against its other
occurrences in the same corpus and against a named public-domain lexical
authority---Joseph Henry Thayer's revision of Grimm's \emph{Clavis Novi
Testamenti}, cited by headword and page. Thayer is a nineteenth-century
lexicon and is used as an identified working authority, not as a modern
critical one; where its judgement is disputable this is said.

\subsection{\emph{Othonia}: the linen cloths}

\emph{Othonion} is a diminutive of \emph{othon\=e}, which in the New Testament
means a sheet: the vessel let down from heaven in Peter's vision at Joppa is
``as it were a great linen sheet'' (Acts 10:11; 11:5). Thayer glosses the
diminutive as ``a piece of linen, small linen cloth: plur.\ strips of linen
cloth for swathing the dead,'' and lists every New Testament
occurrence.\endnote{Thayer, \emph{A Greek-English Lexicon of the New
Testament, being Grimm's Wilke's Clavis Novi Testamenti}, corrected edition
(New York, Cincinnati, Chicago: American Book Company), s.v.\
\emph{othonion}, p.~439. The title page carries no date; the Internet Archive
record of the copy consulted dates it 1889.}

Those occurrences are worth setting out, because they are few and they are all
in one narrative complex: Jn 19:40, of the burial; Jn 20:5, 6, 7, of the empty
tomb; and Lk 24:12. Outside these five verses the word does not occur in the
New Testament at all. Two things follow.

The first is that the word belongs, in this corpus, to a single scene. It is
not a general term for cloth that happens to be used here; it is the word this
tradition uses for what Jesus was buried in.

The second is a genuine difficulty that this study does not resolve. The
Synoptic burial accounts do not use \emph{othonia}. They use \emph{sind\=on},
in the singular: Joseph ``wrapped it in a clean linen cloth'' (Mt 27:59),
``wrapped him in fine linen'' (Lk 23:53). John uses a plural of a diminutive.
Whether \emph{othonia} names strips, or bandages, or simply linens in general
including a large sheet, is a question the New Testament's own usage cannot
settle, because the New Testament's usage is these five verses. Readers should
be told that a good deal of confident writing about the shape of the burial
wrappings rests on a lexical base this narrow. Nothing in this study's
argument depends on the answer.

The Vulgate translates \emph{othonia} twice differently in five verses:
\emph{linteis} at Jn 19:40 (``ligaverunt illud linteis cum aromatibus''),
\emph{linteamina} at Jn 20:5--7 and Lk 24:12. The Greek does not vary there;
the Latin does.

\subsection{\emph{Soudarion}: the cloth that was over the head}

This is the word on which the modern claim stands or falls, and it repays
exact treatment.

\emph{Soudarion} is not a Greek word. It is the Latin \emph{sudarium} taken
into Greek, and \emph{sudarium} is formed from \emph{sudor}, sweat. Thayer's
entry is explicit about the derivation and gives the sense as ``a handkerchief,
i.e.\ a cloth for wiping the perspiration from the face and for cleaning the
nose,'' with a second use: ``also used in swathing the head of a corpse [A.V.\
napkin].''\endnote{Thayer, s.v.\ \emph{soudarion}, p.~581.}

There are four occurrences in the New Testament, and they should be listed
because the list is the argument:

\begin{studyframe}\small
\textbf{Lk 19:20} --- ``Lord, behold here is thy pound, which I have kept laid
up in a napkin.'' A servant's cloth, used to wrap and store money.

\textbf{Jn 11:44} --- ``And presently he that had been dead came forth, bound
feet and hands with winding bands. And his face was bound about with a
napkin.'' Lazarus. Greek \emph{h\=e opsis autou soudari\=o periededeto}, his
face had been bound round with a \emph{soudarion}. Latin \emph{facies illius
sudario erat ligata}.

\textbf{Jn 20:7} --- the cloth in the empty tomb, ``that had been about his
head.''

\textbf{Acts 19:12} --- cloths carried from Paul's body to the sick; the Douay
has ``handkerchiefs.''
\end{studyframe}

Three of the four are cloths associated with a body---two of them explicitly
with the head or face of a corpse. The fourth is a wrapping-cloth for money.
Not one of the four is a table napkin, and no New Testament text describes a
\emph{soudarion} in use at a meal.

That fact is not decisive by itself, because a word may denote at table what it
denotes elsewhere. But it is the first thing a reader should know before being
told what ``every Jewish boy'' understood a folded one to mean; and it is
striking that the closest New Testament analogue to a domestic use of a
\emph{soudarion}---the servant's cloth in the parable of the pounds---is a
cloth used for wrapping, not for wiping at table. Section~8 takes the question
up properly.

Jn 11:44 deserves a further note, because it is the only other place where the
Fourth Gospel puts a \emph{soudarion} on the head of a dead man, and the
contrast is deliberate. Lazarus comes out of his tomb still wearing his
grave-clothes and has to be untied: \emph{lysate auton}, ``loose him, and let
him go.'' In chapter 20 the wrappings are in the tomb and the man is not. The
evangelist who wrote both scenes knew what he was doing with the second.

\subsection{\emph{Keimena}: lying}

\emph{Keimena} is the present participle of \emph{keimai}, to lie. It is
applied to the \emph{othonia} three times---the other disciple ``seeth the
linen cloths lying'' (v.~5), Peter ``beholdeth the linen cloths lying''
(v.~6), and in Luke Peter ``seeth the linen cloths lying alone'' (Lk
24:12)---and it is applied to the \emph{soudarion} only in the negative:
\emph{ou meta t\=on othoni\=on keimenon}, ``not lying with the linen cloths.''

The contrast in verse 7 is therefore not, as it is often reported, between
cloths lying and a cloth folded. It is between cloths lying \emph{there} and a
cloth that was not lying \emph{with them}. What the Greek denies of the
face-cloth is not the lying but the company.

\subsection{\emph{Entetyligmenon}: the crucial word}

The participle applied to the face-cloth is \emph{entetyligmenon}, perfect
passive of \emph{entyliss\=o}. Thayer's entry is short enough to give
whole:

\begin{studyframe}\small
``\emph{en-tyliss\=o}: 1 aor.\ \emph{enetylixa}; pf.\ pass.\ ptcp.\
\emph{entetyligmenos}; \emph{to roll in, wrap in}: [with accusative and dative]
\emph{sindoni}, Mt.\ xxvii.\ 59; Lk.\ xxiii.\ 53; Ev.\ Nicod.\ c.\ 11 fin.\
\emph{to roll up, wrap together}: pass.\ Jn.\ xx.\ 7. (Arstph.\ Plut.\ nub.\
987; Athen.\ 3 p.~106 sq.)''\endnote{Thayer, s.v.\ \emph{entyliss\=o},
p.~219. Greek transliterated; the lexicon's abbreviations for the Gospel of
Nicodemus, Aristophanes, and Athenaeus are reproduced as printed and were not
independently checked at those loci.}
\end{studyframe}

Two facts about this word govern everything that follows.

\textbf{It is the burial verb.} There are exactly three occurrences in the New
Testament. Two of them describe what Joseph of Arimathea did to the body of
Jesus on Friday afternoon: \emph{enetylixen auto sindoni}, ``he wrapped it in
a clean linen cloth'' (Mt 27:59), ``he wrapped him in fine linen'' (Lk 23:53).
The third is Jn 20:7. The evangelist chose, for the state of the face-cloth in
the empty tomb, the verb his contemporaries used for swathing a corpse. The
Latin translator saw this and matched it: \emph{involvit sindone} at Lk 23:53,
\emph{involutum} at Jn 20:7, the same verb both times.

\textbf{It is a perfect, and the perfect is a state.} \emph{Entetyligmenon}
does not report an action performed that morning. It reports a condition
obtaining at the moment of observation: the cloth had been wrapped, and was
wrapped still. Nothing in the participle indicates who did the wrapping or
when, and the natural reference of a perfect in a burial narrative is to the
burial.

The English word ``folded'' does not occur in any of this. It is not in the
Greek, which says wrapped or rolled. It is not in the Latin, which says
\emph{involutum}. It is not in the Douay, which says ``wrapped up.'' It is not
in the Authorized Version, which says ``wrapped together.'' It entered English
devotional writing about this verse by other routes---one of them, ironically,
a Victorian translation of a Father: the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers
rendering of Augustine's Latin \emph{Tractates} prints the Vulgate lemma at
this verse as ``folded up in one place by itself.'' A reader who meets
``folded'' in an English text about John 20:7 is meeting a translator, not the
evangelist.

The point is not pedantry, and it is worth marking that it is conceded from
the other side. Henry Latham, whose 1901 book is the origin of most modern
English writing on the disposition of the cloths and who wanted the
arrangement to mean a great deal, wrote of this same participle:

\begin{studyframe}\small
``Now the word used for `rolled up' (\emph{entetyligmenon}) is employed
(Matthew xxvii.\ 59, and Luke xxiii.\ 53) to express the wrapping of the
Lord's body in the linen cloths, and it implies here that the napkin had been
wrapped round the head and partially retained the annular form thus given it;
I take it to mean that it was \emph{not} folded so as to lie flat like the
cloths.''\endnote{Henry Latham, \emph{The Risen Master: A Sequel to Pastor
Pastorum}, sixth thousand (Cambridge: Deighton Bell and Co.; London: George
Bell and Sons, 1905), p.~43. Greek transliterated.}
\end{studyframe}

And a few pages later, dismissing the notion that angels tidied the tomb:
``The Greek word, however, which is used about the napkin, does not imply a
folding up for neatness' sake.''\endnote{Latham, \emph{The Risen Master},
p.~45.}

\subsection{\emph{Ch\=oris} and \emph{eis hena topon}: where it was}

\emph{Ch\=oris} is an adverb meaning apart, separately, by itself. The Vulgate
renders it \emph{separatim}, the Douay ``apart.'' It is exact and uncontested.

\emph{Eis hena topon} is not. Literally it is ``into one place''---a
preposition of motion governing an accusative, attached to a description of
something at rest. Three readings are current, and the Greek does not
adjudicate between them. It may be the ordinary Koine slackening in which
\emph{eis} does duty for \emph{en}, so that the sense is ``in one place.'' It
may be emphatic: not scattered, but all gathered into a single spot.
\emph{Heis} may be functioning indefinitely, giving the Authorized Version's
``in a place by itself.''

The versions preserve the difficulty rather than solving it. The Vulgate keeps
the accusative---\emph{in unum locum}---and so does the Douay, ``into one
place.'' Westcott, printing ``rolled up in a place by itself'' in his English
column, footnoted it ``lit.\ apart in one place.''\endnote{B.~F. Westcott,
\emph{The Gospel according to St.\ John: The Greek Text with Introduction and
Notes}, vol.~2 (London: John Murray, 1908), p.~341, marginal English and note.}

What the phrase will not bear is a claim about the shape of the cloth. It
locates; it does not describe. Everything about the cloth's condition is
carried by \emph{entetyligmenon}, and that word says wrapped.

\subsection{What the description amounts to}

Assembling the six words, the sentence Peter is said to have seen reports the
following and no more:

\begin{studyframe}\small
The linen cloths were lying there. The head-cloth was not lying there with
them. It was separate, and it was in a wrapped-up condition, and it was in one
place.
\end{studyframe}

That is a strange thing to find, and it is a strange thing to write down sixty
years later. It is not, on the face of it, a description of tidiness. It is a
description of two things in different states in different parts of a small
chamber---and one of them still in the shape it had been given when it was
wound round a head. Whether that fact will support the weight two traditions
have put on it is the business of the sections that follow.
