# Scope: The Linen Cloths at the Empty Tomb

Provider: `claude`. Collection: `articles/scripture`. Governing profile:
`guidance/articles.md`, Scriptural studies section, with `guidance/editorial.md`
and `guidance/repository.md` universal. As-of date for every online witness:
25 July 2026.

## The claim under test

The study exists to test one sentence, stated in the maintainer's own words and
quoted at the head of section 1:

> St. Peter knew that Jesus had risen because he noticed how the burial cloths
> had been left.

The sentence is treated as an object of examination throughout. It is nowhere
assumed, and the study's own conclusion about it is divided rather than flat.

## The unit

One passage: John 20:3–10, with Luke 24:12 as the only other canonical narrative
of Peter at the tomb. Controls inside the same corpus: John 11:44; 19:38–42;
2:22; 12:16; Luke 19:20; 23:53; 24:1–14, 21–24, 34, 41; Matthew 27:59–66;
28:11–15; Acts 10:11; 11:5; 19:12; Mark 16:1–8.

The unit is small enough that every witness bearing on it can be read. It was.

## Questions worked

1. **Textual and philological.** What do `othonia`, `soudarion`, `keimena`,
   `entetyligmenon`, `chōris` and `eis hena topon` mean, on the evidence of
   their other occurrences and a named lexicon? What do the Vulgate and the
   Douay do with them?
2. **The centre.** To whom do the evangelists attribute the observation of the
   cloths, and to whom the inference? Do the harmonizations offered since
   antiquity close the gap?
3. **Why the arrangement should matter, explanation one.** Is Chrysostom's
   argument that the arrangement tells against theft sound, and how widely was
   it made?
4. **Why the arrangement should matter, explanation two.** Is the modern
   folded-napkin master-and-servant custom attested anywhere?

## Findings, in brief

**On the claim under test: divided, and the division is the finding.** The
observation is Peter's — John 20:6–7 is one sentence whose subject is Simon
Peter, and the face-cloth is never said to have been seen by anyone else. The
inference is not his: John gives the believing to the other disciple in the
singular, and Luke has Peter go away wondering, a participle Luke pairs with
`apistountōn` twenty-nine verses later. The premise survives in part and fails
in its operative half.

**The strongest counterargument is patristic, not sceptical, and is given at
full strength.** Chrysostom reads John 20:8 collectively and evidentially;
Cyril of Alexandria more explicitly still, calling the two disciples competent
witnesses because two in number as the Law enjoined and saying they infer the
Resurrection from the linen clothes; Ambrose says flatly that Peter could not
have doubted; and Aquinas, having given Augustine's reading as the resolution,
sets Chrysostom's beside it without closing it off. The study answers this
rather than ignoring it, and records that the maintainer's sentence is a
respectable minority reading with excellent credentials, not a modern invention.

**On the anti-theft argument: ancient, narrowly transmitted, sound in structure
and modest in reach.** One ancient source of any length (Chrysostom), thence
Theophylact, thence Aquinas. Augustine, Ambrose, Cyril, Gregory and Bede all
handle this material and none of them makes it. Westcott's own hedge — it was
"scarcely less clear" that the body had not been taken away by friends — is
where its strength ends.

**On the folded napkin: unattested, and this is the study's principal negative
result.** No Mishnaic, Talmudic, Toseftan, midrashic or minor-tractate text
located describes a napkin gesture as a master-to-servant signal. The searches,
their totals and their boundaries are printed in section 8. The one ancient
rabbinic ruling about a cloth at table (m. Berakhot 8:3 with b. Berakhot 52b) is
a ritual-purity dispute; the rabbis' actual end-of-meal marker (b. Berakhot 42a)
is the removal of the table; the corpus does record a `sudar` used as a
deliberate signal (m. Sanhedrin 6:1) and it halts an execution; Lightfoot, who
would have found such a custom if it existed, has no note on John 20:7 at all;
and the Greek participle means wrapped, not folded — a point conceded by the
author of the modern collapsed-cloths reading. The claim is datable to 2006 at
the latest and has never, in any instance located, cited a source.

**A correction recorded.** The date usually given for the claim's origin, 2007,
traces to a single labelled guess in TruthOrFiction's January 2008 assessment,
copied thence into GotQuestions and onward. A dated 2006 appearance was located.

## Excluded, deliberately

- The Shroud of Turin and the Sudarium of Oviedo. A different question with a
  different literature; section 10 states the exclusion and says why.
- The historicity of the Resurrection and of the empty tomb. The claim under
  test is about what two texts assert concerning one man's state of mind.
- Adjudication between Augustine's minimizing reading and the intermediate
  readings of `episteusen`. Both costs of Augustine's reading are stated; the
  study does not decide.
- The shape of the burial wrappings. The lexical base is five verses.
- Modern copyrighted Johannine commentaries and the two 1973 *Heythrop Journal*
  articles on this verse. Not reached, not relied on, listed as not reached.

## Open questions carried in the publication

Four, set out in section 10: the shape of the wrappings; the text of Luke 24:12;
whether the collapsed-cloths reading is right; and the Corpus Christianorum
numbering of Bede's homilies, which could not be verified and which bounds one
negative result to Migne's arrangement only.
