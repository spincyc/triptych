\section{What the checked Christian witnesses do}

The earlier edition grouped several Christian witnesses under a broad
negative. Exact-locus inspection overturns that result. The witnesses do not
form one answer, and their differences must be retained.

\subsection{Augustine: a bounded non-treatment}

Augustine discusses the impossibility of numbering the stars and allegorizes
the sunset and darkness in the covenant vision. At \textit{City of God}
XVI.23--24 he does not ask whether the stars could be seen at the hour of the
command. That is a negative result at two identified chapters, not a statement
about Augustine's whole corpus or Christian tradition.

\subsection{Ambrose: visionary movement}

Ambrose, \textit{De Abraham} II.8.48, treats Abram's being led outside as a
visionary movement beyond bodily confines. He does not settle a waking hour.
His reading instead supplies a concrete ancient instance of the vision answer
and prevents the study from treating Christian reception as silent.

\subsection{Chrysostom and Ephrem: an interval}

John Chrysostom, \textit{Homilies on Genesis} 36.13 and 37.3--6, places the
initial divine appearance by night and the sacrifice through the following day
to sunset. Ephrem's \textit{Commentary on Genesis} XII.2--5 is more explicit:
the words come at night, the commanded work occupies the day from dawn to
evening, and the later manifestation occurs that evening. Both witnesses
resolve the sequence by supplying elapsed time that Genesis does not narrate.
Their readings answer the difficulty without becoming a single patristic rule.

\subsection{Theodoret and the remaining limit}

Theodoret's \textit{Questions on Genesis} 66--67 does not address the hour.
That is only a local negative. Jerome's authenticated \textit{Hebrew Questions
on Genesis} remains unchecked, as do wider portions of the authors named here.
No author-wide or tradition-wide silence follows from this bounded corpus.

\subsection{Philo dissolves the clock}

Philo's treatise on this chapter reads the going-out as the soul's exit from
the body and the senses, the setting sun as the setting of the human intellect,
and the trance as prophetic possession. Immediately after the command to count
the stars he considers their uncountability, not the hour at which they could
be seen.\endnote{Philo, \textit{Quis rerum divinarum heres sit}, chapters
XVI--XVII, XX, LI--LIII, in Yonge's public-domain English. The checked extent
supports this local result; it does not establish Philo's treatment of every
possible related locus.} His allegorical treatment remains useful evidence for
one reception of the temporal markers. It no longer supplies a cause for an
alleged patristic silence.

\subsection{Josephus removes the evidence}

Josephus' identified retelling retains the posterity-like-stars comparison and
the commanded sacrifice while omitting the command to go outside and look and
all the temporal markers that generate the question.\endnote{Josephus,
\textit{Antiquities} I.10.3 in Whiston's numbering, read in the registered
public-domain witness.} This is a finding about that retelling's architecture,
not evidence that the question was absent from Christian tradition.

\subsection{The Targums keep the sequence}

Targum Onkelos renders the three controlling markers closely. Targum
Pseudo-Jonathan expands the dread and darkness at verses 12 and 17 but inserts
no night before verse 5, morning after verse 6, or explicit interval between
the promise and the rite.\endnote{Targum Onkelos and Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on
Gen 15:5, 12, and 17, read in the Aramaic and, for Pseudo-Jonathan, in
J.~W.~Etheridge's public-domain English of 1862.} The negative is bounded to
those inspected loci. It does not prove that every targumic witness preserves
the same sequence.

\subsection{Later Catholic commentators}

Cornelius a Lapide explicitly infers night from the command at verse 5:
\textit{erat ergo tunc nox}. At verse 12 he explains Abram's deep sleep by
daytime labour. Read together, the notes imply a night, a following day of
work, and a later sunset, although he does not spell out that chronology or
reconcile it with ``in that day.''\endnote{A Lapide on Gen 15:5 and 15:12,
1700 printing, read in the registered OCR-level witness; the text remains
uncollated against page images.}

The 1609 Douay apparatus has no note at verse 5. Haydock likewise supplies no
note there after using the verse at 15:1 as evidence that Abram was awake.
These are local publication-apparatus negatives. They remain worth reporting
without making them stand for Catholic reception as a whole.

\subsection{What the versional evidence cannot explain}

Swete's identified Greek printing weakens the chapter's closing temporal chain,
but Augustine's Old Latin retains ``in that day.'' Chrysostom supplies one
identified Greek Christian reception, while Ambrose and Ephrem supply different
Latin and Syriac receptions. These witnesses do not permit the claim that one
Greek form explains patristic silence. They permit only the more modest
finding that several Christian readers resolved or reframed the sequence in
different ways.
