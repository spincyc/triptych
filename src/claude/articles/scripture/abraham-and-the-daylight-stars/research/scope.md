## Claim boundary

The study reaches a conclusion about what Genesis 15 asserts about its own
sequence of hours. It reaches none about the historicity of the episode, the
composition of the chapter, its date, or what a man standing in Canaan would
have seen. It does not adjudicate the Jewish and Christian readings of Genesis
15:6, does not treat justification, and does not treat covenant theology.

## Result reached

The claim survives, narrowed and conditioned:

1. Genesis 15 read as one continuous scene in the order it narrates places the
   star-promise before its own sunset; every witness consulted preserves that
   arrangement, and none inserts an interval.
2. The claim holds as a claim about the text, not about a recoverable hour in
   the world.
3. It holds **only under one reading of the narrative syntax** — the reading on
   which the consecutive chain from 15:1 to 15:18 reports one continuous
   episode. That reading is natural and is supported by four independent
   considerations, but it is not compelled by grammar, and the study says so in
   those words.

## Structural findings

- **The difficulty was seen, and stated as a dilemma.** Abarbanel's seventh
  prefatory question on Genesis 15 (Warsaw 1862) puts it in the maintainer's own
  two moves: it cannot have been day because the stars are not visible by day,
  and it cannot have been night because of "and the sun was about to set" at
  15:12. This is the study's central historical finding and the reason it can be
  published without eccentricity.
- **The tradition's refusal of the daylight is astronomical, not exegetical.**
  Abarbanel says so explicitly (`i-efshar ... ki einam nir'im bayyom`). The
  sequence is granted; the premise about the sky is what forces the
  supplements.
- **Five distinct and mutually inconsistent answers exist**, and the study keeps
  them apart rather than harmonizing: the whole chapter as vision (Maimonides,
  Radak, Chizkuni); an unmarked intervening day (Ibn Ezra, and independently
  a Lapide); the going-out as deliverance from astrology (b. Shabbat 156a,
  Genesis Rabbah 44:12, Rashi, Ramban, Radak, Rabbeinu Bahya, Malbim); a
  transport above the firmament (Genesis Rabbah 44:12, Rashi's third reading,
  Rabbeinu Bahya); supernaturally enlarged sight (Malbim).
- **Nahmanides says the vision of 15:1 was a daytime vision.** This is the datum
  on which the study's claim that the vision frame does not remove the daylight
  most depends, and it is a claim Nahmanides makes for an unrelated reason
  (grades of prophecy).
- **The Septuagint de-temporalizes.** Swete's text has no equivalent of the
  Hebrew `alatah` at 15:17 and `ekei` for `bayyom hahu` at 15:18; the Greek
  chapter never reaches night. Augustine's Old Latin agrees at 15:17 and
  disagrees at 15:18, which bounds the second claim and is stated in the body.
- **Material negative result: the Fathers did not ask.** Bounded to Philo,
  Josephus, both Targums, Augustine, the 1609 Douay apparatus, a Lapide, and
  Haydock, each read at its own locus. The cause is identified: the Greek text
  removed the darkness, and Philo and Augustine allegorized every temporal
  marker in the chapter.
- **The "out from under the stars" reading is attested, and earlier than
  usually credited.** Philo has the astral-fatalism reading in the first
  century, attached to 15:7 and the departure from Ur; the Talmud transfers it
  to 15:5. Rashi and Ramban both rank it second, behind the tent. Both ancient
  versions render the going-out spatially and `habbet` as an upward look, which
  tells against the above-the-firmament construal.
- **The recurrences neither preserve nor dissolve the problem.** Genesis 15:5 is
  the only place in the star-promise tradition where anyone is commanded to
  perform an act of sight; everywhere else the stars are a term inside a speech.
- **Paul is unaffected.** He quotes the oracle of 15:5 and the reckoning of
  15:6, never the command to look. The study says so plainly and refuses to
  construct a rhetorical argument the apostle does not make.

## Included scope

The Masoretic Hebrew of Genesis 15 and its verbal forms; Pesachim 6b on
`ein muqdam ume'uhar` with Rav Pappa's limitation; the vision frame at 15:1 and
its extent; Swete's Septuagint of the chapter with his apparatus; the Clementine
Vulgate from a page image with its marginal and foot apparatus; the Challoner
and 1609 Douay English; Targum Onkelos and Pseudo-Jonathan at the three markers;
Philo's `Quis rerum divinarum heres sit`; Josephus, `Antiquities` I.7.2 and
I.8.2; Shabbat 156a; Genesis Rabbah 44:10 and 44:12; Augustine, `De civitate
Dei` XVI.23–24 in Latin and English; Rashi, Ibn Ezra, Ramban, Radak, Chizkuni,
Rabbeinu Bahya, the Tur, Malbim, the Netziv, and Abarbanel at their own loci;
Maimonides, `Guide` II.46; Cornelius a Lapide and Haydock on Genesis 15; the
countability tradition from Philo through Augustine and a Lapide to Malbim; the
eight recurrences of the star-promise; Romans 4 and Galatians 3.

## Excluded scope

Source criticism of Genesis 15; the date and composition of the chapter; the
"four hundred years" chronology; the identity of Eliezer and the obscurity of
15:2; the theology of the covenant between the pieces; the doctrine of
justification; Jewish–Christian polemic over 15:6; comparative ancient Near
Eastern covenant ritual; astronomy beyond what the witnesses themselves assert.

## Authority classes and boundaries

- **Sacred Scripture** governs the textual claims and is quoted only from the
  registered Douay–Rheims in Vulgate numbering.
- **Ancient versions** (Septuagint, Vulgate, Targums) are textual witnesses,
  not authorities on meaning.
- **Rabbinic and medieval Jewish commentary** is reception evidence. It is not
  treated as Catholic teaching, and its disagreements are preserved.
- **Patristic and later Catholic commentary** is reception evidence.
  A Lapide and Haydock are private commentators, not magisterial witnesses.
- **Project synthesis:** the sign-as-demand reading; the extension of Rav
  Pappa's limitation from halakhic derivation to narrative chronology; the
  observation that the above-the-firmament reading would incidentally dissolve
  the daylight problem; the five-answer classification; the reading of the
  Septuagint's changes as a consistent de-temporalizing tendency.

## Material qualifications

Carried in the rendered `Scope, Method, and Qualifications` appendix. The
consequential ones: the Septuagint claims are claims about Swete's printed text
and apparatus, with Rahlfs, the Göttingen `Genesis`, and Wevers not consulted;
the Hebrew was read in a digital text and not collated against a printed
Masoretic Bible; Jubilees, the Genesis Apocryphon, Targum Neofiti, the Samaritan
Pentateuch, and the Peshitta were not reached, and any of them could contain a
temporal marker that would settle the question against the claim; Jerome's
`Hebrew Questions`, Ambrose's `De Abraham`, Chrysostom on Genesis, and
Theodoret were not reached and are reported only as later authors report them;
a Lapide, Haydock, the 1609 Douay, Swete, Philo, and Josephus were read in
optical-character text layers and not collated against page images.
