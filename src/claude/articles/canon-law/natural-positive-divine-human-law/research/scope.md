- **Governing question:** what is the classical taxonomy of law — eternal, natural, divine positive, human (ecclesiastical and civil) — where does it come from, and what work does it do in the current canon law of the Latin Church?
- **Thesis (argued in epistemic order, not announced):** the taxonomy is two asymmetric distinctions (author; mode of institution), not four equal boxes — there is no humanly authored "natural law," and the eternal law stands over the scheme as its ground rather than inside it as a species; the vocabulary comes from Roman jurisprudence through Isidore (Etym. V), the theory from Aquinas (ST I-II qq. 90–97: natural law as participation of the eternal law; human law derived by conclusion and determination), the canonical reception from Gratian's opening distinctions; and the 1983 Code uses the map operatively — subjects of merely ecclesiastical law (c. 11), the positive-law lifecycle (cc. 7–9, 20–21), divine law as boundary (cc. 22, 24 §1, 199 1°, 748, 1290), divine institution (cc. 113, 129, 145, 207, 375, 1008), marriage (cc. 1059, 1075, 1084, 1163), penal law (cc. 1315, 1399), custom (cc. 5, 23–28), and dispensation (cc. 85–93, 1078–1080) — while the CCC (1950–1960) and Veritatis splendor (12, 40–45) teach the same participation doctrine against autonomist and heteronomist readings. Boundaries: the Church claims to declare, determine, fortify, and adjudicate divine law but not to make, amend, or dispense it; the lex-iniusta doctrine is a validity-and-prudence doctrine, not a resistance charter; the new-natural-law dispute is reported, not adjudicated.

## Argument order

1. Two canons and a question: c. 11's *mere*, c. 748 §1's *omnes homines*, c. 1075's declare/constitute — the Code presupposes a map it nowhere draws.
2. The inherited vocabulary: Isidore, Etym. V.ii–vi and xxi at the Lindsay page images; fas/ius; ius/lex/mos; the tripartite ius naturale/civile/gentium; the qualities of law; what Isidore lacks (no slot yet for revealed positive law).
3. – 5. The treatise on law worked through: q. 90 (definition, four articles); q. 91 (the kinds, five articles); q. 92 (effects); q. 93 (eternal law; derivation of all law); q. 94 (first precept, order of inclinations, universality, immutability, indelibility); q. 95 (necessity; conclusion vs determination; Isidore vindicated); q. 96 (limits; conscience and the two kinds of unjust law; subjection; letter and intention — the origin of dispensation); q. 97 (change; custom's three powers; the divine-law ceiling).
6. The shape of the taxonomy: two distinctions crossed asymmetrically; the empty fourth square; the taxonomy table (kind | author | institution/promulgation | mutability | dispensability/custom | loci).
7. Gratian's opening distinctions: D.1 (humanum genus duobus regitur; the divine=natural equation), D.4 (promulgation and confirmation by use; Telesphorus), D.8 (natural law prevails in dignity; uana et irrita), D.9 (ecclesiastical and secular constitutions contrary to natural law penitus excludendae); c. 6 §2 as the Code's own instruction of continuity.
8. The 1983 Code's working uses: lifecycle (cc. 7–9, 20–21); merely ecclesiastical law; divine law as boundary; divine institution; marriage; penal; the verified inventory table with the bounded search note.
9. Custom (cc. 23–28 + c. 5) worked canon by canon against q. 97 and D.8.
10. Dispensation (cc. 85–93) worked canon by canon; cc. 1078–1080 as the machinery under load; declare/constitute/dispense.
11. The magisterial exposition: CCC 1950–1953 (the taxonomy in one paragraph), 1954–1960 (the catechetical seven); VS 40–45 (participated theonomy), 12; DH 3.
12. Boundaries and debates: what the Church claims; what lex iniusta does not license; the reported debates (new natural law vs classical; register disputes within canon law; natural law in public argument); the claims-and-record audit; the question answered.
