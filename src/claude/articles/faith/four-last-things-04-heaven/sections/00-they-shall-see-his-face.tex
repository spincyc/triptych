\section{They Shall See His Face}

The Church ends her year with her eyes lifted. On the last Sunday after Pentecost the 1962 Missal reads the discourse of the Lord on the end of the age, with its promise that the nations ``shall see the Son of man coming in the clouds of heaven with much power and majesty''; and on the First Sunday of Advent, beginning the year again, it reads the same promise in Luke with a command attached: ``look up and lift up your heads, because your redemption is at hand.''\endnote{Matt 24:30; Luke 21:28. Douay--Rheims (Challoner). \emph{Missale Romanum} (1962), Dominica XXIV et ultima post Pentecosten, Evangelium (Matt 24:15--35); Dominica I Adventus, Evangelium (Luke 21:25--33).} The last weeks of the year are full of judgment, and rightly so. But the season was opened by another feast, and its first word was not fear. On All Saints both Missals read the Beatitudes, among them the one that names the whole of what this last of the last things is: ``Blessed are the clean of heart: for they shall see God.'' The 1962 Missal sings that verse again at the Communion of the day, so that the promise is on the Church's lips at the moment Christ is received; the Postconciliar Missal sets beside the Gospel a reading from the first epistle of John.\endnote{Matt 5:8. \emph{Missale Romanum} (1962), In festo Omnium Sanctorum, Evangelium (Matt 5:1--12) and Communio (Matt 5:8--10). \emph{Ordo lectionum Missae}, editio typica altera (1981), n.~667: Apoc 7:2--4, 9--14; 1 John 3:1--3; Matt 5:1--12a.}

That reading holds, in two sentences, nearly everything the Church knows about heaven, and the reason it cannot yet be told more fully:

\begin{quote}
Behold what manner of charity the Father hath bestowed upon us, that we should be called and should be the sons of God. \ldots\ Dearly beloved, we are now the sons of God: and it hath not yet appeared what we shall be. We know that when he shall appear we shall be like to him: because we shall see him as he is.\endnote{1 John 3:1--2, Douay--Rheims (Challoner).}
\end{quote}

The sentence begins in ignorance and does not apologise for it. \emph{It hath not yet appeared what we shall be.} Then it gives the reason, and the reason is the promise. What we shall be waits on what we shall see: we shall be like him \emph{because} we shall see him as he is. Likeness follows sight. No one can describe the end, because the end is a likeness produced by a sight that this life, lived by faith, does not give. The silence of revelation about what heaven is like is therefore not a gap in what was revealed. It is part of what was revealed, and it is full of hope. A doctrine with gaps would invite filling. This one tells us why it cannot be filled, and the telling is itself the gift. The apostle draws the practical consequence in the next verse: ``every one that hath this hope in him sanctifieth himself, as he also is holy.''\endnote{1 John 3:3, Douay--Rheims (Challoner).}

\subsection{The city described by what it lacks}

The last two chapters of Scripture describe the city of God at length, and nearly every line of the description takes something away. In the new heaven and the new earth ``the sea is now no more.'' ``God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes: and death shall be no more. Nor mourning, nor crying, nor sorrow shall be any more, for the former things are passed away.'' ``I saw no temple therein. For the Lord God Almighty is the temple thereof, and the Lamb.'' ``The city hath no need of the sun, nor of the moon, to shine in it.'' ``The gates thereof shall not be shut by day: for there shall be no night there.'' ``There shall be no curse any more.''\endnote{Apoc 21:1, 4, 22, 23, 25; 22:3, Douay--Rheims (Challoner).} No sea, no death, no mourning, no crying, no sorrow, no temple, no sun, no night, no shut gate, no curse. The Apocalypse speaks of heaven mainly by subtraction, as one must speak of what the hearer has no positive notion of: it clears away every condition of this life that wounds, until the cleared space is ready for what will fill it.

Then, among the negations, one clause stands that takes nothing away:

\begin{quote}
And they shall see his face: and his name shall be on their foreheads.\endnote{Apoc 22:4, Douay--Rheims (Challoner).}
\end{quote}

Everything else in the vision is said for the sake of that line. The gates are open because there is nothing left to fear; there is no temple because the whole city stands in the presence of God; there is no sun because ``the glory of God hath enlightened it: and the Lamb is the lamp thereof.''\endnote{Apoc 21:23, Douay--Rheims (Challoner).} And the name written on the foreheads is the seal of belonging to the one whose face is seen.

The saints have never thought this made heaven poorer. Alphonsus, setting the joys of paradise before his readers, gives them a homely warning about the measure of their imagination:

\begin{quote}
Were a horse capable of reasoning, he would, if he expected a rich feast from his master, imagine it to consist in excellent hay and oats; for these are the only species of food of which he has any idea. It is thus we form our notions of the happiness of Heaven.\endnote{Alphonsus Liguori, \emph{Preparation for Death}, Consideration XXIX, first point (Dublin: James Duffy, 1843).}
\end{quote}

The images of Scripture are not hay and oats. They are revealed, and the Church gathers them with reverence: ``Scripture speaks of it in images: life, light, peace, wedding feast, wine of the kingdom, the Father's house, the heavenly Jerusalem, paradise.''\endnote{\emph{Catechism of the Catholic Church} 1027.} But every one of them is gathered around a centre that no image can draw, because its object has no shape. A picture can show a city, a feast, a garden and a multitude in white. It cannot show the vision of God, and the vision of God is what the city, the feast and the multitude are for.

\subsection{Heaven is a Person, seen}

So the last of the last things is not, in the first place, a place, though the Church rightly speaks of it as one; nor a reward, though it is the reward of the just; nor even a reunion, though it is that too. Heaven is a Person, seen. The Catechism states the order of causes plainly: ``The beatific vision, in which God opens himself in an inexhaustible way to the elect, will be the ever-flowing well-spring of happiness, peace, and mutual communion.''\endnote{\emph{Catechism of the Catholic Church} 1045.} The rest, the company, the risen body, the song: all of it flows from the seeing, and all of it is enjoyed by those who see.

That is why the Church, at the end of her year, does not ask her children first to imagine heaven. She asks them to desire it, with the desire of the psalm: ``As the hart panteth after the fountains of water; so my soul panteth after thee, O God. My soul hath thirsted after the strong living God; when shall I come and appear before the face of God?''\endnote{Ps 41:2--3, Douay--Rheims (Challoner).} And when one of her children is carried out of the church to the grave, she does not explain heaven over the coffin. She sings the soul into it: \latin{In paradisum deducant te Angeli}.
