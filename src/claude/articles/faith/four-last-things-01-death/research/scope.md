# The Debt of Nature — Research Scope and Qualification Record

## Work identity and governing question

- **Provider and collection:** Anthropic Claude; faith and theology articles.
- **Genre and profile:** discursive theological article; `guidance/articles.md` governs. Theology governs the whole argument. One section states current Latin-Church discipline for the dying; that material follows the canon-law requirements of the profile (governing code, jurisdiction, promulgating authority, as-of date) but the article makes no canonical conclusion and gives no advice on a case.
- **Series position:** Volume One of *The Four Last Things*, a four-part Claude series whose remaining volumes treat judgment, hell, and heaven. Each volume is a complete study and none depends on another. Volumes two, three, and four are being authored concurrently by other agents; this volume refers to them by title only, never by page or section, and creates no shared TeX fragment.
- **Series relationship to the GPT edition:** an independent Claude edition of the leaf `articles/faith/four-last-things-01-death`. It shares the leaf title and subject territory with the GPT edition but is an independent work: its research, argument order, examples, section structure, and prose were produced without reading the GPT edition's sections, main file, or PDF. Only that edition's `research/scope.md` was consulted, for topic parity (mortality, sin and bodily corruptibility, Christ's death, soul/body distinction, intermediate state, resurrection, preparation, accompaniment, grief). The Claude edition deliberately does not build its spine on the GPT edition's governing claim (death as the last enemy entered and defeated by Christ, with accompaniment, proportionate care, and grief). Its own spine is: the name and the enumeration; the definition of death and its dogmatic supports; the unlocatable instant; the natural/penal distinction; the two tenses of the New Testament; the state of the separated soul and the magisterial boundary; the particular judgment's substance and name; the *ars moriendi*; and the Church's law and liturgy for the dying. Christ's transformation of death is treated in one section and is structurally subordinate rather than the spine.
- **Language, rite, jurisdiction, currentness:** English. No liturgical rite governs a conclusion. The canonical material is the 1983 Code of Canon Law for the Latin Church, as of 2026-07-26. Every date in the article and in these records is the UTC calendar day; the render timestamp is likewise UTC.
- **Intended reader:** a serious general Catholic reader able to follow careful distinctions but not presumed to know scholastic terminology.
- **Governing question:** What is human death, why does the tradition count it first among the last things, and where does the Church's teaching about it stop?
- **Thesis:** the fourfold enumeration is a devotional and pedagogical ordering with no conciliar promulgation, and death holds first place because it is the only member of the set that anyone can observe; death is the separation of soul and body, a definition given dogmatic edges by Vienne and Lateran V, so that dying is the dissolution of a single nature and not the release of a passenger; the instant of dissolution is real and unlocatable, a result Augustine reached and canon 1005 quietly presupposes; "death is natural" and "death is the wages of sin" answer two different questions and are not competing accounts of one; Christ's work cancels the penal meaning while leaving the natural fact standing until the resurrection, which is the exact shape of the New Testament's two tenses; what survives death is a conscious subject that is not the person, in a condition Aquinas calls contrary to its nature; *Benedictus Deus* defines a when and a what and nothing else, and the popular confidence about the interval has no support in any document examined; the accounting at death is immediate and irrevocable while the communion with the dead is not broken; the *ars moriendi* is influential devotional literature of unknown authorship and no doctrinal authority whose central counsel survives in one paragraph of the Catechism; and the Church's practice for the dying inverts the popular picture at both ends, since anointing is for the sick rather than only for the dying and viaticum, not anointing, is the last sacrament.

## Argument order and adopted terms

The body moves in the order in which its own evidence becomes available:

1. **The Verse That Named Them** — Ecclus 7:40 read at its own locus and in its context of works of mercy; the plural *novissima tua*; Denys the Carthusian's *De quatuor novissimis*; the fourfold list as two stations and a fork rather than a sequence; the Catechism's own six headings and its placement of death under the resurrection of the body; death first because it is the only observable last thing.
2. **What Comes Apart** — the definition and its commitments; Augustine's three deaths (De civ. Dei XIII.2, XIII.6); Vienne DS 902 and Lateran V DS 1440–1441; CCC 365–366 and GS 14; ST I.75.4; the Christological test case at ST III.50.2–3.
3. **The Moment No One Occupies** — De civ. Dei XIII.9–11; the present-instant comparison; canon 1005 and the labelled project synthesis about jurisprudence over an admitted indeterminacy, with its counterargument and defeater; an express boundary excluding medical criteria.
4. **Two Questions, and Only One Answer Each** — CCC 1006 and 1008; Gen 2:17 and 3:19, Wis 1:13 and 2:23–24, Rom 5:12 and 6:23 read at their own loci; Trent session V canons 1–2 in Latin and English with an exact statement of what is not defined; ST I-II.85.5 (the pillar and the stone), I-II.85.6 (matter and form, the knife and the rust, *supplevit defectum naturae*), II-II.164.1 ad 1; the major labelled project synthesis with three reasons, the strongest counterargument at full strength, and the finding that would defeat it.
5. **The Wage Paid, the Enemy Still in the Field** — 2 Tim 1:10, Heb 2:14–15, 1 Cor 15:25–26; Cyprian *De mortalitate* 8–9; De civ. Dei XIII.4–5 and XIII.7; CCC 1009–1011; Phil 1:21–24; a labelled synthesis mapping the two tenses onto the two questions.
6. **What the Soul Is Doing** — the Old Testament's reticence (Ps 6:6, Job 14, Eccles 12:7) and its late development (Wis 3:1–3, CCC 992); the New Testament loci each at its own place, with the observation that 2 Cor 5:4 states a preference against the unclothed condition and a caution about reading the Lazarus parable as description; ST I.89.1 and *Super I Cor.* 15 lect. 2 with its *reportatio* caveat; John XXII's *Ne super his* and *Benedictus Deus* with the exact boundary of what is defined; LG 49; CDF 1979 nn. 3–5 and the bounded status of the resurrection-in-death proposal; a closing statement of the whole question by weight.
7. **The Judgment at the Door** — Heb 9:27–28 read inside its own argument; CCC 1021–1022 and the distinction between the defined substance and the theological name, with a bounded negative result about the phrase; the end of the time of merit; what death does not sever; handoff to volume two by title.
8. **The Craft** — Comper's evidence that the *ars moriendi* is not Gerson's; the six chapters, the five temptations, Anselm's interrogations, the complaint about physicians and false comfort; the provision for exhortation "by a priest, or for need by another"; CCC 1014 and the inverted petition against sudden death; an explicit statement that none of it is magisterial.
9. **What the Church Actually Does** — James 5:14–15; Trent session XIV chapters I–III and canons 1–4, with chapter III as the load-bearing locus; SC 73–74; CCC 1514–1532; viaticum as the last sacrament with cann. 921–922; CCC 1020 and the Prayer of Commendation; the return to Ecclus 7:37–40 and the handoff to the remaining volumes.

**Adopted terms.** *Novissima* is used in the plural sense the Vulgate has. *Separation of soul and body* is used in the technical sense fixed by Vienne and Lateran V. *Natural* and *penal* are used in the senses distinguished at ST I-II.85.6 and II-II.164.1 ad 1 and nowhere else. *Definitive*, *authoritative non-definitive*, *common* or *school theology*, *disputed opinion*, and *project synthesis* are the article's weight classes and are restated in the terminal synopsis. New project images (the anthropology with the sound turned down; the demolition rather than the departure; the hand playing an instrument it was not shaped for; the river arriving at the sea; the account that is closed while the communion is not) are project synthesis.

## Included scope

1. the scriptural origin of the phrase *novissima* and the character of the fourfold enumeration;
2. the definition of death as separation, with its conciliar supports and its Christological test case;
3. Augustine's aporia about the instant and the Church's disciplinary response to it;
4. the natural and penal accounts, what Trent defines and does not, and the Thomistic reconciliation;
5. the transformation of death's significance in Christ, with the patristic refusals to sentimentalize it;
6. the Old Testament's reticence, the New Testament loci, and the Thomistic analysis of the separated soul;
7. the John XXII controversy, the definition of *Benedictus Deus*, and the exact boundary of what is defined;
8. the 1979 letter, the bounded status of the resurrection-in-death proposal, and the tripartite state at LG 49;
9. the particular judgment's defined substance and theological name, and the end of the time of merit;
10. the *ars moriendi* tradition: authorship, contents, reception, and what the Catechism retained;
11. the Church's sacramental and liturgical practice for the dying under current Latin law.

## Excluded scope

The determination of death by medical criteria, organ procurement, withholding or withdrawing treatment, euthanasia and assisted suicide, and all applied bioethics; purgatory and the final purification; hell and its punishments; the beatific vision beyond the timing and object defined in 1336; the general judgment and eschatological chronology; the resurrection of the body beyond what the definition of death requires; limbo and the fate of the unbaptized; Christology and soteriology, presupposed and undeveloped; the theology of martyrdom; funeral rubrics, cremation, burial law, and the pastoral care of the bereaved; private revelations and near-death experiences; the scholastic disputes over the fixity of the separated will and the twentieth-century debates over a final option at death; and any judgment about the state, culpability, or destiny of any person.

## Material limits

Fully stated in the terminal appendix.
