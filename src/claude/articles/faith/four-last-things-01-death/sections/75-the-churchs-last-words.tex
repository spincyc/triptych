\section{The Church's Last Words}

When the sacraments have been given and the agony begins, the Church does not fall silent. The Roman Ritual provides an order for the commendation of a departing soul, and the Baltimore \emph{Manual} gives its rubric in English: ``Then while the soul is in the agony of its departure, the Priest recites the following prayers.''\endnote{\emph{Rituale Romanum}, Ordo commendationis animae, rubric before the prayer \latin{Proficiscere}, in the English of \emph{A Manual of Prayers} (1889), 514.} Some of the prayers are addressed to God for the dying man, and some to the dying man himself. The first of them begins \latin{Proficiscere, anima christiana}, ``Go forth, O Christian soul,'' and it is the Church's farewell to her child.

A prayer for pardon follows, \latin{Deus misericors}, and after it the commendation proper, which opens with the words on the debt of humanity already heard and then surrounds the departing soul with the whole court of heaven: ``When, therefore, thy soul goeth forth from thy body, may the glorious company of Angels meet thee; may the council of the Apostles who shall judge the world greet thee; may the triumphant army of white-robed Martyrs come out to welcome thee; may the band of shining Confessors, crowned with lilies, encircle thee; may the choir of joyous Virgins receive thee; and unto the bosom of blessed rest may the embrace of the Patriarchs clasp thee; gentle and joyful may the Face of Jesus Christ appear to thee, and may He award thee a place among those who stand before Him for ever.''\endnote{\emph{Rituale Romanum}, Ordo commendationis animae, the prayers \latin{Deus misericors} and \latin{Commendo te}, in \emph{A Manual of Prayers} (1889), 515--517.}

The prayer knows the danger it is asking God to avert. It names ``the terror of darkness, the gnashing of teeth in the flames, the agonies of torment,'' and prays that the soul may never know them. It bids Satan ``give way'' and ``flee away into the vast chaos of eternal night.'' And it closes with the sight that is the end of every Christian life: ``Mayest thou behold thy Redeemer face to face; and, standing ever before Him, gaze with blessed eyes on the Truth made manifest.''\endnote{\latin{Commendo te}, \emph{A Manual of Prayers} (1889), 517--518.} Here, at the door, the Church holds both of the last things that lie beyond it together, the fire and the Face, and prays for the one with all her strength. At the deathbed they are not subjects of study. They are the two ends of a road a man is walking that night.

As the soul is about to depart, the Baltimore \emph{Manual} directs those present to pray more earnestly than ever, and if the dying can no longer speak, to say again and again in his ear the name of Jesus and such words as these: ``Into Thy hands, O Lord, I commend my spirit.---O Lord Jesus Christ, receive my spirit.---Holy Mary, pray for me.---O Mary, mother of grace, mother of mercy, do thou protect me from the enemy, and receive me at the hour of death.''\endnote{\emph{A Manual of Prayers} (1889), The Last Agony, 531; Luke 23:46.} The first of them are the words in which Christ himself died. The Christian dies in the words of his Lord.

\subsection{Come to his assistance}

The moment of death passes unmarked by any sign that the bystanders can see. The Church marks it by a responsory, said as soon as the soul has gone:

\begin{quote}
Come to his assistance, ye Saints of God, come forth to meet him, ye Angels of the Lord: Receiving his soul: Offering it in the sight of the Most High.

\emph{V.} May Christ receive thee, who hath called thee, and may the Angels bear thee into Abraham's bosom.

\emph{R.} Receiving, etc.

\emph{V.} Eternal rest grant unto him, O Lord, and let perpetual light shine upon him.

\emph{R.} Offering, etc.\endnote{The responsory \latin{Subvenite, Sancti Dei}, \emph{Rituale Romanum} (1872), Ordo commendationis animae; English from \emph{A Manual of Prayers} (1889), The Last Agony, 531--532.}
\end{quote}

\noindent The living cannot follow the soul, but they can send it on with the saints and the angels. In the \latin{Subvenite} the Church speaks to heaven on behalf of one who has just left her sight, and asks heaven to come out to meet him.

\subsection{Life is changed, not taken away}

Then the Church carries her dead into her churches and offers for them the Sacrifice of the Mass. In the Roman Missal of 1920, every Mass for the dead, and so the Masses of All Souls' Day, has a Preface that answers in a single sentence everything the Fathers said about death:

\begin{quote}
It is truly meet and just, right and profitable for us, at all times, and in all places, to give thanks to Thee, O holy Lord, Father almighty, eternal God: through Christ, our Lord, in Whom the hope of a happy resurrection has shone on us, so that those whom the certain fate of dying renders sad, may be consoled by the promise of future immortality. For with regard to Thy faithful, O Lord, life is changed, not taken away; and the house of their earthly dwelling being destroyed, an eternal dwelling in heaven is obtained.\endnote{The Preface for the Dead, in Lasance, \emph{The New Roman Missal} (1945), Masses for the Dead. Latin in the \emph{Missale Romanum} (typical edition of 1920): \latin{Tuis enim fidelibus, Domine, vita mutatur, non tollitur: et, dissoluta terrestris hujus incolatus domo, \ae terna in coelis habitatio comparatur}.}
\end{quote}

\noindent \latin{Vita mutatur, non tollitur}. The Preface does not deny the sadness. It names ``the certain fate of dying'' and admits that it ``renders sad.'' Nor does it deny that the earthly house is destroyed; it says so in the words of the Apostle.\endnote{2 Corinthians 5:1, Douay--Rheims (Challoner).} But it sets beside the sadness the promise, and beside the ruined house the eternal one. This is the whole Christian teaching on death, prayed rather than argued: the wrench is real, the debt is paid, and the life that was given in baptism is changed in its condition and is not taken away.

The Office of the Dead says the same in the voice of Job. Its first lesson begs, ``spare me, for my days are nothing.'' Its eighth lesson answers: ``For I know that my Redeemer liveth, and in the last day I shall rise out of the earth. And I shall be clothed again with my skin, and in my flesh I shall see my God.''\endnote{Job 19:25--26, Douay--Rheims (Challoner); the Office of the Dead, Matins, Lectio VIII, \emph{Rituale Romanum} (1872): \latin{Scio enim, quod Redemptor meus vivit, et in novissimo die de terra surrecturus sum}.} The body laid in the grave will see God. That is why the Church buries her dead with honour, and why she does not cease to pray.
