\section{The Debt of Nature}

The Church says what death is in a single sentence: ``In that `departure' which is death the soul is separated from the body.''\endnote{\emph{Catechism of the Catholic Church} 1005, official English (Vatican web text).} The sentence is short because the faith behind it is settled. The Council of Vienne defined that the rational soul is of itself and essentially the form of the human body, and the Fifth Lateran Council that this soul is immortal and is multiplied with the bodies into which it is infused.\endnote{Council of Vienne, constitution \latin{Fidei catholicae} (1312), DS 902; Fifth Lateran Council, session VIII, bull \latin{Apostolici regiminis} (1513), DS 1440.} A man is therefore not a spirit lodged in a body, to be let out at the end like a bird from a cage. He is one living thing, soul and body together, and death undoes that unity. What dies is the man. What survives is his spiritual soul, still knowing and loving, which waits in its separation for the body God will give back to it on the last day. That the souls of the just, once purified, see God even before they take up their bodies again is the teaching of Benedict~XII, expounded in \emph{What Death Does Not Finish}.\endnote{Benedict XII, constitution \latin{Benedictus Deus} (1336), DS 1000: \latin{etiam ante resumptionem suorum corporum}.}

Because body and soul belong together, their parting is violent, and the Fathers did not pretend otherwise. Augustine will not let the faithful call it gentle. ``As regards bodily death, that is, the separation of the soul from the body, it is good unto none while it is being endured by those whom we say are in the article of death. For the very violence with which body and soul are wrenched asunder, which in the living had been conjoined and closely intertwined, brings with it a harsh experience, jarring horridly on nature so long as it continues\ldots''\endnote{Augustine, \emph{De civitate Dei} XIII.6, trans. Marcus Dods (Edinburgh: T. \& T. Clark, 1871).} Sometimes, he adds, the anguish is forestalled by a sudden stroke. However it comes, ``when it is piously and faithfully borne, it increases the merit of patience, but does not make the name of punishment inapplicable.''\endnote{\emph{De civitate Dei} XIII.6, Dods.} Here is the first thing the Christian must know about death. Whatever grace may do with it, it is in itself a wrench, a tearing apart of what God joined, and to feel it so is no failure of faith.

\subsection{Natural, and the wages of sin}

Why should there be such a tearing at all? The Church gives two answers and keeps both. ``In a sense bodily death is natural, but for faith it is in fact `the wages of sin.'\,''\endnote{CCC 1006, quoting Romans 6:23.} It is natural because man is made of the dust, a body composed of elements that, left to themselves, come apart. It is the wages of sin because God did not leave man to himself. Scripture says it twice over: ``For God made not death, neither hath he pleasure in the destruction of the living,'' and ``the wages of sin is death.''\endnote{Wisdom 1:13; Romans 6:23; Douay--Rheims (Challoner).} The Council of Trent defines that Adam, when he transgressed the commandment in paradise, lost the holiness and justice in which he had been set and incurred the death with which God had threatened him.\endnote{Council of Trent, session V (1546), decree on original sin, canon 1, DS 1511.}

Thomas Aquinas joins the two answers with an image from Aristotle. Sin does not cause death in the way a blow causes a wound. It causes death as the man who pulls away a prop causes what was resting on it to fall: ``thus it is stated in \emph{Phys.} viii, text. 32, that `by displacing a pillar a man moves accidentally the stone resting thereon.' In this way the sin of our first parent is the cause of death and all such like defects in human nature, in so far as by the sin of our first parent original justice was taken away.''\endnote{Thomas Aquinas, \emph{Summa theologiae} I-II, q.~85, a.~5, corpus, trans. Fathers of the English Dominican Province.} The stone was always heavy. God's gift held it up. When the gift was lost, the stone fell, and it falls still on every child of Adam. The doctrine is not a theory of biology. It is a statement about a gift: man was made for a life that the dust of which he is formed could not of itself secure, and he lost that life by turning from the Giver.

Ambrose, returning on the seventh day to the grave of his brother Satyrus, looks at the same sentence of Genesis from the side of mercy. He notices that God did not say to Adam, ``because thou hast hearkened to the voice of the woman thou shalt return to the earth.'' The curse fell on the ground and its thorns. The return to the earth was named as the term of man's labour: ``In sweat shalt thou eat thy bread until thou return to the earth.'' ``Death is given for a remedy,'' Ambrose concludes, ``because it is the end of evils. \ldots{} You see that death is rather the goal of our penalties, by which an end is put to the course of this life.''\endnote{Ambrose, \emph{De excessu fratris sui Satyri} II.38, trans. H. de Romestin, in \emph{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, second series, vol.~10.} Augustine stresses the penalty and Ambrose the remedy, and the Church keeps both. Death is the punishment of a race that sinned; it is also the mercy that will not let a fallen life go on for ever in its misery.

\subsection{The debt paid}

The tradition has a name for death that holds all of this together. It calls it a debt. The anonymous \emph{Craft of Dying} of the fifteenth century counts among the blessings of a good death the ``paying of [the] debt of natural duty.''\endnote{\emph{The Book of the Craft of Dying}, ch.~1, ed. Frances M. M. Comper (London: Longmans, Green, 1917), brackets as printed.} The Church's own prayer at the deathbed says the same, as the priest commends the dying man to God: ``I commend thee to Almighty God, dearly beloved brother, and commit thee to Him whose creature thou art; that, when thou shalt have paid the debt of humanity by passing through death, thou mayest return to thy Maker, who formed thee from the dust of the earth.''\endnote{\emph{Rituale Romanum}, Ordo commendationis animae, the prayer \latin{Commendo te}, in \emph{A Manual of Prayers for the Use of the Catholic Laity} (New York: Catholic Publication Society, 1889), 516; Latin \latin{humanitatis debitum}.} The debt is owed by nature, since every man is dust; it is owed by justice, since every man is a son of Adam; and it is paid only once. The word gives death its gravity without despair. A debt is real and cannot be evaded. But a debt paid is a debt discharged, and the one who pays it goes free.

So the Christian looks at death with both eyes open. He does not call it a friend, because Scripture calls it an enemy, and the last to be destroyed.\endnote{1 Corinthians 15:26, Douay--Rheims (Challoner): ``And the enemy, death, shall be destroyed last.''} He does not call it nothing, because it is the wrench that Augustine felt. But neither does he meet it as the pagan meets it, as the end of everything. For the debt that Adam contracted has been met by another, and the enemy that holds the world has itself been made to serve.
