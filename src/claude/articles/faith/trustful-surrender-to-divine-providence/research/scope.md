- **Intended reader:** a serious lay reader who has met the counsel of surrender to divine providence, most likely through the small devotional volume of that name, and wants to know what it means, where it comes from, and where its limits lie.
- **Governing question:** What does a Catholic actually surrender when he surrenders to divine providence, and what does he not — does the doctrine subtract anything from creaturely agency, deliberation, prudence, resistance to evil, or the hope of salvation?
- **Thesis:** surrender subtracts none of these. It is the graced ordering of a will that remains a real secondary cause, resting on the distinction between what is in our power (the care) and what is not (the event). The boundary against quietism, fatalism, and presumption is not modern caution but a line drawn twice in juridical form — in *Coelestis Pastor* (1687) and *Cum alias ad apostolatus* (1699) — and available for inspection proposition by proposition.

## Argument and section order

The body moves in epistemic order:

1. the counsel as it actually presents itself, with the two independent difficulties (textual and doctrinal) distinguished;
2. the textual history of the classic English handbook, tested against the public-domain originals of both named authors, with an attribution table;
3. the scriptural loci in the Douay–Rheims, with the grammar the tradition supplies for the hard sayings (fault/penalty; CCC 304's rule of reading);
4. the Thomistic architecture: providence as plan and government as execution; universal extension and chance; immediacy of design with mediated execution; contingency preserved; God working in every agent and moving the will without coercion; antecedent and consequent will; the evil of fault never willed; fate; patience;
5. the history of abandonment as a school: Francis de Sales's two wills and holy indifference; Guyon, Bossuet, and Fénelon; the attribution history of the treatise transmitted under Caussade's name; Ramière's editorial fence;
6. the boundaries, worked through the condemned propositions of 1687 and 1699 with a four-column table, then fatalism, presumption, passivity and prudence, and the compressed devotional locution;
7. suffering, secondary causes, and the rules of speech that follow (the Joseph pattern; lament; why secondary causes matter pastorally);
8. a positive account of what surrender is: the four acts, the present moment, an inventory of what is and is not surrendered, and a bounded pastoral note.

An appendix manifests the witnesses and editions with their rights bases, ceilings, and consequential negative results; the terminal apparatus is the Scope, Method, and Qualifications appendix, the References, and the generation metadata.

## Consequential textual findings

The textual section reports three identifications, each with its own evidentiary ceiling:

1. **Part I** of the English anthology is an extract from Saint-Jure, *De la connaissance et de l'amour du Fils de Dieu*, book 3, ch. 8 (§§ I–VIII). Basis: identical opening authority and locus (ST I, q. 19, a. 4) and the correspondence of the fourteen practice-of-conformity heads with §§ VII–VIII. Not a line-by-line collation.
2. **Part II, ch. IV** is a compilation from at least four Colombière sermons (*Sur la soumission à la volonté de Dieu*; *Sur la confiance en Dieu*; *Sur la prière*; *Sur les adversités*), matched by the printed theses in the 1832 *Œuvres*, vol. 5. Sermon-by-sermon collation was not performed.
3. **Part II, ch. V**, printed under Colombière's name, corresponds to Saint-Jure's § X (*Exercice particulier envers la providence divine*): four movements rendered as three heads in the same order, with the same maternal image and the same two psalms. Strongly evidenced, not certain: a parallel in Colombière's own corpus was searched for by keyword over five volumes and the 1684 *Réflexions chrétiennes*, and not found, but the corpus was not exhaustively examined.

The section also reports reception drift (extracts circulating under Colombière's name alone) and the ordinary Blessed→Saint change across printings.

## Included and excluded scope

Included: the publishing and attribution history of the English handbook and the identification of its parts; the scriptural loci and the rules for reading the hard sayings; the Thomistic account of providence, government, non-competitive causality, contingency, the divine will, permitted evil, fate, presumption, and patience; the history of abandonment from de Sales through the quietist controversy to the Ramière recension; the condemned propositions with their graded censures; the distinction of surrender from quietism, fatalism, presumption, and imprudent passivity; the pastoral discipline governing what may be said to a sufferer; and a positive account of the acts constituting surrender.

Excluded: predestination, physical premotion, and *scientia media*, and any adjudication of the Thomist–Molinist controversy; a complete theodicy; a history of Western mysticism or a general assessment of Madame Guyon; Bossuet's own theology of prayer beyond his reported role; critical-edition study of any text examined; the canonical or historical assessment of Molinos's trial; psychological or medical advice; direction for any particular case; and any claim about the interior state, culpability, or salvation of any individual.

## Material qualifications

1. The three textual identifications carry the ceilings stated above; identification 3 is stated as strongly evidenced rather than certain.
2. The publisher's claim that Saint-Jure's work was the constant companion of the Curé of Ars is reported as the publisher's; no independent witness was checked.
3. Nothing in the textual section impugns the 1961 imprimatur, which attests doctrinal review of the English book, not authorship or textual criticism.
4. The Thomistic account of divine motion of the will affirms non-coercion and imputability; it does not settle how efficacious grace and free consent are related, which remains disputed among permitted schools.
5. "Permissive will" is not treated as a second faculty in God; the governing formula is that the one simple divine will knowingly wills not to prevent a defect it could prevent while causing every positive actuality.
6. The fault/penalty distinction licenses no inference from a particular affliction to a particular punishment.
7. De Sales's sentence about preferring hell to heaven is quoted with its governing "supposition of an impossible thing" and is not offered as a state to be sought.
8. Fénelon's condemnation contains no charge of heresy; the two acts of 1687 and 1699 are kept distinct in kind and in censure.
9. The dates for *Coelestis Pastor* conflict between the two witnesses consulted (20 November and 2 November 1687); both are recorded and no argument depends on the resolution.
10. The attribution of *L'Abandon à la providence divine* to Caussade is reported as doubted at the ceiling of the named witness; no alternative attribution is asserted.
11. Ramière's preface is a nineteenth-century editor's apparatus, quoted as such and argued from its content.
12. The rules of speech for consolation are project synthesis from CCC 309–314 and the Thomistic distinctions; they have no ecclesiastical standing.
13. Nothing in the article is spiritual direction, medical or psychological advice, or counsel for a person in danger; where surrender might be read as requiring silence or endurance in the face of abuse, crime, or life-threatening illness, the article states expressly that it does not.
