\section{Scope, Method, and Qualifications}

\subsection{Reader, question, and method}

This is a discursive metaphysical and theological article for a serious general reader, able to follow careful distinctions but not presumed to know scholastic terminology. It is Part I of a three-part series, \emph{The Creature Before God}; each part is a complete essay, and this one carries the series' foundation. Its governing question is what a creature apprehends when the metaphysics of received being is recognized as the present truth about the knower---the condition this essay names \emph{ontological vertigo}---and whether that apprehension must end in recoil or can be consented to without self-deception. Its claim: existence in creatures is received, not possessed; the reception is presently and continuously sustained; the dependence is asymmetric, the sustaining source receiving nothing in return; recoil misreads this asymmetry through a same-floor rivalry picture that the argument itself rules out; and consent, reading the asymmetry as gift, terminates naturally in the analogical judgment that God loves all existing things---at which point natural reason reaches its boundary.

The argument proceeds in epistemic order: the becoming/being distinction and the existential-inertia alternative, stated without theological premises; the Aristotelian and Platonic instruments with their historical limits; the Thomistic essence--existence distinction, participation, and the First Way with its own stated limits; conservation and divine intimacy, argued philosophically and then attested by patristic and magisterial witnesses cited \emph{as} received teaching; the reflexive turn, the coinage, and the one-sided real relation; the diagnosis of recoil; and consent, the analogical judgment of divine love, and apophatic restraint. Revealed claims (free creation, the Word as sustainer, Trinity) are labeled as received where they appear and are nowhere presented as conclusions of the philosophical ascent. English is the working language; no liturgical rite, civil or canonical jurisdiction, or mutable discipline governs any conclusion. All online witnesses were checked on 2026-07-24.

\subsection{Included and excluded scope}

Included are: the distinction between causes of becoming and causes of being, with existential inertia named as the live alternative; Aristotle on potency, act, and the dependence of nature on pure actuality, and Plato on participation, each with stated limits; the real distinction of essence and existence in creatures; being by participation and its causal principle; the First Way with its two limits (no proof of a temporal beginning; identification of the terminus as ``God'' deferred to further argument); conservation (ST I, q.~104, a.~1) and divine innermost presence (I, q.~8, a.~1); the patristic conservation witness of Irenaeus and Athanasius; the dogmatic teaching of free creation from nothing and the Catechism's conservation paragraph; the reflexive first-person turn with Augustine's record; the coinage and definition of ontological vertigo; the one-sided real relation (I, q.~13, a.~7); the diagnosis of recoil via noncompetitive causality; humility and magnanimity as jointly true estimates; consent; the analogical judgment that God loves all things (I, q.~20, a.~2); the natural-reason boundary at the Trinity (I, q.~32, a.~1); and apophatic restraint.

Excluded are: any argument from a temporal beginning of the universe (Kalam or otherwise); physical cosmology; a defense of every premise against every contemporary objection, including a full answer to existential-inertia and brute-fact positions; adjudication among Thomist, Scotist, Suarezian, and analytic accounts of the real distinction; the mechanics of divine concurrence and human freedom beyond what the recoil diagnosis requires; theodicy and the problem of evil; grace, mystical experience, and deification (reserved to Parts II--III); Trinitarian theology; and any claim about the psychology or culpability of particular persons.

\subsection{Material qualifications}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.45em,itemsep=0.25em]
\item \emph{Ontological vertigo}, \emph{recoil}, and \emph{consent} as used here are coined, reader-facing terms defined in the body. They are not received technical vocabulary and are attributed to no historical author.
\item The essay's positive case presumes the success of the participation argument it compresses; existential inertia and brute-fact accounts are named as serious alternatives and are answered only to the extent of exhibiting the rival picture, not refuted exhaustively.
\item The First Way is presented with Aquinas's own limits: it concerns essentially ordered, presently operating dependence; it does not prove a temporal beginning (ST I, q.~46, a.~2), and ``this everyone understands to be God'' is a report of usage whose justification lies in the subsequent questions, summarized here rather than executed.
\item Conservation is the non-interruption of the single act of giving being, not repeated creation or a succession of divine decisions; the essay's ``at every moment'' language quantifies over creaturely time, not over acts in God.
\item Divine innermost presence (ST I, q.~8, a.~1) is causal presence, not pantheistic identity; Creator and creature remain really distinct, and participation does not make creatures parts, modes, or fragments of God.
\item The one-sided real relation (ST I, q.~13, a.~7) denies a creature-caused accident or change in God. It does not make divine knowledge, will, or love fictional, and the essay states this beside the claim.
\item The freedom of creation and its ordering to an end are received dogma (\emph{Dei Filius}), not conclusions of the essay's philosophical thread; the essay marks this seam explicitly where the council is quoted.
\item Athanasius attributes conservation to the Word and Irenaeus to God's will and grace; both are cited as witnesses to the antiquity of the conservation conviction, not as philosophical premises, and their confessional content is flagged.
\item Augustine's arrest scenes involve grace, scripture, and moral crisis beyond the philosophical apprehension this essay isolates; they are cited as the classic first-person record, not as demonstration, and no affective experience is made normative or required.
\item ``God loves all existing things'' (ST I, q.~20, a.~2) is an analogical philosophical judgment about what God wills and causes; it does not disclose the divine inner life, entail an emotion in God, or demonstrate the Trinity, which natural reason cannot attain (ST I, q.~32, a.~1).
\item Humility's ``nothing from oneself'' concerns non-aseity, not worthlessness; magnanimity's esteem of received gifts is not pride. Recoil is defined as a hardened interpretation, not as the initial shudder, and no judgment is made about the culpability of any person's response.
\item Consent to creaturehood is agreement with a metaphysical truth. It is not quietism, submission to injustice, or supernatural faith; the step from consent to adoration is named, not developed, and its lived form is deferred to Part II.
\item Pseudo-Dionysius is an anonymous Christian author of the late fifth or early sixth century, not the convert of Acts 17; his denials are cited as apophatic method, not as retraction of the essay's analogical conclusions.
\item The Greek limits paragraph states the standard scholarly reading of \emph{Metaphysics} XII and the \emph{Timaeus}; no claim is made that Plato or Aristotle anticipated Christian doctrine.
\end{enumerate}
