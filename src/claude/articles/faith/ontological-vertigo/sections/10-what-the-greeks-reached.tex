\section{What the Greeks Reached}

Aristotle's contribution begins with an observation about change so plain that its power is easy to miss. Whatever changes acquires something it did not have: the cold water becomes hot, the seed becomes an oak, the unlit lamp lights. Before the change, the thing possessed the new state only as a capacity---Aristotle says \emph{potentially}; after, it possesses the state in fact---\emph{actually}. And nothing confers on itself what it does not have. The water cannot heat itself; something already hot, or already in act in the relevant way, must do it. Potency does not lift itself into act, any more than an empty pocket funds a purchase. So every change points away from the changing thing to something actual; and Aristotle argues at length that actuality is prior to potency---in definition, and in the decisive sense in being: the capable exists for the sake of the achieved, and behind every capacity stands something already in act that awakens it.\endnote{Aristotle, \emph{Metaphysics} IX ($\Theta$), ch.~8 (1049b--1050b): ``actuality is prior to potency,'' prior ``in formula'' and ``in substantial being''; ``one actuality always precedes another in time right back to the actuality of the eternal prime mover.'' Quoted from the public-domain translation of W.~D. Ross, \emph{The Works of Aristotle Translated into English}, vol.~VIII: \emph{Metaphysica} (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1908); wording checked 2026-07-24 in an Internet Archive scan of the 1908 printing.}

Follow that pointing finger through a series. The water is heated by the fire, the fire sustained by its fuel and air, and so on. Where the members of such a series act \emph{simultaneously}---each moving only because something is now moving it, like the last car of a train drawn by the car ahead---the series cannot do without a first member whose actuality is not on loan. Deferral is not explanation; a chain of borrowers, however long, never adds up to an owner. Aristotle drew the conclusion in the twelfth book of the \emph{Metaphysics}: ``there is a mover which moves without being moved, being eternal, substance, and actuality.'' And then, in a sentence whose reach exceeds its grammar, he added: ``On such a principle, then, depend the heavens and the world of nature.''\endnote{Aristotle, \emph{Metaphysics} XII ($\Lambda$), ch.~7 (1072b), Ross translation as above; wording checked 2026-07-24. The train image and the borrower image are this essay's, not Aristotle's; they illustrate what the scholastics later called an essentially ordered causal series, in which each member's causing exists only while the prior member is causing.} \emph{Depend} is his word. The world of nature, for Aristotle, is not a house standing after its builder; it hangs, now, from pure actuality.

Plato had already reached a structurally similar result from a different side. What strikes him is not change but multiplicity: many beautiful things, no one of which is beauty. Each is beautiful in some respect, for a while, from an angle; each has beauty the way a borrower has money. In the \emph{Phaedo} Socrates offers what he calls the safest answer to the question why anything is beautiful: ``nothing makes a thing beautiful but the presence and participation of beauty,'' so that ``by beauty all beautiful things become beautiful.''\endnote{Plato, \emph{Phaedo} 100c--e, in the public-domain translation of Benjamin Jowett; wording checked 2026-07-24 at Project Gutenberg (ebook no.~1658). The safest-answer passage belongs to Socrates' account of his ``second sailing''; its generalization from beauty to being is the later Platonic and Christian-Platonic development, not a claim of the dialogue itself.} Strip the example and keep the structure: whatever possesses a perfection partially, derivatively, and in company with many others that possess it too, points beyond itself to something that does not \emph{have} that perfection but simply \emph{is} it. The many borrow; somewhere stands what is not a borrower. Later thinkers would ask the obvious next question---what happens when the perfection in question is not beauty or equality but existence itself?---and the shape of Plato's answer was waiting for them.

It matters, for honesty and for everything that follows, to state precisely what the Greeks did \emph{not} conclude. Aristotle's unmoved mover is an explanation of motion, not of being. It moves the heavens the way the beloved moves the lover---as an object of desire---and there is no suggestion that it chose the world, knows the world's particulars, or gave the world existence; the cosmos alongside it is eternal, underived, and simply there. Plato's forms are patterns, not makers; where a maker appears in his story, in the \emph{Timaeus}, it is a craftsman god who shapes materials he did not produce, looking at a model he did not invent. Neither philosopher has any notion of a cause that supplies \emph{that things exist at all}. Creation from nothing is not a Greek idea; it was not rejected by the Greeks, merely never conceived.\endnote{This paragraph states the standard scholarly boundary rather than a controversial reading: the mover of \emph{Metaphysics} XII moves as final cause (``as beloved,'' 1072b); the Demiurge of the \emph{Timaeus} orders pre-existing materials toward an eternal model. Neither text contains creation \emph{ex nihilo} or a freely willed world. The point is not a defect of intelligence but of question: the Greek arguments answer ``why is there motion?'' and ``why are there many so-and-sos?''---not ``why is there anything?''}

And yet the instruments were now on the table. A distinction between potency and act, sharp enough to show that nothing actualizes itself. A logic of essentially ordered series, strong enough to show that simultaneous dependence must terminate in something that depends on nothing. A grammar of participation, supple enough to describe having-by-borrowing and to posit, at the source, one that is what the others merely have. What remained was to aim all three at the fact the Greeks never isolated: existence. That took a different intellectual world---one which had reasons of its own, not derived from philosophy, for suspecting that to exist is to be given.
