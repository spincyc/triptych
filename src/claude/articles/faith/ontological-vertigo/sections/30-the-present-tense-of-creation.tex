\section{The Present Tense of Creation}

If existence is received the way the song is received, then the receiving has no expiration. Aquinas faces the point directly in the question he devotes to whether creatures need to be kept in being, and his answer is the song, not the house: ``the being of every creature depends on God, so that not for a moment could it subsist, but would fall into nothingness were it not kept in being by the operation of the Divine power.''\endnote{\emph{Summa theologiae} I, q.~104, a.~1, corpus (\latin{esse cuiuslibet creaturae a Deo, ita quod nec ad momentum subsistere possent, sed in nihilum redigerentur, nisi operatione divinae virtutis conservarentur in esse}); wording checked 2026-07-24, English at New Advent, Latin at Corpus Thomisticum. Aquinas is quoting Gregory the Great's \emph{Moralia} for the formula and making it his own conclusion.} His illustration is the one this essay has been using since its first page. The sun does not build brightness into the air and move on; ``the light in the air is by the continual influence of the sun,'' and every creature stands to God ``as the air is to the sun which enlightens it.'' The sun has light by nature; the air is bright by sharing something not its own. So God alone is being by essence, ``whereas every creature has being by participation.''\endnote{Same article and corpus; the sun-and-air comparison and the being-by-participation conclusion are Aquinas's own wording in the Dominican translation. Conservation, on this account, is not a sequence of fresh creative acts or divine decisions but the non-interruption of the one act of giving being; the difference between creation and conservation is a difference in the effect's history (beginning versus continuing), not in the divine action.}

Two consequences follow, and both matter later.

First, this dependence is not a defect that better engineering could remove. Aquinas presses the thought to its limit: God could not make a creature that would keep existing after the divine sustaining ceased, ``as neither can He make it not to have received its being from Himself.''\endnote{\emph{Summa theologiae} I, q.~104, a.~1 (``God cannot grant to a creature to be preserved in being after the cessation of the Divine influence: as neither can He make it not to have received its being from Himself''); checked 2026-07-24 as above.} A self-sufficient creature is not a rarity; it is a contradiction, like a borrowed loan that belongs to the borrower. To be a creature \emph{is} to be the receiving.

Second, the sustaining cause is not far away, watching its effect from outside. What is most one's own in any thing is its existence---``existence is that which is innermost in each thing and most fundamentally inherent in all things,'' Aquinas writes---and since existence is precisely what is being given, the giver is present at the very point of the gift: ``God is in all things, and innermostly,'' present to each thing as long as it has any being to be present to.\endnote{\emph{Summa theologiae} I, q.~8, a.~1, corpus (\latin{Esse autem est illud quod est magis intimum cuilibet, et quod profundius omnibus inest\ldots{} unde oportet quod Deus sit in omnibus rebus, et intime}); checked 2026-07-24 as above. The presence asserted is causal presence by essence, power, and operation, not pantheistic identity: precisely because God gives being to what is \emph{other} than God, the intimacy and the distinction rise together.} Distance would make the picture easier---a deist clockmaker is a comfortable landlord---but the argument will not permit it. The nearer you go to the core of any creature, the nearer you are to the act by which God is holding it.

None of this was invented in the thirteenth century. Long before the scholastic vocabulary existed, Christian writers had said the same thing in their own registers, and it is worth hearing two of them, because they show that present-tense conservation is not a Thomistic refinement but the common ancient conviction. Irenaeus of Lyons, in the second century, answering people who thought souls were natively immortal: the heavens and everything made ``continue throughout a long course of time according to the will of God,'' and ``life does not arise from us, nor from our own nature; but it is bestowed according to the grace of God.''\endnote{Irenaeus, \emph{Adversus haereses} II.34.3 (heavens, stars, and all created things continuing according to the will of God; life bestowed, not arising from our nature) and II.34.4 (the soul ``partakes in that life bestowed upon her by God''); quoted from the Roberts--Rambaut translation in \emph{Ante-Nicene Fathers}, vol.~1 (1885), wording checked 2026-07-24 at the New Advent transcription.} Athanasius, in the fourth, stating the ground rule of all created natures: ``the nature of created things, inasmuch as it is brought into being out of nothing, is of a fleeting sort, and weak and mortal, if composed of itself only''---and therefore the Word of God, ``quickening and sustaining all things everywhere,'' holds the universe in existence at every moment.\endnote{Athanasius, \emph{Contra Gentes} 41 (created nature, brought into being out of nothing, fleeting ``if composed of itself only'') and 42 (the Word ``quickening and sustaining all things everywhere''); quoted from the Robertson translation in \emph{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, 2nd series, vol.~4 (1892), wording checked 2026-07-24 at the New Advent transcription. For Athanasius the sustainer is specifically the Word---a claim of Christian confession, not of philosophical demonstration, and cited here as witness to the antiquity of the conservation conviction rather than as a premise.}

The Catholic Church has fixed this conviction in her most deliberate registers, and honesty about method requires saying plainly what kind of claim that is. The First Vatican Council teaches that the one true God, ``by an absolutely free plan'' (\latin{liberrimo consilio}), created both orders of creatures out of nothing---and created not from need: ``not for increasing his own beatitude, nor for acquiring it, but to manifest his perfection through the goods he imparts to creatures.''\endnote{First Vatican Council, dogmatic constitution \emph{Dei Filius} (24 April 1870), ch.~1 (\latin{non ad augendam suam beatitudinem, nec ad acquirendam, sed ad manifestandam perfectionem suam per bona, quae creaturis impertitur, liberrimo consilio simul ab initio temporis utramque de nihilo condidit creaturam}), with the corresponding canon (\latin{De Deo\ldots} can.~5) condemning the claim that God created by necessity rather than by will free from all necessity. Latin checked 2026-07-24 at the Holy See web text; the English renderings here are a labeled working gloss governed by the quoted Latin. The council's definition is received dogma, taught on the authority of revelation; the \emph{freedom} of creation in particular goes beyond anything the philosophical argument of the preceding sections established.} The Catechism condenses conservation into one sentence: ``With creation, God does not abandon his creatures to themselves. He not only gives them being and existence, but also, and at every moment, upholds and sustains them in being, enables them to act and brings them to their final end.''\endnote{\emph{Catechism of the Catholic Church} 301, official Vatican English web text, wording checked 2026-07-24; compare CCC 300 on transcendence and intimate presence, which quotes Augustine's \emph{Confessions} III.6.11. These are citations of the Church's teaching as teaching; the philosophical ascent of sections 2--4 stands on its own arguments, and the reader who rejects those arguments is not being asked to accept their conclusion on conciliar authority smuggled in afterward.} Notice that the dogmatic sentences say \emph{more} than the philosophy proved. That the sustaining source is \emph{free}---that the world is chosen, not exuded---and that the sustaining is aimed at an end, are teachings the Church holds from revelation. The philosophical argument delivered present dependence on subsistent being; it did not, by itself, deliver a giver who could have refrained. Keeping that seam visible is not pedantry. It is the difference between an ascent and a sermon.

So the picture stands complete on its two supports, argued and attested: existence is not a possession but a present reception; the receiving has no intermission; the giver is nearer to each thing than its own innermost parts, and gains nothing by the transaction. Stated so, in the third person, about horses and hexagons and helium, it is a tidy piece of metaphysics---interesting, orderly, cold to the touch. It stops being cold at the moment of a small grammatical substitution that the whole argument has been quietly licensing all along. Every premise that applied to \emph{things} applies to the one reading this sentence.
