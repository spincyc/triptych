\section{This Is True of Me}

Make the substitution slowly, because speed is the usual way of escaping it.

I exist. Not as a premise borrowed from Descartes---nothing here turns on proving it---but as the plain condition of my asking anything. My nature, like the horse's and the hexagon's, does not include existence: what I am could be exhaustively described by someone who did not know whether I had ever been born, as my grandparents in 1930 could have been described, completely, without a word about me. So my existing is not mine the way my triangularity would belong to a triangle. It is exercised in me now, and it is either self-supplied now or received now, and it is not self-supplied---nothing in the preceding argument allowed an exception for the arguer. At this instant, in the middle of this sentence, I am the lit air and not the lamp. I am being done.

The tradition has a word for what can happen at this point, and the word is not ``conclusion.'' Augustine, who made this discovery in the first person and wrote it down more nakedly than anyone before him, describes the double shock of it. The first shock is where the sustaining source turned out to be. Not far away, at the top of some ladder of beings, but closer to him than he was to himself: ``You were more inward to me than my most inward part; and higher than my highest''---\latin{interior intimo meo et superior summo meo}.\endnote{Augustine, \emph{Confessions} III.6.11, in the Pilkington translation (\emph{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, 1st series, vol.~1), wording checked 2026-07-24 at the New Advent transcription; Latin checked 2026-07-24 at the Latin Library text of Book III. The sentence is retrospective: Augustine is describing the God he had been seeking ``according to the sense of the flesh'' while the sought one was nearer than the seeking.} The second shock is what he himself turned out to be. Turned toward what simply is, he registered his own manner of existing as if for the first time: ``I trembled with love and fear; and I found myself to be far off from You, in the region of dissimilarity''; and of everything below God he saw that ``they neither altogether are, nor altogether are not.''\endnote{Augustine, \emph{Confessions} VII.10.16 (trembling ``with love and fear''; \latin{regio dissimilitudinis}, rendered ``region of dissimilarity'' by Pilkington) and VII.11.17 (``I viewed the other things below You, and perceived that they neither altogether are, nor altogether are not''); checked 2026-07-24 as above. Augustine's scene includes elements---grace, scripture, moral crisis---far beyond the philosophical arrest this essay isolates; it is cited as the classic first-person record of that arrest, not as a proof of anything.} That is not a man learning a doctrine. That is a man losing his footing---finding that the floor he stands on is not floor but hand.

This essay proposes a name for the experience, and the name is a coinage, offered as such and attributed to no one: \emph{ontological vertigo}. It means the reflexive apprehension of asymmetric dependence in the order of being---the arrest that occurs when the metaphysics of the preceding sections stops being about things and is recognized, by a particular knower, as the present truth about himself. Three clarifications keep the term honest. It is an act of intellect, not a required feeling; the trembling Augustine reports may accompany it or not, and nothing below argues from the sensation. It is not doubt; the vertigo is precisely proportional to how much one takes the argument to have \emph{succeeded}. And it is not rare in principle, only in practice, because the fact it apprehends is the most evenly distributed fact there is. What is rare is holding still for it. The knowledge that I do not hold myself in being is like the knowledge that I will die: universally available, structurally resisted, and transformed the moment it moves from the third person to the first.

Why \emph{vertigo}? Because of the asymmetry, which is the part that ordinary talk of ``depending on God'' fails to convey. Dependencies we know are mutual, if only slightly. The nursing infant depends utterly on its mother---and the mother's body, schedule, and heart are reorganized around the infant. Every creaturely relation has some return current. Here there is none, and Aquinas states it with a serenity that can read as coldness until it reads as the whole point: creatures are really related to God, ``whereas in God there is no real relation to creatures, but a relation only in idea.''\endnote{\emph{Summa theologiae} I, q.~13, a.~7, corpus (``Since therefore God is outside the whole order of creation, and all creatures are ordered to Him, and not conversely, it is manifest that creatures are really related to God Himself; whereas in God there is no real relation to creatures, but a relation only in idea''; \latin{in Deo non est aliqua realis relatio eius ad creaturas, sed secundum rationem tantum}); checked 2026-07-24, English at New Advent, Latin at Corpus Thomisticum. The technical claim is that creation adds no accident to God, effects no change in God, and constitutes God in no way; it does not mean that God's knowing, willing, and loving of creatures are fictions---these are God's own substance, not accidents caused in him by us.} The council said the same thing dogmatically: creation was not for increasing God's beatitude, nor for acquiring anything. My existing adds nothing to what sustains my existing. The relation on which everything about me hangs makes, from the other side, no difference at all---not because the other side is indifferent in the way of a negligent father, but because it is already the fullness that a difference would have to be added to. I am, at this moment, wholly suspended from what I cannot inconvenience, enrich, threaten, repay, or affect. The infant at least kicks. Before God I cannot even kick, since the kicking, too, is on loan.

Vertigo, then, in the strict sense: the ground's refusal to push back. A physical fall at least ends; this is the discovery that, ontologically, I am always falling---and that the falling is called existing, and that it is painless, ordinary, and sustained on purpose from underneath. What a mind does in the seconds after seeing this is, in miniature, the whole religious question, and the options are exactly two.
