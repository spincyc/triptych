\section{The Objection at Full Strength}

\subsection{Celsus: the frogs in the marsh}

The oldest surviving version of the objection is also the funniest. In the second century the pagan philosopher Celsus, whose attack on Christianity survives in the long quotations Origen made in order to answer it, compared Jews and Christians to ``frogs holding council in a marsh, or to worms crawling together in the corner of a dunghill,'' croaking to one another that ``God shows and announces to us all things beforehand; and that, abandoning the whole world, and the regions of heaven, and this great earth, he becomes a citizen among us alone, and to us alone makes his intimations.''\endnote{Origen, \emph{Contra Celsum} IV.23, in the public-domain Ante-Nicene Fathers English translation (Frederick Crombie), checked at the New Advent transcription, 24 July 2026. Celsus's words survive only as quoted by Origen; the attribution is therefore to Celsus as reported, which is how the ancient text itself presents it.}

Strip the mockery and a real argument remains. The cosmos is vast; humanity is one species on one planet; Israel was one small people within that species; Jesus was one man within that people. Each narrowing, Celsus thinks, makes the claim less credible, because a rational God's attention should be distributed like sunlight---evenly, over the whole. Special address is what tribal gods do, and tribal gods are projections of tribal vanity.

\subsection{Spinoza: election dissolved into law}

The modern Enlightenment gave the argument its philosophical dress. Spinoza's \emph{Theological-Political Treatise} devotes a chapter to the vocation of the Hebrews precisely in order to dissolve it. True blessedness, he argues, cannot be a possession one holds against others: ``Every man's true happiness and blessedness consist solely in the enjoyment of what is good, not in the pride that he alone is enjoying it, to the exclusion of others.'' Whatever the Hebrew nation possessed, therefore, it possessed under the universal laws by which God---which for Spinoza means the order of nature---governs everything. In wisdom and virtue, he concludes, ``they were on a par with their fellows, and God was equally gracious to all''; their much-advertised election ``consisted only in the temporal happiness and advantages of independent rule.''\endnote{Spinoza, \emph{Tractatus theologico-politicus} (1670), ch.~3, in R.~H.~M. Elwes's public-domain English translation (1883), checked at the Project Gutenberg edition, 24 July 2026. Spinoza's identification of God with the immanent order of nature is his own system's premise, not a shared assumption of this article; the quotation is used to state the objection, not to concede its metaphysics.}

Notice what has happened. Election has not been refuted by historical evidence; it has been ruled out in advance, because on Spinoza's terms a God identical with universal law \emph{cannot} single anything out. Choice requires a chooser, and a chooser who could have done otherwise. The denial of particular election is not a discovery about Israel. It is a corollary of a prior decision about what God is.

\subsection{Lessing: the ugly broad ditch}

A century later, Gotthold Lessing gave the objection its epistemological form, and its most honest voice. Even granting the Gospel miracles as historical facts, he argued, they remain the wrong \emph{kind} of truth to ground religion: ``Zuf\"allige Geschichtswahrheiten k\"onnen der Beweis von notwendigen Vernunftswahrheiten nie werden''---accidental truths of history can never become the proof of necessary truths of reason, as the sentence may be rendered. Between the contingent report and the eternal conclusion yawns what he famously called ``der garstige breite Graben,'' the ugly broad ditch, which he confessed himself unable to leap however earnestly he tried.\endnote{G.~E. Lessing, \emph{\"Uber den Beweis des Geistes und der Kraft} (Braunschweig, 1777). The German wording quoted here is as attested in standard German transcriptions of the essay; the English renderings in the text are the present article's own working translations and are labeled as such, no public-domain English translation having been identified. The verification limits of this citation are recorded in the terminal appendix and the research record.}

Lessing's ditch assumes a definite picture of what religion is for: the delivery of necessary, universal truths---the existence of God, the moral law---to the reason that already, in principle, possesses them. If that is the cargo, then history really is the wrong vehicle. No pile of contingent facts adds up to a necessity, just as no number of thrown stones adds up to a proof in geometry. On this picture the particular is scaffolding at best: Jesus matters as an occasion for truths that could, in principle, have been reached without him, and an educated humanity will eventually thank the scaffolding and dismantle it.

Celsus, Spinoza, and Lessing disagree about much, but their objections share a single load-bearing premise: that the universal is the natural home of the divine, and the particular a derogation from it. The abstract---law, essence, necessary truth---is noble; the concrete---this people, this man, this bread---is common. Test that premise, and the whole structure stands or falls with it.
