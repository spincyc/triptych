\section{Scope, Method, and Qualifications}

\subsection{Reader, question, and method}

This is a discursive philosophical and theological article for a serious general reader who respects the empirical sciences and may hold, explicitly or by unexamined habit, that empirical testability is the boundary of the knowable or the real. Its governing question is whether that exclusivist thesis---named ``instrumentalism'' in these pages by a deliberate coinage---survives examination, and what standing metaphysical reasoning about God retains once it does not. The article's claim is that the thesis fails by self-application and by misdescribing the sciences it honors; that knowledge is proportioned to its objects; that reasoning from sensible effects toward their ground is therefore admissible and disciplined; and that the Catholic Church is dogmatically committed to the competence of natural reason so to reason. The article clears and defends this space; it does not execute a demonstration of God's existence.

The argument proceeds in epistemic order: the instrumentalist case at full strength; its internal collapse on philosophical grounds alone; the epistemology of scientific practice; the Thomistic account of knowledge from sensible effects; and only then the received scriptural and dogmatic witnesses, cited as the Church's teaching rather than as conclusions of the preceding philosophy. English is the working language; no liturgical rite, civil or canonical jurisdiction, or mutable discipline governs any conclusion. Online witnesses were checked on 2026-07-24.

\subsection{Included and excluded scope}

Included are: the strongest form of the exclusivist appeal to scientific success; the self-application problem of the verifiability criterion and the criterion's documented technical collapse; the knowledge presupposed by empirical practice (logic and mathematics, induction, testimony and memory, norms of inquiry, the judging knower); the proportioning of methods to objects; the Thomistic account of demonstration \emph{quia} from sensible effects and its structural limits; the scriptural witness of Wisdom 13 and Romans 1; the dogmatic definition and canon of \emph{Dei Filius} on natural knowledge of God, with CCC 31--39 and \emph{Fides et ratio} 83 and 88; and the restoration of empirical method to its proper scope.

Excluded are: execution or evaluation of any demonstration of God's existence, including the five ways; a history of logical positivism or of the Vienna Circle's members; adjudication of realism versus instrumentalism in the philosophy of science; philosophy of mind and the hard problem of consciousness; foundations of mathematics; probabilistic, fine-tuning, or miracle-based apologetics; arguments from present gaps in scientific explanation; biblical criticism of the Book of Wisdom beyond its canonical status; and any claim about the salvation, culpability, or good faith of particular persons.

\subsection{Material qualifications}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.45em,itemsep=0.25em]
\item ``Instrumentalist'' is a coined, reader-facing term defined in the first section. It is not the philosophy-of-science doctrine of instrumentalism, not empiricism as such, and not methodological naturalism; scientists as such are not its referent.
\item The collapse of the verifiability criterion does not establish the truth or meaningfulness of any metaphysical claim. The article draws only the negative conclusion that the blanket dismissal fails.
\item The self-application objection is presented with Ayer's ``definition'' rejoinder, and the reply is answered on its merits; the objection is not treated as unanswerable by mere assertion.
\item The Quinean holist alternative to sentence-level verificationism is acknowledged and answered briefly; a full treatment of confirmation holism is outside scope.
\item The presupposition inventory claims that the listed items are knowledge not delivered by instruments; it does not claim they are unrevisable, innate, or independent of experience in every sense. The Thomistic sense-origin thesis is affirmed alongside it.
\item The contingency reasoning sketched in the fourth section is a compressed \emph{shape} of classical argumentation, labeled as such; its needed refinements (against, for example, the fallacy-of-composition and brute-fact objections) are not supplied here.
\item Aquinas's account is adopted as the article's philosophical framework where Catholic theology permits schools; it is not presented as the only orthodox account of natural knowledge.
\item Romans 1:19--20 functions in Paul's argument as grounding culpability for idolatry; the article's use of it for natural knowability follows the Church's received reading, explicitly so labeled.
\item \emph{Dei Filius} defines the \emph{capacity} of natural reason (\emph{cognosci posse}); it does not canonize any particular proof, assert that all persons achieve such knowledge, or make natural knowledge sufficient for salvation. The article states these limits where the definition is quoted.
\item The canon's English is a working gloss governed by the quoted Latin; the chapter's English follows CCC 36's official rendering.
\item \emph{Fides et ratio} is authoritative papal teaching whose philosophical assessments bind as such teaching binds; the article supplies independent philosophical argument rather than resting the case on the encyclical's authority.
\item The claim that the criterion ``died among friends'' concerns the documented technical literature; it does not assert that no religious thinker criticized positivism.
\item No claim is made that the Catholic Church's historical record on particular scientific episodes is beyond criticism; the argument concerns her doctrinal commitments, not her administrative history.
\end{enumerate}
