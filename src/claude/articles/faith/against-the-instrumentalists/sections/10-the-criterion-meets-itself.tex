\section{The Criterion Meets Itself}

Take the criterion as Ayer stated it: a sentence is literally significant only if the proposition it expresses is analytic---true in virtue of the meanings of its terms, like ``all bachelors are unmarried''---or empirically verifiable. Now ask what kind of sentence \emph{that} is.

It is not a report of observation. No one has seen meaninglessness through an eyepiece, and no experiment has an outcome that would confirm or refute the claim that unverifiable sentences lack meaning. Nor is it an empirical generalization about how people use words, for if it were, it would be refuted daily: speakers of every language confidently use, understand, paraphrase, and translate sentences about God, the soul, and the ground of the world, and an empirical linguistics would have to record that such sentences function meaningfully in every speech community on earth. The criterion was never meant as a description of usage; it was meant as a verdict on usage.

Is it then analytic---true by definition? Ayer, pressed on the point, took this way out: the criterion, he wrote in 1946, is put forward as a definition, though not an arbitrary one.\endnote{Ayer, \emph{Language, Truth and Logic}, introduction to the second edition (1946; printed as an appendix in the Pelican issue), opening pages of the section ``The Principle of Verification.'' Checked 2026-07-24 in the same scan. Ayer proposes that the principle be treated as a definition rather than an empirical hypothesis about meaning, while claiming it is faithful to what ``meaning'' ordinarily means in scientific and everyday factual discourse---a claim the ordinary use of religious and metaphysical language sits awkwardly beside.} But a definition is a proposal, and a proposal can be declined. If ``meaning'' is defined so that only the empirically testable has it, then the metaphysician loses the word and keeps everything the word used to name: his sentences still assert, still contradict one another, still invite belief and denial; only now some other term must be found for what they have. A stipulation can redraw a map; it cannot execute the inhabitants. The criterion was advertised as a discovery about language---something we had all somehow failed to notice about our own speech---and under examination it shrank to a resolution about how its adherents proposed to talk. Resolutions carry no anathemas. Hume's bonfire needed a warrant, and a warrant, on the criterion's own terms, is exactly what the criterion could not be: not analytic without becoming optional, not empirical without becoming false, and if neither, then by its own test not even meaningful. It is difficult to name another doctrine in the history of philosophy that so precisely describes the conditions of its own destruction.

The self-application problem was noticed early, and it was not the only wound. The criterion also proved impossible to formulate. If ``verifiable'' means \emph{conclusively} verifiable by finite observations, then every universal law of nature is meaningless, since no finite set of observations conclusively establishes a claim about all cases; the criterion built to protect science would have condemned it root and branch. So the requirement was weakened: a sentence is significant if observation is \emph{relevant} to it, if some experiential statements can be derived from it with the help of auxiliary assumptions. But that let everything back in, for with suitably chosen auxiliaries, observational consequences can be extracted from any sentence whatever, including the metaphysical ones the criterion was designed to expel. Ayer repaired the formulation in 1946; the repair was broken in its turn; each subsequent tightening either strangled physics or readmitted metaphysics.\endnote{The wounds were inflicted mostly from inside the empiricist camp: Isaiah Berlin, ``Verification'' (1939), and Alonzo Church's 1949 review of Ayer's second edition showed that both the original weak criterion and its repaired successor admitted arbitrary sentences. Carl G. Hempel's survey, ``Problems and Changes in the Empiricist Criterion of Meaning,'' \emph{Revue Internationale de Philosophie} 4, no.~11 (1950): 41--63 (reprinted in Leonard Linsky, ed., \emph{Semantics and the Philosophy of Language} [Urbana: University of Illinois Press, 1952], 163--185, and revised in Hempel's \emph{Aspects of Scientific Explanation} [New York: Free Press, 1965]), walked through the successive formulations---complete verifiability, complete falsifiability, translatability into an empiricist language---and found each one either excluding manifest science (universal laws, mixed quantifications, theoretical terms) or excluding nothing. Hempel concluded that cognitive significance admits of degrees and cannot be captured by any sharp verifiability or falsifiability test. This narrative of the criterion's career was checked 2026-07-24 against the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's article ``Carl Hempel''; Hempel's 1950 text itself was identified but not independently collated for this essay, and no wording from it is quoted.} By 1950 Carl Hempel, himself a logical empiricist and one of the movement's most rigorous minds, had surveyed every proposed version and delivered what amounted to an obituary: no criterion of the required sharpness could be drawn, and cognitive significance was at best a matter of degree. Ayer himself, reissuing his book in 1946, opened the new introduction by conceding that ``the questions with which it deals are not in all respects so simple as it makes them appear.''\endnote{Ayer, introduction to the second edition, first paragraph. In later decades Ayer's assessments of the book grew harsher still; those remarks circulate in interview form and are deliberately not relied on here. The 1946 concession, printed in his own book, is sufficient for the argument.} Understatement is also a philosophical genre.

Two features of this collapse deserve emphasis, because they determine what may and may not be concluded from it.

First, the criterion was not refuted by theologians defending their territory. It was dismantled by logicians and philosophers of science, most of them unsympathetic to metaphysics, who simply could not make the rule sort sentences the way it was supposed to. The verification principle died of internal injuries, among friends. When the Catholic Church much later described positivism's central claim as one that ``critical epistemology has discredited,'' it was reporting the coroner's finding, not issuing it.\endnote{John Paul~II, encyclical \emph{Fides et ratio} (14 September 1998), no.~88: scientism ``is the philosophical notion which refuses to admit the validity of forms of knowledge other than those of the positive sciences,'' relegating ``religious, theological, ethical and aesthetic knowledge to the realm of mere fantasy''; ``In the past, the same idea emerged in positivism and neo-positivism, which considered metaphysical statements to be meaningless. Critical epistemology has discredited such a claim\ldots'' Official English text at vatican.va, checked 2026-07-24. The encyclical is cited here only as a witness that the Church's response engages this exact position; its doctrinal use comes later in the essay.}

Second---and this must be said as plainly against the essay's own interest as possible---the collapse of the criterion does not prove that any metaphysical sentence is true, or even that all metaphysical sentences are meaningful. A rule that fails to condemn a prisoner does not thereby acquit him. Some candidate metaphysics is surely confused; some sentences that look like assertions surely are not. What the collapse removes is something narrower and more important: the \emph{blanket} verdict, the classification that spared the instrumentalist the labor of reading the volume before burning it. Hume's librarian asked two questions of the book's spine. After Hempel, the book has to be opened, and each argument in it met the way arguments are met---by counterargument. For half a century, in departments where the positivist creed was taught as hygiene, a generation learned that the question of God had not been answered but \emph{dissolved}, and that the dissolution was a result of modern logic. It was not. The dissolution failed by the standards of modern logic, and the question stands where it stood, unread and unanswered, waiting.

That is a purely negative result, and if the matter ended there, this essay would be an exercise in demolition. It does not end there. The criterion's failure has a cause, and the cause is more instructive than the failure. The rule could not draw a boundary around knowledge at the edge of the empirically testable because knowledge never had that boundary---not even inside the laboratory the rule was built to flatter.
