## Reader and governing question

A serious general reader, student, or researcher who wants one coherent,
evidence-controlled life. Governing questions: (1) what can be established
about the life of a man who wrote almost nothing about himself, when the
documentary base is a vast authenticated corpus of impersonal teaching plus
biographical witnesses assembled two generations later for a canonization;
(2) how the writing was actually produced — commissions, institutions,
collaborators — and why it stopped on 6 December 1273; (3) what the Church's
official reception (canonization 1323, the 1325 Paris revocation, Doctor
1567, *Aeterni Patris* 1879, the Leonine edition, the twentieth-century
legislation, canon 252 §3, the Catechism's citations) has actually claimed,
act by act, and what it has not.

## Thesis and boundary

Thomas's biography is the inverse of Jerome's problem: an enormous,
well-authenticated body of writing and an extremely thin personal record.
The life must be reconstructed from institutional traces and from
canonization-era memory that is late, promotional, and honest about almost
none of its chains; the reception history, by contrast, is unusually well
documented. A truthful biography keeps those registers distinct, preserves
the open questions (birth year; the captivity's duration; the temptation
story's late witnesses; the "straw" remark's second-hand chain; the exact
relation of the 1277 articles to his theses; the relics' itinerary), and
lets official acts say only what they say. Historical synthesis only: no
doctrinal adjudication, no relic authentication, no canonical opinion, no
rights or specialist review claimed.

- reception: Doctor 1567; *Aeterni Patris* 1879 (Leo XIII — explicitly

## Excluded or deliberately bounded

- no complete catalogue, chronology, or authenticity census of the corpus;
  the works named are those needed by the life and reception;
- no exposition of the theology beyond the loci actually quoted; no history
  of Thomism as a school beyond the reception acts checked;
- no reconstruction of Siger of Brabant, Bonaventure, Pecham, or the
  university quarrels beyond what the biography requires;
- no medical or psychological diagnosis of the December 1273 event, the
  final illness, or the death; the poisoning legend is omitted as an
  extraneous late claim not needed by subject, retained source, or material
  reception history;
- no forensic authentication of any relic, tomb, portrait, or miracle; no
  item-level relic census;
- no independent canonical opinion on any censure, revocation, or modern
  norm; official acts are reported to their objects only;
- no reproduction of protected critical editions, translations, or
  databases; the Corpus Thomisticum web edition is used for brief locus
  verification only.

## Material uncertainties

- birth year 1224/5 (older literature 1225 or 1227) and every dependent age;
- the year of the Dominican habit (1244 in modern accounts; 1240–43 in the
  1912 Encyclopedia) and the detention's duration (about one year in modern
  accounts; "nearly two years" in the older literature);
- the student itinerary 1245–48 (Paris first vs Cologne first);
- the historicity of the temptation-and-girdle story and the "dumb ox"
  anecdote (canonization-era witnesses only; chains unstated or reaching
  Thomas only through Reginald of Piperno);
- the exact character of the 6 December 1273 event and the wording of the
  "straw" remark (second-hand deposition of 1319; translations vary);
- authorship detail of the Corpus Christi office and associated texts
  (papal-commission tradition early; modern debate identified at
  bibliographic level; the Catechism itself marks Adoro te devote "attr.");
- which 1277 articles aimed at Thomas (scholarly disagreement preserved);
  the exact date of the 1325 revocation (1324 in the old literature);
- the relics' complete itinerary and present item-level custody (Toulouse
  corpus reported; head/arm claims at Naples and elsewhere unadjudicated).
