\section{Drawing the Boundary: Prescription and Polemic}

The same forensic instinct that indicted the persecutors organized Tertullian's
war on heresy. His master-stroke, the \work{Prescription Against Heretics},
transfers to theology the Roman litigator's \textit{praescriptio}---a plea that
defeats an opponent's case before its merits are argued. Heretics appeal to
Scripture; Tertullian answers that the Scriptures are the property of the
churches that received them from the apostles, so that outsiders have no
standing to argue from them at all. The rule of faith, publicly taught in the
apostolic churches, settles in advance what the documents mean. Anyone who
doubts where those churches are may ``run over the apostolic churches, in
which the very thrones of the apostles are still pre-eminent in their
places''---Corinth, Philippi, Ephesus; and for an African, Rome is nearest:
``How happy is its church, on which apostles poured forth all their doctrine
along with their blood! where Peter endures a passion like his Lord's! where
Paul wins his crown in a death like John's'' (\work{Prescription} 36)---an
early and independent Latin witness to the Roman deaths of Peter and Paul. The
treatise also fixed his most famous antithesis: ``What indeed has Athens to do
with Jerusalem? What concord is there between the Academy and the Church?''
(\work{Prescription} 7). The sentence is a standing temptation to
misquotation: its author was soaked in the philosophy he here forswears, used
Stoic materials throughout his theology, and elsewhere calls the philosophers
to witness. It is a rule against mixing authorities, not a ban on thought.

The prescription in hand, Tertullian still argued the merits, at enormous
length, against named opponents: Hermogenes the painter, the Valentinian
schools, the philosophical psychologies answered in \work{On the Soul}, and
above all Marcion of Pontus, whose severed god of pure goodness and
mutilated Scriptures he pursued through five books. \work{Against Marcion} is
also the anchor of his chronology. Its preface confesses a publishing history:
a hurried first edition, then a fuller one lost ``by the fraud of a person who
was then a brother, but became afterwards an apostate,'' who circulated a
faulty copy, and now a third, definitive edition (\work{Against Marcion} 1.1).
And in its first book he dates Marcion's novelty ``to the fifteenth of the
Emperor Severus'' (\work{Against Marcion} 1.15)---the fifteenth year of
Septimius Severus, 207/208, the one absolute date Tertullian ever gives for his
own time of writing.

\witnesslimit{Marcion, Hermogenes, Praxeas, and the Valentinians survive
almost entirely in the words of their enemies. Tertullian's reports are
controlled polemic: modern scholarship uses them cautiously and does not treat
his reconstruction of any opponent as neutral description. This study cites
them for Tertullian's side of the argument only.}

For Latin Christianity the consequences were permanent. The prescription
argument---Scripture read within the apostolic churches under the rule of
faith---became a standing instrument of Catholic controversy long after its author's
own standing had become an embarrassment to those who used it.
