# Saint Augustine of Hippo — tradition audit

Provider: Anthropic Claude. Web checks 2026-07-24/25. This record reviews
received traditions about Augustine: earliest located witness, later
reception, historical status, and the treatment adopted in the publication.

## 1. The garden conversion and "tolle lege" (Conf. 8.12.28–30)

- **Earliest witness:** Augustine himself, Conf. 8.12.28–30 (checked
  verbatim at newadvent.org/fathers/110108.htm), written c. 397–400 about
  late summer 386: the fig tree, the tears, the voice "as of a boy or
  girl... chanting, and oft repeating, Take up and read; take up and read,"
  the Antony precedent, Romans 13:13–14, "all the gloom of doubt vanished
  away."
- **Contemporary silence:** the Cassiciacum dialogues (386–387) do not
  mention garden, voice, or fig tree (reported through the critical
  literature; the dialogues were not inspected first-hand — ceiling
  recorded).
- **Critical debate:** Harnack-era critics already argued the Confessions
  "projected upon the recluse of 386 the sentiments of the bishop of 400"
  and that the garden conversion was "not to Christianity, but to
  philosophy" (CE 1907 "Life of St. Augustine," checked, reporting and
  rejecting the thesis). Courcelle (Recherches, 1950; Les Confessions,
  1963) treated key elements as literary figuration; O'Donnell's commentary
  on 8.12 (checked at faculty.georgetown.edu/jod/conf/comm8.html) reports
  the state of the question, argues against reading the scene as free
  fiction ("if Courcelle had adduced other passages where A. makes up
  pretty little scenes out of whole cloth..."), records Courcelle's own
  point that the open Antony comparison tells "in favor of historicity,"
  and cites Sizoo's naturalistic child-cry proposal (Vig. Chr. 12, 1958).
- **Later reception:** the Golden Legend retells the scene (checked);
  Benedict XVI's 27 Feb 2008 audience treats the conversion as a lifelong
  journey in stages (checked, paraphrase).
- **Status:** decisive resolution at Milan in 386 secure (A + external
  consequences); descriptive detail single-witness and debated.
  **Publication treatment:** scene quoted in full; dedicated witnesslimit
  preserves Courcelle, Sizoo, the Harnack-era debate, and the Cassiciacum
  silence; no certification or dissolution.

## 2. The pear tree (Conf. 2.4.9)

- **Witness:** Augustine only (checked verbatim): "There was a pear-tree
  close to our vineyard..."; fruit flung "to the very swine"; "having no
  inducement to evil but the evil itself."
- **Status:** not a legend but subject-authored confession, narrated c. 30
  years later as a theological set piece on gratuitous sin; no external
  control. **Publication treatment:** quoted with a witnesslimit stating
  class-A genre status; its fame assigned to reception of the Confessions.

## 3. "Grant me chastity and continency, but not yet" (Conf. 8.7.17)

- **Witness:** Augustine (checked verbatim, with the surrounding sentence:
  "I was afraid lest You should hear me soon...").
- **Reception:** detached from context as a witticism in popular quotation
  culture.
- **Status:** authentic wording; the epigram reading inverts its function
  (penitent self-diagnosis of the divided will, not a rake's joke).
  **Publication treatment:** quoted in narrative context; the reception
  section states the contextual correction explicitly.

## 4. The relic translations (Hippo — Sardinia — Pavia)

- **Earliest located witness:** Paul the Deacon, Hist. Lang. 6.48 (Foulke
  1907, checked verbatim at elfinspell.com): Liutprand, hearing the
  Saracens were defiling the Sardinian resting place "where the bones of
  the bishop St. Augustine had been formerly carried on account of the
  devastation of the barbarians," ransomed and reburied them at Ticinum
  (Pavia). This one sentence carries both translations; the first is
  undated and agentless in it.
- **Later tradition:** Golden Legend (checked): 280 years in Sardinia;
  Genoa arrival; churches founded at each overnight stop; burial in
  S. Peter "Cieldore" (San Pietro in Ciel d'Oro); also the stolen-finger
  miracle tale. Attribution of the first translation to bishops exiled by
  the Vandal kings (c. 500, often tied to Fulgentius) circulates in the
  reference literature; no early witness was located in this research
  (bounded negative; outstanding lead — Bede's chronicle notice was not
  inspected).
- **Current usage:** Benedict XVI, Pavia homily 22 Apr 2007 (checked):
  "the King of the Longobards acquired Augustine's remains for the City of
  Pavia so that today they belong to this City in a special way" (M).
- **Status:** eighth-century report secure as report; pre-Paul chain
  unresolved; identification of the Pavia bones with the Hippo burial is
  received tradition. **Publication treatment:** Paul quoted verbatim;
  witnesslimit states the gap, the inference structure, and the limits of
  the 1695/1728-era recognitions (not detailed; outside scope); papal
  usage reported as reception fact, not authentication.

## 5. The child by the seashore (Trinity legend)

- **Negative early record (bounded):** absent from Augustine's works as
  checked, from Possidius (checked), and — per Caxton's own testimony —
  from the Golden Legend's Latin, French, and English exemplars.
- **Earliest located witness in this research:** Caxton's 1483 English
  Golden Legend, Life of S. Austin (checked at sourcebooks.fordham.edu),
  with Caxton's disclaimer: "which I have seen painted on an altar of
  S. Austin at the black friars at Antwerp, howbeit I find it not in the
  legend, mine exemplar, neither in English, French, ne in Latin." The
  child ladles the sea into a pit with a spoon; "I shall lightlier and
  sooner draw all the water of the sea... than thou shalt bring the
  mystery of the Trinity and his divinity into thy little understanding";
  the child vanishes.
- **Reported earlier carriers:** thirteenth-century exemplum literature
  (Caesarius of Heisterbach) and an apocryphal Augustine–Cyril letter are
  reported in the secondary literature (medievalists.net summary, checked);
  not inspectable in this research — outstanding lead.
- **Status:** late legend, iconographically driven; theologically apt.
  **Publication treatment:** narrated in the reception section as legend
  with Caxton's disclaimer quoted; bounded negative stated.

## 6. The Te Deum legend

- **Witness:** Golden Legend, Life of S. Austin (checked): at the baptism
  Ambrose and Augustine sang alternately, "as it is read," composing the
  hymn.
- **Audit:** CE 1912 "The Te Deum" (checked): the tradition "can be traced
  back to the end of the eighth century" (Hincmar's report; the
  pseudo-Datius chronicle is 11th-century); modern scholarship's candidate
  author is Niceta of Remesiana. No contemporary witness.
- **Status:** legend; source of the name "Ambrosian hymn."
  **Publication treatment:** reported as legend in the reception section
  with the CE hymnological status.

## 7. The name Monnica / Monica

- **Witness:** O'Donnell, commentary on Conf. 9.13.37 (checked): "This is
  the only place in any of A.'s works where we are told her name...
  The manuscripts are almost unanimous in favor of the spelling"
  (i.e., Monnica); Frend's Berber-lineage suggestion noted there as a
  named hypothesis.
- **Status:** received spelling Monica is conventional; the manuscript
  spelling is Monnica; nothing further (etymology, ethnicity) is
  established. **Publication treatment:** appendix states both spellings;
  prose uses Monica; no ethnic inference drawn.

## 8. Monica's relics (Ostia — Rome, 1430)

- **Witness:** CE "St. Monica" (checked): Martin V ordered the relics
  brought to Rome in 1430; d'Estouteville's church (Sant'Agostino by
  received identification; CE names it as the church he "built... in
  honour of St. Augustine").
- **Status:** reported tradition; the 1945 discovery of a fragment of her
  Ostia epitaph is often cited in modern literature but was not verified
  at source in this research (negative result; outstanding lead — not
  used in the publication).
- **Publication treatment:** one reported-level sentence in the relic
  subsection; no authentication claim.

## 9. "Doctor of Grace" and the doctorate

- **Acts checked:** Boniface VIII's 1298 decretal ordering the feasts of
  the four Latin doctors (CE 1912 "Doctors of the Church," checked);
  "Doctor of Grace" usage (CE "Teaching of St. Augustine," checked:
  "he is not only the Doctor of Grace, he is also the Doctor of the
  Church"); Celestine I's praise within about a year of the death
  (Augustinum Hipponensem, checked, quoting "one of the best teachers").
- **Audit:** each act limited to its object; the title is received usage,
  not a formal conferral located in this research. **Publication
  treatment:** the title is introduced as reported reception usage.

## 10. Reformation and Jansenist appropriations

- **Witness:** CE "Teaching of St. Augustine" (checked): "Luther and
  Calvin were content to treat Augustine with a little less irreverence
  than they did the other Fathers"; Harnack's influence question quoted
  there; "Jansenistic abuse of his works" phrase.
- **Status:** reception history reported through an identified
  public-domain survey; no first-hand Reformation sources inspected
  (ceiling stated). **Publication treatment:** one subsection, quotes
  attributed to the survey, no adjudication of the theological claims.
