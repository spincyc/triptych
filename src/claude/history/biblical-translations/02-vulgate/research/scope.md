## Reader and governing question

A serious nonspecialist who meets "the Vulgate" in biblical, Catholic,
liturgical, patristic, medieval, or text-critical discussion. Governing
questions: who made the Latin Bible's parts, on whose authority, from
what sources, against what resistance; how a contested novelty became
the common Bible of the Latin West; and what the official acts (Trent
1546, the editions of 1590/1592, the Nova Vulgata 1979) did and did not
do.

## Thesis

The Vulgate is not one act but a tradition: a documented papal prompting
covers at most Jerome's conservative Gospel revision (382–384); the rest
of the New Testament was revised by an unknown hand (Rome, c. 393–410);
the Old Testament is Jerome's Hebrew-based project (c. 390–405/6), with
Tobit and Judith rendered hastily from Aramaic and Wisdom, Sirach,
Baruch, and Maccabees left in their Old Latin form against the drift of
his own canon prefaces; the Gallican (Greek-based) Psalter, not his
Hebrew Psalter, is the Psalter of the received text. "The Vulgate" as a
named authoritative edition is a product of medieval use and
early-modern authority, and the Clementine, the critical editions, and
the Nova Vulgata answer different questions.

## Included

- Old Latin (Vetus Latina) background: plurality of transmitted forms;
  Augustine's innumerable-translators remark (De doctrina 2.11.16) and
  the Itala remark (2.15.22) with its scholarly dispute; the
  single-translation-per-book conclusion of the Beuron editors; the
  fourth-century meaning of *editio vulgata*;
- Damasus and the Gospels revision 382–384 (preface *Novum opus*; Letter
  27); the limits of the Damasan evidence; the anonymous revision of the
  rest of the NT and *Primum quaeritur* (negative authorship result);
- the three Psalters (Rome revision and the Roman Psalter question;
  Gallican; iuxta Hebraeos);
- Bethlehem and *Hebraica veritas* c. 390–405/6; which books Jerome
  translated, revised, or left untouched; Tobit ("work of one day") and
  Judith ("one short night's work"); the *Prologus galeatus* canon and
  the prefaces' tension with church usage (Solomon preface; Judith's
  Nicaea remark);
- contemporary resistance: Augustine Letters 71 (Oea) and 82; Jerome
  Letter 112; Rufinus, Apology 2.33–36; the Clementine 'hederam' at
  Jonah 4:6 verified in a registered 1914 printing;
- gradual medieval adoption: mixed texts; Cassiodorus; Codex Amiatinus;
  Alcuin and Theodulf as distinct programs; Paris Bibles, Langton
  chapters, correctoria; the settling of the name; Gutenberg 1454–56;
- Trent Session IV (canon decree and *Insuper*), exactly quoted, with
  Divino afflante Spiritu 21's juridical gloss; the Douay–Rheims
  relationship;
- the Sixtine debacle and Sixto-Clementine (1590/1592) with explicit
  evidence ceilings (promulgation dispute, motives, difference-count
  spread);
- modern era: Oxford (Wordsworth–White) NT 1889–1954; Benedictine Roman
  OT 1926–1995 (commission 1907; Abbey of St. Jerome 1933); Stuttgart
  1969/2007; Beuron Vetus Latina Institute 1945; Nova Vulgata (1979
  Scripturarum thesaurus; editio typica altera 1986; Liturgiam
  authenticam 2001); Dei Verbum 22; CCC 131.

## Terminology controls

Vulgate (received collection, flagged as anachronistic before Trent);
Old Latin/Vetus Latina (modern collective label); *editio vulgata*
(fourth-century sense = common Septuagintal text); Gallican Psalter vs
Psalterium iuxta Hebraeos vs Roman Psalter (attribution disputed);
*Hebraica veritas* (program and polemic); "authentic" (Trent's juridical
sense as glossed by DAS 21); "typical edition" (normative for a defined
use); critical edition vs church edition throughout.

## Material uncertainties preserved

Damasus's exact request; the identity of the rest-of-NT reviser
(Pelagius/"Rufinus the Syrian" hypotheses); Roman Psalter attribution
(De Bruyne); per-book dates of the Hebrew translations; Tobit/Judith
sources and procedure; Jerome's Nicaea remark on Judith; textual
identities of Cassiodorus's Bibles; Amiatinus's relation to the codex
grandior; promulgation status of *Aeternus ille* and the full motives
for the Sixtine withdrawal; Sixtine–Clementine difference counts
(reported as a 2,000–5,000 spread, none endorsed).
