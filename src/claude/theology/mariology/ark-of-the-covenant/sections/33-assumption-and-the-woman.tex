\section{The Assumption and the Woman Clothed with the Sun}

Two promises were made earlier and both fall due here. The narrative panel
ended with one sentence held in reserve: at the far end of the Bible, John
saw the ark. The reception panel ended with a splice pointed at one text:
since 1969 the Roman Church has read the ark verse and the Woman clothed
with the sun as a single lesson on the day she celebrates Mary's
Assumption. The principle governing this last examination can be stated in
one plain sentence: the canon answers the question \emph{where is the ark?}
not with a place but with a vision of heaven, and the Church answers the
question \emph{where is Mary?} from the same page. Whether those two
answers are one answer is precisely what must now be weighed, and weighed
by the rule this study has used throughout: attestation first, reception
second, neither confused with the other, the absence of either never
mistaken for falsity.

\subsection{The psalm of the resting place}

Begin with the verse that has followed the ark since Solomon's dedication
and now stands over Mary's feast. Psalm 131 (the Hebrew counts it 132)
prays the ark into its rest; and the Douay Bible, by an accident of its own
textual inheritance, lets the reader see the verse's two ancient forms
without leaving the base text. The psalter follows the Gallican and
Septuagint line; the Chronicler's quotation of the same prayer follows the
Hebrew:

\begin{witnessbox}{Psalm 131:8 and 2 Paralipomenon 6:41, Douay-Rheims}
Arise, O Lord, into thy resting place: thou and the ark, which thou hast
sanctified. \emph{(Psalm 131:8)}

Now therefore arise, O Lord God, into thy resting place, thou and the ark
of thy strength: let thy priests, O Lord God, put on salvation, and thy
saints rejoice in good things. \emph{(2 Paralipomenon 6:41)}
\end{witnessbox}

\noindent
The ark of thy strength; the ark, which thou hast sanctified. One Bible,
both traditions. The Greek psalter reads \term{h\=e kib\=otos tou
hagiasmatos sou}, the ark of thy holiness or sanctification, and the Latin
of the Church's use renders it as a noun-phrase, \term{arca
sanctificationis tuae} --- the form the Assumption liturgy and the
constitution of 1950 employ, native to the study's Latin edition though not
to its English, whose relative clause (``which thou hast sanctified'') says
the same thing more quietly.\endnote{Psalm 131:8, Douay-Rheims, verified at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/21131.htm}; 2 Paralipomenon 6:41 at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/14006.htm}. LXX Ps 131:8 \term{h\=e kib\=otos
tou hagiasmatos sou} verified in the study's Wave-1 philological
instruments (Rahlfs corpus). The Clementine Vulgate's \term{tu et arca
sanctificationis tuae} is cited as reported wording: it was not collated
against the registered Clementine edition for this study; the same Latin
stands verified, however, in the \work{Acta Apostolicae Sedis} text of
\work{Munificentissimus Deus} 26 (AAS 42 (1950) 762) and in Migne's Latin
column of Hesychius (PG 93:1463--1464), both read for this study. The
MT/LXX divergence (``strength'' vs.\ ``holiness'') is the standard textual
datum; the Douay's two renderings preserve it inside one canon.}

Before the verse was Mary's it was Christ's, and the evidence for that
priority is older than any Marian use of it. Primasius, bishop of
Hadrumetum in the mid-sixth century, commenting on the very verse of the
Apocalypse this section will reach, quotes the psalm and applies it to the
incarnate Lord: the ark seen in heaven is \term{mysterium incarnationis
Christi}, the mystery of Christ's Incarnation, and \term{Exsurge, domine,
in requiem tuam, tu et arca sanctificationis tuae} is the Lord arising into
his rest with the ark of his own sanctified flesh --- an editorial
paraphrase, not a translation, of a Latin text read for this study in a
transcription based on the critical edition.\endnote{Primasius,
\work{Commentarius in Apocalypsin}, Book III on Apoc 11:19 (CCSL 92, ed.\
Adams): \term{Templum ecclesia, arca testamenti mysterium incarnationis
Christi\ldots Hinc psalmus: ``Exsurge, domine, in requiem tuam, tu et arca
sanctificationis tuae.''} Latin wording verified against a full
transcription of the CCSL 92 text published at
\url{https://turretinfan.blogspot.com} (August 2023 post); the CCSL page
range (175--190 as given by the transcriber) and the PL 68 columns were not
independently confirmed, so the citation is carried at transcription level
with that caution. All paraphrases editorial.} The medieval Latin office
agrees with him: as the patristic panel's chant-database audit recorded,
the one responsory verse built on Psalm 131:8 was sung in Paschaltide ---
the Lord risen with the ark of his body --- and never, in the indexed
manuscripts, at the Assumption.\endnote{Cantus ID 006069a, verse of the
Paschaltide responsory \work{Alleluia audivimus ea in Ephrata},
\url{https://cantusindex.org/id/006069a}; the full CANTUS evidence,
including the negative for the Assumption, is set out with the patristic
panel's Latin findings above.} The oldest typological career of the
Assumption proof-verse is paschal and Christological.

Its Marian career has already been dated, act by act, in the two preceding
sections, and is only gathered here: Hesychius of Jerusalem, in the decades
around 420, the first man in this study's evidence to read the verse of the
Virgin by name; Anthony of Padua in 1231, the earliest Western application
to the Assumption that the audit could find; Liguori in 1750, fusing the
psalm with the procession of 2 Kings 6 (= 2 Samuel 6) in a devotional
classic; \work{Munificentissimus Deus} in 1950, quoting the verse twice on
its way to the definition; the Roman Vigil Mass since 1969, singing verse 8
as its refrain while the ark procession is read.\endnote{Hesychius, Homily
V \work{De S.~Maria Deipara}, PG 93:1463--1464; Anthony of Padua as quoted
at \work{Munificentissimus Deus} 29; Liguori, \work{Glories of Mary},
second Assumption discourse; MD 26 and 29; Lectionary 621. Each witness is
quoted and sourced in the patristic and reception sections above; none is
re-quoted here, per the study's derive-once rule.} The verse's history is
the study's whole lesson in miniature: a Christological antiquity, a Marian
development, a modern liturgical embrace --- and, by the rule paid at the
toll-gate, the lateness of the last two subtracts nothing from their
reality as acts of the reading Church, provided no one backdates them. No
one here will.

\subsection{The ark and the Woman: Apocalypse 11:19--12:1}

The text itself must be seen whole, because the numbers that divide it are
a thirteenth-century convenience and the vision knows nothing of them. At
the sounding of the seventh trumpet, heaven's temple opens:

\begin{witnessbox}{Apocalypse 11:19--12:1, Douay-Rheims}
And the temple of God was opened in heaven: and the ark of his testament
was seen in his temple, and there were lightnings, and voices, and an
earthquake, and great hail. And a great sign appeared in heaven: A woman
clothed with the sun, and the moon under her feet, and on her head a crown
of twelve stars:
\end{witnessbox}

\noindent
``ark of his testament'' is Douay's rendering of the same construct
elsewhere printed ``ark of the covenant''; the terminology section of the
first panel owns that note. The reader who has followed this study wants to
know one thing: did the ancients read these two sentences
together?\endnote{Apocalypse 11:19 and 12:1, Douay-Rheims, verified at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/73011.htm} and
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/73012.htm}. The Douay storm-list lacks the
``peals of thunder'' of modern versions (Greek \term{brontai}; Vulgate and
Douay ``voices''); quoted as printed. The chapter divisions are the
thirteenth-century system associated with Stephen Langton; reading across
them is not a modern liberty.}

The answer has two halves, and both must be printed, because a popular
sentence and its popular refutation are each half wrong. The ancient
commentators were checked at this verse, Greek and Latin, for this study.
Victorinus of Pettau, the earliest Latin commentator (d.~c.~304), reads the
opened temple as ``a manifestation of our Lord,'' the ark's appearance as
``the preaching of the Gospel and the forgiveness of sins, and all the
gifts whatever that came with Him,'' and the woman as ``the ancient Church
of fathers, and prophets, and saints, and apostles.'' Oecumenius, whose
sixth-century work is the earliest full Greek commentary and the first to
read the woman of 12:1 directly as Mary --- ``the mother of our Savior,''
pure in soul and body, of the same substance as we are --- reads the ark of
11:19 as something else entirely: the hidden good things of the age to
come, the things eye has not seen (1 Corinthians 2:9), revealed to the
saints. The verdict sentence writes itself: the one ancient commentator who
held both pieces in his hands did not join them. Andrew of Caesarea
(c.~611) makes the ark the good things concealed in Christ, ``in whom all
the fullness of divinity dwelt bodily'' (Colossians 2:9), reports that
``some'' had understood the woman to be the Theotokos, and sides with
Methodius: she is the Church. Cassiodorus's summary skips the ark of 11:19
altogether while touching the woman partly Marially; the anonymous Greek
scholia pass over ark and woman in silence. So the first half of the
finding, hardened by the audit: \emph{no ancient commentator reads the ark
of Apocalypse 11:19 as Mary} --- not even the one who read the very next
verse of her.\endnote{Victorinus, \work{Commentary on the Apocalypse}
(Jerome's recension), ANF 7, read at
\url{https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0712.htm}; quotations verbatim from
that public-domain translation. Oecumenius, \work{Commentary on the
Apocalypse} 6.17.7 and 6.19.1--3, trans.\ Suggit, FOTC 112 (2006), read in
the archive.org collection
\url{https://archive.org/details/the-fathers-of-the-church-a-new-translation-147-volumes}
(pp.~106--108): the ark's opening means ``that the hidden good things, and
in addition some new mysteries, were revealed to the saints,'' with 1 Cor
2:9; the woman is ``the mother of our Savior.'' Andrew of Caesarea,
\work{Commentary on the Apocalypse}, ch.~33, trans.\ Constantinou, FOTC 123
(2011), pp.~136--137, same collection; Constantinou's note identifies
Andrew's ``some'' as Oecumenius, and reports (with Prigent) that Epiphanius,
\work{Panarion} 78.11, already knew unnamed readers applying Apoc 12 to
Mary, and that Quodvultdeus in the mid-fifth century combined the ecclesial
and Marian readings. Cassiodorus, \work{Complexiones} 16--17, and the
anonymous Greek scholia (Scholion 37), in FOTC 144 (2022), same collection.
Quotations from the FOTC volumes are kept to brief attributed scale;
the surrounding accounts are paraphrase.}

The second half overturns what apologetic and anti-apologetic literature
alike assume. An ancient commentator \emph{does} join the ark and the woman
--- the most influential of them all. Tyconius, the Donatist whose
commentary of about 380 shaped the whole Latin tradition, writes at 11:19
that ``it is understood that the church is the Ark of the Covenant,'' and
quotes Jeremias 3:16 at the verse as his warrant --- the prophecy, weighed
in the second panel, that the old ark would neither be remembered nor
remade; and at 12:1, by his rule of genus and species, he makes heaven, the
temple in heaven, and the woman clothed with the sun ``one and the same
thing,'' the Church. He even supplies the structural claim the modern
lectionary needs: at 11:19, he says, the vision recapitulates from the
nativity of the Lord, and chapter 12 restates the same content more
extensively. Primasius repeats the genus-species chain for temple, heaven,
and woman --- ``the church clothed with Christ'' --- though he lifts the ark
out of it and gives it, as quoted above, to the Incarnation; and Bede, in
Marshall's Victorian English, carries the incarnational ark to its most
finished form: in the Church, ``the ark of His Incarnation is
laid open to the whole world,'' the veil rent, and ``like the heavenly
manna in clean gold is the Divinity in a holy body.'' Elsewhere Bede can say
simply ``the ark of the Lord's body.''\endnote{Tyconius, \work{Expositio
Apocalypseos} on Apoc 11:19--12:1 (Book Four; the passage at CCSL 107A,
ed.\ Gryson, p.~175 per the translation's own apparatus), trans.\
Gumerlock, FOTC 134 (2017), pp.~120--121, read in the archive.org
collection cited above; the short phrases quoted are at brief attributed
scale, the rest paraphrase. Primasius on 12:1 (``the church clothed with
Christ'') from the CCSL 92 transcription cited above; at 12:2--5 he admits
an alternative reading ``specifically about the person of Christ,'' within
which he asserts Mary's painless childbirth --- Mary appears in his
commentary, but never as ark or woman.
Bede, \work{The Explanation of the Apocalypse}, trans.\ E.~Marshall
(Oxford, 1878), pp.~79--81 (on 11:19--12:1), p.~40 (on 6:9, ``the ark of
the Lord's body''), p.~107 (on 15:5, tied back to 11:19), read at
\url{https://archive.org/details/explanationapoc00bedegoog}; quotations
verbatim from this public-domain translation.} So the corrected finding,
in full: the structural continuity of 11:19 with 12:1 --- one movement, ark
and woman a single referent --- is anciently attested; what has no ancient
witness is the \emph{Marian} identification of either term at this verse.
The ancient ark of the Apocalypse, where it is not the Church, is Christ's
flesh --- the flesh, Primasius adds in the same breath, of one begotten of
the inviolate Virgin in a new birth. The ancient reading stands one
explicit step short of Mary, and no ancient took the step.

Two more facts frame the verse, and the study concedes both in full. First,
within the Apocalypse the ark belongs to a furnished heavenly sanctuary
that the book keeps steadily in view: the altar of 6:9 and 8:3--5, the ark
of 11:19, the temple opening again at 15:5 under its wilderness name, the
tent of the testimony. Oecumenius and Andrew both read this architecture as
the archetype of Bezalel's tent --- the pattern shown to Moses, of which
the tabernacle was the copy --- and Bede expressly ties 15:5 back to 11:19.
Second, the modern critical account of the verse's function: the storm
formula that closes each septet of the book grows by one member at each
recurrence --- 4:5, 8:5, 11:19, 16:18--21 --- so that, as Bauckham's
analysis has it, the theophany of 11:19 marks the climactic close of the
trumpets and encompasses what follows; the ark's appearance is covenant
theophany in the manner of Sinai, and the woman of 12:1, for the critical
mainstream as for the patristic majority, is the people of God, with the
Marian sense as reception history. This is a school's verdict resting on
that school's premises --- but here, uniquely in this study, the school and
the Fathers substantially coincide, and honesty says so
plainly.\endnote{Heavenly-sanctuary architecture: Oecumenius 8.23.1 and
Andrew ad 15:5, FOTC volumes as cited above; the pattern-copy logic is that
of Hebrews 8:5 and 9:23. Modern function: R.~Bauckham, \work{The Climax of
Prophecy} (1993), p.~8, on the expanding theophany formula, quoted at one
remove from a transcription at \url{https://exegesisandtheology.com};
D.~Aune, \work{Revelation 6--16} (WBC 52B, 1998), p.~676, and G.~K.~Beale,
\work{The Book of Revelation} (NIGTC, 1999), p.~618 (Exodus 15 echoes,
Sinai theophany), as reported at \url{https://readingacts.com}. All three
are reported literature, not consulted in the editions named.}

Nothing in what the Church now does with the verse requires denying any of
that. It requires only seeing what she did. The Assumption constitution
itself used the Woman of chapter 12 guardedly and never cited 11:19; the
1950 Mass put Apocalypse 12:1 at the entrance and still read no ark text.
It was the postconciliar lectionary, in 1969, that began the day's first
reading at 11:19a and ran it into the vision of the Woman, then closed the
formulary with the Visitation Gospel --- so that in one Mass the Church
reads the ark seen in the opened temple, the Woman crowned with stars, and
Elizabeth's question at the door, the question this study traced to 2 Kings
6:9. The splice is deliberate, datable, and hers. On the principles this
study adopted at the toll-gate --- the liturgy as a privileged act of the
reading Church --- that selection is not decoration; it is the Church
saying, in the idiom proper to her, where she finds Mary in the canon's
last vision. The reading is received; it is not, and should never be
printed as, an exegesis the Fathers drew.\endnote{Lectionary 622 (Apoc
11:19a; 12:1--6a, 10ab; Gospel Lk 1:39--56) and Lectionary 621, verified at
\url{https://bible.usccb.org} as recorded in the reception section, where
\work{Munificentissimus Deus} 27's guarded use of the Woman and its silence
about 11:19 are also documented. The selections are described, not quoted,
per the study's rights policy.} One adjacent thread deserves its footnote
and no more: the ``hidden manna'' promised to the conqueror at Apocalypse
2:17, which modern exegesis hears against the hidden ark-and-urn of the
Maccabean and apocalyptic tradition; the ancient commentators heard the
Bread come down from heaven --- Victorinus glossing immortality, Andrew the
Bread of Life --- and only the modern instruments connect the urn. The two
readings converge on the same Christ; only the second is
ancient.\endnote{Apoc 2:17, \term{tou manna tou kekrymmenou}. Victorinus ad
loc., ANF 7 (``the hidden manna is immortality''), at the New Advent page
cited above; Andrew's eucharistic reading as reported in the FOTC
translation literature. The stamnos connection (Ex 16:32--34; Heb 9:4;
2 Mach 2:4--8; 2 Baruch 6 and 29) is a commonplace of the modern critical
commentaries (Beale, Aune, reported); no ancient witness for that specific
linkage was found in the study's audit, and no liturgical or magisterial
use exists to report.}

What remains is the study's own act, and it is labeled as
such.\endnote{Synthesis materials: 2 Machabees 2:7--8, Douay-Rheims,
verified at \url{https://drbo.org/chapter/46002.htm} and block-quoted in
the narrative panel; only fragments are re-quoted below. Apocalypse 11:15
is paraphrased with its locus (its Douay wording was not separately
captured in the study's verification records, so no quotation is offered).
The Tyconius and lectionary data are cited in the endnotes above.}

\begin{synthesisbox}
The second letter of 2 Machabees left the hidden ark under a promise with a
horizon: the place unknown ``till God gather together the congregation of
the people, and receive them to mercy,'' and then disclosure --- ``the Lord
will shew these things, and the majesty of the Lord shall appear, and there
shall be a cloud,'' the cloud of Moses and of Solomon. The Apocalypse's
seventh trumpet sounds precisely such a horizon: the moment the loud
voices in heaven proclaim that the kingdom of this world has passed to the
Lord and to his Christ (Apocalypse 11:15), the temple opens and the ark is
seen. The project reads the one text as answering the other: the disclosure
promised for the ingathering, granted at the ingathering --- not a relic
produced from a cave, but the reality the relic guarded, shown at last
where it always belonged. At this same verse the ancients stood at a
crossroads. Tyconius turned toward the Church: the ark superseded, Jerusalem
become the throne (Jeremias 3:16 quoted at the verse). The lectionary of
1969, and this study following it, turn the other way at the same
crossroads: toward the Mother of God, the living ark whom the next sentence
of the vision shows crowned in heaven --- the ark of the Assumption. And
the two turns are not rivals. The Church and the Mother of God are not
competitors for the figure; the Fathers who read the Woman as the Church
and the liturgy that reads her as Mary are reading one mystery at two
distances, for Mary is what the Church will be, and the ark that went up is
the first of the vessels of the Presence to reach its resting place. This
reading is the project's own synthesis: its verbal skeleton is verified
above, its structural continuity is anciently attested in Tyconius, its
Marian form is attested by no ancient commentator and received in the
Church's lectionary.
\end{synthesisbox}

\noindent The table should be read the way the study was written. The
attestation column is honest to the point of severity: most rows show no
ancient witness for the Marian reading, and where antiquity speaks at all
it speaks of Christ first --- his body the gilded ark, his flesh the ark of
the Incarnation, his resurrection the psalm's arising. The reception column
is equally real: a catechism, a dogmatic constitution's argument, two
rites' liturgies. The named distinction holds the two columns apart and
holds them both: attestation is not reception, and the absence of either is
not falsity. A tradition that put Christ first and let his Mother's title
grow from his --- ark as bearer, figure from figure --- is not a tradition
caught inventing; it is a tradition developing in the only order sound
doctrine allows.\endnote{The table is compiled from the study's graded
research records as corrected in its second research pass; every row's
texts, loci, and evidence states are given at the row's discussion in the
body sections, and no row asserts anything not established there. This is
the study's single derived summary of the parallels; per its records
discipline, no other table of them is printed anywhere in the document.}

Where, then, is the ark? The question opened this study, and it can now be
answered three times over. History answers: unknown --- a last sighting
under Josiah, then silence. The canon answers: seen --- in the opened
temple of heaven, at the last trumpet, when the kingdom is the Lord's. The
liturgy answers: assumed --- the living ark carried into the temple not
made with hands, while the Church sings the psalm of the resting place. And
the three answers end in one sentence, which is the whole of this study's
finding and the only map it offers: the ark is with God, carried into his
rest by the One it carried.
