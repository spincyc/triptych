\section{The Fathers: The Making of a Title}

The Fathers gave the ark to Christ before they gave it to his Mother;
and when at last they gave it to his Mother, they did not take it back
from Christ. That is the whole story of this section, and it can be
told with dates. The philology ended with a promise: the Church's
reading of the Visitation is older than the concordances that now
defend it. The promise is kept here, but honestly --- as development,
not as antiquity flat. Explicit predication ---
``Mary \emph{is} the ark'' --- cannot be documented before the fifth
century in any language. What comes earlier is something else, and in
some ways something greater: a third-century Christological ark
typology in which the Virgin already stands at the ark's making, and,
in Syriac, a genuine fourth-century poetry that lets the figure rest
on Mary a full generation before the Council of Ephesus. Every
witness below is printed under its true name and its true date. Where
a famous attribution fails, the failure is stated as a finding.

\subsection{The Christological ark comes first}

The oldest ark exegesis in the Church is about the body of Christ.
Hippolytus of Rome (d.~c.~235), commenting on Daniel, reads the ark of
incorruptible wood as the Lord's own incorrupt humanity. The reader
who has met this Father in the apologetics collections has almost
certainly met him saying something slightly --- and consequentially ---
different:

\begin{witnessbox}{Hippolytus, fragment on Daniel, ANF translation (1886)}
``\ldots the Saviour appeared and showed His own body to the world,
(born) of the Virgin, who was the ark overlaid with pure gold, with
the Word within and the Holy Spirit without.''
\end{witnessbox}

In that Victorian English the relative clause fastens to the Virgin:
\emph{she} is the ark. So read, Hippolytus becomes the earliest
``Mary is the ark'' witness in the language, endlessly reproduced as
such. But the sentence is not a stray fragment. It belongs to the
genuine, continuously preserved \work{Commentary on Daniel}, at
IV.24.3, and in the modern translation of the full text the ambiguity
disappears. There the Savior comes from the Virgin and then offers
into the world ``the Ark, his own body,'' gilded in pure gold ---
within by the Word, without by the Spirit; two paragraphs later the
commentary repeats the equation, the Savior arriving in the world
bearing the incorruptible ark that is his own body. The celebrated
Marian reading of Hippolytus is a translation artifact. What he
actually witnesses is in its own way load-bearing: the ark's
incorruptible wood is Christ's flesh, \emph{and the Virgin is where
that flesh came from}. A second fragment, on the Shepherd Psalm, says
the same ---
the ark of imperishable wood is ``the Saviour Himself,'' sinless in
his humanity, ``of the virgin and the Holy Ghost inwardly, and
outwardly of the word of God, like an ark overlaid with purest
gold.''\endnote{Hippolytus, exegetical fragments, trans.\
Roberts--Donaldson, ANF vol.~5,
\url{https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0502.htm} (the Daniel fragment:
``the Saviour appeared and showed His own body to the world, (born) of
the Virgin, who was the ark overlaid with pure gold''; the Ps~22/23
fragment: ``the ark made of imperishable wood was the Saviour
Himself''). The Daniel passage = \work{Commentarium in Danielem}
IV.24.3 (CPG 1873), confirmed by content-match against T.~C.~Schmidt's
translation of the complete commentary (1st ed.\ 2010, chronicon.net;
\url{https://www.pergrazia.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/0205_hippolytus_commentary-on-daniel_2010.pdf}),
where the Savior ``offered the Ark, his own body, into the world,''
and IV.24.5 repeats: the Savior arrived ``bearing the incorruptible
ark, that is his own body.'' The translation-ambiguity analysis, with
the Migne locus PG 10:647, at
\url{https://parochianus.blog/2016/09/09/the-ark-saint-hippolytus/}
(reported). The Psalm fragment is transmitted separately, reportedly
via Theodoret; fragment-level caution applies to it, not to the Daniel
commentary.}

The same pattern recurs in fifth-century Alexandria and
Constantinople. Among the works transmitted with Cyril of Alexandria
is a catena-like compilation on Old Testament texts, of doubtful
authenticity and labeled so here. It glosses the ark that went before
Israel with the flat equation \term{kibōtos ho Christos},
``the ark is Christ'': as the ark of incorruptible wood and pure gold
carried the law within it, so Christ's humanity, incorrupt in respect
of sin, held within it the Word of God.\endnote{\work{Collectio
dictorum Veteris Testamenti} transmitted among Cyril of Alexandria's
works, on Num 10:33, at approximately PG 77:1221--1224 (column markers
1215 and 1229 flank the passage); read in the Patrologia Graeca scans,
\url{https://archive.org/details/PatrologiaGraeca}. Opening words
verified in the scan: \term{Kibōtos ho Christos}. The compilation is
catena-like and of doubtful authenticity; it is cited here only as a
witness to the pattern, not as Cyril.} And Proclus of Constantinople,
in a Theophany oration transmitted under his name, turns even Noah's
ark the same way: Noah framed an ark of incorruptible wood, but
Christ, the spiritual Noah, built \term{ek tēs aphthorou Marias tēn
tou sōmatos kibōton} --- from the incorrupt Mary the ark of his own
body.\endnote{Proclus (transmitted), Oratio VII, \work{In sancta
Theophania}, PG 65:759--760, read in the Patrologia Graeca scan
(\url{https://archive.org/details/PatrologiaGraeca}). This oration is
not among the five homilies Constas edits as genuine; authenticity
remains to be sorted, and the text is used here only for the shape of
its typology.} The grammar of all three texts carries the whole
development: the ark is Christ's body; Mary is the quarry of the
incorruptible wood. She is inside the figure from the beginning ---
but as source, not yet as subject.

\subsection{Ephrem: allusion before Ephesus}

The first texts in which the figure comes to rest on Mary herself are
not Greek or Latin but Syriac, and they are earlier than almost anyone
expects. Ephrem the Syrian (d.~373), in the genuine \work{Hymns on the
Nativity}, sings the Nativity in the language of the ark's victory
over Dagon (1~Kings 5 = 1~Samuel 5): ``There rose from the Rib a hidden power,
which cut off Satan as Dagon: for in that Ark a Book was hidden that
cried and proclaimed concerning the Conqueror.'' The ark before which
Dagon fell is here the bearer of the proclaiming Book --- and the
bearer is she. In a second hymn the holy household itself becomes the
sanctuary: ``Joseph rose to minister before his Lord, Who was in
Mary. The priest ministered before Thy ark by reason of Thy holiness.
Moses carried the tables of stone\ldots and Joseph bare about the pure
Tablet in whom the Son of the Creator was dwelling.''\endnote{Ephrem,
\work{Hymns on the Nativity}: the Dagon stanza at Nat.~4.112--114
(Beck/McVey numbering) = Rhythm III in J.~B.~Morris, \work{Select
Works of S.~Ephrem the Syrian} (Oxford, 1847), p.~20, whose wording is
quoted (scan: \url{https://archive.org/details/selectworksofsep41ephr});
the Joseph stanzas at Nat.~16.16--17 = Morris, Rhythm XI,
pp.~51--52. The same hymns in the public-domain NPNF II/13 as Nativity
Hymns 3 and 11,
\url{https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3703.htm}. Kathleen McVey's
editorial note to her 1989 translation states the Marian reading for
the genuine corpus --- ``Mary is the Ark; cf.\ Nat.\ 4.113'' --- read
in the accessible text of her volume
(\url{https://archive.org/details/ephrem-the-syrian-hymns}); her
further cross-reference to Nat.~12.3 could not be confirmed by search
and is not relied on.}

This is not the later predication-formula; it is allusive typology,
woven rather than asserted. But it is genuine Ephrem, it is
Mary-referred on the judgment of the standard modern translator of
these hymns, and its author died in 373 --- fifty-eight years before
the Council of Ephesus. The often-quoted ``Greek Ephrem'' prayers
that address Mary as ark and urn of manna are another matter. They
belong to a later pseudonymous corpus and prove nothing about the
fourth century.\endnote{The \work{Precationes ad
Deiparam} and \work{Threni} cited by Livius from Assemani's Greek
Ephrem (Opp.\ Gr.\ III, 522, 575 --- reported loci) belong to the
Ephraem Graecus corpus, whose Marian prayers are generally regarded as
later and not Ephrem's; Sebastian Brock's guide to the editions
(\url{https://syri.ac/brock/ephrem}) is the control for what counts as
genuine.}

\subsection{The fifth century: Greek and Syriac together}

Then comes Ephesus (431), the definition of the Theotokos, and within
a generation the thing itself: explicit, public, liturgical
predication, in Greek and in Syriac at once.

The Greek series can be dated man by man. (Through this subsection
the English renderings of the Greek homilists are editorial, except
where Livius's are credited.) Proclus of Constantinople (d.~446/7),
whose fifth oration is received as genuine in the modern critical
study of his homilies, preaches Mary in a litany of titles: mother,
handmaid, cloud, bridal chamber, and ark of the Master ---
\term{kibōtos, ou ton nomon bastasasa, alla ton nomothetēn
kyophorēsasa}: an ark not carrying the law, but having borne in her
womb the Lawgiver.\endnote{Proclus, Oratio V, \work{Laudatio in
S.~Virginem ac Dei genitricem Mariam}, \S3, PG 65:719D--720, read in
the Patrologia Graeca scan
(\url{https://archive.org/details/PatrologiaGraeca}); Oratio V spans
cols 716--721. Genuine: it is among the five homilies edited as
authentic by Nicholas Constas, \work{Proclus of Constantinople and the
Cult of the Virgin in Late Antiquity} (reported). Livius's citation
``PG 65:710,'' copied through the apologetics lists, is a misprint for
720. A further ``ark gilded within and without'' litany stands in
Oratio VI (PG 65:753--756), but that oration's authenticity is not
covered by Constas's five and it is not relied on here.}

Hesychius, the presbyter-exegete of Jerusalem (d.~after 451), whose
Marian homilies are dated to the 410s and 420s, preaches an ark
wider, longer, and more glorious than Noah's: that one an ark of
animals, this one of life; that one carried Noah, this one Noah's
Maker; that one had second and third stories, this one the whole
fullness of the Trinity. He is also, on present evidence, the first
man in Greek to read the psalter's ark verse of the Virgin by
name: \emph{Arise, O Lord, into thy resting place: thou and
the ark, which thou hast sanctified} (Ps 131:8), a verse whose full
career waits for the last section of this study.
In Livius's Victorian rendering: ``Arise, Lord, into Thy rest, Thou
and the Ark of Thy sanctification, which is very evidently the
Virgin.'' If you are the pearl, Hesychius argues to his Lord,
fittingly is she the ark.\endnote{Hesychius of Jerusalem, Homily V
\work{De S.~Maria Deipara}: the Noah-surpassing litany at PG
93:1461--1462, the Ps 131:8 exegesis at PG 93:1463--1464, both read in
the Patrologia Graeca scan
(\url{https://archive.org/details/PatrologiaGraeca}); the Latin column
runs ``Surge, Domine, in requiem tuam, tu et arca sanctificationis
tuae, quae est haud dubio Virgo Deipara,'' and the Greek continues
\term{ei gar sy margaritēs, eikotōs ekeinē kibōtos}. The English
sentence in the body is Thomas Livius's translation, \work{The Blessed
Virgin in the Fathers of the First Six Centuries} (1893) --- whose
column citation ``PG 93:460--464,'' still propagated in the
apologetics lists, is simply wrong (the homilies stand at PG
93:1453--1477). The homily's standing in Michel Aubineau's critical
edition of the festal homilies (Subsidia Hagiographica 59) has not yet
been consulted; the attribution is reported under that caution. Dating
of the Marian homilies to the 410s--420s: the Oxford Cult of Saints
record E05888 (reported).}

Chrysippus of Jerusalem (d.~479), preaching
in all likelihood for the city's fifteenth-of-August feast, takes up
the same psalm verse and answers it: ``ark truly royal\ldots ark most
precious'' --- an ark, he goes on, not of all the animal kinds as
Noah's was, nor of the stone tablets as Israel's desert companion, but
the ark whose architect and indweller, pilot and merchant,
fellow-traveler and guide is the Maker of all
creation.\endnote{Chrysippus of Jerusalem, \work{Oratio in sanctam
Mariam Deiparam}, ed.\ M.~Jugie, \work{Homélies mariales byzantines}
II, Patrologia Orientalis 19.3 (1926), 336--343; the ark passage at PO
19, pp.~338--339, read in the scan
(\url{https://archive.org/details/patrologiaorient19pariuoft}):
\term{kibōtos gar ontōs basilikē, kibōtos lian polytimos hē
aeiparthenos theotokos}. The short English phrases quoted are
Livius's (1893). Setting and probable feast: the Oxford Cult of Saints
record E04681,
\url{http://csla.history.ox.ac.uk/record.php?recid=E04681}
(reported).}

Around these named preachers stands the anonymous witness that
outlived them all. The Akathist hymn, composed in the fifth or sixth
century, salutes Mary in its twenty-third stanza as tabernacle of God
the Word and \term{kibōte chrysōtheisa tō Pneumati}, ark gilded by
the Spirit. Its popular ascription to Romanos the Melodist is late
and unproven, and no ark epithet has been found in Romanos's genuine
corpus.\endnote{Akathist hymn, stanza 23
(the stanza beginning \term{Psallontes sou ton tokon}; ``Oikos 12'' in
the liturgical numbering); Greek text read at
Greek Wikisource (the \term{Akathistos hymnos} page; exact URL in the
leaf's source audit) and in the transcription at lyricstranslate.com.
Anonymous, fifth--sixth century (post-Ephesus, before the 626 siege to
which the later prooimion is tied); the Romanos ascription is late and
unproven, and searches of his 59 genuine kontakia turned up no ark
epithet.}

Two texts often shelved beside these must be moved, with their labels
showing. The Annunciation homilies transmitted under Gregory
Thaumaturgus (d.~c.~270) do contain the full predication --- ``For the
holy Virgin is in truth an ark, wrought with gold both within and
without, that has received the whole treasury of the sanctuary'' ---
but they are pseudonymous products of the post-Ephesus homiletic
boom, as the ANF's own editors already flagged. They witness the
fifth-century flowering, not the third.\endnote{Ps.-Gregory
Thaumaturgus, First Homily on the Annunciation (CPG 1775; the set
reported at PG 10:1145--1177), ANF 6, at
\url{https://ccel.org/ccel/schaff/anf06/anf06.iii.iv.v.i.html}, which
carries both the ark sentence quoted and the editors' authenticity
note (``even Pellicia objects that this is a spurious work'').
Likewise the ``living ark'' oration \work{De Simeone et Anna} under
the name of Methodius of Olympus (d.~c.~311) is pseudonymous,
fifth--sixth century or later (PG 18:347--381; dating discussion at
\url{https://www.roger-pearse.com/weblog/2023/07/25/the-date-and-authenticity-of-the-oration-concerning-simeon-and-anna-of-pseudo-methodius/};
ANF 6 prints it with editorial doubt,
\url{https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0627.htm}).}

The same disposition governs the celebrated Coptic homily of the
Turin papyrus, which apostrophizes the Virgin as ark of the new
covenant, clothed with purity instead of gold, containing the golden
vessel of the true manna --- the flesh in which divinity resides. It
circulates everywhere as ``St.~Athanasius says Mary is the Ark.'' In
fact it is a homily attributed to Athanasius in a Coptic papyrus; the
attribution is disputed, and no scholarly defense of it has been
located.\endnote{The Turin homily: ed.\ L.-Th.~Lefort, ``L'homélie de
S.~Athanase des papyrus de Turin,'' \work{Le Muséon} 71 (1958), 5--50
and 209--239 (no public-domain English exists; the ark passage is
described, not quoted, from the reported text). Lefort himself is
reported not to have insisted on authenticity; David Frankfurter
cites the piece as Ps.-Athanasius (reported); discussion at
\url{https://turretinfan.wordpress.com/2008/05/08/athanasius-homily-of-the-papyrus-of-turin-pseudographic/}.
A distinct Greek Annunciation homily among Athanasius's spuria (PG 28)
carries an ``ark of sanctification'' and is pseudonymous by
long-standing consensus; the two get conflated in the lists. A
reported ``sacred tabernacle'' in Theodotus of Ancyra (d.~before 446),
Hom.~4 --- tabernacle and temple epithets, not the ark proper --- is
cited in the lists at ``PG 74,'' a propagated typo for PG 77
(Theodotus's works stand at PG 77:1307--1432); the claim itself
remains an unverified lead and is not used here.}

And then the finding that changes the map. At the very moment the
Greek preachers were coining their litanies, a Syriac poet-bishop had
the whole Visitation typology --- the one the philology section
reconstructed echo by echo --- explicit and complete. Jacob of Serugh
(c.~451--521), in his metrical homilies on the Annunciation and the
Visitation, presents Mary abiding in Zachary's house like ``an ark
full of mysteries''; regarded by the household as an ark full of fire;
carried on the road as an ark full of scriptures, in whom dwelt the
interpretation of prophecy's mysteries. And he does not leave the
comparison decorative. The babe exults in Elizabeth's womb so that
Mary's magnificence might exceed the ark's --- John's leap answering
David's dance before the ark (2~Kings 6 = 2~Samuel 6). Mary's
three months in the priest's house stand where the ark's three months
in Obededom's house stood, a blessing on the roof that sheltered
her.\endnote{Jacob of Serugh, homilies \work{On the Annunciation} and
\work{On the Visitation}, in the eight Marian homilies edited by
P.~Bedjan (Paris, 1902), pp.~614--774; English in M.~Hansbury,
\work{On the Mother of God} (SVS Popular Patristics 19, 1998; in
copyright), the ark passages at Hansbury pp.~53, 74, and 80 (``ark
full of mysteries,'' Hansbury 53). Reported here via the full text of
J.~Puthuparampil, \work{Mariological Thought of Mar Jacob of Serugh
(451--521)},
\url{https://archive.org/details/mariologicalthou0000jame}, whose
section on Mary as ark supplies the loci; the wording should be
re-verified against Hansbury's pages before any longer quotation, and
none is offered. Note a conflation abroad in the popular lists: the
``more beautiful than the Ark\ldots David had danced'' quote sometimes
pinned on Maximus of Turin is this Jacob of Serugh passage.} This is
the full Lucan parallel --- leap, dance, three months, blessed house
--- preached in West Syriac around the year 500, centuries before any
concordance. The reading the modern critics call contrived was, to a
poet with the Peshitta in his memory, simply visible.

\subsection{The Latin negatives, printed as findings}

The West is another country, and honesty requires saying so plainly:
the two greatest Latin Fathers yield no genuine Mary-ark text.
Augustine (d.~430), expounding the very psalm verse Hesychius was
reading Marially in the same decades, turns it elsewhere: ``He is our
Head; His ark is His Church: He arose first, the Church will arise
also''; and he registers, as other men's view, that ``The Body of
Christ, that was born of Mary, has been understood by some to be the
ark of sanctification.'' Church or Christ's body --- not Mary.\endnote{Augustine, \work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 131, \S8,
trans.\ Tweed, NPNF I/8, at
\url{https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1801132.htm} (both sentences
quoted verbatim from that page). The Assumption sermon among his works
(Sermo 208 in the appendix numbering) is pseudonymous --- no
Assumption feast existed in Augustine's Africa (reported).}

For Ambrose (d.~397) no Marian ark text could be found at all after a
directed search: his genuine ark exegesis is Noachic-ecclesial or
Christological. The finding is recorded as unfound-after-search
rather than as proof of absence. What circulates everywhere under his
name --- and, in a second printing, under the name of Maximus of Turin
--- is one and the same anonymous sermon, printed twice in Migne:
``saltavit propheta David ante arcam. Arcam autem quid nisi
S.~Mariam dixerimus? Siquidem arca intrinsecus portabat testamenti
tabulas, Maria autem ipsius testamenti gestabat heredem'' --- David
the prophet danced before the ark; and what shall we call the ark but
holy Mary? --- for the ark carried within it the tables of the
testament, while Mary bore the testament's own heir; the ark shone
within and without with the gleam of gold, holy Mary within and
without with the splendor of virginity (an editorial paraphrase, not a
translation). The sermon is used here under its only honest label: a
Latin sermon transmitted under both Ambrose's and Maximus's names,
author unknown, probably of the fifth or sixth century or
later.\endnote{The doublet sermon: PL 17:689 (Sermo XLII among the
Ambrosian spuria) = PL 57:737--740 (Sermo CIV among Maximus's, Bruni's
numbering); the Latin quoted was read in the PL 57 scan,
\url{https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_1847_57},
cols 739--740. Bruni's own admonitio, read there, reassigns it on four
manuscripts' evidence: the sermon ``Maximi nomen praefert. Quamobrem
ejus auctorem non fuisse Ambrosium, sed Maximum judicamus.'' Modern
citations split along the two printings without noticing (a 2021
survey cites it as Maximus, PL 57; a 2024 survey as Ps.-Ambrose, PL
17:689, \url{https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/16/1/17}) --- which is how
the apologetics lists came to count one sermon as two witnesses. No
Marian ark sermon in the genuine Maximus corpus (CCSL 23) surfaced in
any source consulted; Mutzenbecher's disposition of this sermon
remains to be checked. English excerpts: Livius (1893), p.~77.}

One more negative belongs beside these, because the lists assert the
contrary. Cyril of Alexandria's famous homily against Nestorius at
Ephesus hails the Theotokos as revered treasure of the whole world,
unquenchable lamp, crown of virginity, scepter of orthodoxy, temple
that cannot be destroyed, place of him whom no place can hold --- and
it contains no ark. The word \term{kibōtos} occurs nowhere in it, nor
in the great encomium transmitted under his name.\endnote{Cyril of Alexandria, Homily 4 at Ephesus, PG
77:991--996, read in the Patrologia Graeca scan (Greek and Latin;
epithet litany at col.\ 992) and checked against two independent
English renderings
(\url{https://www.crossroadsinitiative.com/media/articles/mary-mother-of-god-theotokos-cyril-of-alexandria/};
\url{https://erickybarra.wordpress.com/2018/01/21/5th-century-marian-devotee-st-cyril-of-alexandria-376-444/}):
verified negative for \term{kibōtos}. Same negative for Homily 11,
\work{Encomium in S.~Mariam Deiparam}, PG 77:1029--1040, and for an
OCR sweep of the whole span of the lesser homilies (cols
981--1116, approximate span).}

\subsection{The summit, and the medieval West}

The Greek development has a summit, and everyone has always known
where it is. John of Damascus (d.~c.~749), preaching on the
Dormition, opens his second homily with the sentence that would
eventually be read at Rome itself. As it circulates in English:
``Today the sacred and living ark of the living God, who conceived
her Creator Himself, takes up her abode in the temple of God, not
made by hands,'' and the lesson continues that David her forefather
exults. The third homily hails the true and
living ark of the Lord; the first applies the ark verse of the psalm
to her passage into rest.\endnote{John of Damascus, three homilies on
the Dormition (CPG 8061--8063; PG 96:700--761; Homily II spans PG
96:722--754). The English sentence quoted is the circulating text of
Hom.~II \S2, reported at PG 96:723 by converging secondary witnesses
(full text at
\url{https://malankaraworld.com/library/shunoyo/shunoyo-Homily-st-John-Dmascus-2.htm});
it appears to be Mary H.~Allies's public-domain translation,
\work{St.~John Damascene on Holy Images, followed by Three Sermons on
the Assumption} (London, 1898), though that identification has not
been confirmed line by line. The Latin of the same sentence ---
``Hodie sacra et animata arca Dei viventis\ldots Et David exsultat
ejus parens'' --- is verified in the 1942 Breviarium Romanum
(\url{https://archive.org/details/breviarium-romanum-1942}) and
quoted in the next section. Hom.~III (``the true and living Ark'') and
Hom.~I's use of Ps 131:8: reported.} With the Damascene the title is
no longer an epithet in a litany; it is the structural figure of a
feast --- the living ark carried into the temple not made with hands.
And it is exactly this homily that the Roman Breviary would appoint
as the Matins lessons of the Assumption from 1568 to 1950, a
transmission that belongs to the next section.

And the medieval West? It carried the figure quietly, and mostly did
not preach it --- a double finding, and both halves matter. The quiet
carrier came first. The hymn \work{Quem terra, pontus, sidera},
ascribed by long tradition to Venantius Fortunatus (d.~c.~610) and
sung in the office of Our Lady continuously thereafter, confesses in
its third stanza: ``Beata Mater munere, / Cujus, supernus Artifex, /
Mundum pugillo continens, / Ventris sub arca clausus est'' --- blessed
Mother, by whose gift the supernal Artificer, who holds the world in
his palm, was enclosed beneath the ark of her womb (an editorial
paraphrase, not a translation).\endnote{\work{Quem terra, pontus,
sidera} (in its pre-Urbanite form \work{Quem terra, pontus,
aethera}), stanza 3, verified as printed in the Breviarium Romanum,
Pars Aestiva (1942 printing), Assumption Matins,
\url{https://archive.org/details/breviarium-romanum-1942}; indexed
for the medieval office as Cantus 008375:04. The Fortunatus ascription
is the standard attribution, reported, not independently verified
here.}

Against that one singing stanza stand the silences, each verified in
its sources. The treatise that governed the medieval
West's whole Assumption theology --- \work{Cogitis me}, written
c.~860 by Paschasius Radbertus and read for centuries at Matins under
Jerome's name --- builds the feast entirely on the Song of Songs; the
word \term{arca} does not occur in it. Bernard's Assumption sermons
contain one ark only, and it is Noah's, in an ascetic digression.
Thomas Aquinas, asking in the \work{Summa} what the ark signified,
answers with the whole Hippolytan inheritance and no Marian clause at
all: ``He is also signified by the ark: because just as the ark was
made of setim-wood, so was Christ's body composed of most pure
members''; the golden pot within is ``His holy soul, having manna,
i.e.\ `all the fulness of the Godhead.'\thinspace'' Across the eleven
questions of the \work{Summa} on Our Lady and the infancy of Christ
the word ark never appears.\endnote{\work{Cogitis me} (Ps.-Jerome, Ep.~9; Paschasius
Radbertus, c.~860), PL 30:121--147, read in the scan

The chant repertory says the same thing with numbers. In the indexed

\subsection{What development looks like}

Now the pattern can be stated as a principle: in the Fathers,
substance precedes predication, and Christ precedes every other
referent. The third century reads the ark of incorruptible wood as
the incorrupt flesh of Christ, with the Virgin as its source. The
fourth, in Syriac, lets the figure rest on her allusively. The fifth,
in Greek and Syriac at once --- Proclus before 446, Hesychius and
Chrysippus in Jerusalem, Jacob of Serugh by about 500, the anonymous
Akathist --- says it outright, and says it without ever un-saying the
Christological reading, since every one of these texts defines her as
ark precisely by what she carried. The West keeps the Christological
reading almost undiluted for a millennium, one hymn stanza excepted,
until Greek lessons and late feasts carry the Marian figure in.

This is not the profile of a legend. A legend grows loudest where
evidence is thinnest and backdates its credentials; the backdated
credentials here --- Ps.-Gregory, Ps.-Methodius, the Turin homily,
the double-named Latin sermon --- were removable, this section has
removed them, and the fifth-century flowering stands without their
help. It is the profile of development: a figure whose substance was
in the Church's hands from the beginning, articulated explicitly when
the Theotokos definition gave the articulation its grammar. What the
Fathers achieved was attestation --- ancient, datable, cross-lingual.
Whether the Church made that attestation her own is a question about
reception; and reception belongs to the liturgy, to which the
Fathers, in the person of the Damascene read aloud at Roman Matins,
now hand the title.
