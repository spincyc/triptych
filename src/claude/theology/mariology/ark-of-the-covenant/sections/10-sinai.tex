\section{Sinai: The Ark Commanded}

The ark of the covenant enters Scripture as a working drawing. Israel is
encamped at the foot of Sinai; the mountain has been smoke and trumpet and a
voice so terrible that the people begged not to hear it again; and when Moses
goes up into the cloud to receive the pattern of God's dwelling, the first
thing dictated to him is a piece of joinery. ``Frame an ark of setim wood,''
he is told, ``the length whereof shall be of two cubits and a
half.''\endnote{Exodus 25:10, 15--18, 20--22, Douay-Rheims (Challoner), read
at \url{https://drbo.org/chapter/02025.htm}. All Scripture quotations in this
section are Douay-Rheims wording as printed at the drbo.org chapter pages
cited in these notes. Modern versions render \term{setim} as ``acacia'' (so
the RSV at Ex 25:10, cited here as a comparative modern witness, not as base
text).} Setim is the acacia of the desert; the thing specified is a wooden
chest with a lid, overlaid with gold, small enough to be carried on two
poles. The modern reader, who came for thunder, may fairly feel the
anticlimax, and had better feel it honestly. Nothing in the specification is
beyond a competent carpenter, and the text does not pretend otherwise. But
the order of the blueprint is itself the first lesson. A palace is designed
outward from its throne room, and the tabernacle is designed outward from
this box: the ark stands at the head of the pattern, and, as readers have
long observed, everything else in the sanctuary is arranged around it.

The specification continues. Rings of gold, and staves to run through them:
``And they shall be always in the rings, neither shall they at any time be
drawn out of them'' (25:15) --- built, from the first day, to move. Above
the chest, ``Thou shalt make also a propitiatory of the purest gold''
(25:17), and two cherubims of gold ``on the two sides of the
oracle\ldots{} covering the oracle\ldots{} their faces being turned towards
the propitiatory wherewith the ark is to be covered'' (25:18, 20). Within
it, ``the testimony which I will give thee'' (25:16) --- the tables of the
law. And then the sentence for which the whole chapter exists: ``Thence will
I give orders, and will speak to thee over the propitiatory, and from the
midst of the two cherubims, which shall be upon the ark of the testimony''
(25:22). Where modern versions have God promise to \emph{meet} Israel there,
the Douay's blunter idiom keeps the function on the surface: orders will be
given, speech will happen, and the box is the appointed place of it. The ark
is not a reliquary, not an idol, not an instrument to be worked. It is
furniture built for a voice.

\subsection{The words of the thing}

This study keeps one English Bible beneath it, the Douay-Rheims of
Challoner, and the Douay's words for the ark's parts are not the words most
readers know. Three of them need fixing now, once, so that every later
section can use them without apology.

\emph{Propitiatory.} Where modern versions print ``mercy seat,'' the Douay
prints \emph{propitiatory}: the lid of ``the purest gold'' laid upon the
ark. The Hebrew is \term{kapporet}, from a root that means at once to cover
and to atone; the Greek translators rendered it \term{hilastērion} --- at
its first occurrence with a doubled expression, \term{hilastērion epithema},
``a propitiatory, a cover'' --- and Jerome's Latin is \term{propitiatorium},
whence the Douay's word.\endnote{Septuagint wording from the Elpenor edition
(the Greek text of the Church of Greece, with the ark command at LXX Ex 25:9
and the propitiatory at LXX 25:16; the Elpenor versification runs one verse
behind the Hebrew in this stretch), read at ellopos.net. Clementine Vulgate
as printed at drbo.org: Ex 25:17 ``Facies et propitiatorium de auro
mundissimo''; Ex 25:22 ``Inde praecipiam, et loquar ad te supra
propitiatorium, ac de medio duorum cherubim.''} It is a word with a long
future in this study, and Panel II will follow it; for the present it is the
golden lid, nothing more.

\emph{Oracle.} The Douay uses this one word for two different things, and
the reader must watch it. In Exodus and Leviticus ``the oracle'' is the zone
of the propitiatory itself: the cherubims stand ``on the two sides of the
oracle''; God will appear ``in a cloud over the oracle'' (Leviticus 16:2).
In the books of Kings the same word names the temple's whole inner chamber,
the \term{debir}, the windowless room where the ark will one day rest ---
the Vulgate's \term{oraculum}, the place of speaking: ``Oraculum autem in
medio domus, in interiori parte fecerat, ut poneret ibi arcam foederis
Domini'' (3 Kings 6:19 = 1 Kings 6:19).\endnote{Douay ``oracle'' for the
propitiatory zone: Ex 25:18, 20 (\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/02025.htm});
Lev 16:2, 13 (\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/03016.htm}). For the inner
chamber: 3 Kings 8:6 (\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/11008.htm}); the Latin
of 3 Kings 6:19 from the Clementine Vulgate as printed at drbo.org.} Both
senses are named from the same fact, the promise of Exodus 25:22: God speaks
there. The context will always show which is meant.

\emph{Covenant, testament, testimony.} In Exodus the chest is ``the ark of
the testimony,'' the testimony being the tables. Elsewhere its running name
is ``the ark of the covenant of the Lord''; but the Douay also prints, in
three places, ``the ark of the testament'': ``But the ark of the testament
of the Lord and Moses departed not from the camp'' (Numbers 14:44); the
``ark of the testament'' of Hebrews 9:4; and --- the last time the ark is
named in the Bible --- ``the ark of his testament'' seen in the opened
temple of heaven (Apocalypse 11:19), a name to remember, since the Bible's
final sight of the ark stands under it.\endnote{Num 14:44 at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/04014.htm}; Heb 9:4 at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/65009.htm}; Apoc 11:19 at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/73011.htm}. The Douay renders the
covenant construct now ``covenant,'' now ``testament''; ``ark of the
testimony'' translates a second Hebrew construct, \term{aron ha-edut}, but
the referent is the same chest.} Covenant and testament translate one and
the same phrase; this study says ``ark of the covenant'' throughout and asks
the reader to hear the other two as the same name. The nouns themselves are
plain ones. The Hebrew \term{aron} is an ordinary word for a chest; the
Greek Bible's \term{kibōtos} is the very noun it uses for Noah's vessel ---
``until the day that Noe entered into the ark'' (Luke
17:27).\endnote{Luke 17:26--27 at \url{https://drbo.org/chapter/49017.htm};
the Greek \term{kibōtos} is verified in the project's records at LXX Ex
25:9, Heb 9:4, and Apoc 11:19.} What hallows the box is not its noun but
its construct: \emph{of the covenant}, \emph{of the testimony}, \emph{of
God}. Finally, the Douay's book names are used throughout: the books modern
Bibles call 1--2 Samuel and 1--2 Kings are here four books of Kings, so
that the temple chapters stand in 3 Kings; Paralipomenon is Chronicles; and
the psalms carry Vulgate numbers.

\subsection{Two makers, two arks?}

Exodus assigns the making. God names Bezalel the son of Uri, of the tribe
of Juda, fills him with the spirit of God for the work, and sets the ark
first in his commission; and in the execution account the blueprint is
repeated nearly word for word --- \term{epoiēse Beseleēl tēn kibōton},
``Beseleel made the ark,'' as the Greek Bible puts it. He is the only named
craftsman of the ark in the Hebrew text.\endnote{Exodus 31:1--11 and
37:1--9, cited in paraphrase; the loci are verified in the project's
Scripture corpus, but the Douay wording of these verses was not separately
re-anchored, so no English quotation is printed. The Greek sentence is LXX
Ex 38:1 (Elpenor edition, ellopos.net), where the differently ordered Greek
construction account places the making of the ark.}

Deuteronomy remembers differently. There Moses himself recounts: ``thou
shalt make an ark of wood''; ``And I made an ark of setim wood''; and, of
the tables, ``\ldots{}put the tables into the ark, that I had made, and
they are there till this present, as the Lord commanded me'' (Deuteronomy
10:1, 3, 5).\endnote{Deuteronomy 10:1--8, Douay-Rheims,
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/05010.htm}.} In Deuteronomy the maker is
Moses, the making comes before he ascends again with the blank tables, and
the account knows no propitiatory, no cherubims, no gold --- a box for the
tables and nothing else. Two kinds of explanation hold the field.
Harmonists, ancient and modern, have posited a provisional chest later
replaced by Bezalel's finished work, a solution transmitted in rabbinic
tradition; critical scholarship instead assigns the plain chest and the
golden ark to different literary strata with different theologies of the
divine presence, and von Rad influentially read Deuteronomy as deliberately
demoting the throne of God to a container for documents.\endnote{Both the
harmonistic two-ark proposal (transmitted in rabbinic tradition) and the
source-critical assignment (a plain Deuteronomic chest; a golden Priestly
ark) are reported literature, summarized at one remove in the project's
research records and not verified at their loci for this study.} This study
states the tension and leaves it standing: both accounts are canonical,
neither is embarrassed, and no verse resolves them.

\subsection{What lay within, and what lay before}

The prepositions of the Pentateuch are exact, and it will pay to notice
them now. \emph{In} the ark goes the testimony: ``And thou shalt put in the
ark the testimony which I will give thee'' (Exodus 25:16). But the omer of
manna that Aaron lays up as a memorial is placed \emph{before} the
testimony (Exodus 16:33--34), and Aaron's rod that blossomed is laid up, in
the same phrase, before the testimony, as a token against the rebellious
(Numbers 17:10).\endnote{Exodus 16:33--34 and Numbers 17:10, cited in
paraphrase with loci; the before-the-testimony placement is explicit at
both. Douay wording for these verses was not re-anchored in the project's
records, so no quotation is printed.} Before, not in. The precision will
matter, because the Epistle to the Hebrews will one day list within the ark
``a golden pot that had manna, and the rod of Aaron, that had blossomed,
and the tables of the testament'' (Hebrews 9:4), where the books of Kings,
at the ark's installation in the temple, will find nothing in it but the
tables. That knot is real; this study unties nothing. It is stated here,
taken up when the ark enters the temple, and carried openly in the study's
closing records. One more distinction of the same kind: the book of the law
that Moses writes at the end of his life is placed at the ark's side, as a
witness against Israel --- the tables in the ark, the book beside
it.\endnote{Deuteronomy 31:9, 24--26, cited in paraphrase; loci verified in
the project's Scripture corpus.} A people who kept even their furniture's
prepositions straight were not casual about this chest.

\subsection{The law of approach}

When the tabernacle is at last reared, Moses puts the testimony into the
ark, sets the propitiatory above it, carries it within, and hangs the veil;
and the sign that the work is accepted follows at once. ``The cloud covered
the tabernacle of the testimony, and the glory of the Lord filled it.
Neither could Moses go into the tabernacle of the covenant, the cloud
covering all things and the majesty of the Lord shining, for the cloud had
covered all'' (Exodus 40:32--33).\endnote{Exodus 40, Douay-Rheims,
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/02040.htm}. Douay Exodus 40 has thirty-six
verses; the cloud-and-glory pericope stands at Douay 40:32--36 = Hebrew and
RSV 40:34--38. Dual citation is given once here; Douay numbers are used
hereafter.} Even Moses cannot enter while the glory is there. Yet the
promise of Sinai is kept: when he does go in to speak with the Lord, the
voice comes to him from above the propitiatory, from between the two
cherubims (Numbers 7:89).\endnote{Numbers 7:89, cited in paraphrase; locus
verified in the project's Scripture corpus.}

Around this presence Israel's law builds a whole grammar of handling. The
staves stay ``always in the rings'': the ark is never to be gripped
directly, and never unready to move. On the march it is carried by the sons
of Caath --- but only after Aaron and his sons have gone in and wrapped it,
veil and covering and cloth: ``then shall the sons of Caath enter in to
carry the things wrapped up: and they shall not touch the vessels of the
sanctuary, lest they die'' (Numbers 4:15); and again, ``Let not others by
any curiosity see the things that are in the sanctuary before they be
wrapped up, otherwise they shall die'' (4:20).\endnote{Numbers 4:5--6, 15,
20, Douay-Rheims, \url{https://drbo.org/chapter/04004.htm}.} ``By any
curiosity'' is the Douay's phrase, and worth remembering; it will return in
this study at Beth-shemesh. And the porterage is not menial work but a
consecration: ``At that time he separated the tribe of Levi, to carry the
ark of the covenant of the Lord, and to stand before him in the ministry,
and to bless in his name until this present day'' (Deuteronomy 10:8).

Then there is the approach of the one man on the one day. Leviticus 16
warns Aaron ``that he enter not at all into the sanctuary, which is within
the veil before the propitiatory, with which the ark is covered, lest he
die,'' for God says, ``I will appear in a cloud over the oracle.'' Once in
the year the high priest comes within, and comes furnished: with incense,
so that its cloud ``may cover the oracle, which is over the testimony''
(16:13), and with the blood of the bullock and of the goat, sprinkled
``towards the propitiatory to the east''
(16:14--15).\endnote{Leviticus 16:2, 12--15, Douay-Rheims,
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/03016.htm}.} Blood and cloud, one man, one
day: the propitiatory is the one place on earth where atonement and
presence are appointed to meet, and the whole theology of Panel II hangs
from this chapter. Notice meanwhile what kind of law this is. The Presence
is not forbidden to Israel; it is appointed --- a way in, a time, a
wrapping, a tribe, a day. Holiness here is not a wall but a manner of
approach, and the narrative to come will show, more than once, what its
breach costs.

\subsection{Throne, footstool, container}

What was it, then, this gilded chest with its golden lid and its attendant
cherubims? The learned literature has returned three answers. Some read the
ark as a throne, the seat of the invisibly enthroned Lord --- the older
German scholarship's ``empty throne,'' refined by Haran into a single
composite structure of chest, propitiatory, and cherubim, and set by
Mettinger within a whole pre-exilic theology of Sion, the King unseen upon
the cherubims. Others, with de Vaux --- the Dominican whose \work{Ancient
Israel} long served Catholic readers as the standard account --- read it as
a footstool: the cherubims' wings form the throne, and the chest lies
beneath the feet of the enthroned God. And Deuteronomy itself calls it a
container, ``an ark of wood'' made to hold the tables and nothing else.
Seow's survey observes that the ancient Near East knew a custom joining the
last two readings: treaty documents were laid up at the feet of the god, so
that a box for covenant tablets and a footstool for the covenant Lord are
not two objects but one. The newest full-length study, Eichler's, rejects
throne and footstool alike and argues that ark and cherubim formed a
deliberately empty focus, an aniconic guard against picturing God at
all.\endnote{Reported literature, consulted at one remove (reviews, survey
articles, and publisher listings; each position remains to be spot-checked
at its own pages before further weight is placed on it): Roland de Vaux,
O.P., \work{Ancient Israel: Its Life and Institutions}, trans. John McHugh
(1961); Menahem Haran, \work{Temples and Temple-Service in Ancient Israel}
(Oxford: Clarendon, 1978); Tryggve N. D. Mettinger, \work{The Dethronement
of Sabaoth: Studies in the Shem and Kabod Theologies} (Lund: Gleerup,
1982); C. L. Seow, ``Ark of the Covenant,'' in \work{The Anchor Bible
Dictionary}, vol. 1 (New York: Doubleday, 1992); Gerhard von Rad, ``The
Tent and the Ark,'' in \work{The Problem of the Hexateuch and Other
Essays}, trans. E. W. Trueman Dicken (1966); Raanan Eichler, \work{The Ark
and the Cherubim}, FAT 146 (Tübingen: Mohr Siebeck, 2021), whose
bibliographic details are verified at the publisher's listing and whose
argument is reported from reviews.}

The canon will not settle this dispute, because the canon pulls all three
ways. Deuteronomy's box holds the tables; David couples ``the ark of the
Lord, and the footstool of our God'' (1 Paralipomenon 28:2); the one psalm
that names the ark bids Israel ``adore in the place where his feet stood''
(Psalm 131:7 = Hebrew 132:7); and the histories name God as sitting upon
the cherubims precisely when they name the ark (1 Kings 4:4; 2 Kings 6:2 =
1 Samuel 4:4; 2 Samuel 6:2).\endnote{1 Paralipomenon 28:2, Douay-Rheims,
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/13028.htm}; Psalm 131:7,
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/21131.htm}. The Douay coordinates ``the ark
of the Lord, and the footstool of our God'' as two items; the reading of
the ark as itself the footstool is an interpretation of the verse, not its
surface.} The scholarly quarrel is really over which function is original
and which is reinterpretation --- and that question each scholar decides by
his prior dating of the sources, not by any datum about the object itself.
It is well to say so plainly, and to say also what every school concedes:
no physical trace of the ark exists or is expected; a portable shrine of
the Lord stands in the earliest strata every school accepts; and the ark
was gone before the Second Temple stood. The dispute lives in the stretch
between Sinai and the settled land, precisely where the texts are fewest.

Whether the chest that Solomon's priests carried into the oracle was the
chest that Bezalel made is a question of fact, and questions of fact are
settled by evidence, not by temperament. The evidence is textual and
comparative, for the object has been gone these five-and-twenty centuries,
and every party argues from the same documents. One distinction does the
necessary work here: \emph{attestation is not existence}. That the ark's
full theology is articulated late is certain; that the object is a late
invention is a separate claim, which late articulation cannot by itself
establish --- development of doctrine about a thing is not evidence against
the thing. Scholars who read the narratives as monarchic or exilic
composition --- Ahlstr\"om and van der Toorn may be consulted at their best
--- conclude from the lateness of the sources to the lateness of the
object; the inference is exactly as strong as the premise that nothing in
Israel's memory outran its archives. Egyptologists and comparatists who set
the ark beside the gilded barque shrines and pole-borne chests of the Late
Bronze age --- Kitchen, Noegel, and now Eichler's full study of the ark and
cherubim --- find a design at home in the centuries the Bible itself claims
for it, and in no later ones.\endnote{The compositional case: G. W.
Ahlstr\"om, ``The Travels of the Ark: A Religio-Political Composition,''
\work{Journal of Near Eastern Studies} 43 (1984); Karel van der Toorn and
Cees Houtman, ``David and the Ark,'' \work{Journal of Biblical Literature}
113 (1994). The Late Bronze comparative case: Kenneth A. Kitchen, \work{On
the Reliability of the Old Testament} (Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 2003); Scott
B. Noegel, ``The Egyptian Origin of the Ark of the Covenant,'' in
\work{Israel's Exodus in Transdisciplinary Perspective}, ed. T. E. Levy,
T. Schneider, and W. H. C. Propp (Cham: Springer, 2015); Eichler as in the
previous note. All are reported literature, consulted at one remove; none
was opened at the page for this study.} The Church, for her part, has never
defined the carpentry; she receives the ark as Scripture presents it, a
real thing really commanded, and reads its whole career --- made, carried,
lost --- as ordered to the One it prefigured. This study therefore reports
the range, adopts no school's unexamined premise, and proceeds on the
received history, which no discovered fact contradicts.

\subsection{The ark sets out}

In the second year the camp breaks up from the mountain. The cloud is
``over them by day,'' and the ark does not travel in the baggage: it goes
first, and Israel's oldest war-song goes with it.\endnote{Numbers
10:33--36, Douay-Rheims, \url{https://drbo.org/chapter/04010.htm}. Verse
34, the cloud ``over them by day,'' is quoted only in the fragment
captured in the project's records and is otherwise paraphrased; the
witness block below accordingly prints verses 33 and 35--36.}

\begin{witnessbox}{Numbers 10:33, 35--36, Douay-Rheims}
\ldots{}the ark of the covenant of the Lord went before them, for three
days providing a place for the camp.

And when the ark was lifted up, Moses said: Arise, O Lord, and let thy
enemies be scattered, and let them that hate thee, flee from before thy
face.

And when it was set down, he said: Return, O Lord, to the multitude of the
host of Israel.
\end{witnessbox}

Three days ahead of the people, seeking out their resting place: the rest
of this history is that verse enacted. The ark is on the road, and the next
thing in its path is a river.
