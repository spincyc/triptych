\section{The pattern: what the journey taught}

The road is behind us: Sinai to Silo, Aphec to Sion, the temple, the
silence. A reader who has walked it has not been following the fortunes
of a treasure. He has been following a teaching, delivered not in
propositions but in itineraries, plagues, processions, and one locked
room entered once a year. It is time to step back and say what the road
taught. One principle is the spine: the ark's
story is told as the itinerary of the glory of God among men. Three
more hang from it. The ark's holiness is the manners God requires of
creatures in his presence. The ark's golden cover is the place where
blood and mercy met, and the New Testament gives that place's own name
to Christ. And the ark's entrances were sung---by Israel at the gates
of Sion, and still, of Christ, at the doors of churches.

\subsection{The presence: the itinerary of the glory}

The Bible narrates the ark's end not as the loss of an object but as
the departure and return of the glory of God. The canon's own
vocabulary makes the equation before any theologian does. When the ark
was taken at Aphec, the wife of Phinees, dying in childbirth, named her
son Ichabod. The narrator has her say it twice: ``The glory is departed
from Israel, because the ark of God was taken'' (1 Kings 4:22; in the
Hebrew naming, 1 Samuel 4:22).\endnote{1 Kings (1 Samuel) 4:21--22,
Douay-Rheims (Challoner),
read at \url{https://drbo.org/chapter/09004.htm}. The verses are quoted
in full in the Aphec section above; the ark--glory equation
(\term{kabod}) is theirs, not this study's.} Ark and glory,
\term{kabod}, are one loss. Israel's psalmody remembered Aphec in the
same terms:

\begin{witnessbox}{Psalm 77:60--61 (Hebrew 78:60--61), Douay-Rheims}
``And he put away the tabernacle of Silo, his tabernacle where he dwelt
among men. And he delivered their strength into captivity: and their
beauty into the hands of the enemy.''
\end{witnessbox}

On the surface of this English the ark is nowhere in sight. The Douay,
following the Latin psalter's Greek line, prints ``their strength\ldots{}
their beauty,'' as though Israel's own force had gone captive. In the
Hebrew the pronoun is God's: he delivered \emph{his} strength
(\term{oz}) into captivity, \emph{his} beauty or glory (\term{tiferet})
into the enemy's hand. And what was carried to Azotus that day was the
ark. Reading the psalm's ``strength'' and ``beauty'' as the ark is
therefore a synthesis. It stands on the Hebrew wording and on the
Ichabod verses, not on the English surface.\endnote{Psalm 77:60--61
Douay-Rheims, read at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/21077.htm}. The Douay follows the
Gallican/Septuagint pronouns (``their''); the Masoretic text reads
``his strength\ldots{} his glory'' (\term{oz}, \term{tiferet}), terms
the commentators take as ark titles in this psalm's retelling of 1
Kings 4. The ark identification is printed here as source-grounded
synthesis resting on the Hebrew and on 1 Kings 4:21--22, not as the
Douay text's own statement.} The glory departed; and the glory came
back. At the dedication of Solomon's temple the sign of Sinai
returned: ``a cloud filled the house of the Lord,'' and the priests
could not stand to minister, ``for the glory of the Lord had filled the
house'' (3 Kings 8:10--11 = 1 Kings 8:10--11), the scene quoted in full
in the temple section above.\endnote{3 Kings (1 Kings) 8:10--11,
Douay-Rheims, read at \url{https://drbo.org/chapter/11008.htm};
block-quoted in the temple section.}

Then, at the end, the departure was shown to a prophet in slow motion.
Ezechiel, a priest among the exiles, watched the glory leave the
temple by stations, as if reluctant to go. It rose from the cherub to
the threshold of the house, and the house filled with the cloud
(Ezechiel 9:3; 10:4). It went out from the threshold and rested over
the cherubim at the east gate (10:18--19). At last it went up from the
midst of the city and stood upon the mountain east of the city
(11:22--23)---the Mount of Olives, as readers ancient and modern have
identified that mountain. Of the ark, in all this, not one word. The
vision has already outgrown the furniture: the Presence departs, and
what is left behind is left to the fire. But the same prophet was shown
the return along the same road. The glory of the God of Israel came
from the way of the east, entered by the east gate, and filled the
house. The voice named the place his throne and the place of the soles
of his feet, where he will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel
for ever (43:1--7). And in Ezechiel's visionary temple there is no ark
at all. The throne is now the sanctuary itself.\endnote{Ezechiel
9:3; 10:4; 10:18--19; 11:22--23; 43:1--7, cited by locus and given here
in paraphrase; the wording of each station was verified for the
research record. The identification of the mountain on the east side of
the city (Douay: ``the mount that is on the east side of the city'') with
the Mount of Olives is the common reading,
ancient and modern.} Jewish memory kept the same shape: a tradition in
the Talmud counts ten stages of the Shekinah's withdrawal from the
temple, the last from the city to the Mount of Olives, where it
tarried, calling Israel to return.\endnote{b.~Rosh Hashanah 31a, with
Lamentations Rabbah, proem 25; reported at one remove, the loci not
read for this study. Cited as Jewish reception of the same canonical
shape, not as an exegesis of Ezechiel.}

This is the frame in which Jeremias's word about the ark---the only
prophetic text that names it---stops sounding like an obituary and
starts sounding like theology. The days come, says the prophet, when
they shall say no more ``The ark of the covenant of the Lord''; it
shall not come upon the heart, nor be remembered, nor missed. The
Hebrew adds a precision the Douay's ``neither shall
that be done any more'' carries less sharply: it shall not be
\emph{made again}. Why not? Because ``At that time Jerusalem shall be
called the throne of the Lord'' (Jeremias 3:16--17). The ark is not to
be replaced, because what it was a vessel of will be present in a
larger mode: not a chest in a city but the city itself become the
throne. The ancients read the verse exactly this way, and joined it to
the verse where this whole history will end. Tyconius, whose commentary shaped
the whole Latin tradition on the Apocalypse, quotes Jeremias 3:16 at
Apocalypse 11:19 to say that the ark seen in heaven is the Church---the
old vessel surpassed, the figure filled.\endnote{Jeremias
3:16--17, Douay-Rheims, read at \url{https://drbo.org/chapter/28003.htm};
the Hebrew of 3:16 ends \term{welo yeaseh od}, ``neither shall it be
made again.'' Tyconius, \work{Expositio Apocalypseos} on Apoc 11:19
(ed. Gryson, CCSL 107A; English trans. Gumerlock, FOTC 134, read for
the research record): the temple opened in heaven is the Church, ``the
church is the Ark of the Covenant,'' with Jeremias 3:16 quoted at the
verse as warrant. His reading, and the whole ancient commentary
tradition on that verse, is treated in the Assumption section below.}
Whether that is the last word about the ark of Apocalypse 11:19 is a
question whose hour has not yet come; what matters here is the shape. The arc
even closes where the glory paused. From the Mount of Olives, where
Ezechiel saw the glory stand as it left the city, the Glory in flesh
wept over Jerusalem, and from that mountain he ascended (Luke
19:37--41; Acts 1:9--12). This arc---presence, departure, return---is a
theological reading of the canon's own architecture, not a report about
where a gilded chest was carried. The honest range of historical
opinion on that question was given already, and is not altered here.
But the architecture is really there. The journey ended with John's one
sentence about where the ark was finally seen; this arc is the reason
the canon could end the story nowhere else.

\subsection{The holiness: the Presence is not managed}

A glory that elects its own dwelling sets the terms of its company.
That is the second lesson of the road: the presence of God is a gift
that cannot be conscripted and a holiness that cannot be handled. The
narrative taught it in two forms---presumption, which would use the
Presence, and familiarity, which would handle it.

It cannot be conscripted. At Hormah the presumptuous attack went up
without the sign of the covenant: ``But the ark of the testament of the
Lord and Moses departed not from the camp'' (Numbers 14:44)---and
Israel was cut to pieces.\endnote{Numbers 14:44, Douay-Rheims, read at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/04014.htm}. ``Ark of the testament'' is
the Douay's rendering here of the same construct printed ``ark of the
covenant'' elsewhere; the terminology section above owns this note.}
At Aphec the elders reversed the arrangement and fetched the ark
\emph{to} the battle, reasoning that its arrival would save them
from their enemies (1 Kings 4:3). The narrative answered with the
capture itself. God is not carried into service. The man who finally
understood this was David, on the worst day of his life. Fleeing
Absalom, with the priests standing ready to bring the ark into exile
with him, he sent it back: ``Carry back the ark of God into the city:
if I shall find grace in the sight of the Lord, he will bring me again,
and he will shew me it, and his tabernacle'' (2 Kings
15:25).\endnote{2 Kings (2 Samuel) 15:24--29, Douay-Rheims, read at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/10015.htm}; v.~25 quoted. The elders'
reasoning at Aphec is paraphrased from 1 Kings 4:3.} That sentence
inverts the elders' logic at Aphec. It is also the last the reader sees
of the ark in the second book of Kings: the king who will not use God
is the king God brings home.

It cannot be handled. The journey had two death-scenes: the men of
Bethsames struck for looking into the ark, and Oza struck ``for his
rashness'' when he put out his hand to steady it. Their textual
difficulties were narrated above; they are real, and they were left
standing. But the theology does not hang on the cruxes. In both scenes
the offense is the same: a creature treating the throne of the
Presence as an object among objects, to be inspected in the one case,
managed in the other. The question the men of Bethsames asked
afterward---who can stand before the Lord, this holy God? (1 Kings
6:20)---is the narrative's own summary, and it is a question, not a
complaint.\endnote{1 Kings 6:19--20 (the Bethsames crux, with the
number as the Douay prints it) and 2 Kings 6:6--7 (Oza, Douay ``struck
him for his rashness'') are quoted and discussed in their own sections
above; 6:20 is paraphrased here. Loci at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/09006.htm} and
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/10006.htm}.} The law had said all of
this in advance, in the dry idiom of transport regulations. The sons of
Caath were to carry the holy things already wrapped, ``and they shall
not touch the vessels of the sanctuary, lest they die,'' nor look on
them ``by any curiosity'' before the wrapping (Numbers 4:15, 20). What
reads at first like ritual bookkeeping is reverence codified: the
statute assumes that the danger is not malice but familiarity---the
slow decay by which the holiest thing in the camp becomes, to its
porters, luggage.\endnote{Numbers 4:4--20, Douay-Rheims, read at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/04004.htm}; vv.~15 and 20 quoted. The
Douay's ``by any curiosity'' renders the Vulgate's interpretive gloss;
Challoner's note at 1 Kings 6:19 wires that verse to this one, as the
catastrophe section records.} The two lessons are symmetrical.
Presumption treats God as a weapon; irreverence treats him
as furniture. The ark's whole discipline---poles never drawn out,
veils, wrappings, shoulders, distance---exists to prevent both, and to
train a people in the truth both errors deny: the Presence is a person,
and the person is holy.

\subsection{The propitiatory: mercy at the center}

At the dead center of all this holiness, the law placed mercy. The one
day a man entered the inner room, he came with blood, and the place he
brought it was the ark's golden cover---the \term{kapporet}, the
Douay's ``propitiatory,'' the ``mercy seat'' of the familiar modern
rendering. Leviticus 16 built the room's whole meaning around that
surface. Aaron may not come at all times within the veil, ``for I will
appear in a cloud over the oracle,'' and on the day he comes, the blood
of the victims is sprinkled toward and before the propitiatory
(Leviticus 16:2, 14--15).\endnote{Leviticus 16:2, 12--15,
Douay-Rheims, read at \url{https://drbo.org/chapter/03016.htm}. The
Douay's ``oracle'' here names the propitiatory zone, one of the two
uses of that word disambiguated in the terminology section above.}
Atonement, in Israel's central rite, is not transacted at the altar in
the court. It is finished at the throne, on the gold between the
cherubim, where God had promised to speak.

The word itself then makes a journey no student of the New Testament
should be allowed to miss. The Greek translators rendered
\term{kapporet} with \term{hilasterion}; at its first occurrence the
phrase is doubled, \term{hilasterion epithema}, ``a propitiatory, a
cover'' (Exodus 25:17, Septuagint). The Epistle to the Hebrews
uses the same word in its inventory of the old sanctuary: ``And over it
were the cherubims of glory overshadowing the propitiatory: of which it
is not needful to speak now particularly'' (Hebrews 9:5). And Paul,
writing to the Romans, sets Christ forth under this very word: God has
put him forward as \term{hilasterion}, in his blood (Romans 3:25,
cited here from the Greek). Whether Paul means the ark's cover
precisely or propitiation generally, the lexicons have argued for over
a century, and the dispute belongs to them. What is not disputed is the
word. The vocabulary of the ark's lid is applied, in the New
Testament, to the crucified Lord.\endnote{The chain: \term{kapporet}
(root \term{k-p-r}, to cover, to atone); Septuagint Exodus 25:17
(Elpenor numbering 25:16) \term{hilasterion epithema}, verified in the
research record; Vulgate \term{propitiatorium}; Hebrews 9:5,
Douay-Rheims, read at \url{https://drbo.org/chapter/65009.htm}, with
the Greek \term{katas\-kiazonta to hilasterion} verified; Romans 3:25
cited from the Greek, the Douay wording of that verse not being among
this study's re-anchored loci. The mercy-seat rendering of Romans 3:25
against the general sense ``propitiation'' has been contested since
Deissmann (1903), in part because Paul's noun lacks the article; the
question is reported, not adjudicated, here.}

The Fathers drew the consequence at the empty tomb. St.~John alone
records that Magdalen, stooping into the sepulchre, saw two angels in
white sitting where the body of Jesus had lain, one at the head and one
at the feet (John 20:12). St.~Gregory the Great, preaching on the
scene about the year 591, turned it toward the inner room of the
tabernacle. In his reading the two angels are the two Testaments---
``Possumus etiam per duos angelos duo Testamenta cognoscere''---and as
the two cherubim that cover the propitiatory gaze at one another with
their faces turned toward it, so the two Testaments face each other
across one center. Then comes the question that carries the whole
figure: ``Quid vero per propitiatorium nisi incarnatus Dominus
figuratur?''---what is figured by the propitiatory, if not the
incarnate Lord? (an editorial paraphrase, not a
translation).\endnote{Gregory the
Great, \work{Homiliae in Evangelia} 25.3, on John 20:11--18 (PL
76:1189--1196); Latin read at la.wikisource.org (\work{Homiliarum in
Evangelia}, Homilia XXV): ``Possumus etiam per duos angelos duo
Testamenta cognoscere\ldots{} Unde et duo cherubim quae propitiatorium
tegunt sese invicem aspiciunt versis vultibus in propitiatorium\ldots{}
Quid vero per propitiatorium nisi incarnatus Dominus figuratur?'' The
English gloss above is an editorial paraphrase, not a translation.}
Gregory's figure is an allegory of the Scriptures: two Testaments, one
mercy seat, the Lord between them. The more sensory form of the same
reading---the slab of the empty tomb itself as the \term{kapporet},
the two angels as its cherubim flanking the place of the vanished
Presence---is early modern and modern. It runs from Lightfoot's Hebraic
commentary into current scholarship and preaching, a legitimate
development of Gregory's insight; but it should wear its
date.\endnote{The direct tomb-as-propitiatory form is reported from
John Lightfoot, \work{Horae Hebraicae et Talmudicae} on John 20:12, as
transmitted by Adam Clarke's commentary at the verse (``So were the
cherubim placed at each end of the mercy-seat''); reported at one
remove, the seventeenth-century text not opened for this study.}
Either way the third lesson holds. The ark's center was a covering for
sin and a meeting place with God; the gospel's center is a person who
is both.

\subsection{The gates: the King of glory}

The glory has a title in Israel's songbook: the King of glory. Israel
sang the ark's entrances under that title, and the Church has never
stopped singing them---of Christ. Psalm 23 (in the Hebrew numbering 24)
stages an entrance as a dialogue at the doors. The gates are commanded
to lift up their heads, that the King of glory may come in. A voice
within asks who this King of glory is. The answer comes: the Lord
strong and mighty, the Lord mighty in battle, the Lord of hosts. The
ark is not named in the psalm. Hearing in it the liturgy of the ark's
entry into the sanctuary---the Lord entering his house as King, as at 2
Kings 6 and 3 Kings 8---is an interpretation, proposed in antiquity and
argued in form-critical dress by the school of Gunkel and Mowinckel,
whose reconstruction of a festival procession remains a hypothesis and
is reported here as one.\endnote{Psalm 23:7--10, Vulgate numbering
(Hebrew 24), given in
paraphrase; the Douay wording of this psalm was not among the loci
re-anchored for this study, and no English edition is quoted. The
entrance-liturgy setting: Gunkel, \work{Einleitung in die Psalmen};
Mowinckel, \work{Psalmenstudien} II (1922); reported as the
form-critical school's hypothesis, the monographs not opened for this
study.}

What is not hypothesis is what Christians did with the psalm, almost
from the beginning. Justin Martyr, before the year 165, put the
dialogue at the ascension. The rulers in heaven ``are commanded to open
the gates of heaven, that He who is King of glory may enter in,'' and,
failing to recognize the ascending Lord in his unglorious flesh, they
ask, ``Who is this King of glory?''---and the Holy Spirit answers,
``The Lord of hosts.'' Elsewhere he insists the psalm was spoken
neither of Solomon nor of Ezechias but ``to this our Christ
alone.''\endnote{Justin Martyr, \work{Dialogue with Trypho} 36 and 85;
English of the Ante-Nicene Fathers edition (Roberts--Donaldson
American edition, vol.~I), read at newadvent.org.} Irenaeus, in the
\work{Demonstration of the Apostolic Preaching}, has the angels who
accompany the ascending Christ call the same summons up to the powers
above, who ask their astonished question and receive the psalm's own
answer.\endnote{Irenaeus, \work{Demonstration of the Apostolic
Preaching} 84, read at tertullian.org; given in paraphrase.} And the
old descensus tradition turned the same dialogue downward. In the
\work{Gospel of Nicodemus}, a compilation of the fourth or fifth
century on older material, the voice like thunder before the doors of
Hades cries, ``Lift up your gates, O ye rulers\ldots{} and the King of
glory shall come in,'' Hades demands to know who this King of glory
is, and is told: ``The Lord strong and mighty, the Lord mighty in
battle.''\endnote{\work{Gospel of Nicodemus}, Descent into Hell, ch.~5
(21); Ante-Nicene Fathers text (vol.~VIII), read at
earlychristianwritings.com. The compilation's date is reported.} The
gates of heaven, the gates of hell: antiquity aimed the psalm in both
directions, and both readings are pure Christology. Neither names the
ark. Both assume exactly what the ark's processions had taught: when
this King approaches a threshold, it is the threshold that must move.

And the entrance liturgy is not a museum piece. In the Byzantine rite,
to this day, the bishop consecrating a church carries the relics in
procession around the building, halts before its closed doors, and
chants the psalm's summons; the question comes back from within the
empty building; the doors are opened, and the King of glory enters his
new house. The older Roman pontifical staged the same dialogue at the
church door, with the bishop's staff, at the \emph{Tollite portas}. And
once a year the Roman lectionary appoints these very verses as the
responsorial psalm of the feast of the Presentation of the Lord, their
refrain asking the psalm's question and answering it of the Lord---sung
while the Gospel shows the Lord entering his temple carried in his
mother's arms.\endnote{Byzantine consecration rite: reported from the
Greek Orthodox Archdiocese's published description of the service
(goarch.org). Roman pontifical \emph{Tollite portas} dialogue:
standard liturgical history, reported. Roman lectionary, feast of the
Presentation (2 February): responsorial Psalm 23(24):7--10, selection
verified at the USCCB daily-readings page for 2 February 2026; per the
rights policy of this study the lectionary's copyrighted refrain is
described, not quoted. The Presentation tableau is described here as
the liturgy's own scene, not claimed as a typology.} That scene is
described here and left where the liturgy leaves it; its questions will
be asked in order, when Luke is opened.

The pattern, then, can be named in one sentence: the ark is what the
presence of God among men looks like before Christ---glory that elects
a dwelling and cannot be detained; holiness that blesses the reverent
house and breaks the presuming hand; mercy seated at the very center of
danger; a King whose entrances make the gates themselves stand up. The
whole of Israel's experience of God-with-us was compressed into a box
two cubits and a half long. Then the box was taken away, and the
prophets said the Presence would come again in a larger mode. One
question remains: what does the same pattern look like after
Christ---and did anyone, in the event, look like it? Honesty requires
one short pause before answering: a word about what kind of reasoning
can answer such a question at all.
