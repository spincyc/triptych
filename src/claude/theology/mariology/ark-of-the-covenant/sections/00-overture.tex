\section{Overture: The Question of the Ark}

The ark of the covenant is the most famous lost object in the world,
and the modern instinct, on hearing its name, is to ask where it is.
The instinct is not foolish. The ark was a real chest of real wood,
carried by real poles on real shoulders through a landscape whose towns
can still be walked. So concrete a thing invites a concrete question,
and the guesses have been concrete accordingly: a cave on Mount Nebo, a
chamber under the temple pavement, a chapel in Aksum. This study
reports those answers, with their dates and their witnesses, and hides
none of them.

But the Bible, which watches the ark more closely than any other
source, declines to end the story that way. The historical books let
the ark vanish without an obituary. At some point no writer marks, it
is simply no longer there, and the traditions that later arose to fill
the silence disagree with one another. The New Testament names the ark
of the covenant only twice: once in the Epistle to the Hebrews, to
describe its furniture and decline to expound it; and once in the
canon's final book, to show it---not found, but seen, in the opened
temple in heaven, one verse before the great sign of the woman clothed
with the sun.\endnote{Hebrews 9:4--5; Apocalypse 11:19
and 12:1; Douay-Rheims (Challoner) numbering and naming, read at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/65009.htm},
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/73011.htm}, and
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/73012.htm}. All web loci cited in these
notes were read on 2026-08-15 unless another date is stated; full
bibliographic detail and witness identifications are in the
References.}

The canon's answer to ``where did the ark go?'' is an answer of a
different kind, because the question was of a different kind than it
looked. When a king moves his court, the antiquarian hunts for the old
throne; the courtier follows the king. The ark was never important as
furniture. It was important as the appointed meeting place of God and
Israel, and to trace it honestly is to trace not a relic but a
Presence.

\arkthesis{The ark's story is complete only when it is read forward.
The chest built at Sinai carries the Presence of God through Israel's
history, vanishes without an obituary, and is seen again in heaven at
Apocalypse 11:19, immediately before the sign of the woman clothed with
the sun. The Church---in her philology, her Fathers, her liturgy, and
her magisterium---reads the Virgin Mary as the ark of the new covenant.
This study tells the whole journey, presents that reading with its
evidence assessed on two distinct axes---attestation history and
ecclesial reception---preserves its disputes, and keeps its devotional
application under the primacy of Christ.}

The work stands in three panels and a coda, and each panel does one
job. The first panel tells the journey entire, from every canonical
ark text: the design and construction at Sinai, with the transport law
and the meeting promise; the crossing of the Jordan and the seven days
at Jericho; the long residence at Silo and the catastrophe that ended
it, when the ark was carried into battle like a weapon and taken like a
trophy; Dagon on his face in Azotus; the deaths at Bethsames and the
parked decades at Cariathiarim; David's two processions, the first
ending in a death and a question, the second in dancing; the temple
dedication, when the ark comes to rest and the glory fills the house.
Then the long silence of the books of Kings; the last sighting under
Josias; the word in Jeremias that the ark would neither be remembered
nor visited nor spoken of again; the plunder lists of 587 that name the
snuffers and not the ark; and finally the ancient traditions of its
fate, each told with its own actor and horizon. Where the text of
Scripture is itself in dispute---a number at Bethsames, a word at the
death of Oza---the dispute is printed, not smoothed. The reader who
wants to know what happened to the ark will find here everything the
sources actually say, at the confidence the evidence actually supports.

The second panel stops the narrative and asks what the journey taught.
It follows the theology the texts themselves build: the glory that the
ark's loss was thought to end and did not, the holiness that refused to
be used as a talisman by the very nation that carried it, the golden
propitiatory where blood met mercy once a year, the gates commanded to
open for the King of glory. It then pays a necessary toll: a short,
candid account of what typology is and is not. That account states
what the Church means, with the Second Vatican Council and the
Catechism, by reading the Old Covenant's works as prefigurations of the
New---and what such reading never claims about the human author or the
literal sense. The reader is asked to cross no bridge before it is
built.

The third panel presents the Church's reading of Mary as the ark of
the new covenant, and presents it as evidence, not as assertion. It
begins with the philology of the Visitation: Luke's first chapter set
beside the ark's procession in 2 Kings 6 (2 Samuel 6 in modern
naming). That case is heard at its verified strength and its verified
limits, with the scholars who built it and the scholars who reject it
both quoted at their strongest. The panel then hears the Fathers, each
text carrying its own date and its own authenticity verdict. It hears
the liturgy and the magisterium, dated act by act, from the Akathist
hymn to the Litany of Loreto to the Assumption definition of 1950 and
the present lectionary. And it ends where the canon ends: with Psalm
131, the Assumption, and the opened temple of Apocalypse 11:19 flowing
into the sign of the woman. One table near the close---the study's
only summary---grades every parallel on the two axes at once. The coda
leaves argument behind and asks what the whole story requires of those
who carry Christ within them, as the baptized do.

One promise of method governs all of it, and it is made here once:
facts exact, framework Catholic, labels honest. Questions of
fact---who wrote a homily, what a verse says, where a word occurs---are
settled by evidence, and by nothing else. A pseudonymous sermon is
called pseudonymous even when its sentence is beautiful; a wrong
reference is corrected even when it is venerable. Questions of
interpretation are not settled by the minimalism of any one school.
Critics are reported as a school, with their premises stated, and the
liturgical and magisterial reception of a reading is treated as real
evidence about its truth, since the Church reading her own Scripture in
her own worship is not a bystander.

Throughout, two things are measured separately and said separately:
how anciently a reading is attested, and how the Church has received
it. A reading may be old and never received, or received and
explicitly articulated late---and late articulation is what development
looks like, not what error looks like. Where this study proposes a
synthesis of its own, the proposal is labeled and framed so the reader
can see the seam. Resemblance will never be dressed up as exegesis.
The reader is owed the evidence at its true weight, and will get it.

That is the whole plan. The journey is long, and it starts where every
covenant thing starts, on the mountain where God told a freed people,
in cubits and gold, exactly how He wished to be met. Begin at Sinai.
