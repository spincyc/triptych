\section{Reception: Liturgy, Devotion, Magisterium}

The Fathers coined the title; it remained for the Church to pray it. This
section dates that praying, act by act, and so gives the second axis --- the
reception weighed at the toll-gate --- its evidence. The kinds of act are
kept apart as carefully as the dates. A hymn sung in Lent, a litany approved
by a bull, a devotional classic, a Matins lesson, a dogmatic constitution,
and a lectionary rubric are different sorts of ecclesial speech, and each is
named below for what it is. The whole chain stands in one table at the end.

\subsection{The Byzantine liturgy}

The oldest continuous liturgical home of the title is Byzantine. The
Akathist hymn, whose ark-line the previous section weighed among the fifth-
and sixth-century witnesses, is not a text the liturgy quotes but a text it
sings entire. The Triodion prescribes it for the Fridays of Great Lent, and
on the fifth of them celebrates it in its own right. Its psi-stanza --- the
twenty-third of its twenty-four alphabetic stanzas --- hymns Mary as the
living temple, \term{empsychon naon}, and greets her: \term{chaire, Kib\=ote
chrys\=otheisa t\=o Pneumati} --- ``Hail, Ark gilded by the Spirit'' (the
English rendering is editorial). The gilding of Exodus 25 is transposed onto
the overshadowing of Luke 1:35. It is the tightest one-line form the
typology has ever taken, and the Byzantine rite prays it every
Lent.\endnote{Akathist,
psi-stanza, read in the received ecclesiastical Greek text (transcription at
\url{lyricstranslate.com}); the stanza hails Mary as \term{empsychon naon},
greater than the Holy of Holies, and \term{Kib\=otos} gilded by the Spirit.
Renderings editorial. The Triodion rubric (four Friday sections in Lent, the
whole on the fifth Friday and Saturday, the ``Saturday of the Akathist'') is
the standard modern rubric as published in Byzantine liturgical resources; it
was not checked against a printed Triodion.}

At the Dormition (15 August) the ark enters not through the readings but
through psalmody and hymnography. The three Old Testament lessons of Great
Vespers are Genesis 28, Ezechiel 43--44, and Proverbs 9; 2 Kings 6 (= 2
Samuel 6) is read at no point in the feast. But the verses appointed with
the aposticha at Great Vespers, and the Alleluia verses of the Divine
Liturgy, come from Psalm 131 (the Hebrew counts it 132): verse 8, in which
the Lord is called to arise into his resting place, he and the ark he has
sanctified, with verse 11, the oath sworn to David.

The first canon of the feast, which the service books ascribe to Cosmas of
Maiuma, calls Mary the animate ark. It also narrates, from the ancient
Dormition traditions, the episode of Jephonias, the man who laid insolent
hands on her funeral bier and lost them --- the liturgy composing its own
Oza antitype, the fear of 2 Kings 6:6--7 re-taught at Mary's burial. In
its sixth ode the same canon hands her the whole furniture of the sanctuary:
candlestick of the immaterial Light, golden censer, the jar and the staff,
the divinely inscribed tablet, the holy ark, the table of the Word of life.
(These texts are described rather than quoted; the accessible English
service books are derivative and their translations unidentified.) One
troparion of this canon --- God who kept her virgin in childbirth kept her
body incorrupt in the tomb --- is the very Byzantine text
\work{Munificentissimus Deus} would quote in 1950 under the citation
\work{Menaei Totius Anni}. The identification of the constitution's citation
with this ode is the study's own, and it is textually exact in
substance.\endnote{Dormition texts read in two derivative English service
books: the Reader Menaion posted at \url{st-sergius.org} (file Emenaion
08-15.pdf) and the Great Vespers text of the Antiochian Archdiocese of North
America (``Aug 14 VESP''), both searched in full. Aposticha verses and
Liturgy Alleluia = Ps 131:8 and 131:11 LXX; Canon I, Ode 3, troparion 2
(Jephonias, the ``animate ark''); Ode 6, troparion 2 (the furniture
catalogue); Ode 6, troparion 1 (virgin in birthgiving, body untouched by
corruption), corresponding in substance to the Byzantine text quoted at
\work{Munificentissimus Deus} 18 with endnote 12 (\work{Menaei Totius
Anni}). The ascription of the canon to Cosmas of Maiuma is the service
books' own and was not checked critically. The translations are unidentified
and presumptively copyrighted; hence description, not quotation. A negative
worth recording: 2 Kings 6 itself appears in no reading of the feast.}

At the Entrance of the Theotokos into the Temple (21 November) the Byzantine
rite makes its most explicit lectionary use of the ark. Great Vespers, as
the Greek Archdiocese's published outline of the feast reports, reads
Exodus 40 (the tabernacle erected, the ark installed, the glory filling it),
3 Kings 7:51--8:11 (the ark carried into the holy of holies, the glory
filling the house), and Ezechiel 43:27--44:4 (the shut east gate). The child
entering the temple is hymned as the true ark entering the true holy of
holies. One disclosure is owed here, the same the Kebra Nagast received in
the narrative panel: the feast's story of Mary dwelling in the temple rests
on the Protoevangelium of James, an apocryphon transmitted under an
apostolic name, not on canonical history. The lectionary's typological act
is real reception; the narrative it adorns is not thereby
historicized.\endnote{Entrance readings as reported at the Greek Orthodox
Archdiocese of America feast page, \url{goarch.org/entrance-theotokos}; the
service book itself was not consulted, and the readings are cited on that
report's authority. The Protoevangelium substrate of the feast is the
standard account of its origin. Note that the two rites reach the same
typology by different routes: the Byzantine rite through Ps 131 and
hymnography, the postconciliar Roman rite through the Chronicler's ark
narrative (below).}

\subsection{Loreto, Liguori, and the devotional chain}

In the West the title's chief carrier was not a liturgical book but an
approved devotion. The Litany of Loreto sets \term{Foederis arca} among its
symbolic invocations, between the House of Gold and the Gate of Heaven. Its
history, so far as it can be established, is short. The earliest known
genuine Marian litany, in a twelfth-century Mainz codex, shows no ark
invocation in the specimens its historian Angelo De Santi printed. The
earliest surviving print of the Loreto form is a Dillingen booklet of about
1558, circulated by Peter Canisius. De Santi's collations imply that
\term{Foederis arca} stood already in the sixteenth-century core, and no
source examined records it as a dated later addition, as \term{Auxilium
christianorum} and the twentieth-century invocations are. Sixtus V approved
the litany by the bull \work{Reddituri} (11 July 1587); a decree under
Clement VIII (1601) suppressed all other Marian litanies for public use and
so fixed the text. Who coined the invocation, and when, cannot be said. Its
content simply compresses the Greek hymnographic commonplace into two Latin
words.\endnote{Litany history per
Angelo De Santi's article ``Litany of Loreto,'' \work{Catholic Encyclopedia}
(1913), vol. 9, read at Wikisource; De Santi was the litany's principal
historian, and his monograph \work{Le litanie lauretane} (1897; French ed.
1900) remains the lead for the 1558 text. The Mainz and Venice codices were
not inspected; the core status of \term{Foederis arca} is reported on De
Santi's evidence, not verified letter-perfect in the 1558 print --- direct
inspection of that print has not been made for this study. The 1587
and 1601 acts are documented in the same literature.}

The litany did not pray alone. In the 1570s, preaching at Louvain, Robert
Bellarmine argued the Assumption from the ark: ``And who, I ask, could
believe that the ark of holiness, the dwelling place of the Word of God, the
temple of the Holy Spirit, could be reduced to ruin?'' --- words
\work{Munificentissimus Deus} would quote four centuries later (MD
34).\endnote{Bellarmine as quoted in \work{Munificentissimus Deus} 34,
endnote 37: \work{Conciones habitae Lovanii}, n. 40, \work{De Assumptione
B. Mariae Virginis}; English text at
\url{https://www.vatican.va/content/pius-xii/en/apost_constitutions/documents/hf_p-xii_apc_19501101_munificentissimus-deus.html}.
The sermon itself was not consulted; Bellarmine's Louvain preaching dates
1569--1576, making this a witness roughly contemporary with the litany's
diffusion.} Yet within decades the greatest Catholic commentator of the age
shows the other side of the ledger. Cornelius a Lapide's commentary on Luke
1:39--56, searched in both the English translation and the Latin, contains
no ark, no Obededom, no reference to 2 Kings 6; the three months of Luke
1:56 are explained from Ambrose and Origen as ministry to
Elizabeth.\endnote{Cornelius a Lapide, \work{The Great Commentary}, tr.
Mossman, Luke ch. 1, searched at \url{catholicapologetics.info} and in the
archive.org scan \url{https://archive.org/details/TheGreatCommentaryOfCorneliusALapideV4};
Latin \work{Commentaria in Sacram Scripturam}, t. 8, archive.org item
\url{https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_b2Nz6EPyf08C}, the block on Lk
1:39--56 scanned. Both negative for ark, Obededom, and 2 Kings 6. Caveats:
the Mossman series abridges, and the Latin scan's OCR is poor; a page-image
check of an Antwerp edition remains open.} The devotional title flourished
in a Church whose exegesis of the Visitation knew nothing of the parallel.
The two chains are distinct, and neither may be backdated into the other.

Alphonsus Liguori's \work{Glories of Mary} (1750) deployed the title three
ways. In the Visitation discourse, Obededom's blessed house supplies the
measure: ``But with how much greater blessings are those persons enriched who
receive some loving visit from this living ark of God, as was the divine
mother!'' In the second Assumption discourse the angels plead in the words of
Psalm 131:8 that the Lord ``bring with thee also the living ark of thy
sanctification, namely, thy mother, who was the ark sanctified by thee when
thou didst inhabit her womb''; and as David brought up the ark with shouting
and trumpet, so ``with far more splendid and glorious pomp he ordained that
his mother should enter into heaven.'' And in a novena of meditations on the
litany itself he glosses the invocation \term{Foederis arca}: Mary ``an ark
more spacious than that of Noe'' --- \term{Arca Noe largior} --- an
attribution to Hesychius made at Liguori's word. This is pious analogy, not
a source-claim about Luke. But the fusion of Psalm 131:8 with the procession
of 2 Kings 6 --- the exact pairing that the philology of the Visitation
section and the definition of 1950 would each make in its own register ---
stands assembled here, devotionally, two centuries before
either.\endnote{\work{The Glories of Mary}, first complete
English translation (New York, 1862; Kenedy printing, 1888), archive.org item
\url{https://archive.org/details/thegloriesofmary00liguuoft}, searched in
full: Discourse V (Visitation); Discourse VIII (second discourse on the
Assumption), First Point, with the footnoted Latin \term{Surge, Domine, in
requiem tuam, tu et arca sanctificationis tuae} (Ps 131:8); Novena of
Meditations upon the Litany, Seventh Day. \work{Munificentissimus Deus} 35
itself cites the work (its endnote 39: \work{The Glories of Mary}, Part 2,
d. 1). The Hesychius attribution rests on Liguori's citation and was not
independently verified.}

The nineteenth century carries the chain into magisterium and dogmatics.
\work{Ineffabilis Deus} (1854) has the Fathers beholding Mary's privilege
``in that ark of Noah, which was built by divine command and escaped entirely
safe and sound from the common shipwreck of the whole world,'' and
celebrating her as ``the ark and house of holiness which Eternal Wisdom
built'' --- ark language, echoing the Latin psalm's \term{arca
sanctificationis}, in the bull of the Immaculate Conception a century before
the Assumption definition.\endnote{\work{Ineffabilis Deus},
English text
searched at \url{papalencyclicals.net}; both phrases verbatim there. The
Latin of ``the ark and house of holiness'' was not checked against the
\work{Acta} of Pius IX and is cited from the English witness.}

Matthias Scheeben's dogmatics (1882) treats the ark as ``as much a prototype
of Mary as of the humanity of Christ,'' and reads Luke 1:35 as the power of
the Most High descending ``into Mary as into the true ark of the covenant,''
dwelling in her more perfectly than it had dwelt in the cloud over the old
ark. He also polices the boundary himself: the propitiatory he judges
``rather a prototype of Christ.'' The Christological priority on which the
patristic panel insisted is thus native to the modern dogmatic tradition
too.\endnote{Scheeben, \work{Mariology}, vol. I, tr. Geukers (St. Louis:
Herder, 1946), ET pp. 36--40 and 81, searched in the archive.org scan
\url{https://archive.org/details/matthias-joseph-scheeben-t.-geukers-mariology};
German original \work{Handbuch der katholischen Dogmatik} III (1882),
pagination not established. Quotations from the Geukers translation, kept to
brief attributed scale.}

One eloquent silence belongs in the record. Newman's meditations on the
litany for the month of May gloss thirty-one titles, and \term{Foederis
arca} is not among them. The word ark never occurs in them, nor in his two
Assumption discourses, nor in the Marian sections of the \work{Letter to
Pusey} that were searched. His Marian architecture is the Second Eve. The
chain does not need him; the record should show that its most eloquent
nineteenth-century English witness chose other
figures.\endnote{Newman, \work{Meditations and Devotions}, Part I
(Meditations on the Litany of Loretto, for the Month of May);
\work{Discourses to Mixed Congregations} 17--18; \work{Letter to Pusey},
sections 3--4 with the appended Notes --- all downloaded from
\url{newmanreader.org} and searched; zero occurrences of ``ark.'' Verified
negative at the level of these files.}

\subsection{The Roman office and the acts of 1950}

The Roman rite's own history is easily misstated, because its Mass and its
office diverge. The Assumption Mass before 1950 contains no ark and no Psalm
131:8 anywhere in its propers. Its Gospel was Martha and Mary (Luke
10:38--42) --- as it happens, the same Gospel the Byzantine Liturgy of the
Dormition reads. But the office is another matter. At Matins the Church sang
Fortunatus's hymn \work{Quem terra, pontus, sidera}, with its sixth-century
stanza on the Maker whom the world cannot contain: \term{ventris sub arca
clausus est}, closed beneath the ark of her womb. And from the Pian breviary
of 1568 down to 1950 --- verified here in a 1942 printing --- the second
nocturn of 15 August read John Damascene's second homily on the Dormition,
beginning:

\begin{witnessbox}{Breviarium Romanum, 1942 printing, 15 August, Matins, lectio iv}
\term{Hodie sacra et animata arca Dei viventis, quae suum in utero concepit
Creatorem, requiescit in templo Domini, quod nullis est exstructum manibus.
Et David exsultat ejus parens\ldots} --- ``Today the sacred and living ark of
the living God, she who conceived her Creator in her womb, takes her rest in
the temple of the Lord not built with hands; and David her forefather
exsults'' (an editorial paraphrase, not a translation).
\end{witnessbox}

The Greek summit-text of the patristic panel was thus the Roman Church's own
Matins lesson every 15 August for nearly four centuries. In 1950 the revised
office kept the Damascene lessons and promoted the ark into the hymnody. The
new Matins hymn carries the stanza \term{Arca non putri fabricata ligno /
Manna tu servas, fluit unde virtus, / Ipsa qua surgent animata rursus / Ossa
sepulcris} --- an ark built of no rotting wood, you keep the manna from which
flows the power by which ensouled bones will rise again from their tombs (an
editorial paraphrase, not a translation). The new Mass formulary (\work{Signum
magnum}, introduced 1951) set Apocalypse 12:1 at the entrance and the
Visitation-Magnificat Gospel at the center --- and still contained no
explicit ark text, for Apocalypse 11:19 was not read. The ark reached the
Roman office centuries before it reached the Roman Mass.\endnote{Breviarium
Romanum, Pars Aestiva, 1942 printing, archive.org item
\url{https://archive.org/details/breviarium-romanum-1942}, feast of 15
August at scan pp. 887--895: Fortunatus hymn stanza (in the Urban VIII
revision, from the Common of the BVM); Damascene lessons iv--vi, lectio iv
quoted verbatim above; no Ps 131:8 anywhere in the pre-1950 feast and
octave block scanned. The 1950 office is verified from the bound-in
supplement of the same volume (new hymn stanza quoted verbatim; Damascene
lessons retained; Gospel Lk 1:41--50). The 1568 starting point of the
Damascene lesson is reported per G. DiPippo's compendium of breviary
reforms,
\url{https://www.newliturgicalmovement.org/2009/09/compendium-of-reforms-of-roman-breviary_17.html};
whether the \term{arca} sentence stood in the 1568 editio princeps remains
to be checked there. The pre-1950 and 1951 Mass formularies are reported
from standard liturgical surveys, the missals not opened. Caution for
readers who verify: the popular site divinumofficium.com serves the
post-1950 lessons even under its earlier rubric settings and is not a
usable witness for the pre-1950 office of this feast.}

\work{Munificentissimus Deus} (1 November 1950) is the chain's magisterial
summit, and precision about what it does is owed. In its paragraph 26,
theologians and preachers ``following in the footsteps of the holy Fathers''
are described as applying to the Assumption the psalm and the type together:

\begin{witnessbox}{Munificentissimus Deus 26 --- AAS 42 (1950) 762--763, with the vatican.va English}
\term{sunt qui Psaltae sententiam inducant: ``Surge, Domine, in requiem tuam,
tu et Arca sanctificationis tuae''; atque Arcam foederis, incorruptibili
ligno instructam atque in Dei templo positam, quasi imaginem cernant
purissimi Mariae Virginis corporis\ldots} --- some ``have looked upon the Ark
of the Covenant, built of incorruptible wood and placed in the Lord's temple,
as a type of the most pure body of the Virgin Mary, preserved and exempt from
all the corruption of the tomb and raised up to such glory in heaven.''
\end{witnessbox}

The constitution's own note at the words ``the holy Fathers'' identifies its
exemplars. In the Latin of the \work{Acta Apostolicae Sedis} the note reads
\term{hom.\ II, 2, 11} --- John Damascene's second Dormition homily at
numbers 2 and 11, number 2 being the \term{sacra et animata arca} passage
itself --- together with the Encomium ``attributed to St.\ Modestus,'' the
attribution flagged by the constitution's own wording. It is a \term{Cf.}
citation: the constitution cites the Damascene ark texts in its apparatus
and does not quote them in its body.

Beyond paragraph 26, paragraph 29 reports Anthony of Padua arguing from
Isaias 60:13 that the ark of his sanctification ``has risen up, since on
this day the Virgin Mother has been taken up,'' and paragraph 34 gives the
Bellarmine sentence quoted above; the word ark occurs in exactly three
paragraphs of the constitution --- 26, 29, and 34. The dogmatic definition
itself, paragraph 44, uses no ark language at all. The type stands in the
argument, not in the defined formula --- reception at the highest level,
exercised with the tradition's own
restraint.\endnote{\work{Munificentissimus Deus}: English at
\url{https://www.vatican.va/content/pius-xii/en/apost_constitutions/documents/hf_p-xii_apc_19501101_munificentissimus-deus.html};
Latin at AAS 42 (1950) 753--773, the ark passages and their footnotes at
pp. 762--764, read in the scan
\url{https://www.vatican.va/archive/aas/documents/AAS-42-1950-ocr.pdf}.
Latin footnote 20: \term{Cf. S. Ioan. Damasc., Encomium in Dormitionem\ldots
hom. II, 2, 11; Encomium in Dormitionem (S. Modesto Hierosol. attributum)};
the vatican.va English edition prints only ``n. 11,'' dropping the ``2.''
Footnote 27 in the AAS reads \term{Is., LX, 13}; the ``Is 61:13'' of the
vatican.va English page is a misprint confined to that edition (Isaias 61
has eleven verses). The location of the ark language (paragraphs 26, 29,
34, and there only) is by full-text search of the English edition. Damascene Hom. II, 2 = PG 96:723
as reported by the standard apparatus; the column was not checked against
PG for this section.}

Fourteen years later the most authoritative conciliar text on Mary passed
the title over entirely. The word \term{arca} does not occur in \work{Lumen
Gentium}, in Latin or in English. Chapter VIII prefers ``temple of the Holy
Spirit'' (LG 53), and its list of invoked titles is Advocate, Auxiliatrix,
Adjutrix, Mediatrix (LG 62). The finding is printed because popular
literature often implies conciliar endorsement of the title; there is none to
cite. By the two-axis rule the silence subtracts nothing --- but it is a
silence, and the record keeps it.\endnote{\work{Lumen Gentium}, English and
Latin texts downloaded from vatican.va and searched in full: zero hits for
ark/\term{arca} in any inflection. Verified negative.}

\subsection{From Marialis Cultus to the Catechism}

\work{Marialis Cultus} (1974) restored the title to papal speech, twice. At
MC 6, on the restored solemnity of the Annunciation, the liturgies celebrate
the Virgin who, by receiving the one Mediator into her womb, ``became the
true Ark of the Covenant and true Temple of God'' --- in the Latin,
\term{vera Foederis Arca}, the litany's exact phrase, capitalized. At MC 26,
reflecting on Luke 1:35, the Fathers are said to have seen in the Spirit's
intervention what transformed her into ``the `Ark of the Covenant' or `the
Ark of Holiness''' (\term{Foederis vel Sanctificationis Arcam}). The note
appended there is a papally curated patristic dossier in miniature ---
Severus of Antioch, Hesychius, Chrysippus, Andrew of Crete, John Damascene,
with the Akathist's psi-stanza cited in the companion note --- the
magisterium doing in an apparatus what the patristic panel of this study did
at length.\endnote{\work{Marialis Cultus}, English and Latin texts at
vatican.va, both searched. MC 6 Latin: \term{in corpore suo suscipiens unum
Mediatorem (cf 1 Tim 2,5), vera Foederis Arca verumque templum Dei}. The MC
26 note's ark loci: Severus of Antioch, Hom. 57: PO 8, 357--358; Hesychius
of Jerusalem, Homilia de sancta Maria Deipara: PG 93, 1464; Chrysippus of
Jerusalem, Oratio in sanctam Mariam Deiparam 2: PO 19, 338; Andrew of
Crete, Oratio V: PG 97, 896; John Damascene, Oratio VI, 6: PG 96, 672; the
preceding note (Temple/Tabernacle titles) cites Hymnus Akathistos, str. 23,
ed. Pitra, \work{Analecta Sacra} I, 261. Negatives for completeness: MC
never names the Litany of Loreto, never cites 2 Kings 6, and describes the
Visitation feast (MC 7) without ark language.}

The postconciliar lectionary (1969) then did what no Roman Mass formulary
had done. Its selections are described here by reference, as the rights of
the liturgical translations require; the reception datum is in any case the
Church's choice and pairing of texts, not their modern English wording. At
the Vigil of the Assumption (Lectionary 621) the first reading is 1
Paralipomenon 15:3--4, 15--16; 16:1--2 --- David bringing the ark up to the
tent he had pitched for it. The responsorial psalm is 131 (the lectionary
counts it 132), with a refrain drawn from verse 8, the
resting-place-and-ark verse; the Gospel is Luke 11:27--28. At the Mass of
the day (Lectionary 622) the entrance antiphon keeps \work{Signum magnum}
(Apocalypse 12:1). The first reading begins at Apocalypse 11:19a --- the
verse before the medieval chapter division, the ark seen in the opened
temple --- and runs into 12:1--6a and 10ab; the Gospel is Luke 1:39--56, the
Visitation. For the first time the Roman Mass of the Assumption reads the
ark narrative, prays the ark psalm, and joins the ark verse to the Woman and
to the Visitation within a single formulary. What that splice means is the
business of the study's final panel; that it was made, and made in 1969 and
not before, is the datum here. The current Liturgia Horarum matches it at
Lauds, where the intercessions pray: \term{Verbum aeternum, quod Mariam
habitationis tuae arcam incorruptibilem elegisti --- libera nos a corruptione
peccati} --- Eternal Word, who chose Mary as the incorruptible ark of your
dwelling: free us from the corruption of sin (an editorial paraphrase, not a
translation).\endnote{Lectionary selections verified at the USCCB readings
pages, \url{bible.usccb.org}, Lectionary nn. 621 and 622; the Ordo Lectionum
Missae itself was not opened. The lectionary and refrain translations are
copyright CCD and ICEL respectively and are therefore described, not quoted,
per the study's rights policy. The Liturgia Horarum Lauds intercession is
quoted from the Latin as served at
\url{https://www.societaslaudis.org/en/2026-08-15/hebdomada-xix-per-annum/in-assumptione-b-mariae-virginis-sollemnitas/liturgia-horarum/},
cross-checked at \url{universalis.com}; both are derivative electronic
witnesses, and the editio typica altera was not opened. The Office of
Readings psalmody of the day (Pss 23, 45, 86 in Vulgate numbering) does not
include Ps 131.}

The Catechism gathers the whole into one clause. Commenting on the angel's
``the Lord is with thee,'' CCC 2676 reads: Mary ``is the daughter of Zion in
person, the ark of the covenant, the place where the glory of the Lord
dwells'' --- in the Latin, \term{est ipsamet filia Sion, Foederis Arca, locus
in quo Domini gloria commoratur}, the litany's capitalized phrase once more.
Here two typologies that the Visitation section kept deliberately distinct
are fused: Daughter Zion, the city and people whom the Lord indwells, and
the ark, the vessel that carries him. The fusion is itself reception, not
ancient exegesis. Elsewhere the Catechism receives the Daughter-Zion reading
in so many words --- ``It was quite correct for the angel Gabriel to greet
her as the `Daughter of Zion': `Rejoice.'\thinspace'' (CCC 722; cf. 489) ---
and Joseph Ratzinger had made the fusion in his own voice, Marian devotion
being ``the praise of him\ldots whom she bears, the true, incorruptible,
indestructible Ark of the Covenant'': the ark predicated of Christ first,
and of Mary as his bearer.

The ark clause of CCC 2676 stands under the Annunciation greeting, not under
Elizabeth's. It is the next article, 2677, that opens with Luke 1:43 ---
``whence is this to me, that the mother of my Lord should come to me'' ---
the classic echo of 2 Kings 6:9, which the Catechism quotes without citing 2
Kings 6. The juxtaposition is the Catechism's; the identification remains
the exegetes'.\endnote{CCC 2676--2677: Latin editio typica at
\url{https://www.vatican.va/archive/catechism_lt/p4s1c2a2_lt.htm}; English
second edition read at the St. Charles Borromeo mirror
(\url{scborromeo.org}), where CCC 489 and 722 were also verified. CCC 2676
Latin in full: \term{Maria, in quam Ipse Dominus venit ut habitet, est
ipsamet filia Sion, Foederis Arca, locus in quo Domini gloria commoratur:
ipsa est ``tabernaculum Dei cum hominibus''} (with Soph 3,14.17 and Apc 21,3
in the apparatus). Ratzinger, \work{Daughter Zion} (tr. McDermott; Ignatius,
1983; German 1977), quoted from the Ignatius excerpt in \work{Catholic World

John Paul II shows the same two-axis texture within a single pontificate.
His Angelus address of 10 July 1983, in the series on the Loreto
invocations, is a full ark catechesis. The title, he says, \term{viene da
molto lontano} --- comes from very far back --- and from the Annunciation
onward \term{il suo grembo diviene l'arca della Nuova alleanza}, her womb
becomes the ark of the new covenant, the sanctuary where the incarnate
Presence of God came to dwell (editorial paraphrases of the quoted Italian).
Yet \work{Redemptoris Mater} (1987), his Marian encyclical, never uses the
word, working instead with the daughter of Zion; and his Visitation
catechesis of 1996 builds Elizabeth's ``mother of my Lord'' on Psalm 109:1,
not on 2 Kings 6. Reception is act by act; it was never a uniform
policy.\endnote{John Paul II, Angelus, 10 July

\subsection{The chain, dated}

\noindent The table reads two ways, and both readings are true. The title is
ancient: a sixth-century Latin hymn, an anonymous Byzantine masterpiece, a
Greek homily read at Roman Matins from 1568 --- no one invented the ark of
the covenant as a name for Mary in modern times. The liturgy's embrace of the
texts is largely modern: 1 Paralipomenon 15--16, the Psalm 131:8 refrain, and
Apocalypse 11:19 entered the Roman Mass only in 1969. And the exegetical
case for reading Luke 1 through 2 Kings 6 found no commentator before the
twentieth century in this study's audit, though Liguori had assembled its
devotional raw materials by 1750. The Church knew what she was doing both
times: when she prayed the title for centuries without a philological
warrant, and when, the philology in hand, she finally set the old texts into
the feast itself. Development has exactly this shape. One text remains, the
one the lectionary's splice points at: the ark seen in the opened temple, and
the Woman clothed with the sun. To that text, and to the master table of the
whole study's evidence, the last panel now turns.
