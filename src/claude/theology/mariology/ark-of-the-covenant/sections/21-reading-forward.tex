\section{Reading Forward: The Rules of Typology}

The last section ended with a question: what does the presence of God among
men look like after Christ? The third panel of this study will set out the
Church's answer so far as it concerns the ark --- her long habit of reading
the Virgin Mary as the ark of the new covenant. But before a single parallel
is laid on the table, the reader is owed a confession, and it is this:
resemblance is cheap. Set any two solemn processions side by side, in any two
literatures, and they will share a vocabulary --- a going up, a shouting, a
carried holy thing, a crowd. Two texts written in the same Greek, steeped in
the same Scriptures, drawing on the same small stock of ancient scenes, will
echo each other whether or not anyone intended it. Whole libraries of
nineteenth-century comparative religion were built on parallels no weaker
than some that will appear in the next section, and those libraries are now
cautionary tales. A reader who has watched parallel-hunting done badly is
right to keep his hand on his wallet. Seeing a likeness is not yet knowing a
meaning.

It follows that what a resemblance proves depends on what reading is --- and
that this must be settled before the resemblances are shown, not negotiated
while they are being shown. This section is a toll-gate. The toll is paid
once, here, so that the traffic through the last panels can move without
stopping at every verse to renegotiate the rules of the road.

\subsection{What Typology Is}

Typology is a claim about the authorship of history before it is a claim
about the art of any writer. In a well-made novel, the thing set quietly on
the mantel in the second chapter is aimed at the last chapter, and no
analysis of the second chapter alone will explain it; only the author's one
intention, holding both chapters at once, explains it. Typology claims that
the two Testaments are such a book --- and that its prose is not merely words
but events, because its Author disposes events as freely as a novelist
disposes sentences.

This is not a private theory of the devout imagination; it is the Church's
stated rule of reading. The Second Vatican Council's constitution on divine
revelation puts the rule in two halves.\endnote{Second Vatican Council,
dogmatic constitution \work{Dei Verbum} (18 November 1965), \S 12, given
here in paraphrase, not quotation: the interpreter must carefully search out
the meaning the sacred writers really intended, attending to literary forms
and to the conditions of their time and culture; and, since sacred Scripture
must be read and interpreted in the same Spirit in which it was written,
must attend no less carefully to the content and unity of the whole of
Scripture, taking into account the living Tradition of the whole Church and
the analogy of faith.} The interpreter must first search out what the human
authors really intended, with every instrument that serves that search ---
literary forms, historical setting, the conventions of the age. Nothing in
what follows will excuse this study from that duty. But because Scripture is
inspired, the same paragraph requires that it be read and interpreted in the
same Spirit in which it was written, and it names three tests of such
reading: the content and unity of the whole of Scripture; the living
Tradition of the whole Church; and the analogy of faith, the coherence of
revealed truths among themselves. The Catechism receives this rule and gives
the present practice its name: from apostolic times the Church has read
the two Testaments as one divine plan by means of typology, which discerns
in the works of God under the old covenant prefigurations of what he
accomplished in the fullness of time in his incarnate
Son.\endnote{\work{Catechism of the Catholic Church}, \S\S 128--130, in
paraphrase; with \S\S 112--114, which restate \work{Dei Verbum} 12's three
criteria. \S 129 adds the two guards noted in the text: Christians read the
Old Testament in the light of Christ crucified and risen, and such reading
must not make the Old Testament's own standing as revelation forgotten; the
New Testament, equally, must be read in the light of the Old.} Two words of
that sentence deserve their full weight. Typology \emph{discerns}: the
figure is found, not manufactured, and a discernment can be mistaken, which
is why evidence will matter at every step. And it discerns in God's
\emph{works}: deeds, persons, objects --- a chest of wood as much as a word
of prophecy --- because the Author of this book writes with events.

From this follows the point on which the whole first panel of this study was
staked: typology presupposes the reality of the events it reads. A figure
built on nothing signifies nothing. That is why the study spent its opening
panel on the box itself --- its construction, its journeys, its
catastrophes, its disappearance --- and settled questions of fact by
evidence at every turn. The canon itself models the resulting reticence and
the resulting confidence at once: the epistle to the Hebrews inventories the
ark's furniture --- the golden pot, the rod, the tables --- and then closes
the veil with the words ``of which it is not needful to speak now
particularly'' (Hebrews 9:5, Douay-Rheims).\endnote{Hebrews 9:1--5,
Douay-Rheims (Challoner), read at \url{https://drbo.org/chapter/65009.htm};
v.~5 quoted.} The New Testament's own author signals that the furniture
means more than the letter has yet said, and declines to say it hastily.
This study can hardly claim a better warrant for speaking carefully.

\subsection{What Typology Is Not}

Three confusions, each fatal, must be shut out at the gate. Typology is not
literal exegesis. The human author of 2 Kings 6 (= 2 Samuel 6) wrote of
David's procession, and no reading offered in this study will pretend
otherwise or overwrite his letter; the Catechism itself warns that the
typological reading must never cost the Old Testament its own standing as
revelation. Typology is not a claim about a writer's psychology. Whether
Luke consciously wove the ark narrative into his first chapter is a real
question --- a philological question, and the next section will weigh it
with the philologists' own tools --- but the typological claim does not
stand or fall with Luke's private intentions, because it rests on the
intention of Another; a demonstrated Lucan design would strengthen the
attestation, not create the meaning. And typology is not decoration. A
figure, if true, teaches; if false, it deceives; in neither case is it
ornament. The stakes are doctrinal, which is exactly why the Church binds
the practice with rules and why this study will bind itself with one more.

\subsection{The Two-Axis Rule}

Every disputed reading in the remaining panels will be handled by one named
distinction, stated formally here and nowhere else: \textbf{attestation is
not reception, and the absence of either is not falsity.} Attestation is the
historical trail of a reading --- who read the figure this way, when, in
what words, under whose name. It is a question of fact, settled by loci,
dates, and manuscripts, and this study's research records settle it without
sentiment. Reception is the Church's own adoption of a reading --- in her
liturgy, her catechisms, her solemn teaching. It too is a question of fact,
settled by dated public acts. The two run on separate axes: a reading may be
anciently attested yet never received into worship; it may be received at
the highest levels though no Father ever drew it; and a reading poor on both
axes may still be true, for late articulation is how development of doctrine
ordinarily looks from the outside. Concretely, every reading printed in what
follows will be declared in one or more of four states, in any combination:
philologically anchored in the verified text; anciently attested, with its
witnesses named and their names checked; liturgically or magisterially
received, with the act and its date; or the project's own synthesis, set
apart in a labeled frame so that the boundary is structural and not merely
verbal. The study will say which, every time.

One instrument more must be declared, because the next section will lean on
it from its first page. The philological case to be examined there is
cumulative: not one echo but a cluster of them, gathered in the span of a
few verses. A cumulative case must be weighed twice over. Each strand is
weighed alone, and a strand that fails alone will be printed here as
failing; the study's negatives are findings, not embarrassments. Then the
bundle is weighed as a bundle, for the question a cluster raises is not
whether any single echo compels but how likely it is that so many should
meet by accident in so small a space. Neither weighing can substitute for
the other: a rope is not refuted by showing that no one thread would hold
the load, and a heap of threads is not yet a rope. Nor is denial the only
honest rival of a cumulative case; the stronger rival is a different frame
that claims to explain the same bundle more simply, and the reader will
meet exactly such a rival frame in the next section, proposed by the
critics themselves. All of this is arithmetic that either party to the
dispute can accept; where the parties actually part company is the next
question, and it is best seen from the ditches.

The rule cuts in both directions, and the reader should watch it cut. There
are two ditches beside this road, and the study has met travelers in both.

Consider first the minimalist. Grant his conditional in full: if an allusion
is real only when it can be demonstrated --- shared words rare enough to
signify, in matching order, beyond coincidence --- then several of the
echoes in the next section fail the test, and the critical school that says
so will be quoted there in its own words, at its own loci, with its premises
stated fairly.\endnote{The school --- Brown, Fitzmyer, Schürmann, Bovon,
with Isaacs's grounds --- is quoted verbatim and cited at exact loci in the
Visitation section below and in its notes; its shared premise, a strict
verbal-echo criterion for allusion, is stated there in the body.} That work
is honest and this study needs it. The ditch lies one step further on: the
inference from ``the allusion is unproven as the evangelist's intention'' to
``the reading is false.'' That step silently assumes that the only meaning
in Scripture is the human author's demonstrable meaning --- which was
precisely the question the toll-gate was built to keep open. Philology can
weigh words, and its negative verdicts will be printed here as findings. It
cannot weigh the intentions of God. The minimalist who thinks it can has not
lost an argument; he has collapsed two axes into one.

Now the enthusiast, and grant his conditional too: if the Church's reading
is true, one would expect the ancients to have seen something --- and, in
substance, they did, earlier and more widely than the skeptic suspects, as
the panel on the Fathers will show. But the enthusiast is not content with
the pedigree the record supplies; he improves it. Quotations migrate under
greater names; a sermon by no one becomes a sermon by a Doctor of the
Church; a translator's loose clause becomes a third-century witness; an
unchecked reading passes from book to book because it ought to be
true.\endnote{Each habit is documented at its place below: a sermon joining
David's dance before the ark to the Virgin, transmitted under two great
names, its one author unknown, and a celebrated ``earliest Marian ark''
text that proves an artifact of a nineteenth-century translation (the
section on the Fathers); and an ancient variant long repeated in apologetic
literature without an edition check --- which, as it happens, survived the
check when this study's research finally made it (the Visitation section).}
Notice what each embellishment concedes: that only an ancient pedigree
could make the reading true --- the minimalist's own axiom, swallowed
whole, with forgery added to make up the arrears. The Church does not need
the forgery and is not honored by it. Both ditches, in the end, are one
error: both make attestation the sole measure of truth. The two-axis rule
refuses the axiom, and keeps the evidence.

One piece of the rule remains to be justified: counting reception as
evidence at all. The warrant is already in hand, for the council names the
living Tradition of the whole Church among the criteria of Spirit-led
reading, and nowhere is that Tradition more public, more deliberate, or more
precisely datable than in worship. The theologians' manuals count the
liturgy among the \term{loci theologici} --- the established places where
theology goes to find its evidence --- and this study will use it as
exactly that. When the Church appoints a lesson and a psalm to a feast, she
is not decorating a ceremony; she is performing an act of interpretation
before her whole people, and signing it with a date. A lectionary is a
commentary whose author is the reading Church. Such acts can be catalogued
like any other witnesses, and the reception panel will catalogue them ---
act by act, date by date --- on the same evidentiary terms as the
manuscripts of the Fathers.

The toll is paid. The reader knows what will be claimed and what will not:
facts settled by evidence, figures discerned under rule, every reading
wearing its state on its sleeve. The bar lifts, and the road beyond it
climbs --- as it happens, into the hill country of Juda.
