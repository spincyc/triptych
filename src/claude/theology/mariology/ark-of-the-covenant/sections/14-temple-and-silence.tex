\section{Temple and silence}

Nothing in the ark's story is stranger than the way it ends, because it does
not end. The most famous lost object in the world has no loss narrative. A
reader opening the books of Kings expects either the story of how the ark
perished or some claim about where it survives; Scripture gives neither. It
gives an arrival told in glory; a silence of nearly four centuries, broken
three times, each time more strangely; an inventory that does not mention
it; and, from the prophet who watched the catastrophe come, an obituary
written before the death. This section follows the texts to the edge of that
silence and ends where the canon itself ends the question.

\subsection{The house at last}

The entry is told in 3 Kings 8 (in modern Bibles, 1 Kings 8). Solomon
assembles ``the ancients of Israel''; the ark is carried up ``out of the city
of David, that is, out of Sion''---out of the tent David pitched for it---and
set ``into the oracle of the temple, into the holy of holies under the wings
of the cherubims'' (8:6), the inner room prepared for it from the first
(3 Kings 6:19), under Douay's name for that room, the oracle.\endnote{3 Kings
8:1, 3, 6--11, 21, Douay-Rheims (Challoner), verified at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/11008.htm}. The synoptic difference in the
bearers is preserved, not harmonized: 3 Kings 8:3 ``and the priests took up
the ark''; 2 Paralipomenon 5:4 ``the Levites took up the ark'' (verified at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/14005.htm}). On ``oracle'' as Douay's word both
for the inner sanctuary and for the propitiatory zone, see the terminology
discussion in the Sinai section.}

Three details of the installation notice earn their keep. First, the staves:
their ends ``were seen without in the sanctuary before the oracle,'' and
``there they have been unto this day'' (8:8). The poles are never to be drawn
out (Ex 25:15): the ark stands in the house of its rest still rigged for the
road, and ``unto this day'' means the narrator could still point to them---a
clause the rabbis would later lean on. Second,
the contents: ``Now in the ark there was nothing else but the two tables of
stone, which Moses put there at Horeb, when the Lord made a covenant with the
children of Israel, when they came out of the land of Egypt'' (8:9). The
parallel at 2 Paralipomenon 5:10 states the same exclusion in a substantially
identical formula, the subordinate clause differing---``when the Lord gave
the law''---nothing but the tables.\endnote{2 Paralipomenon 5:10,
Douay-Rheims: ``And there was nothing else in the ark but the two tables
which Moses put there at Horeb when the Lord gave the law to the children of
Israel, at their coming out of Egypt''; verified at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/14005.htm}. The two formulas are substantially
identical but not verbatim in the Douay text; the tension between this
exclusion and the fuller inventory of Hebrews 9:4 is carried as an open crux
in the study's apparatus.} And Solomon's prayer presses the exclusion to an
identity: ``And I have set there a place for the ark, wherein is the covenant
of the Lord, which he made with our fathers'' (8:21). Not the tables of the
covenant: the covenant. In Solomon's mouth the ark's whole content is a
relation between God and Israel.

Third, the cloud.

\begin{witnessbox}{3 Kings 8:10--11, Douay-Rheims}
\ldots a cloud filled the house of the Lord, And the priests could not stand
to minister because of the cloud: for the glory of the Lord had filled the
house of the Lord.
\end{witnessbox}

The sign that took possession of the tabernacle at Sinai (Ex 40:32--33,
Douay numbering) returns over the ark in the finished house: the cloud, the
glory, the ministers driven out by the Presence they serve. The glory whose
departure a dying woman had named at the ark's capture (1 Kings 4:21--22)
has come home. It is the ark's highest moment. It is also its last.

\subsection{The long silence}

After 3 Kings 8 the ark never again appears in the books of Kings. Not at
the schism, not in the temple plunderings and repairs that Kings records
across three centuries, not in the reform of Josiah as Kings tells it. Kings
never narrates the ark's removal, destruction, or hiding. In the whole canon
that silence is broken three times.

The first is a prayer. In 701 BC, with Sennacherib's letter spread out before
the Lord, Hezekiah prays in the temple: ``O Lord God of Israel, who sitteth
upon the cherubims, thou alone art the God of all the kings of the earth:
thou madest heaven and earth'' (4 Kings 19:15 = 2 Kings 19:15).\endnote{4
Kings 19:15, Douay-Rheims, verified at
\url{https://www.drbo.org/chapter/12019.htm}. The parallel at Isaias 37:16
prints ``sittest'' where 4 Kings prints ``sitteth''---an internal variation
of the 1899 Douay edition, quoted here as each place stands.} The title is
the ark's own; Scripture attaches it to the ark verbally at 1 Kings 4:4 and
2 Kings 6:2. Prayed in the temple, it is the strongest canonical indication
that the cherubim throne was a living cult reality two full centuries after
Shishak of Egypt stripped the temple treasuries about 925 (3 Kings
14:25--26). The old conjecture that the ark left with Shishak must explain
why Kings, so ready to record Shishak taking everything, never hints the ark
went too, and why the throne-title is still prayed in 701: the conjecture is
gratuitous. Honesty adds the calibration: the epithet also occurs in
psalmody as a fixed divine title (Ps 79:1; 98:1, Vulgate numbering), so it
could be formulaic; and the temple's own ten-cubit cherubim of olive wood
(3 Kings 6:23--28), which no raider could carry, would have stood over an
empty floor. The prayer leaves the conjecture with nothing to stand on; it
does not strictly refute it.\endnote{The calibration follows
the project's fate-chronology research record. Combined with the Josiah
notice below, the canonical data place the loss between Josiah's reform and
587 if the Chronicler's notice is historical, and in any case give no
positive footing earlier than Manasseh for a disappearance.}

The second is an order. In Josiah's reform, about 622, the king tells the
Levites: ``Put the ark in the sanctuary of the temple, which Solomon the son
of David king of Israel built: for you shall carry it no more: but minister
now to the Lord your God'' (2 Paralipomenon 35:3 = 2 Chronicles
35:3).\endnote{2 Paralipomenon 35:3, Douay-Rheims, verified at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/14035.htm}. The Babylonian Talmud reads this
same verse as Josiah \emph{hiding} the ark (b.\ Yoma 52b; see the fate
traditions below).} This is the ark's last appearance in the historical
books, and it is strange, for it implies the ark was out of its place and
being carried about. Three readings are on record. Either it had been
removed under Manasseh---perhaps to make room for the graven image of
4 Kings 21:7---and Josiah restored it; or the Levites had been bearing it in
processions; or the notice is the Chronicler's idealizing
construction---Kings' own account of Josiah has no ark in it---telling us
about the Chronicler's liturgy rather than Josiah's temple; this last is
reported as the majority critical view. The text does not decide among
them, and neither shall we. What no reading removes is the plain fact:
chronologically, the trail of the ark as an object ends at this verse.

The third is the obituary. In the days of Israel's increase, Jeremias
prophesies, ``they shall say no more: The ark of the covenant of the Lord:
neither shall it come upon the heart, neither shall they remember it,
neither shall it be visited, neither shall that be done any more. At that
time Jerusalem shall be called the throne of the Lord: and all the nations
shall be gathered together to it'' (Jeremias 3:16--17).\endnote{Jeremias
3:16--17, Douay-Rheims, verified at \url{https://drbo.org/chapter/28003.htm};
the verse's opening clause, on Israel's multiplying in the land in those
days, is paraphrased above. The Douay's closing clause ``neither shall that
be done any more'' renders Vulgate \emph{nec fiet ultra}; the Hebrew is
blunter---\term{welo yeaseh od}, it shall not be made again.} This is the
only prophetic text that names the ark, and it names it in order to
relativize it. The verse presumes an audience for whom the ark's absence
is, or is soon to be, a live wound; and it answers the wound not with
recovery but with enlargement---the whole city become ``the throne of the
Lord,'' the nations gathered to it. The loss is
absorbed, not mourned. The Bible's own first theology of the disappearance
is already a theology of supersession by something greater.

\subsection{The year of the inventory}

Then 587, and the lists. When the Chaldeans burned the house, the narrator
of 4 Kings 25 (= 2 Kings 25) itemized what they broke up and carried away:
the two bronze pillars, the sea and its bases, the pots and shovels, the
snuffers and dishes and firepans and bowls---an accounting that descends to
the fire-irons of the altar service. The ark is not in the list. It is likewise absent from
the fuller parallel list in Jeremias 52, and from the inventory of vessels
Cyrus later restored.\endnote{4 Kings 25:13--17 was checked item by item in
the study's research records (the Douay wording of the list is described
here, not quoted); the parallels at Jeremias 52:17--23 and 1 Esdras
[Ezra] 1:7--11 are standard and are reported, not re-verified.} Call the
argument what it is: an argument from silence. But when a careful clerk
inventories a looted house down to the fire-irons and never mentions the
one famous thing in it, the omission is itself a datum---though of what
kind, the list alone cannot say. Either the ark was already gone before the
Chaldeans came, or it perished unrecorded in the burning of the house. Both
positions are held by serious scholars. The canon narrates neither.

\subsection{The fate traditions}

Into that silence tradition spoke, abundantly and not with one voice. Each
witness follows with its date, its actor, and its horizon, in its own
framing. The earliest is also the canonical one: inside the second letter
prefixed to 2 Machabees, the writer appeals to what ``is found in the
descriptions of Jeremias the prophet'' (2 Machabees 2:1):

\begin{witnessbox}{2 Machabees 2:4--5, 7--8, Douay-Rheims}
\ldots the prophet, being warned by God, commanded that the tabernacle and
the ark should accompany him, till he came forth to the mountain where
Moses went up, and saw the inheritance of God. \ldots Jeremias came thither
he found a hollow cave: and he carried in thither the tabernacle, and the
ark, and the altar of incense, and so stopped the door. \ldots The place
shall be unknown, till God gather together the congregation of the people,
and receive them to mercy. And then the Lord will shew these things, and
the majesty of the Lord shall appear, and there shall be a cloud as it was
also shewed to Moses, and he shewed it when Solomon prayed that the place
might be sanctified to the great God.
\end{witnessbox}

Some who followed came up afterward to mark the way, and could not find
it; Jeremias blamed them for the attempt (2:6). Three observations. The passage names its own source---Scripture is here
canonizing a tradition and telling the reader that it is doing so. Its
geography is Nebo, ``the mountain where Moses went up.'' And its horizon is
not archaeological but eschatological: the place unknown till God gather
the congregation of the people; the appointed sign of disclosure the cloud
``as it was also shewed to Moses''---the very cloud of Exodus 40 and of
3 Kings 8:10--11, completing the inclusio in the text's own words. This is
consolation with a memory, not a map.\endnote{2 Machabees 2:1--8,
Douay-Rheims, verified at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/46002.htm}; v.\ 6 is paraphrased above, and
the block quotation elides it. The letter carrying the passage (2 Machabees
1:10--2:18) invites the Jews of Egypt to keep the new feast of the
dedication; its authenticity and date are themselves debated in the
literature, which does not touch its canonical standing as Scripture
reporting a tradition.}

And it has a family. The \work{Lives of the Prophets}, a Jewish treatise on
the prophets' deaths and burial places from the first century of our era,
transmitted by Christian hands, tells in its Jeremias chapter how the
prophet, before the destruction, made the ark be swallowed up in a cliff of
rock; how none shall bring it out but Aaron, and none unfold its tables but
Moses; and how in the resurrection the ark will rise first of all, come
forth from the rock, and be set on Sinai, where the saints will gather to
it. The rock lies in the wilderness between the two mountains where Moses
and Aaron are buried, and a cloud of fire stands over it by night, for the
glory of God will never cease from his law. Here the hiding tradition says
aloud what 2 Machabees implies: the hidden ark is an object of resurrection
hope.\endnote{\work{Vitae Prophetarum}, Jeremias
chapter (Torrey's vv.\ 9--15; Hare's 2:11--19 in \work{OTP} 2, reported).
Paraphrased, not quoted: the accessible English is C.\ C.\ Torrey,
\work{The Lives of the Prophets} (SBL Monograph 1, 1946), read at
\url{http://lotp.thebookofenoch.info/} with the edition at
\url{https://archive.org/details/SBLMS1}, and the 1946 copyright-renewal
status was not established, so the study paraphrases. The chapter's
sign-clause about the Gentiles worshipping a piece of wood reads as
Christian; whether it is an interpolation in a Jewish work or evidence of
Christian redaction of the chapter is debated, and the witness is used here
with that caution.}

The expectation was not confined to Judea, nor even to the ark. Josephus
records that in the year 36 a Samaritan---a man, he says, ``who thought
lying a thing of little consequence''---raised a multitude with exactly
this hope: he ``bid them to get together upon Mount
Gerizzim, which is by them looked upon as the most holy of all mountains,
and assured them, that when they were come thither, he would show them
those sacred vessels which were laid under that place, because Moses put
them there.'' Pilate met the crowd with troops and executed the leaders;
the massacre cost him his office. Note the precision: the depositor is
Moses, the mountain is Gerizim, and Josephus says sacred vessels---not the
ark. The words must not be sharpened. The episode proves that holy things
hidden in a mountain, awaiting disclosure, were a live and politically
explosive expectation in the first century.\endnote{Josephus,
\work{Antiquities} 18.85--87 (Whiston's 18.4.1), Whiston's translation,
verified at \url{https://lexundria.com/j_aj/18.85-18.89/wst}. The sequel
(\S\S 88--89), Vitellius sending Pilate to Rome, dates the episode to
AD 36. The expected discloser is usually identified with the Samaritan
restorer-figure, the Taheb (reported).}

The apocalypses written after the year 70 read 587 through their own
catastrophe and pushed the custody one step higher. In 2 Baruch, the night
before the Chaldeans breach the walls, it is an angel who descends into the
Holy of Holies, takes from it the veil, the holy ark, the mercy-seat, the
two tables, and the rest of the holy things, and commands:
``Earth, earth, earth, hear the word of the mighty God, and receive what I
commit to you, and guard them until the last times, so that, when you are
ordered, you may restore them, so that strangers may not get possession of
them.''\endnote{2 Baruch 6:4--10, R.\ H.\ Charles's translation (1896,
public domain), verified at
\url{https://www.pseudepigrapha.com/pseudepigrapha/2Baruch.html} and
cross-checked verbatim at the Wesley Center Online. Charles reads ``holy
ark'' in the list at 6:7; other renderings of the Syriac are reported to
read ``ephod'' there, so the argument leans on the list as a whole, not on
the single noun.} In 4 Baruch it is Jeremias again, but at God's own
command, who is told to ``consign them to the earth,'' the earth being
charged: ``Guard the vessels of the temple service until the gathering of
the beloved.''\endnote{4 Baruch (\work{Paraleipomena Jeremiou}) 3:7--11
(OTP numbering; editions differ), in the public-domain translation of
Robert A.\ Kraft and Ann Elizabeth Purintun, verified at
\url{http://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/rak//publics/pseudepig/ParJer-Eng.html}.}
Destruction becomes divine act; concealment, divine custody; restoration, a
promise with a fixed though unknown date.

The rabbis preserved the debate rather than a verdict. The Mishnah records
a temple tradition that the ark lay hidden under the wood-store of the
temple court, and tells of a priest who noticed that one flagstone there
was different from the others and died before he could finish telling
it---whereby they knew the ark was hidden there. The Talmud makes Josiah
the hider, acting on our very verse, 2 Paralipomenon 35:3, and foreseeing
the exile; with the ark, it says, went the jar of manna, the flask of
anointing oil, and Aaron's rod. But on the next folios stands the opposing
baraita: Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Simeon ben Yohai held that the ark was
carried to Babylon with the vessels; Rabbi Judah ben Lakish, that it was
hidden in its place---his prooftext the staves seen ``unto this day'' of
3 Kings 8:8. Babylon and beneath-the-temple stand side by side in the same
tractate, and neither is allowed to erase the other.\endnote{m.\ Sheqalim 6:1--2; b.\ Yoma 52b;
b.\ Yoma 53b--54a, read via the Sefaria API
(\url{https://www.sefaria.org/Yoma.52b};
\url{https://www.sefaria.org/Yoma.53b};
\url{https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Shekalim.6.1}). Paraphrased
throughout: the William Davidson (Steinsaltz) English is CC BY-NC and is
not quoted, per the study's rabbinica policy.} And one ancient voice
dissents from the whole hiding genre: in the mourning-woman vision of
4 Esdras---a book outside the Catholic canon, an appendix witness---the
seer laments the sanctuary laid waste and the ark of the covenant
plundered. Despoiled, not hidden.\endnote{4 Esdras (2 Esdras) 10:21--22,
verified in the research records at the Revised Standard Version (Bible
Gateway, \url{https://www.biblegateway.com/}); described here rather than
quoted, per the study's rights rules. Written about AD 100, reading 587
through the lens of 70.}

The family resemblance is the real find. In nearly every witness the ark
is not lost but \emph{kept}, and its return is indexed not to excavation
but to God's regathering of his people. A family that has merely lost its
father's watch says so; this family tells you who is keeping it, and
against what day. That is the shape the canon itself gave the question in
2 Machabees 2:7--8, and the honest record keeps the minority report beside
it: plundered, says 4 Esdras, and gone.

\subsection{The empty room, and the wider world}

Meanwhile there was a second temple, and its inmost room was empty. The
rabbis say that in it stood only a stone from the days of the early
prophets, called the foundation stone, three fingerbreadths above the
floor, where the high priest set the censer on the Day of Atonement.
Josephus describes the room: ``In this there was nothing at all. It was
inaccessible and inviolable, and not to be seen by any; and was called the
Holy of Holies.'' And the pagan witness agrees: Tacitus records that when
Pompey entered the temple by right of victory in 63 BC,
it became common knowledge that there was \term{nulla intus deum effigie
vacuam sedem et inania arcana}---no image of a god within, the seat empty,
the inner mysteries void.\endnote{The
foundation stone (\term{even shetiyyah}): b.\ Yoma 53b, read via Sefaria
and paraphrased (the cognate Mishnah text is m.\ Yoma 5:2, reported).
Josephus, \work{Jewish War} 5.219 (5.5.5), Whiston's translation, verified
at Project Gutenberg ebook 2850 (\url{https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2850});
Greek verified at the Scaife Viewer,
\url{urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0526.tlg004.perseus-grc2:5.219}. Tacitus,
\work{Historiae} 5.9, Latin verified at
\url{https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/tacitus/tac.hist5.shtml}; the English
rendering above is editorial. Tacitus describes 63 BC and the Second-Temple
sanctuary---evidence for the empty room, not directly for the ark's
fate.} For six centuries the holiest room in Israel was, on the testimony
of friend and enemy alike, empty---and was not therefore abandoned. Once a
year the high priest still entered, to a bare stone where the ark had been.
Israel's worship had learned to face an absence without consenting to call
it one.

The ark's afterlife reached beyond Israel. The Qur'an enters the ark's
story at its biblical seam---the return in the days of Saul---and makes the
ark the sign of Saul's kingship: ``there shall come unto you
the ark wherein is peace of reassurance from your Lord, and a remnant of
that which the house of Moses and the house of Aaron left behind, the
angels bearing it'' (Qur'an 2:248, Pickthall's rendering). Pickthall's
``peace of reassurance'' translates \term{sakina}, a word cognate with
\term{Shekinah}: the Qur'an preserves in Arabic the ark's theology of
indwelt Presence. The classical commentators elaborate the ``remnant'' as
the tablets or their fragments and the relics of Moses and Aaron; and
later Islamic eschatology gives the ark a future of its own, in traditions
that have the Mahdi bring it forth---from Lake Tiberias, or a cave near
Antioch---before the end. Those last are reported leads, not verified
texts; but even at one remove they show the same instinct: the ark's
reappearance belongs to the end of days.\endnote{Qur'an 2:248 in Muhammad
Marmaduke Pickthall, \work{The Meaning of the Glorious Koran} (1930;
public domain in the United States since 2026), verified via the quran.com
API, translation resource M.\ Pickthall, verse key 2:248
(\url{https://quran.com/2/248}). The tafsir-level claims (al-Tabari,
al-Qurtubi, Ibn Kathir) and the Mahdi traditions (Nuaym ibn Hammad,
\work{Kitab al-Fitan}, cited by al-Suyuti) are reported from the study's
research records; exact loci unverified.}

And there is Ethiopia. The claim that the ark rests at Aksum, in the
chapel beside the church of Mary of Zion, guarded by one monk and seen by
no one, is a present institutional fact of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church.
Its charter text, the \work{Kebra Nagast}, in which Menelik, son of
Solomon and the queen of Sheba, carries the ark from Jerusalem, must be
dated honestly: it was produced in the early fourteenth century
(1314--1322), compiled by Yeshaq of Aksum as the national epic of the
Solomonic dynasty restored in 1270---some eighteen centuries after the
event it narrates, with no ancient attestation of the Aksum claim behind
it. No scholar regards it as history. But as reception it commands
respect: Ethiopia did not merely claim the ark; it multiplied it into a
living liturgy---the \term{tabot}, the consecrated ark-tablet without
which no Ethiopian altar is an altar, so that every church in that
communion houses an ark---a datum of the kind this study calls reception.\endnote{\work{Kebra Nagast}: composition
1314--1321/22 by Yeshaq (Isaac), \emph{nebura'ed} of Aksum, on the
colophon's testimony as fixed by Conti Rossini, Littmann, and Cerulli
(reported); claims verified at the level of the sourced survey at
\url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kebra_Nagast} and Budge's 1922
introduction at \url{https://sacred-texts.com/afr/kn/kn004.htm}. Edward
Ullendorff is reported (via Munro-Hay) to have examined the Aksum object
in 1941 and judged it an ordinary \term{tabot}; secondhand, not quoted.}
The ark's silence has also fed a modern treasure-hunt literature, from
Templar romance to cellar excavation to fiction; this study declines the
hunt, and keeps the older company of Jeremias 3:16.

Where, then, is the ark? History answers: unknown---the last sighting is
Josiah's order, and after 587 the record is silence and inference. The
traditions answer: kept, in a custody not ours, until God gathers his
people. And the canon does not leave the question open: at the far end of
the Bible, at the seventh trumpet, John saw it---``the ark of his testament
was seen in his temple'' (Apocalypse 11:19)---and there this study will
finish.\endnote{Apocalypse
11:19, Douay-Rheims, verified at \url{https://drbo.org/chapter/73011.htm}:
``And the temple of God was opened in heaven: and the ark of his testament
was seen in his temple, and there were lightnings, and voices, and an
earthquake, and great hail.'' The verse stands immediately before the
woman clothed with the sun (Apocalypse 12:1); the chapter division between
them is medieval. The study returns to the whole passage in its final
panel.}
