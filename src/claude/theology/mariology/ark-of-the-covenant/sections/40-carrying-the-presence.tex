\section{Coda: Carrying the Presence}

What the ark teaches is not, in the end, information about a box. It is
the manners of creatures before the Presence. Three panels of evidence
have gone before this page --- a journey, a pattern, and a reading ---
and this coda adds nothing to them. It asks the question the sources
keep forcing on anyone who reads them slowly: granted all of that, how
am I to behave? For the doctrine every panel rested on is not
antiquarian. The God who spoke from between the cherubims has not
withdrawn from the earth. He makes his abode with the man who loves him
(John 14:23). Paul has to remind the Corinthians that their bodies are
temples of the Holy Ghost (1 Corinthians 6:19). And every communicant
walking back from the altar is, for those minutes, a bearer of Christ
in a sense more literal than any figure in these pages. The modes of
presence differ, and theology keeps them carefully distinct; the
manners they ask of us are of one family. The first grammar of those
manners is Israel's long schooling before the ark.

\subsection{The Visitation as the pattern of apostolate}

Works are the overflow of a presence carried, or they are noise. The
old masters of the interior life put this in a figure, restated here in
the study's own words: a cistern serves the valley below it only
because it is first full, while a bare channel passes water all day,
keeps none, and runs dry the hour its source is cut. The apostle is
meant to be a cistern, not a channel.\endnote{The figure is St.\ Bernard's (Serm.~18 in Cant.), and
it reaches this study at one remove, through Dom Jean-Baptiste
Chautard, \work{The Soul of the Apostolate} (anonymous Gethsemani
translation, 1946), p.~33, read at
\url{http://www.mountsaintbernard.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/Dom-Chautard-The-Soul-of-the-Apostolate.pdf}.
The Bernardine locus is reported from Chautard's own citation and was
not verified in PL for this study; the 1946 translation's copyright
status is unresolved, so its wording is paraphrased, not quoted.}

The first apostolic journey of the new covenant shows what that means.
It was made by a woman newly with child. Mary, overshadowed by the
power of the most High (Luke 1:35), ``rising up'' went ``into the hill
country with haste'' (Luke 1:39). She brought Elizabeth no gift and no
program; she brought only what she carried. At her plain greeting the
house filled with the Holy Ghost --- Elizabeth cried out, and ``the
infant in my womb leaped for joy'' (Luke 1:44). Then the visit of the
Presence became service. She ``abode with her about three months''
(Luke 1:56), the last months of an old woman's difficult waiting, and
her one recorded utterance in that house is praise, the Magnificat
(Luke 1:46--55).\endnote{The canticle's Douay wording is not
quoted here because this study's verification records captured Luke
1:39--56 selectively; the canticle is cited by locus and named, not
quoted.}

The order of the narrative is itself the doctrine. Luke sets the
overshadowing before the journey: she is filled before she is sent,
and she does not go until she carries. Even her haste, the one detail
a busy man reads with relief, will not serve as his alibi. It is not
the haste of someone escaping an empty house but the overflow of a
fullness that cannot wait to become service. And Luke, who records
Elizabeth's cry to the syllable, records not one word of the greeting
that provoked it (Luke 1:40--41). What entered that house with Mary
was not eloquence.\endnote{Luke 1:35, 39, 41--44, 56,
Douay-Rheims (Challoner), read at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/49001.htm}: ``And Mary rising up in those
days, went into the hill country with haste into a city of Juda''
(1:39); ``the infant in my womb leaped for joy'' (1:44); ``And Mary
abode with her about three months'' (1:56). The philological weight of
these wordings against the ark narratives is assessed in Panel III;
they are used here for conduct, not for argument.} Preachers extend
the scene: the house that receives the bearer of Christ is blessed, as
Obededom's house was blessed in the ark's three months there (2 Kings
6:11 = 2 Samuel 6:11). That is homiletics rather than demonstrated
typology, and the project's records say so plainly. But a coda is the
lawful place for homiletics, and this homily claims only the principle
already stated: where Christ is carried in, blessing follows him.

Then the examination writes itself, and it must be made in the first
person or not at all. I have reason to fear that in me the channel has
outrun the cistern: if I speak about God more fluently than I speak to
him; if my works have multiplied in the same years in which my prayer
has shortened; if I go everywhere with haste and could not say, on
arriving, that I carry anything; if the last soul who left my company
went away impressed by me and unchanged toward my Master. The figures
this study has watched --- Levites under the poles, David counting six
paces, a young woman on the hill road into Juda --- never confused the
carrying with the thing carried. Let the whole of Panel I condense to
one question, small enough to take through any doorway on any errand:
\emph{whom do I carry when I go?}

\subsection{Reverence, and the fear of the holy}

The fear of the holy is not the opposite of love. It is love's
grammar. This study did not soften Oza or Bethsames, and it
declines now to turn them into threats. The narratives are not
blackmail, and Panel II drew their lesson exactly: the Presence is not
managed. The law had said it before either incident, and said it to
the very men whose office was carrying: the sons of Caath were to bear
the holy things only after the veiling, forbidden to look on them ``by
any curiosity\ldots\ otherwise they shall die'' (Numbers
4:20).\endnote{Numbers 4:5--6, 15, 20, Douay-Rheims, read at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/04004.htm}: ``Let not others by any
curiosity see the things that are in the sanctuary before they be
wrapped up, otherwise they shall die.'' Challoner's note at 1 Kings
6:19 (``And curiously looked into'') wires Bethsames to this law
from the Douay tradition's own side; the note and the Bethsames crux
are treated in Panel I.} The severity is Scripture's own; the study's
part is only to refuse to explain it away. What God has taken for his
own use is not afterward common. That is the whole grammar, and love
speaks it or speaks something else.

But the same law that forbade the look prescribed the veiling, and the
sentence that threatened the Caathites appointed them their poles and
their shoulders. The severity and the tenderness are one provision.
God did not leave Israel to guess how to come near; he taught them, in
cubits and coverings and an appointed manner of carrying, how the
unbearable could dwell in the middle of the camp and no one be
destroyed. Reverence, in the canon, is not the distance love keeps
from God. It is the nearness God makes possible for love. The
survivors of Bethsames asked who could stand before so holy a God
(1 Kings 6:20 = 1 Samuel 6:20), and the question was better than they
knew. The rest of this study has been the long answer, and it ends
with the Presence carried in a girl's body and laid in a manger. The
holiness never lessened. The provision widened.

The Church never outgrew that grammar; she set it to music. The
Byzantine office of the Dormition, which hymns Mary as the living ark,
carries in its canon the old Transitus episode of Jephonias, the
insolent man who laid hands on the Virgin's funeral bier and lost
them --- the liturgy's own Oza, sung each fifteenth of August. On
the eve of the same feast the Church reads Ezechiel's vision of the
east gate kept shut because the Lord had entered by it (Ezechiel
44:1--2).\endnote{Dormition of the Theotokos, Canon I
(ascribed in the Menaion to Cosmas of Maiuma), Ode 3, second troparion,
witnessed in the Reader Menaion service for August 15 (st-sergius.org,
Emenaion 08-15.pdf); that English translation is unidentified and
presumptively under copyright, so the troparion is described here, not
quoted --- the rights note stands with the reception evidence in Panel
III. The Great Vespers readings of the feast, including Ezechiel
43:27--44:4, were verified in the Antiochian Archdiocese vespers text
for August 14 (antiochianprodsa.blob.core.windows.net); the selections
are cited as facts of selection.} That shut gate is the Fathers'
figure for consecrated things. Ambrose asks, in his work on the
instruction of a virgin, what that gate is if not Mary, shut because
virgin. There --- at the gate, not at Oza's cart --- the old
tradition anchors what it says of St.~Joseph's
continence.\endnote{Ambrose, \work{De institutione virginis} 8.52
(PL 16). The sentence ``Quae est haec porta nisi Maria?\ \ldots\
clausa, quia virgo'' is given as reported in the scholarly literature
on the porta-clausa exegesis; the PL column was not independently
verified for this study. Jerome's parallel application (\work{Comm.\ in
Hiezechielem} XIII, on 44:1--3) is likewise reported. The closed-gate
exposition circulating under Augustine's name (via Aquinas, ST~III
q.~28 a.~3, citing a sermon \work{De Annuntiatione Domini}) belongs to
the pseudo-Augustinian appendix class; the figure is therefore anchored
here on Ambrose.}

The modern devotional argument --- that Joseph held back from his wife
because he knew what befell those who touched the ark --- must be
labeled honestly: no ancient witness for it could be found. Pressed as
an argument it proves too much. Elizabeth embraced Mary unharmed, and
touch as such was never the ark's offense; the Caathites were
commanded to carry it, and the Chronicler locates Oza's fault in
transport against the law (1 Paralipomenon
15:13--15).\endnote{No patristic or medieval witness drawing the
Oza--Joseph inference was found in this project's searches; the trope
circulates in twentieth- and twenty-first-century apologetic writing
without an identified first source. For the offense as unlawful
transport rather than contact as such: Numbers 4:15 with 1 Paralipomenon
15:13--15, treated in Panel I.} It is a modern devotional development,
consonant with the attested grammar of reverence but resting on none of
the ancient texts. The ancient anchor is the shut gate, and it is
enough.

And then the grammar turns on us, who assault no biers and are in
little danger of Oza's zeal, being rarely zealous enough to steady
anything. The examination, again, in the first person. My genuflection
--- has it worn down to the bob of a man glancing at his watch, or is
it still the body's confession of what the lamp means? My communions
--- do I prepare for, and give thanks after, the reception of him whom
the propitiatory only prefigured, with as much recollection as Israel
spent on a gilded chest it was forbidden to see? My speech about holy
things --- has familiarity made it glib, the insider's patter of a man
handling sacred vessels like stock? My silence in church --- do I keep
any? These are not scruples. They are manners, and no one who has
followed the ark from Sinai will mistake the manners of creatures
before the Presence for etiquette.

\subsection{Mary, bearer}

Mary is in this study for one reason, and it is the reason she is in
the Gospel: she carried Christ. The oldest ark typology the project
verified is Christological. Hippolytus's ark of imperishable wood is
the Lord's own body, its incorrupt timber from the Virgin; when the
explicitly Marian title flowers in the fifth century, it flowers as a
way of saying that same thing about her Son. John Damascene, on the
day of her passing, called her the sacred and living ark of the living
God, the ark that had conceived its own Maker.\endnote{Hippolytus,
\work{Commentary on Daniel} IV.24, and the fifth-century development,
with texts and authenticity labels, are set out in Panel III's
patristic section. John Damascene, second homily on the Dormition,
n.~2, as reported at PG 96:723 (the homily spans PG 96:722--754); the
sentence and its long liturgical career --- including its centuries as
a Matins lesson of the Roman office --- are given with their witnesses
in Panel III.} A modern abbot, writing for apostles, closed his book
before a Byzantine icon of the Mother of God and read the whole
interior life off its geometry: Jesus her center, her breath, her
life; she carrying him, magnifying his voice, holding him out to be
seen. His word for her was the monstrance.\endnote{Chautard, \work{The Soul
of the Apostolate}, Epilogue (p.~139 of the 1946 edition cited above);
paraphrased, not quoted.} A monstrance is precious metal shaped
entirely around what it holds. Empty, it is a curiosity in a case, and
no one who kneels before it kneels to the gold.

Everything the Church says of Mary under the ark's figure has exactly
that shape, and the Church says so in her own voice: Mary's maternal
office adds nothing to the one mediation of Christ and takes nothing
from it, but shows its power. Whatever she carries, and whatever grace
her carrying brings near, is his.\endnote{Second Vatican Council, \work{Lumen Gentium} 60--62,
paraphrased, not quoted: the constitution teaches that Mary's maternal
office in no way obscures or lessens the unique mediation of Christ
but shows its power, and that every salutary influence of hers flows
from the superabundance of his merits. Panel III records the adjacent
negative finding: the constitution itself never uses the ark title.}
Israel's reverence ran through the ark to the Presence between the
cherubims; the Church's runs through Mary to her Son. A devotion to
her that stopped at her would be the figure emptied --- the gold
without the manna.

One practice, then, and this book has done its work if the reader
takes nothing else from it. The next time I enter a church I will look
first for the small flame beside the tabernacle. Israel had a cloud
over the tent by day and ``a fire by night'' --- and that ``throughout
all their mansions,'' the Douay's old word for every stopping-place of
the pilgrimage. When the camp moved, the ark went before it, ``for
three days providing a place for the camp'' (Exodus 40:36 Douay =
40:38; Numbers 10:33).\endnote{Exodus 40:36, Douay-Rheims (= 40:38 in
versions following the Hebrew versification), read at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/02040.htm}: ``For the cloud of the Lord
hung over the tabernacle by day, and a fire by night\ldots\ throughout
all their mansions.'' Numbers 10:33, read at
\url{https://drbo.org/chapter/04010.htm}: ``the ark of the covenant of
the Lord went before them, for three days providing a place for the
camp.''} The lamp keeps that watch still, two steps from the door of
any parish church, at the end of the long road this study has walked
from Sinai. I will genuflect like a man who has read the first panel;
keep a moment of silence like a man who has read the second; and go
out carrying what I have received, as she went into the hill country.
The ark still goes before the camp.
