\section{The Roman Canon in Each Book, and the Three Added Prayers}

The Roman Canon is the oldest continuously used Eucharistic Prayer in the Latin Church and the one text about which Trent legislated directly: ``If anyone shall say that the canon of the Mass contains errors and is therefore to be abrogated: let him be anathema.''\endnote{Council of Trent, session XXII (17 September 1562), \emph{De sacrificio missæ}, canon VI: ``Si quis dixerit, canonem missæ errores continere, ideoque abrogandum esse: anathema sit.'' Read at the page image of \emph{Canones et decreta sacrosancti œcumenici Concilii Tridentini}, editio stereotypa undecima (Leipzig: Tauchnitz, 1887), printed p.~121, Internet Archive item \texttt{canonesetdecreta00coun\_0}, leaf n128, retrieved and hashed 25 July 2026; verified visually.} It is therefore the locus at which a claim of rupture would be most damaging if true, and the locus at which a claim of continuity would be most valuable if demonstrable. Both books print it. The comparison can be made line by line.

\subsection{What is the same}

From \emph{Te igitur} to \emph{Per ipsum}, the Roman Canon stands in the third typical edition with the same prayers in the same order as in the 1962 book, and in almost every clause with the same words. \emph{Te igitur} is identical, including \emph{una cum famulo tuo Papa nostro N.\ et Antistite nostro N.} So is the \emph{Memento, Domine}. \emph{Communicantes} is identical apart from the parenthesising discussed below, and its five proper forms for Christmas, Epiphany, Easter, Ascension and Pentecost are all retained. \emph{Hanc igitur} is identical, with its paschal variant retained. \emph{Quam oblationem}, \emph{Unde et memores}, \emph{Supra quæ}, \emph{Supplices te rogamus}, \emph{Memento etiam}, \emph{Nobis quoque peccatoribus}, \emph{Per quem hæc omnia} and the doxology \emph{Per ipsum} stand with their words unchanged.\endnote{The collation is between \emph{Missale Romanum} (1962), \emph{Canon Missæ}, printed pp.~300--311 (artifact pp.~381--392), and \emph{Missale Romanum}, editio typica tertia, \emph{Prex eucharistica I seu Canon romanus}, nn.~83--98, artifact pp.~347--354. Within the 1962 witness, printed pp.~306--307 (artifact pp.~387--388) were collated visually 25 July 2026; the remaining pages were read at the artifact's embedded text layer. The 2002 witness is a secondary digital reproduction, not the printed altar book; see the scope appendix.}

That is a considerable finding, and it should be stated before the differences, because it is the larger fact. The claim that the reform abolished the Roman Canon is false in the plainest sense: the Canon is printed first among the Eucharistic Prayers in the current Missal, and the rubric governing the choice among them says of it that it ``may always be used.''\endnote{\emph{Institutio Generalis Missalis Romani} (2002), n.~365(a), artifact pp.~55--56: ``Prex eucharistica prima, seu Canon romanus, qui semper adhiberi potest, opportunius profertur diebus quibus assignantur \emph{Communicantes} propria, aut in Missis quæ \emph{Hanc igitur} propriis ditantur, necnon in celebrationibus Apostolorum et Sanctorum, quorum mentio fit in ipsa Prece; itemque diebus dominicis, nisi, ob rationes pastorales, præferatur Prex eucharistica tertia.''}

\subsection{What is different}

The differences are of three kinds, and it is worth keeping them apart because they have very different weights.

\textbf{The institution narrative.} Here the words themselves change.

\begin{ledgertable}{Locus}{Change}
Words over the bread & 1962: \emph{Hoc est enim Corpus meum.} 2002: \emph{Accipite et manducate ex hoc omnes: hoc est enim Corpus meum, quod pro vobis tradetur.} The Pauline clause from 1 Cor 11:24 is added, and the invitation \emph{Accipite et manducate}, which in the 1962 book stands in the narrative introduction, is now printed as part of the formula the rubric marks off\\
Words over the chalice & 1962: \emph{Hic est enim Calix Sanguinis mei, novi et æterni testamenti: mysterium fidei: qui pro vobis et pro multis effundetur in remissionem peccatorum.} 2002: \emph{hic est enim calix Sanguinis mei novi et æterni testamenti, qui pro vobis et pro multis effundetur in remissionem peccatorum.} \emph{Mysterium fidei} is removed from the formula\\
After the consecration of the chalice & 1962: \emph{Hæc quotiescumque feceritis, in mei memoriam facietis}, said after the chalice is set down. 2002: \emph{Hoc facite in meam commemorationem}, printed inside the narrative, immediately after the chalice formula\\
Acclamation & 1962: none. 2002: the priest says \emph{Mysterium fidei} and the people answer with one of three acclamations, the first of which is drawn from 1 Cor 11:26\\
Gestures within the narrative & 1962: the priest signs the host and the chalice with the cross at \emph{benedixit}; after each consecration he genuflects, rises, shows the species to the people, replaces it, and genuflects again; from the consecration until the ablutions he keeps thumb and forefinger joined. 2002: the crosses at \emph{benedixit} are gone; the priest bows slightly at the words, shows the species, replaces it, and genuflects once\\
\end{ledgertable}

Two of these deserve comment, and the first requires a correction that cuts against the reform's defenders.

The removal of \emph{mysterium fidei} from the chalice formula is the single most-discussed textual change in the whole reform, and it is regularly minimised on the ground that the phrase was never part of the sacramental form. The 1962 Missal contradicts that defence in its own front matter. Its treatise \emph{De defectibus in celebratione Missæ occurrentibus}, printed among the preliminary matter of every altar edition, states the form of the sacrament in terms: ``The words of consecration, which are the form of this Sacrament, are these: \emph{Hoc est enim Corpus meum}. And: \emph{Hic est enim Calix Sanguinis mei, novi et æterni testamenti: mysterium fidei: qui pro vobis et pro multis effundetur in remissionem peccatorum}.''\endnote{\emph{Missale Romanum} (1962), \emph{De defectibus in celebratione Missæ occurrentibus}, V, \emph{De defectibus formæ}, printed p.~LXVII (artifact p.~69), collated visually 25 July 2026. The same paragraph continues: ``Si quis autem aliquid diminueret, vel immutaret de forma Consecrationis Corporis et Sanguinis, et in ipsa verborum immutatione verba idem non significarent, non conficeret Sacramentum. Si vero aliquid adderet vel detraheret, quod significationem non mutaret, conficeret quidem, sed gravissime peccaret.''} On the older book's own account, therefore, \emph{mysterium fidei} belonged to the form, and a priest who omitted it would be subtracting from what that book calls the form of the sacrament.

Two things follow, and they must be kept apart. The first is that the defence just described fails on the older book's own terms, and should be abandoned. The second is that nothing about the validity of the newer rite follows from that, because the case in view is not a priest's private subtraction but a determination of the form by the authority that determines it---and the 1962 book's own paragraph anticipates the distinction it turns on, holding that a subtraction which does not change the signification leaves the sacrament confected, though gravely sinfully if done by private authority. Trent itself declares that ``this power has always been in the Church, that in the dispensation of the sacraments, their substance being preserved, she might determine or change whatever she judged more expedient for the benefit of those receiving them''---which is precisely the power at issue.\endnote{Council of Trent, session XXI (16 July 1562), chapter II, \emph{Ecclesiæ potestas circa dispensationem sacramenti eucharistiæ}: ``Præterea declarat, hanc potestatem perpetuo in ecclesia fuisse, ut in sacramentorum dispensatione, salva illorum substantia, ea statueret vel mutaret, quæ suscipientium utilitati \dots{} magis expedire iudicaret.'' Read at the page image of the Tauchnitz 1887 edition, printed p.~108, Internet Archive item \texttt{canonesetdecreta00coun\_0}, leaf n115, retrieved and hashed 25 July 2026; verified visually. The chapter is about Communion under one kind and the withholding of the chalice, and it is cited here for the principle it states, not for its immediate subject matter.} Whether \emph{mysterium fidei} belongs to the substance is exactly the question the 1962 paragraph does not answer and the 1969 promulgation answers in the negative by legislating.

Beyond that, the phrase is not deleted from the rite: it is promoted to the position of a versicle inviting the people's acclamation, and the acclamations themselves are scriptural. Whether that is a recovery of the phrase's original function---it is not a scriptural phrase, and its origin within the formula is genuinely obscure---or the loss of a confession embedded in the consecratory words is a serious question with real scholarship on both sides, and this study does not pretend to settle it. What it can say is that the phrase's disappearance from the formula and its reappearance eight lines later are one operation, not two, and that any account which mentions the first without the second is not describing the book.

The addition of \emph{quod pro vobis tradetur} runs in the opposite direction from what the popular account of the reform predicts: the reformed formula over the bread is \emph{more} explicit about the offering of the body ``for you'' than the older one. And on the phrase around which most heat has gathered---\emph{pro multis}---the two Latin books read identically. The vernacular rendering ``for all,'' which occupied four decades of controversy in English-speaking countries, was a translation, never a variant of the typical edition; it was corrected in the English Missal of 2011. A comparison of the two Missals as Latin books finds no difference here at all.\endnote{1962, \emph{Ordo Missæ} n.~1104, printed p.~307, collated visually: ``qui pro vobis et pro multis effundetur in remissionem peccatorum.'' 2002, \emph{Ordo Missæ} n.~90, artifact p.~353: ``qui pro vobis et pro multis effundetur in remissionem peccatorum.'' The identical wording recurs in Eucharistic Prayers II, III and IV.}

\textbf{The ritual gestures.} This is the largest non-textual change in the Canon, and the least often quantified; the deepest change in the words themselves is the one just described. The 1962 Canon prescribes twenty-five signs of the cross made by the priest over the offerings, at eight places: three at \emph{hæc dona, hæc munera, hæc sancta sacrificia illibata}; five within \emph{Quam oblationem} (three over both species at \emph{benedictam, adscriptam, ratam}, one over the host at \emph{Corpus}, one over the chalice at \emph{Sanguis}); one over the host and one over the chalice at \emph{benedixit} in the two institution narratives; five within \emph{Unde et memores} on the same pattern as \emph{Quam oblationem}; two in \emph{Supplices}, at \emph{Corpus} and \emph{Sanguinem}, with a further sign the priest makes upon himself; three at \emph{sanctificas, vivificas, benedicis} in \emph{Per quem hæc omnia}; and five at the doxology, three made with the host across the chalice and two between the host and the chalice. In the postconciliar Canon exactly one remains, at \emph{benedicas} in \emph{Te igitur}. The kiss of the altar within \emph{Supplices} is gone; the double genuflection at each elevation is reduced to one; the joining of thumb and forefinger is not prescribed.\endnote{The 1962 gestural rubrics are printed within the Canon text itself at printed pp.~300--312 (artifact pp.~381--393); the postconciliar rubrics at \emph{Ordo Missæ} nn.~84--98, artifact pp.~347--354. The count above is of prescribed signs of the cross over the offerings and does not include the priest's signing of himself or the blessings outside the Canon.}

No word of the Canon changed here. What changed is the ceremonial grammar in which the words are set: a rite that punctuated almost every naming of the offering with a blessing gesture became a rite that names the offering and does not gesture. It is possible to argue that the gestures were a medieval accretion whose removal restores the prayer's shape; it is possible to argue that they were the rite's way of saying with the body what the words say, and that their removal is a loss no textual identity compensates. What is not possible is to describe the postconciliar Canon as ``the same prayer'' without noticing that its performance was substantially rewritten.

\textbf{The brackets.} The current edition places in parentheses the second and third groups of saints in \emph{Communicantes} (from \emph{Iacobi, Ioannis} through \emph{Cosmæ et Damiani}), the corresponding group in \emph{Nobis quoque} (from \emph{Ignatio, Alexandro} through \emph{Anastasia}), and the concluding \emph{Per Christum Dominum nostrum. Amen} of \emph{Communicantes}, \emph{Hanc igitur}, \emph{Supplices} and \emph{Memento etiam}. A rubric before the Canon states that what is enclosed in parentheses may be omitted.\endnote{\emph{Missale Romanum}, editio typica tertia, \emph{Ordo Missæ} n.~32, artifact p.~310: ``In Prece eucharistica prima, seu Canone romano, ea quæ inter parentheses includuntur omitti possunt.'' The bracketed material is printed as such at nn.~86, 87, 94, 95 and 96.} This is a genuine and permanent alteration of the Canon's shape, since a prayer that may be shortened is a different object from a prayer that may not; and it is the change that most directly touches Trent's canon VI, which is about the Canon's integrity rather than its words. It is also, in the reform's own logic, minor: the abbreviation is optional, the saints remain printed in the book, and nothing doctrinal turns on the list.

\subsection{The three added Prayers}

Paul VI's constitution names the addition of three Eucharistic Prayers as one of the reform's principal acts and states that the words of consecration are the same in all of them. Both statements are verifiable in the book: Prayers II, III and IV each carry the institution narrative in the same words as the Roman Canon, differing only in the narrative frame that introduces it.\endnote{\emph{Missale Romanum}, editio typica tertia, \emph{Ordo Missæ} nn.~102--103 (Prex II), 110--111 (Prex III), 117--118 (Prex IV), artifact pp.~355--366. The introductory clauses differ---``Qui cum Passioni voluntarie traderetur,'' ``Ipse enim in qua nocte tradebatur,'' and the corresponding form in Prayer IV---but the formulas over bread and chalice are identical across the four.}

The four prayers are not four versions of one thing. They are of different literary types, and the differences are structural.

\begin{ledgertable}{Prayer}{Shape and character}
I (Roman Canon) & Intercession before and after the consecration; no distinct invocation of the Holy Spirit upon the gifts; the consecratory petition is \emph{Quam oblationem}, which asks God to make the offering \emph{benedictam, adscriptam, ratam, rationabilem, acceptabilemque}; \emph{Supplices} asks that the offering be borne to the heavenly altar by the hand of an angel. Proper \emph{Communicantes} and \emph{Hanc igitur} for the great seasons\\
II & The shortest; carries its own preface but may be used with others; a distinct epiclesis over the gifts (\emph{Hæc ergo dona, quæsumus, Spiritus tui rore sanctifica}) and a second invocation after the anamnesis asking that communicants be gathered into one by the Holy Spirit\\
III & Newly composed; usable with any preface; opens from Mal 1:11 (\emph{ut a solis ortu usque ad occasum oblatio munda offeratur nomini tuo}); a consecratory epiclesis and a communion epiclesis; the anamnesis explicitly offers \emph{hoc sacrificium vivum et sanctum} and asks the Father, ``recognising the Victim by whose immolation you willed to be reconciled,'' to grant that the communicants become one body and one spirit in Christ\\
IV & A fixed and unchangeable preface, because the prayer's structure is a summary of salvation history; may be used when the Mass has no proper preface and on Sundays in Ordinary Time; the anamnesis offers ``his Body and Blood, the sacrifice acceptable to you and bringing salvation to the whole world''\\
\end{ledgertable}

The most consequential structural difference is the epiclesis. The Roman Canon has no explicit invocation of the Holy Spirit upon the gifts; the three added prayers have one, and each has a second invocation after the anamnesis directed to the unity of the communicants. That is a real change in the Latin rite's euchological repertoire, and it is a deliberate importation of a shape more familiar from Eastern anaphoras. It can be defended as a recovery of the Church's wider tradition; it can be criticised as importing into the Roman Rite a theological structure the Roman Rite had never had. Both are arguments about the books, and both are available from the books.

\subsection{The prefaces}

One further difference belongs here because the preface is part of the Eucharistic Prayer and because it is measurable.

The 1962 Missal prints fifteen prefaces in its Order of Mass, each set out in a solemn and---except for the Chrism Mass---a ferial tone, together with a preface for the dead: Nativity, Epiphany, Lent, the Holy Cross, the Chrism Mass, Easter, the Ascension, the Sacred Heart, Christ the King, the Holy Spirit, the Most Holy Trinity, the Blessed Virgin Mary, Saint Joseph, the Apostles, and the Common.\endnote{\emph{Missale Romanum} (1962), \emph{Præfationes}, printed pp.~223--298 (artifact pp.~304--379), enumerated from the running heads of the solemn and ferial series and the \emph{Præfatio defunctorum}; read at the artifact's text layer. The Common preface serves every Mass without a proper one.} The third typical edition prints fifty in the corresponding section of its Order of Mass, before the Eucharistic Prayers, and prints further proper prefaces within particular formularies.\endnote{\emph{Missale Romanum}, editio typica tertia, \emph{Præfationes}, artifact pp.~311--346, counted by heading: two of Advent, three of the Nativity, one of the Epiphany, four of Lent, two of the Passion, five paschal, two of the Ascension, eight for Sundays in Ordinary Time, two of the Most Holy Eucharist, two of the Blessed Virgin Mary, two of the Apostles, two of the Saints, two of Martyrs, one of holy Pastors, one of Virgins and Religious, six Common, and five of the Dead. Prefaces printed with their own formularies elsewhere in the book---for the Trinity, the Sacred Heart, Christ the King, the Chrism Mass and others---are additional to this count, so fifty is a floor and not the book's total.}

The change is therefore large in one direction and worth being precise about. The older book handles the year with a small, memorable and heavily reused repertory: the Common preface serves the great majority of days, and any congregation attending Sunday Mass hears the same handful of texts through the year. The newer book handles it with a wide repertory in which nearly every season and category of saint has its own, and the eight prefaces for Sundays in Ordinary Time replace the older book's single Common on those days. Whichever way one values it, this is one of the reform's clearest reversals of direction: at the offertory and in the Canon's gestures it subtracted, and here it multiplied.

\subsection{The claim that does not survive: ``the new Prayers removed propitiation''}

It is frequently said that the three added prayers replaced the Roman Canon's sacrificial and propitiatory register with a register of thanksgiving and communion. The texts do not bear this. Prayer III asks the Father to ``look upon the oblation of your Church'' and, ``recognising the Victim by whose immolation you willed to be reconciled,'' to grant the fruits of communion, and it goes on to ask that ``this Sacrifice of our reconciliation advance the peace and salvation of all the world''---language whose propitiatory content is explicit and whose vocabulary (\emph{Hostia}, \emph{immolatio}, \emph{placari}, \emph{reconciliatio}) is precisely the vocabulary of Trent's session XXII.\endnote{\emph{Missale Romanum}, editio typica tertia, \emph{Ordo Missæ} n.~113, artifact p.~358: ``offerimus tibi, gratias referentes, hoc sacrificium vivum et sanctum. Respice, quæsumus, in oblationem Ecclesiæ tuæ et, agnoscens Hostiam, cuius voluisti immolatione placari \dots{} Hæc Hostia nostræ reconciliationis proficiat, quæsumus, Domine, ad totius mundi pacem atque salutem.'' Prayer IV, n.~122, artifact p.~365: ``offerimus tibi eius Corpus et Sanguinem, sacrificium tibi acceptabile et toti mundo salutare.''} Prayer IV offers the Body and Blood as ``a sacrifice acceptable to you and bringing salvation to the whole world.'' Prayer II, the shortest, offers ``the bread of life and the chalice of salvation.''

The point is not merely that the words are there. The current \emph{Institutio generalis} cites Prayers III and IV by number, in its opening articles, precisely as the places where the Missal's own \emph{lex orandi} expresses the doctrine Trent defined: the priest, it says, ``offers a living and holy sacrifice, namely the Church's offering and the victim by whose immolation God willed to be appeased''---with a footnote to Prayer III---``and prays that the Body and Blood of Christ may be a sacrifice acceptable to the Father and salutary for the whole world''---with a footnote to Prayer IV.\endnote{\emph{Institutio Generalis Missalis Romani} (2002), \emph{Proœmium} n.~2, artifact p.~8: ``\dots{} ei gratias persolvit et sacrificium offert vivum et sanctum, oblationem scilicet Ecclesiæ et hostiam, cuius immolatione ipse Deus voluit placari, atque orat, ut Corpus et Sanguis Christi sint Patri sacrificium acceptabile et toti mundo salutare,'' with notes 4 and 5 citing \emph{Prex eucharistica III} and \emph{IV}. The same article opens by naming Trent's session XXII as the solemn assertion of the Mass's sacrificial nature and quotes \emph{Sacrosanctum Concilium} n.~47.} The new prayers are not an evasion of Trent; the reform's own instruction offers them as its proof text.
