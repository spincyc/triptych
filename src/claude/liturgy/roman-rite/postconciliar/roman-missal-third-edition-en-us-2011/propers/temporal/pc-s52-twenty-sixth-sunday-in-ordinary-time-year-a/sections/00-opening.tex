\section{Which of the Two Did the Father's Will?}
\label{sec:opening}

At the centre of this Sunday's Mass stands a question that Jesus puts to the
chief priests and the ancients of the people in the temple, and that they
answer against themselves. ``A certain man had two sons: and coming to the
first, he said: Son, go work to day in my vineyard. And he answering, said: I
will not. But afterwards, being moved with repentance, he went. And coming to
the other, he said in like manner. And he answering said: I go, Sir. And he
went not. Which of the two did the father's will?'' They say, ``The first.''
Then comes the sentence the question was built to carry: ``Amen I say to you
that the publicans and the harlots shall go into the kingdom of God before
you. For John came to you in the way of justice: and you did not believe him.
But the publicans and the harlots believed him: but you, seeing it, did not
even afterwards repent, that you might believe him'' (Mt 21:28--32).

{\small\noindent Scripture is quoted throughout in the Douay--Rheims version as
revised by Bishop Challoner, a public-domain study translation made from the
Latin Vulgate. It is not the text proclaimed at Mass: the English heard in the
celebration is that of the \work{Lectionary for Mass for Use in the Dioceses of
the United States of America}, which alone is the approved wording. Psalms are
numbered as the Lectionary numbers them, with the Vulgate number, which the
Douay--Rheims and the Latin books use, in parentheses.\par}
\medskip

The first reading has already put the same question in the language of a
prophet. The exiles of Ezekiel's day complain, ``The way of the Lord is not
right'', and the Lord answers with two cases: the just man who turns away from
his justice and commits iniquity dies in it, and the wicked man who
``turneth himself away from his wickedness \ldots\ shall save his soul alive''
(Ezek 18:25--28). The psalm asks to be shown the Lord's ways and begs him to
remember his compassion and not the sins of youth. Paul asks the Philippians
to be of one mind and to ``esteem others better than themselves''; in the
longer form of the reading he sets before them Christ, who ``emptied himself''
and ``humbled himself, becoming obedient unto death, even to the death of the
cross'', and whom God exalted. Before the Gospel the assembly sings the Good
Shepherd's word, ``My sheep hear my voice''. The Missal's own texts, which
serve this Sunday in all three years of the Lectionary's cycle, set a
confession, three prayers and a pair of Communion antiphons around the
readings. The Entrance Antiphon, made from the prayer of Azarias in the
furnace, confesses that all God has brought upon his people he did in true
judgment, because they sinned. The Collect finds God's almighty power shown
above all in sparing and in mercy. The Prayer over the Offerings asks that the
offering be accepted and open the spring of every blessing, and the Prayer
after Communion that the heavenly mystery repair mind and body. At Communion
the Missal offers either the psalmist's plea that God remember the word in
which he gave hope, or John's sentence, ``In this we have known the charity of
God, because he hath laid down his life for us: and we ought to lay down our
lives for the brethren.''

The \work{Ordo lectionum Missae} marks what it singles out in each reading by
the title it sets over it. Over the first reading stands \latin{Cum averterit se
impius ab impietate sua, animam suam vivificabit}, a condensed form of Ezekiel
18:27; over the Gospel, \latin{Paenitentia motus abiit. Publicani et meretrices
praecedunt vos in regnum Dei}, drawn from Matthew 21:29 and 31; over the second
reading, \latin{Sentite in vobis quod et in Christo Iesu}, from Philippians 2:5.
The introduction
to the \work{Ordo} explains that the relation between the readings of one Mass
is shown by the choice of these titles, and that in Ordinary Time the Old
Testament reading is chosen for its relation to the Gospel while the Apostle
and the Gospel are each read in their own course (\work{Praenotanda} 105--107).
The two titles that belong together both fall on repentance: the wicked man
who turns and lives, and the son who was moved by repentance and went. The
letter to the Philippians is read over four Sundays of Year~A, and this is the
second.

The Sunday's question is therefore what God judges when he judges a man: the
first word or the last deed, the record behind him or the way he is found
walking; and, bound up with it, who the two sons are. The Fathers and Doctors
who expounded these passages answer along three lines, and each line, carried
through every text of the Mass, yields an interpretation of the whole. The
first hears in the parable and the prophecy one law of judgment: God judges
each person where he is found, so that no sinner may despair of turning and no
just man may rest on his past. The second follows Jerome, Chrysostom and
Aquinas in reading the two sons as two peoples, the nations who refused and
afterwards believed and the people who promised at Sinai and did not go; it
keeps the parable's own addressees, the chief priests and ancients who would
not believe John, and it meets Hilary of Poitiers, who reads the sons the other
way round. The third begins from the second reading. Christ is the Son who
obeyed the Father unto death, and the disciple's humility and charity take
their pattern and their power from him; where the shorter form is read, only
Paul's appeal and the name of Christ's mind remain of it. None of the witnesses
behind these interpretations expounds this Mass as a whole. Each
interpretation gives every appointed element a place and closes with four
senses of its own, literal, allegorical, moral and anagogical.

\subsection{The appointed elements at a glance}

\begin{maptable}
Entrance Antiphon & \latin{Omnia, quae fecisti nobis, Domine}; Dan 3:31, 29,
30, 43, 42, a composite adaptation of the prayer of Azarias &
All that the Lord has done to his people he did in true judgment, because they
sinned against him and did not obey his commands; he is asked to give glory to
his name and to deal with them according to the abundance of his mercy.\\
Collect & \latin{Deus, qui omnipotentiam tuam} &
God shows his omnipotence above all in sparing and in mercy; his grace is asked
for those who run toward his promises, that they may share the goods of
heaven.\\
First Reading & Ezek 18:25--28 &
To the complaint that the Lord's way is not right, the Lord answers that the
just man who turns to iniquity dies and the wicked man who turns to judgment
and justice lives.\\
Responsorial Psalm & Ps 25 (24):4--5, 6--7, 8--9; response from v.~6a &
A prayer to be shown God's ways and taught his paths; a plea that he remember
his compassion and forget the sins of youth; the Lord, sweet and righteous,
gives a law to sinners in the way and teaches the meek.\\
Second Reading, longer form & Phil 2:1--11 &
One mind, humility, each esteeming the others better; the mind of Christ Jesus,
who emptied himself, obeyed unto the death of the cross, and was given the name
above all names.\\
Second Reading, shorter form & Phil 2:1--5 &
The same appeal, ending with the call to have the mind that was in Christ
Jesus.\\
Alleluia verse & Jn 10:27, with a speaker formula added &
``My sheep hear my voice. And I know them: and they follow me.''\\
Gospel & Mt 21:28--32 &
The son who refused and afterwards went, and the son who promised and did not;
the publicans and harlots who believed John go before those who did not
afterwards repent.\\
Prayer over the Offerings & \latin{Concede nobis, misericors Deus} &
A petition for the offering's acceptance, and for the opening through it of
the fountain from which every blessing flows.\\
Communion Antiphon, first option & \latin{Memento verbi tui servo tuo,
Domine}; cf.\ Ps 119 (118):49--50, an adaptation &
The servant asks God to remember the word in which he gave him hope, his
comfort in his lowliness.\\
Communion Antiphon, second option & \latin{In hoc cognovimus caritatem Dei};
1~Jn 3:16 &
God's charity is known in this, that he laid down his life for us; so we
ought to lay down our lives for the brethren.\\
Prayer after Communion & \latin{Sit nobis, Domine, reparatio mentis et
corporis} &
A petition that the mystery received from heaven repair mind and body; it
joins the proclaiming of the Lord's death and the sharing of his sufferings to
a share in his glory as fellow heirs.\\
\end{maptable}

\noindent The two forms of the second reading are alternatives, and so are the
two Communion antiphons: the Missal prints the second under \latin{Vel}, and
one of them is used. Every other element is required. The formulary has no
Sequence, Offertory antiphon, proper Preface, prayer over the people or solemn
blessing, and the Lectionary gives no shorter form of the Gospel.
