**Textual observations, not claims of design.** Several strands of vocabulary
recur across the appointed units in the Clementine and the Douay–Rheims.
*Calling*: the servants call the invited (*vocare invitatos*, Matthew 22:3),
call whomever they find (*vocate*, 22:9) and many are called (*vocati*,
22:14); the acclamation ends on the hope of our calling (*spes vocationis
nostrae*). *Feast, fullness and hunger*: the feast of fat things (Isaiah 25:6);
the table and the cup (Psalm 22:5); being full and hungry (*satiari et
esurire*, Philippians 4:12); the rich who hungered (*esurierunt*, Psalm 33:11);
the dinner and the fatlings (Matthew 22:4); the Prayer after Communion's feeding
(*pascis alimento*). *Riches and glory*: riches in glory (Philippians 4:19), the
riches of the glory of his inheritance (Ephesians 1:18), the rich who want
(Psalm 33:11), heavenly glory (*ad caelestem gloriam*, Prayer over the
Offerings). *Clothing and death swallowed*: the guest not clothed in the wedding
garment (*vestitum*, Matthew 22:11) and, outside the appointed texts, the mortal
that puts on immortality when death is swallowed up (1 Corinthians 15:53–54,
which quotes Isaiah 25:8). *Seeing*: the king came in to see the guests
(*ut videret*, Matthew 22:11); we shall see him as he is (1 John 3:2); Lo, this
is our God (Isaiah 25:9). *Mercy going before and following*: the Collect asks
that grace go before and follow (*praeveniat et sequatur*); the psalm says
mercy will follow (*subsequetur*, Psalm 22:6). No source consulted says any
unit was chosen for another; these are the leaf's observations. Only the first
reading and the Gospel are officially correlated (`propers/verified.md`,
Relationship classes).

**Who are the first invited, and what is the burnt city?** Irenaeus, Hilary,
Chrysostom, Jerome and Aquinas identify the first invited with the people of
the former dispensation, Israel; Jerome and Aquinas give that reading as one of
two for the armies. Chrysostom and, as an alternative, Jerome and Aquinas read
the burning of the city as the destruction of Jerusalem under Vespasian and
Titus; Gregory and Hilary read it as the eternal punishment of the persecutors
by angelic armies, and Jerome and Aquinas offer that too. This is a real but
narrow difference about v. 7; neither reading is the parable's plain sense, and
the literal addressees are the chief priests and the elders of the people
(21:23, 45; the *Ordo*'s incipit). Section 3.11 records the magisterial bound on
presenting the identification.

**What is the wedding garment?** A real disagreement, to be preserved. Hilary:
the glory of the Holy Spirit and the whiteness of the heavenly habit, "qui
bonae interrogationis confessione susceptus", received at the confession of
the good questioning, and kept unstained. Hilary does not name baptism in
*Comm. in Matt.* 22.3–7; that his phrase alludes to 1 Peter 3:21, where the
Clementine calls baptism *conscientiae bonae interrogatio in Deum*, and so
places the garment's reception at baptism, is this leaf's reading of the
allusion, a sound inference but not his words. Cyril's *Procatechesis* applies
the garment to candidates for baptism. Chrysostom: "life
and practice", the clean garment received by grace and kept by diligence.
Irenaeus: works of righteousness on which the Spirit rests. Jerome: the Lord's
precepts and the works of law and Gospel that make the new man's garment.
Augustine and Gregory: charity, and expressly **not** baptism or faith, which
the bad share. Aquinas gathers these as putting on Christ by sacrament, by
charity, by remembrance of death and by works, "et si unum deficiat, malum".
The positions differ in what they name; all agree that the garment is required
of those already gathered by the call.

**Is the wedding the present Church or the last banquet?** Gregory: the
present Church, where bad and good mingle, against Luke's supper, the eternal
banquet; Aquinas follows him while giving also the wedding "in communi
resurrectione". Hilary: the wedding is the sacrament of eternal glory to be
received in the resurrection. Augustine: two feasts, the present one of good
and bad and the other of the good alone. All who comment on the inspection
place it at the judgment (Jerome, Gregory, Hilary, Augustine, Aquinas). A
complementary difference of emphasis, which the readings must not flatten.

**How few are chosen?** Augustine: the few are few only beside the bad, many in
themselves. Hilary: "non est paucitas in invitatis, sed raritas in electis",
because the call is without exception and the choice turns on the merit of the
called. Gregory: no one knows whether he is chosen, so all must be humble and
rejoice only in God's mercy. Aquinas: some will not come, some lack the
garment. Complementary.

on the psalm illuminates. Augustine's *De natura et gratia* is quoted only as
Aquinas quotes it; the work was not opened. Cassiodorus and Theodoret on Psalm
22 were not opened (section 4).

**Why it is recorded.** Irenaeus, Hilary, Chrysostom, Jerome and Aquinas
identify the first invited with Israel; Chrysostom says the parable proclaims
"the casting out of the Jews" and calls the murderers "incurably diseased",
Aquinas heads it "de reprobatione Iudaeorum", and Jerome, on Isaiah 25:6, writes
"nequaquam populo Iudaeorum, sed omnibus gentibus". Two of this Sunday's three
readings and the parish homily will draw on that history. The Declaration is
the checked bound on how the identification is presented and applied, and its
*vocatio sine paenitentia* bears directly on a parable about a call.

**Its limit.** It governs presentation and application, not exegesis. It does
not say what the parable means, passes no judgment on any Father's reading, and
gives no licence to soften or omit what a Father says. What it requires here is
this. The identification is reported as the reading of the Fathers who make
it, never in the leaf's own voice. The parable's literal addressees stay the
chief priests and elders (21:23, 45). Gregory's application, which is to his
own hearers ("Nos sumus … qui in nuptiis Verbi discumbimus"), and
Chrysostom's ("Hear ye, as many as having partaken of the mysteries") direct
the moral sense to the baptized. Nothing in the studies or the homily may
present the Jewish people, then or now, as charged with the Passion, as
rejected or accursed by God, or as having had their call revoked. Only short
phrases of the restricted English delivery are quoted.
