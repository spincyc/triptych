**Textual observations, not claims of design.** Several strands of vocabulary
recur across the appointed units in the Clementine, the *Ordo* and the
Missal. *Serving and rendering*: the Collect asks to serve (*servire*) God's
majesty with a sincere heart and to bear a devoted will; the Prayer over the
Offerings asks to serve God's gifts with a free mind (*libera mente
servire*) and to be cleansed by the mysteries we serve (*famulamur*); the
Gospel commands rendering (*reddite*, ἀπόδοτε); the psalm bids the families
of the nations bring (*afferte*) glory to the Lord and, in v. 8, bring
sacrifices (*tollite hostias*, not sung); Mark 10:45, in its context,
contrasts being served with serving (the antiphon omits the contrast).
*Image, likeness and inscription*: the coin's image and inscription (Matthew
22:20); in the Clementine of Isaiah 45:4 the Lord has "made a likeness of"
Cyrus (*assimilavi te*) and called him by name. *The nations and their
rulers*: the nations subdued before Cyrus and the backs of kings turned
(Isaiah 45:1); the gods of the nations (Psalm 95:5) and the Lord who reigns
among them (95:10a); Caesar (Matthew 22:17–21); the rulers of the Gentiles
(Mark 10:42, context); the crooked generation among whom the faithful shine
(Philippians 2:15); the king not saved by his army (Psalm 32:16, context).
*Knowing*: Cyrus has not known the Lord (Isaiah 45:4–5); the Thessalonians'
election is known (1 Thessalonians 1:4); the questioners say "we know"
(Matthew 22:16) and Jesus knows their wickedness (22:18); that all may know
there is none besides the Lord (Isaiah 45:6). *Seeing and the eye*: the
apple of the eye (Psalm 16:8); the eyes of the Lord upon those who fear him
(Psalm 32:18). *Sincerity and its opposite*: a sincere heart (Collect,
*sincero corde*); lips not deceitful (Psalm 16:1, context); simple children
of God (Philippians 2:15, context of the acclamation); hypocrites (Matthew
22:18). *Ransom*: Cyrus lets the captives go "not for ransom" (Isaiah 45:13,
context) and the Son of Man gives his life "a redemption for many" (Mark
10:45). *The rising of the sun*: Isaiah 45:6 *ab ortu solis*. No source
consulted says any unit was chosen for another; these are the leaf's
observations. Only the first reading and the Gospel are officially correlated
(`propers/verified.md`, Relationship classes). Acts 17:7 is not appointed and
is cited only as the narrative context of the second reading's church.

*e*-caudata is written *ae*. Square brackets inside a quotation are the printed
edition's own.

Roles: **direct** = the author is expounding this passage; **parallel** = the
author is expounding the same saying in another Gospel; **doctrinal use** =
the author uses the verse in another argument; **commentator** = a liturgical
commentator on the element as his Mass gave it; **history** = the history of
an element's text; **lead** = located, not relied on. Tier: **A** = ancient,
to Gregory the Great; **L** = medieval, Doctoral or later saintly. No witness
was collated against a modern critical text.

**What are "the things that are God's"?** A real difference, to be
preserved. Jerome reads the saying literally: to Caesar the coin, to God
"decimas primitias, et oblationes, ac victimas", and Christ himself rendered
to God by doing the Father's will. Hilary: body, soul and will, because from
God we hold their origin and increase, so that men render themselves whole.
Augustine (five places) and Ambrose: the image; as Caesar seeks his image on
the coin, God seeks his in man, so "to God yourselves". Augustine introduces
the image reading in *En. in Ps.* 4.8 as what "some understand skilfully" and
makes it his own elsewhere; Aquinas gives Jerome's kind of literal reading
(natural goods such as bread and wine to God, coin to Caesar) and the image
reading as the mystical sense. Cyril (supporting): God asks nothing
corruptible but obedience, faith, love and good works. The positions differ
in what they name and agree that God's claim is not on coin.

**Is Caesar's claim a duty or a concession to those who keep the world's
goods?** A real difference. Chrysostom: rendering is a debt ("not to give but
to render"), both claims can be met, and Caesar's claim ends where godliness
would be harmed. Augustine: Christians render due service to kings, and in
Christ's cause know only the heavenly master. Aquinas: "Vos estis Dei, et
Caesaris". Hilary and Ambrose: the obligation rests on holding what is
Caesar's; whoever keeps nothing of his is not bound, and whoever would owe the
earthly king nothing should leave all and follow Christ. The difference is
in the ground of the debt, not in whether tribute is lawful.

**Why are they hypocrites, when what they say is true?** Agreement by
distinct reasoning. Chrysostom: true words "not with an upright mind";
Jerome: one thing in act, another in voice; Aquinas: one thing in the mouth,
another in the heart, flattery being where the wicked begin; Augustine: "in
one way ye speak, in another way judge". Aquinas adds that they lied
"secundum cor suum", since they held him not a master but a deceiver
(p. 283, Matthew 27:63); Chrysostom that their praise contradicts their own
earlier words ("How said ye then, 'He is a deceiver'").

**Does the counter-question withhold an answer?** Convergence, by distinct
reasoning; no witness read says it does. Augustine, *De serm. Dom.*
II.20.70: the questioners, asked whose image the coin bore, "gave a kind of
answer to themselves … and accordingly from their answer He drew this
inference, Render therefore …", a case he sets apart from the question of
authority, where an answer was withheld. Augustine, *En. in Ps.* 57 §8: Christ
did not say "It is lawful" or "It is not lawful", but "compelled them with
their mouth against themselves to give sentence". Chrysostom, *Hom.* 70.2:
"by their tongue He brings out the decision, and causes them to decide, that
it is lawful … to cause them to be bound by their own answers", and, in 70.1,
the authority question as the one that "did not meet with an answer".
Jerome on v. 19–20: tempters are refuted "suis potissimum … sermonibus", and
Jesus asks not from ignorance but "ut ad sermonem eorum competenter
respondeat". Aquinas, pp. 283–284: no better refutation than "secundum verba
sua … ex responsione elicit veritatem", the question being "ex
dispensatione". All five hold that the counter-question is the means of the
answer, drawn from the questioners' own mouths; the studies may not present
any of them as reading the scene as an evasion.

**Is Christ's knowledge of their mind a sign of his Godhead?** A
complementary difference with a real consequence. Chrysostom's reasoning
(*Hom.* 70.1, tracked lines 39563–39568) is that the questioners' words were
true and respectful and concealed their intent: "And yet their words were
full of much respect, for they both called Him Master, and bore witness to
His truth, and that He was no respecter of persons; but being God, He was
deceived by none of these things. Wherefore they also ought to have
conjectured, that the rebuke was not the result of conjecture, but a sign of
His knowing their secret thoughts". Because nothing in their words betrayed
them, the rebuke could not be a guess; it was knowledge of secret thoughts,
and that is why "He gave them proof of His Godhead, by revealing the secrets
of their hearts" (70.2, lines 39596–39599). Two sentences of the same section
are bounded here so that they are not misread. "Since their wickedness was
now complete and manifest" (line 39557) explains the severity of the rebuke,
which he says was given "by publishing their secret thoughts, and making it
manifest to all with what kind of intent they are coming" (lines
39558–39560): the same sentence has the rebuke make their intent manifest to
all, so it does not treat the intent of this question as public beforehand.
And his note, after Luke, that "they asked also in the presence of the
multitude, so that the testimony should be the stronger" (lines
39512–39514) concerns the questioners' wish for witnesses; he draws from it
only that "in a larger body of spectators they afforded the demonstration of
their folly" (lines 39516–39517), not that the plot could be guessed.
Matthew 22:15 says only that the Pharisees "consulted among themselves how
to insnare him in his speech" (Douay; Clementine *consilium inierunt ut
caperent eum in sermone*); it does not say the consultation was open, and no
study may say so. Hilary (23.1): he knows "interna cogitationum secreta"
because God sees all that is hidden within men; Aquinas (p. 283): "hominis
est respondere ad verba, Dei autem respondere ad mentem", Christ answering
both as God and man. Jerome (col. 163) states the same discernment as a rule
for whoever answers, "Prima virtus est respondentis, interrogantium mentem
cognoscere", and draws no christological conclusion from it. The objection
that the scene shows only a wise respondent's discernment is drawn from
Jerome's rule and, if wanted, from the publicity of the questioning (the
crowd of Chrysostom's note); it is the leaf's statement of the difficulty,
and no witness read argues it. The christological claim therefore rests on
Chrysostom, Hilary and Aquinas, not on Jerome, whose sentence carries the
moral sense of discernment (with Aquinas's "non debemus adulatoribus
credere").
