\runninghead{Afterward He Repented}
\properlane{turning}{Afterward He Repented: Each Is Judged Where He Is Found}
\label{sec:afterward-he-repented}

Heard as a word about every person, the Sunday's first reading and Gospel state
one law of God's judgment. God judges a man as he finds him when his course
has been turned, and not by his first word or his former record. The wicked
man who turns from his wickedness lives, and the just man who turns from his
justice dies; the son who said ``I will not'' and afterwards went did his
father's will, and the son who said ``I go, Sir'' and did not go did not. Two
consequences follow, and the witnesses of this interpretation press both: no
one may despair of turning, and no one may rest on what he has done. Turning
is urgent because it is always open, and it is never finished in this life.

\subsection{Each as he is found}

Jerome's commentary on Ezekiel states the principle as the rule of the whole
chapter. Of the wicked man who afterwards does penance and, turned to better
things, blots out his former sins, he says that he \latin{non iudicetur
vetustate peccati: sed in meum suscipiatur gregem, innovatione virtutis}: let
him not be judged by the oldness of his sin, but be received into my flock by
the newness of virtue. On the just man who falls away he gives the rule both
its edges: \latin{Sicut iustum antea peccatorem non praegravant antiqua
delicta: sic peccatorem qui prius iustus fuerit, non iuvant veteres
iustitiae. Unusquisque enim in quo invenietur, in eo iudicabitur}. As old
offences do not weigh on the just man who was once a sinner, so old justices do
not help the sinner who was once just; for each will be judged in that in which
he is found. To the complaint of v.~25 the Lord, as Jerome reads him, gives the
reason why his way is equal: \latin{Ecce unusquisque in suo peccato moritur, et
in sua iustitia vivificatur: in utroque non praeterita, sed praesentia
[iudicatur]}, each dies in his sin and is given life in his justice, and in
both it is not what is past but what is present that is judged (\work{In
Hiezechielem} VI, on 18:21--25, PL 25, 180; the bracketed word is the editor's
supplement).

The people's complaint and the Lord's answer thus turn on the same word.
\latin{Non est aequa via Domini}: the Lord's way is not level, they say,
because it does not keep to the past, their own or their fathers'. The Lord
answers that his way is level precisely because it is the same in both
directions. The wicked man's record is not held against him once he has
turned, and the just man's record does not shelter him once he has turned
away. Jerome adds a sentence, on v.~23, that governs how every severe word of
the chapter is heard: \latin{Ubicumque autem Dei videtur severa et truculenta
sententia, non homines, sed peccata condemnat}, wherever God's sentence seems
severe and harsh, it condemns not men but sins (PL 25, 180). The chapter's
question, ``Is it my will that a sinner should die \ldots\ and not that he
should be converted from his ways, and live?'' (18:23), is the reason the door
stands open at all.

\subsection{Not even afterwards}

The parable says the same thing as a story, and its application sharpens it.
The chief priests are not blamed for having once refused John; they are blamed
because, having seen the publicans and harlots believe him, they ``did not even
afterwards repent''. John Chrysostom makes this the point of the saying: ``For
an evil thing it is not at the first to choose the good, but it is a heavier
charge not even to be brought round.'' The first refusal is a fault; the
refusal to be turned is worse. Nor does the precedence of the publicans reward
their sins: ``not, as continuing harlots, did they enter in, but having obeyed
and believed, and having been purified and converted, so did they enter in''.
From this Chrysostom draws two warnings in one sentence: ``Let no man then of
them that live in vice despair; let no man who lives in virtue slumber.'' He
gives examples of both movements: ``What more blessed than Judas? but he became
a traitor. What more wretched than Matthew? but he became an evangelist.'' And of
the God to whom the sinner returns he says, in words close to Ezekiel's ``I
will not remember'': ``When we have come back unto the earnest love of God, He remembers not the
former things. God is not as man, for He reproaches us not with the past,
neither doth He say, Why wast thou absent so long a time? when we repent''
(\work{Homilies on Matthew} 67.3--4).

Thomas Aquinas sets this reading beside two others in his lectures on Matthew.
The two sons may be two peoples; \latin{vel duo genera hominum, iusti, et
peccatores}, or two kinds of men, the just and sinners. He defines both at
once: \latin{Non dicuntur iusti quicumque, sed qui profitentur se iustos; et
peccatores non quicumque, sed qui poenitentiam agunt}, not the just in general
but those who profess themselves just, and not sinners in general but those who
do penance. The father's call comes by an inward inspiration that gives the
light of reason, and the word ``to day'' has a reach the story does not
spell out: \latin{Et dicit, Hodie, quasi per totum tempus vitae tuae}, he says
``today'' as if to say, through the whole time of your life. The refusal
\latin{nihil aliud est quam contemnere mandata Dei}, is nothing other than
contempt of God's commands; for the first son's going he cites Jeremiah,
``after thou didst convert me, I did penance'' (Jer 31:19). The comparison of
the two sons is then exact: \latin{Primus non promisit, sed fecit; secundus
promisit, sed non fecit}. The second is the worse, and Aquinas says why: it is
better not to vow than to vow and not pay (Eccles 5:4; 2~Pet 2:21), \latin{est
enim ibi duplex peccatum; peccatum inobedientiae, et transgressio voti}, for
there is a double sin there, the sin of disobedience and the breaking of the
promise. On v.~32, where the chief priests did not even afterwards repent, he
names the worst case of all: \latin{Ille enim est pessimus qui de facto suo non poenitet}, the worst is
he who does not repent of what he has done (\work{Super Matthaeum} c.~21,
Venice 1745, pp.~275--276).

Jerome knew this personal reading and did not make it his own. After giving
the two peoples, he reports: \latin{Alii vero non putant Gentilium et
Iudaeorum esse parabolam, sed simpliciter peccatorum et iustorum}, others think
the parable is not of Gentiles and Jews but simply of sinners and the just
(\work{Commentarii in Matthaeum} III, PL 26, 155--156). His own reading is that
of the two peoples. For Chrysostom and Aquinas the two readings stand together:
both give the peoples first and then turn the parable on persons.

\subsection{Tomorrow}

A promise that stays open invites delay, and Augustine meets the objection in
the sinner's own words. ``God hath promised me forgiveness, whenever I reform
myself I am secure; I read the divine Scripture, `In the day that the wicked
man turneth away from his wickedness, and doeth that which is lawful and right,
I will forget all his iniquities.' '' Augustine grants all of it: ``Thou
speakest well and truly; God hath promised pardon on thy amendment, I cannot
deny it; but tell me \ldots\ who hath promised thee a to-morrow?'' The man who
puts off turning is like the raven: `` `To-morrow, To-morrow;' is the raven's
croak. Moan plaintively as the dove, and beat thy breast'' (\work{Sermo} 82.14).
In another sermon he names the two ways in which the same promise can be
misused: ``these two things are the death of souls, despair, and perverse
hope.'' To those who despair the prophet's word is the voice that raises them;
to those who would make it an excuse, ``what if before to-morrow thine own end
shall be?'' (\work{Sermo} 87.10--11). His answer to delay comes from outside
Ezekiel, from the uncertainty of life, and it is exactly the other half of
Aquinas's ``today'': the father's call stands for the whole of a life, and no
one knows how long the life will be.

\subsection{Old sins and old justices}

Jerome and Aquinas agree that God judges the state in which a person is found
and that return is always open. They part on a consequence. Jerome says,
without qualification, that old justices do not help the one who falls away.
Aquinas, arguing from the same verse, distinguishes. Works done in charity are
``deadened'' by a later mortal sin, because it hinders them from bringing the
man to eternal life; but they are ``revived by Penance'', because they remain
``abiding in the Divine acceptance'', and penance removes only the impediment on
the man's side (\work{Summa theologiae} III, q.~89, aa.~4--5). The difference
is a development in the doctrine of merit, not a disagreement about what Ezekiel
says: both hold that the lapsed man cannot plead his past, and Aquinas adds
that the God who does not remember sins does not forget charity.

Aquinas appeals to the chapter once more, and he answers an objection drawn
from it; the two places give the law both its halves. With Ezekiel 18:22 as
his authority he holds that no sin of a wayfarer is beyond penance, ``because
his will is flexible to good and evil'', and that to say otherwise would be
``derogatory to the power of grace'' and ``contrary to Divine mercy'' (III,
q.~86, a.~1). An objection cites Ezekiel 18:21 to argue that penance need not
be prolonged, since the penitent is forgiven at once. Aquinas grants that
forgiveness comes in the first instant of true penance and adds that the
penitent must persevere: ``True penance not only removes past sins, but also
preserves man from future sins'', and so he ``must persevere in his penance,
lest he fall again into sin'' (III, q.~84, a.~8, obj.~1 and ad~1). The door is
never shut in this life, and the one who has gone through it has not finished
walking.

\subsection{A law given to sinners in the way}

The responsorial psalm is the prayer of a man who wants to walk this way. In
Augustine's exposition it is the voice of one who cannot find the way alone:
the paths he asks for are ``narrow, and known to few'', and he asks to be shown
them because ``I cannot by myself return, unless Thou meetest the wanderer''.
The sins of youth he asks God to ``reserve not for vengeance, but let them be
as if forgotten by Thee''. The verse that follows holds both halves of Ezekiel's
law in one sentence: ``The Lord is gracious, since even sinners and the ungodly
He so pitied, as to forgive all that is past; but the Lord is upright too, who
after the mercy of vocation and pardon, which is of grace without merit, will
require merits meet for the last judgment'' (\work{Enarrationes in Psalmos}
24.4--8). Mercy comes first and forgets; uprightness comes after and
requires.

Robert Bellarmine reads the same verses as a lesson in how God forgets. On
v.~7: ``He places forgetfulness in beautiful opposition to remembrance.
Remember thy mercy, but forget my sins; for one is the cause of the other, for
God then remembers his mercy when he does not wish to remember our sins any
longer.'' On v.~8 the law is given ``to sinners in the way, to persuade them to
abandon the old path, and, from being bad and wicked, to become good and
just''; and on v.~9, where only the meek are taught, he insists that the proud
are not shut out: ``The grace of God, then, first softens and subdues the
proud and the obstinate, and when thus humbled and contrite, `It guides them in
judgment' '' (\work{Commentary on the Book of Psalms}, Ps~24:7--9). Ezekiel's
God, who will not remember the iniquities of the man who turns, and the
psalmist's plea, ``The sins of my youth \ldots\ do not remember'', meet in the
same word. The join is the editor's; Augustine makes a like one only for the
Communion psalm.

\subsection{The Missal's texts in this reading}

In this interpretation the Missal's antiphons and prayers take their places
along the line of the turning, and the placing is the editor's, not a
commentator's. The Entrance Antiphon is the first son's refusal confessed.
Jerome's lesson from the furnace, quoted under ``Each Element in Its
Setting'', that in distress we should confess with the whole heart that we
bear justly whatever befalls us, and Schuster's account of the same chant as
``the sorrowful confession of guilt which leads back the sinner to the way of
reconciliation'', in which ``we see the hope which underlies the act of
contrition, the detestation of the sin, and the implicit purpose of amendment
for the future'' (\work{The Sacramentary}, vol.~3, p.~175), both describe the
moment at which the son who said no begins to go.

The Collect names the God before whom that is possible. Aquinas's first reason
why omnipotence is shown most in sparing is that God \latin{libere peccata
dimittit}, freely forgives sins; Schuster's is that the sinner's restoration
asks more of God than the making of the world. The petition, that those who
run toward God's promises may share the goods of heaven, describes the
turning as a race rather than a single step. The acclamation hears the call
that the father gave in the vineyard. Aquinas says the sheep hear Christ's
voice \latin{credendo, et praeceptis meis obediendo}, by believing and by
obeying his commands, and he joins to the verse the psalm's warning,
\latin{Hodie si vocem eius audieritis}, ``To day if you shall hear his voice,
harden not your hearts'' (Ps 94:8; \work{Super Ioannem} c.~10, lect.~5, p.~668).
On the verse before it he adds that some of those who did not yet believe
were to believe later.

The second reading, in either form, asks each to esteem others better than
himself. Aquinas's reason why a better man can do so honestly, that no one is
without some defect and no one without some good, is given in his words under
the third interpretation (\work{Super Philippenses} c.~2, lect.~1); heard in
this reading, it means that no one's state is fixed, and so no one may be
despised as finished. Chrysostom closes his homily on these verses with Romans
2:13, the doers and not the hearers justified, the text he also set beside the
two sons (\work{Homilies on Philippians} 5). In the longer form the hymn shows
the Son whose obedience went all the way to the cross.

The Prayer over the Offerings asks that the offering be accepted. In the prayer
from which the Entrance Antiphon comes, Azarias asks, with no sacrifice left,
that the people be accepted ``in a contrite heart and humble spirit'' (Dan
3:39): the offering, heard so, is the turned heart. The first
Communion antiphon brings back Ezekiel's own words. Augustine, expounding the
verse, reads \latin{Memento} as the prayer's desire for the promise, \latin{hoc
est, imple promissum servo tuo}, that is, fulfil your promise to your servant;
and among Scripture's ways of speaking of a God who forgets he cites
\latin{Omnes \ldots\ iniquitates eius obliviscar} from Ezekiel 18:22
(\work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 118, s.~15.1, PL 37, 1540). Bellarmine, on
another psalm, ties the same verse to the promise of pardon to penitents: God
``frequently \ldots\ promised pardon to those who do penance'', and ``Hence David
himself previously said, in Psalm 118, `Be thou mindful of thy word to thy
servant, in which thou hast given me hope' '' (on Ps 129:4--5). The second
Communion antiphon asks for the deed. Augustine, on the same verse, will not
let the perfect charity of laying down one's life discourage the one who
thinks it beyond him, and places its beginning in giving to a brother in need,
as the third interpretation sets out; and to the one who claims the name
without the work he says, ``Thou hast the name, and hast not the deeds''
(\work{Homilies on the First Epistle of John} 5.12). The second son had the name of an obedient son. The
Prayer after Communion asks that the heavenly mystery repair mind and body: a
turning that has begun still needs to be restored.

\subsection{Where this interpretation strains}

The parable is not spoken to the world at large. It is spoken in the temple to
the chief priests and ancients of the people, about their answer to John
(Mt 21:23, 32), and Jerome, Chrysostom and Aquinas all read it first of peoples.
A reading of it as a law for every person keeps its moral and loses the
historical charge of its first telling.
Ezekiel's horizon is narrower too. Chapter 18 answers a people's fatalism about
inherited guilt and speaks of life and death within the covenant community;
Jerome's \latin{unusquisque in quo invenietur, in eo iudicabitur} carries it to
the last judgment, and that is his extension of the oracle rather than its
plain horizon. Augustine names the practical difficulty, that an open promise
invites delay, and he answers it from outside the prophecy. Finally, Jerome and
Aquinas do not say the same thing about the justices of the one who falls away,
and the difference stands.

\subsection{The four senses of this interpretation}

\begin{fourSenses}
\item[Literal.] Ezekiel answers people who call God's way unfair: each lives or
dies by the way he is found walking, and God does not will the sinner's death.
Jesus tells the chief priests and ancients of a son who refused and then went
and a son who promised and did not, and says that the publicans and harlots who
believed John go before those who did not afterwards repent.

\item[Allegorical.] The man with two sons is God, who calls by an inward
inspiration, and his ``to day'' is the whole of a life (Aquinas, on Matthew);
on the Alleluia verse Aquinas joins to the sheep's hearing the psalm's ``To day
if you shall hear his voice''. Christ is then the voice that calls ``today''
through a whole life, and God's power, shown most in freely forgiving
(Aquinas, on the Collect), receives the penitent into the flock by the newness
of virtue (Jerome, on Ezekiel). Both joinings are the editor's.

\item[Moral.] Do not despair of what you have been, and do not rest on it
(Chrysostom). Turn today, since no one has promised you tomorrow (Augustine).
Let the deed follow the word: the worst is the man who does not repent of what
he has done (Aquinas), and, where the second Communion antiphon is chosen, a
Christian name without the deeds profits nothing (Augustine).

\item[Anagogical.] ``Each will be judged in that in which he is found''
(Jerome), so the penitent must persevere in his penance (Aquinas), and the
Lord who forgave the past in the mercy of vocation and pardon will require at
the last judgment merits meet for it (Augustine, on the psalm). Where the first
Communion antiphon is chosen, the servant's prayer that God remember his word
is a prayer for the promise's fulfilment (Augustine).
\end{fourSenses}
