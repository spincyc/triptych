\section{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}
\zlabel{triptych:concise:commentary:start}

\subsection{The five hours: the ages of the world, or the ages of a life}

Gregory the Great begins his homily on this Gospel by identifying the
vineyard. The householder is the Creator, and his vineyard is the universal
Church, \latin{ab Abel iusto usque ad ultimum electum}, from Abel the just to
the last of the elect. The hours are the ages in which God has never ceased to
send workers, from the morning that runs from Adam to Noah to the eleventh
hour that runs from the Lord's coming to the end of the world. The workers of
the first four hours are the ancient Hebrew people, and at the eleventh the
Gentiles are called (\work{Homiliae in Evangelia} 19.1). Augustine divides the
day in nearly the same way, from Abel and Noah to ``all Christians, called as
it were at the eleventh hour'', and of each group he repeats one refrain: they
``will receive together with us the blessedness of the resurrection''
(\work{Sermo} 87.5). Aquinas glosses ``this last'' \latin{idest gentili}, that
is, the Gentile (\work{Super Matthaeum} c.~20, lect.~1). They draw the lines in
different places, Gregory beginning with Adam and Augustine with Abel, and
hold in common that the one householder has been at work through every age
and that all the just receive one wage.

Jerome knows that scheme as the exposition of others and gives as his own
view, \latin{Mihi videntur}, that the hours are the ages of a life, from those called from the
womb to those who come in extreme old age; and yet all alike receive the
reward, though the labour differs
(\work{Commentarii in Matthaeum} III, PL 26, 141). St~John Chrysostom states
this
sense as the plain meaning of the figures, the hours being ``them who at
different ages have drawn near to God'' (\work{Homilies on Matthew} 64.3), and
he gives them no reference to the ages of the world. Augustine and Gregory add
it as a second sense and say so: ``Besides that solution of the parable,''
Augustine writes, it ``may be seen to have an explanation in respect even of
this present life'' (87.7). Aquinas sets the two senses side by side. They
answer different questions, how wide God's call has been and how urgent it is
for me, and neither excludes the other. Gregory passes from one to the other.
The Gentiles could plead that no one had hired them; what, he
asks, shall we say in our excuse, who came to the faith almost from our
mother's womb (19.1)? In the history of salvation his hearers belong to the
last hour; in their own lives most were called at the first.

One sentence of the parable does divide the witnesses. Gregory takes ``No man
hath hired us'' as the Gentiles' true plea, for no patriarch and no prophet
had come to them, and Aquinas likewise holds them excused because they had not
the prophets. For Chrysostom the words are the labourers' own and not the
householder's, a point of the parable not to be pressed: ``For that He called
all, as far as lay in Him, from the first even the parable shows'' (64.3). The
ages of the world are a received allegory of the parable and not its plain
sense, for the text names no Abel and no Gentile. For the third interpretation
the day is not decisive; the evening's answer is.

\subsection{How far the pardon reaches, and how long it waits}

Both measures are taken to the first reading, and by the same author. For
Jerome the prophet warns his own people after the crowd of the Gentiles has
taken up the mercies they refused, and the pardon that is not to be thought
incredible reaches both the people of the Jews and all those among the nations
who did not know God (\work{In Isaiam} XV, PL 24, 553--555). On the summons itself he marks the
limit of the time. Seek \latin{dum estis in corpore, dum datur locus
poenitentiae}, while you are in the body, while room is given for repentance
(col.~553). Aquinas names the fit time,
\latin{antequam adversitas vel mors veniat}, before adversity or death comes
(\work{Super Isaiam} c.~55).

The psalm gives the first interpretation its word, and the word is ``all'':
``The Lord is sweet to all'', ``just in all his ways'', ``nigh unto all them
that call upon him''. To the second interpretation the psalm gives the word ``patient''. Augustine asks how
many sinners God puts up with, not condemning but waiting, and then turns on
his hearer: \latin{vocat te nunc, exhortatur te nunc; exspectat donec
resipiscas, et tu tardas!} He calls you now, he exhorts you now; he waits
until you come to your senses, and you delay! It is part of God's great mercy,
he adds, that he has made the day of our death uncertain (\work{Enarrationes
in Psalmos} 144.11, PL 37, 1876).

The second interpretation needs that argument, because the parable, taken
alone, seems to reward delay. Augustine raises the objection himself: ``lest
any put off to come into the vineyard, because he is sure, that, come when he
will, he shall receive this denarius.'' To the man who proposes to come at the
eleventh hour he says,
``whether thou shalt live even to the seventh, no one hath promised thee
\ldots\ Why then dost thou put off him that calleth thee, certain as thou art
of the reward, but uncertain of the day?'' (87.8). Despair and perverse hope,
he says, are the death of souls (87.10--11). The answer is true and comes from
outside the story, since within it every labourer lives until evening. Gregory
finds the thief in the parable, late by punishment and not by age (19.3).
Chrysostom gives the parable one lesson for
each of two audiences: it is spoken ``to the former, that they may not be
proud \ldots\ to the latter, that they may learn that it is possible even in a
short time to recover all'' (64.4).

\subsection{The murmur, and what the householder's reply decides}

No one who receives the kingdom can grumble at it, and the interpretations
part most plainly over what the murmuring is. Gregory refers it to the long
delay of the just who lived before Christ, who were not brought to the kingdom
until he came who opened paradise by his death (19.4). Jerome explains the grievance as envy and observes that
it convicts every hour in turn, since the man of the third hour also worked
less than the man of the first: \latin{Omnis itaque retro vocatio gentibus
invidet, et in Evangelii torquetur gratia}, every earlier calling envies the
Gentiles and is tormented by the grace of the Gospel (PL 26, 141). Chrysostom will have neither as a fact about the
saved: ``There is no one who is thus justifying himself, or blaming others in
the kingdom of Heaven; away with the thought!'' The complaint is a device of
the story (64.3). On his account the murmuring
and the reply, a third of the parable, have little to do, and an account that
leaves the ending idle invites another that begins there.

Four expositors state of these same verses what the reply decides. Augustine
supplies the words the parable leaves unspoken: ``I have paid thee what I
agreed for. To this other it is my will not to render a payment, but to bestow
a gift'' (87.4). For Gregory the
very receiving of the kingdom is the goodness of the householder's will
(19.4). Aquinas sets out the logic. \latin{Balivus enim non potest dare aliquid nisi secundum
merita; Rex autem potest sine meritis}: a bailiff can give only according to
deserts, a king can give without them, and so can God, who is lord of all.
Hence the name of the grievance, \latin{nequam est proprie qui de bonitate
dolet}: he is properly called evil who grieves at goodness. Jerome names the
wish behind the complaint: you desire not so much to receive more yourself as
that another should receive nothing (PL 26, 141--142). Chrysostom agrees so far with the third
interpretation's centre: the Lord frames the story to show ``that the whole is
of His love to man'' (64.4).

The psalm holds together the two words the murmurers thought incompatible,
``gracious and merciful'' and ``just in all his ways''. St~Hilary of Poitiers
explains how both can be said: \latin{habet dei iustitia miserandi
voluntatem}, God's justice has the will to show mercy, and is not thereby so
changed as to cease to be justice, whereas human justice commonly arrives at
being unmerciful (\work{Tractatus super Psalmos} 144.14). Hilary speaks of the
psalm and not of the parable, but his sentence describes the householder's two
dealings: with the first he keeps his contract, which is justice as St~Robert
Bellarmine glosses the psalm's seventeenth verse, ``he renders to all what is due to
them''; with the last he is generous, and takes nothing from his justice.

The contract remains, and it is the third interpretation's strongest
difficulty. The first-hired are paid on an agreement, so the parable does not
say that no one earns anything. Augustine keeps this, ``To the first, it is a
payment; to the other, a free gift'' (87.6); Aquinas keeps it, justice to the one and mercy to the other; and
Jerome keeps it when he sets beside ``Take what is thine'' the Law's own
promise, \latin{Iudaeus in Lege non gratia, sed opere salvatur. Qui enim
fecerit eam, vivet in ea}: under the Law the Jew is saved not by grace but by
work, for the one who does it shall live in it (Lev 18:5, in the form cited at
Rom 10:5; PL 26, 142). Augustine resolves the tension only in his comment on
the psalm, \latin{Merita tua nusquam iactes, quia et ipsa tua merita illius
dona sunt}, nowhere boast of your merits, because your merits themselves are
his gifts (144.11), and that goes beyond what the parable states.

\subsection{``So shall the last be first''}

The Lectionary ends the Gospel on the saying about first and last, and the
witnesses do not agree how it stands to the parable it closes. For Augustine
it states the parable's equality: first and last are made equal ``by equality
of treatment, not by inverting their order'' (87.4). Chrysostom denies that the
saying is drawn from the parable at all. In the parable ``the first did not
become last, but all received the same contrary to hope and expectation''; the
saying foretells something ``more strange'', a dark hint at the Jews and,
among believers, at those who shone at first and fell back and those who have
risen from vice (64.4). Jerome makes the saying the key: the parable is
understood from the same saying at 19:30, and the Lord ranks \latin{non
tempori \ldots\ sed fidei}, not by time but by faith (PL 26, 140--142). Aquinas
records both of the first two senses.

\subsection{What the wage is}

All three interpretations take the denarius as eternal life. The first asks
whether it can be equal. ``That denarius is life eternal, and in the life
eternal all will be equal'', Augustine says; the saints will differ in
brightness, but ``in respect to the living for ever, this man will not live
more than that, nor that than this'' (87.6). Aquinas makes the same
distinction: beatitude is one in its object and unequal in the share each has
of it.

The third asks what that life consists in. Of the coin itself Jerome says,
\latin{Denarius figuram regis habet}, the denarius bears the figure of the
king: you have received the wage I promised you, \latin{hoc est, imaginem et
similitudinem meam}, that is, my image and likeness; what more do you seek?
(PL 26, 142). Two other witnesses say what the last reward is, each of his own
text and neither of the parable. At the psalm's promise that the Lord is near
to all who call upon him in truth, Augustine says that to call in truth is to
want the giver more than his gifts, \latin{quanto beatior eris, cum seipsum
tibi dederit?}, how much more blessed will you be when he gives you himself?
(\work{Enarrationes} 144.22). Gregory, preaching on the Good Shepherd, names
the pasture that the sheep find at last: \latin{Pascua namque electorum sunt
vultus praesens Dei}, the pasture of the elect is the present face of God
(\work{Homiliae} 14.5). A reward of that kind cannot be divided into a larger
and a smaller sum.

The second reading shows a labourer for whom the wage is a person. No Father
read connects Paul's dilemma with the parable, and his place in each
interpretation comes from the letter. For the first he is the labourer
sent to the nations, in Augustine's words ``specially sent as an apostle to
the Gentiles'' (\work{In Iohannis evangelium tractatus} 47.5), at work for a
church of the last hour. For the second he measures life by its fruit: the
present life is needful, Chrysostom says, ``if we bear fruit; since if it be
unfruitful, it is no longer life'' (\work{Homilies on Philippians}~3). For the third he shows
why a man who holds his wage asks for a longer day. Aquinas distinguishes two
loves of God: by one a man wants to enjoy God,
which is the man's own good; by the other he \latin{praeponit honorem Dei,
etiam huic delectationi, qua fruitur Deo}, puts God's honour before even the
delight of enjoying him, and this is perfect charity (\work{Super
Philippenses} c.~1, lect.~3). Chrysostom gives the same account: Paul remains
``for Christ's sake, that I may render more loving unto Him those whom I have
made his servants'' (\work{Homilies on Philippians}~4). The murmurers reckon
their hours against the late comers; Paul adds hours for their sake.

\subsection{Whose work is the coming? The opened heart and the psalmist's wish}

Before the Gospel the assembly asks the Lord to open its heart, and each
interpretation hears the petition differently. The first gives the assembly a
place with Lydia, a Gentile who worshipped God in a Roman colony. For St~Bede
she signifies the Church, which received with the inward devotion of the heart
the preaching that the Jews had driven away (\work{Retractatio in Actus apostolorum} 16, PL
92, 1025). The second hears a request for the hearing in which conversion
begins; Augustine opens his sermon on the parable with the God who does not
cease ``to open our heart, as it were, by the plough of His Word'' (87.1).

The third carries its claim two steps further. The first step is that the
keeping of the commandments is itself to be asked of God, and the first
Communion antiphon is its text. The psalmist does not say that he has kept
what God commanded; he says \latin{Utinam}, ``O! that''. Who, Augustine asks,
wishes for what he has in his own power? \latin{Ergo si optat homo quod
praecipit Deus; ut det ipse quod praecipit, rogandus est Deus}: if a man
wishes for what God commands, then God must be asked to give what he commands
(\work{Enarrationes} 118, s.~4.2, PL 37, 1510). Hilary says the same of the
same verse: unless we are directed by God we shall be weak by our nature
(\work{Tractatus} 118, Aleph~12); the second interpretation hears in the same wish the convert's
own prayer. The Collect prays in the same manner, for the keeping of
the precepts and the arriving at life both stand inside a prayer.

The second step, that the first opening of the heart is God's gift, only
Augustine states among the authors read. From St~Paul's request that God ``may
open unto us a door of speech'' (Col 4:3) he argues that ``even the very
beginning of faith is the gift of God. For it would not be sought for from Him
in prayer, unless it were believed to be given by Him'', and Lydia is his
example: ``she was so called that she might believe'' (\work{De
praedestinatione sanctorum} 20.41). His argument is made from Colossians, with
the verse of Acts as its instance, and the petition of the Alleluia verse is
the Lectionary's adaptation and not Luke's wording. Chrysostom, preaching
through Luke's narrative, reads the verse differently: ``Therefore we need God, to open the heart: but God opens the
hearts that are willing: for there are hardened hearts to be seen''; and,
``The opening, then, was God's work, the attending was hers: so that it was
both God's doing and man's'' (\work{Homilies on Acts}~35). He does not say
where the willingness comes from, and he argues there against no one. On the
parable he says that ``the difference was made by the disposition of them that
were called'' (64.3), and Augustine's sermon on it does not take up why some
come late. The two therefore meet only at Acts 16:14, and the difference between
their readings of it is real. The step rests on Augustine's argument and on no
agreement between the two, and it does not reach the claim with which the
third interpretation began, which Chrysostom states for himself.

\subsection{The shepherd, and the prayers at the altar}

The second Communion antiphon sets beside the householder another figure who
does not calculate. Gregory glosses its verb, \latin{cognosco oves meas, hoc
est diligo}, I know my sheep, that is, I love them (14.3). The first interpretation listens to
the antiphon's context and not to the appointed verse: ``other sheep I have
that are not of this fold'' (10:16). Gregory hears there the redemption of us
who have come from the Gentile people, and one fold made out of two flocks
(14.4). The
second hears Gregory's examination of
conscience, \latin{Videte si oves eius estis}, see whether you are his sheep,
and know him not by belief but by deed (14.4). The third hears a shepherd who gives what no contract could
oblige. He laid down his life \latin{ut in sacramento nostro corpus suum et
sanguinem verteret}, that he might turn his body and blood into our sacrament
(14.1), where the hireling feeds the sheep for temporal wages (14.2); the labourer who counts his hours against his
neighbour's has taken the hireling's view of the vineyard. Aquinas explains
the possessive, \latin{meae, inquam, praedestinatione, vocatione, et gratia},
mine by predestination, calling and grace (\work{Super Ioannem} c.~10,
lect.~4).

The Missal's prayers are patient of all three hearings. They take their
places by their own wording; the placing is the editor's, not a
commentator's. The Entrance Antiphon speaks
of one people and one Lord; its promise to hear is the ground on which a late
cry, even the thief's, is not refused; and in it God names himself his
people's salvation before anything is asked of them. The Collect gathers the law given
to the first-hired into two loves asked of every labourer at every hour, and
since Chrysostom's vineyard is ``the injunctions of God and His
commandments'', it asks for labour in the vineyard and the denarius at
evening. The Prayer over the Offerings speaks of one people's
gifts, \latin{tuae plebis}, completes at the altar the turning that began in
hearing, and reverses the direction of giving, since what is professed is to
be received. The Prayer after Communion names the one wage already begun, asks
the proof of conduct, \latin{et moribus}, and asks a help that is continual
and no wage paid once.

\subsection{Where the four senses part}

The literal sense is common ground, and the allegorical sense is where the
interpretations part. For the first the day is the history of salvation, and
the householder who ``went out'' is Christ made known to the nations after
Pentecost, as Augustine says (87.9). For the second the hours are the ages of
a life, and the thief is the labourer of the eleventh hour, as Gregory says.
For the third the householder's goodness is the goodness of Christ, the good
shepherd, and the likeness given to each labourer alike is Christ; of the coin
Jerome says only that the wage is the king's image and likeness. The moral
senses follow: do not resent those who arrive late; turn today, neither
despairing of the past nor presuming on a tomorrow no one has promised; work
without reckoning, and rejoice at another's undeserved good. The anagogical
senses differ in what the evening is: the end of the world, when all the just
receive the resurrection together; each person's death, whose day God has
hidden in mercy; and the wage itself, God giving himself. A congregation that
has been in the vineyard since morning, a penitent who has come at the last hour, and a labourer who has
begun to count what he is owed all hear the same prayers and receive the same
Communion, the pledge of the one wage placed already in every hand.
