\section*{Note on Sources and Delivery}

{\small

\textbf{Audience and occasion.} An adult parish assembly, at the Mass of the
Twenty-eighth Sunday in Ordinary Time, Year~A: \latin{Dominica XXVIII ``per
annum''} of the \work{Roman Missal}, Third Edition, for the dioceses of the
United States, with no.~142 of the \work{Lectionary for Mass}, for the
occurrence of 11~October 2026. The words hold under either form of the
Gospel. Everything said of the invitation, the refusal, the roads and the
full hall stands in Matthew 22:1--10, which both forms read; the guest without
the wedding garment (22:11--13) is introduced as what Matthew's parable goes
on to tell, and not as words the assembly has necessarily heard. Of the two
Communion antiphons, the first is named as one of the pair the Missal gives.

\textbf{Length and pace.} The spoken body runs to 1,465 words, counted as
whitespace-separated words once comments and markup are removed: a little over
eleven minutes at an unhurried 130 words a minute, a little over eleven and a
half at 125, and a little over twelve at 120. That is arithmetic on the word count; no
speaker has delivered these words and no delivery has been timed. The prose was read
through in full for sense, sentence length and ease of speech, silently and
not aloud.

\textbf{Relation to the reviewed interpretations.} The argument preached is
chiefly the third of the expansive study's three interpretations, \textit{One
to His Farm, Another to His Merchandise: Why the Invited Would Not Come}. The
first refusal is not hostility but neglect for good and necessary things, and
nothing temporal ought to keep anyone from coming to God. Chrysostom and
Aquinas on the excuses, Gregory on the farm, the merchandise and the roads,
Chrysostom on Philippians 4 and Augustine on the psalm of the first Communion
antiphon carry it. It is joined to the first, \textit{The King's Feast for All
Peoples}, for what is refused: Isaiah's feast on the mountain, Jerome's king
who is God almighty making the wedding for Christ and the Church, Gregory's
wedding of the Son with the Church through the Incarnation, and his hearers
who recline at the wedding of the Word. From the second,
\textit{Friend, How Camest Thou In?}, it takes the garment as charity, with
Augustine and Gregory, and Augustine's ``Clothe others''. The comparison in
the study finds that no witness cited denies another interpretation's claim,
and Gregory contributes to all three from different sentences; the
combination preached asserts nothing any of the three denies. Of the dispute
over what the garment is, the homily says only that the Fathers give more
than one answer and follows Augustine and Gregory. The identification of the
first invited with a people is not preached, and the application falls on the
baptized.

Each Father is credited only with what he says of his own passage:
Chrysostom's question who would not come, his reasonable excuses and his
``making light of it'' of Matthew 22, and his plenty that needs virtue of
Philippians 4; Aquinas's just cause that
the Lord does not accept; Jerome's king who is God almighty and makes the
wedding for Christ and the Church; Gregory's wedding of the Son, his farm and
merchandise as earthly labour and worldly gain that will not weigh the
mystery of the Incarnation, his roads where no prosperity accompanies, his
garment of charity and his hearers who recline at the wedding of the Word;
Augustine's garment that is not baptism but charity, his ``Clothe others''
and his rich who lack bread. The following connections are the homily's own,
drawn from the wording of the texts, and no Father makes them: Gregory's
\latin{intentus} of the man gone to his farm (38.5) set beside the Collect's
\latin{intentos} on good works (the study links the Collect's word to the
attention the invited gave to farm and trade; matching it to Gregory's word is
the homily's own observation); Paul of
Philippians 4 as the guest who would have come; the Philippians' gift as a
way of coming closer; the farm and the merchandise turned into the wedding
garment by giving; this Mass as the King's feast spread now, with the Prayer
over the Offerings and the Prayer after Communion read as its course; and the
servants still sent to farm, shop and roads.

Left to the studies: the burnt city and its dispute; Hilary's ambition and
avarice and his garment of the Spirit; Jerome's lesser crime of the busy;
Irenaeus on the one Father of the wedding; the Alleluia verse; the Entrance
Antiphon and the responsorial psalm; the second Communion antiphon; Aquinas
on the use of every state and on desire filled in glory; Bellarmine on the
psalm; and the sacramentary history of the orations.

\textbf{Texts quoted, and their route.} Scripture is quoted in the
Douay--Rheims version as revised by Bishop Challoner, a public-domain study
translation, at Mt 22:4--6, 9--10 and 12 (with 21:23 and 22:11 narrated), Is
25:6 and~8, and Phil 4:12--14. It is not the English proclaimed at Mass, which
is that of the \work{Lectionary for Mass for Use in the Dioceses of the United
States of America} and is not reproduced. The Vulgate's \latin{ad exitus
viarum} of Mt 22:9 is explained, not quoted. The Collect, the Prayer over the
Offerings, the Prayer after Communion and the first Communion antiphon are
described after the study's descriptions of the Latin of the \work{Missale
Romanum}; the descriptions are not translations and are not the prayers'
words. Chrysostom's homilies on Matthew and on Philippians and Augustine's
sermons and exposition of the psalm are quoted in the English of the Nicene and
Post-Nicene Fathers; each of those quotations was read in the tracked text at
the locus below. Augustine's account of the garment that is not baptism, with his
clause that no one attains to God without baptism, is followed in that English
without quotation marks. Jerome, Gregory and Aquinas are reported without
quotation marks, in English that renders the Latin the study prints; Gregory's
Latin was read again in the tracked text, and Jerome's on the retained page
image of Migne's column, at the loci below.

\textbf{Exact loci.} Chrysostom, ``For who would not choose to come to a
marriage~\ldots'', ``The excuses seem to be reasonable~\ldots'' and ``not for
press of business, but from `making light of it'~\ldots'': \work{Homilies on
Matthew} 69.1; his hearers ``having partaken of the mysteries'': 69.2;
``For as want inclines us to do many evil things, so too doth plenty'':
\work{Homilies on Philippians} 15.
Aquinas, \latin{Videbantur habere iustam causam exterius; sed Dominus non
recipit: quia nulla temporalia debent detinere de veniendo ad Deum}:
\work{Super Matthaeum} c.~22, p.~281. Jerome, \latin{Rex iste qui fecit
nuptias filio suo, Deus omnipotens est. Facit autem nuptias Domino nostro Iesu
Christo et Ecclesiae}: \work{Commentarii in Matthaeum} III, on Mt 22:2, PL 26,
col.~159. Gregory, \latin{Apertius ergo atque
securius dici potest quia in hoc Pater regi Filio nuptias fecit, quo ei per
incarnationis mysterium sanctam Ecclesiam sociavit}: \work{Homiliae in
Evangelia} 38.3; \latin{In villam quippe ire est labori terreno immoderate
incumbere, in negotiationem vero ire est actionum saecularium lucris inhiare},
with the mystery of the Incarnation not weighed: 38.5; \latin{illi plerumque
facile ad Deum veniunt, quos in terrenis actibus prospera nulla comitantur}:
38.6; the garment as charity: 38.9; \latin{Nos sumus, fratres charissimi, qui
in nuptiis Verbi discumbimus}: 38.11. Augustine, the garment that is not
baptism, without which no one attains to God, but charity: \work{Sermo}
90.5--6; ``Do not say; `we are too poor to
have that garment.' Clothe others~\ldots'': \work{Sermo} 95.7; ``Those rich
then lack, they lack, and what is heavier, they lack bread'' and ``I am the
Living Bread~\ldots'': \work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 33, section 14 of the
English text.
