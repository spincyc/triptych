**What the witnesses agree and where they differ.** Jerome and Aquinas agree
that God judges the state in which a person is found and that return is always
open. They differ in how far a lapse cancels past good: Jerome, on v. 24,
"non iuvant veteres iustitiae"; Aquinas, using the same verse, holds such works
deadened, not destroyed, and revived by penance. That is a doctrinal
development of the verse's consequence, not a disagreement about its
meaning, and the studies must attribute each position to its author. Jerome's
second reading of vv. 26–29 (Israel and the nations) is his own and is the
home of the `two-peoples` reading; he names Luke's form of the husbandmen, not
the parable of the two sons.

**Later tier, bounded.** No medieval or later saintly *commentary* on Ezekiel
18 is held. Aquinas's uses in the *Summa* are doctrinal. Hugh of Saint-Cher's
*Postilla* is held only in restricted artifacts that were not opened; Albert,
Rabanus Maurus and Denis the Carthusian on Ezekiel are not registered (section 4).

The two agree that the psalm's "way" is the way of the commandments given to
sinners by mercy, and that the meek or humble are those God can lead.
Augustine sets mercy and judgment in order (mercy first, merits required at
the end); Bellarmine stresses grace moving the will and the proud first made
humble. Complementary. Cassiodorus, Theodoret and Aquinas's *Postilla* on this
psalm were not opened (section 4); Aquinas's *Postilla* on Psalm 24 is not
held.

Augustine and Aquinas agree that hearing is believing and obeying and that
following is imitation, and both read 10:26 of predestination. They differ in
what the saying implies for those addressed. Augustine applies it to them
without distinction: they were predestined to everlasting destruction. His
"be ye sheep" is addressed to his own congregation, not to them. Aquinas keeps
the gloss but distinguishes within the crowd: none of them were then
preordained to believe, some were preordained to believe later. Because the
saying fell on a crowd and on no determinate person, it did not take away
hope. That is a real difference, narrower than a contradiction, and it bears
on the `two-peoples` reading: Augustine is the contrary position there, and
Aquinas's hope is for some of those addressed, not for a people as such.
Gregory's one fold is made of those chosen from each people, at 10:16. The
Lectionary's inserted speaker formula has no comment in any witness.

### 3.11 A magisterial bound on presenting the `two-peoples` identification

| Witness and locus | Read as | Role | What it says |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Second Vatican Council, *Nostra aetate* 4 | English, the registered Vatican web state of 2026-07-24, fetched whole over verified TLS at the re-entry and matched to its SHA-256 and 17,231 bytes before reading; the whole article read; Latin not compared | official control on exposition, not reception | The Church's bond with Abraham's stock. Jerusalem did not recognize the time of her visitation, and the Jews in large number did not accept the Gospel, yet "God holds the Jews most dear for the sake of their Fathers" and does not repent of his gifts and calls. The Jewish authorities and those who followed them pressed for Christ's death, but what happened in the Passion "cannot be charged against all the Jews, without distinction, then alive, nor against the Jews of today". The Jews "should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God, as if this followed from the Holy Scriptures". Catechesis and preaching are to teach nothing that does not conform to the truth of the Gospel and the spirit of Christ. The Church decries hatred, persecutions and displays of anti-Semitism |

**Why it is recorded.** Jerome, Chrysostom and Aquinas identify the second son
with the Jewish people (section 7, first row). Jerome goes further: the people
killed the heir and thought itself heir (PL 26, col. 156), and on Ezekiel
18:29 "Usque hodie Israel blasphemat Deum" and the vineyard goes to other
husbandmen (PL 25, col. 181). The expansive study, the concise study and the
parish homily will all draw on the `two-peoples` reading. The Declaration is
the checked bound on how that reading is presented and applied to present-day
readers and hearers.

**Its limit.** It governs presentation and application, not exegesis. It does
not say what the parable means, it passes no judgment on any Father's reading,
and it gives no licence to soften or omit what a Father says. What it requires
here is this. The identification is reported as the reading of the Fathers who
make it, never in the leaf's own voice. The parable's literal addressees stay
the chief priests and elders (Matthew 21:23, 45). Jerome's harsh sentences are
attributed to him and set beside his own *ad utrumque populum* and
Chrysostom's "as having a hope, if they were willing". Nothing in the studies
or the homily may present the Jewish people, then or now, as charged with the
Passion or as rejected or accursed by God. The moral application goes to the
baptized, as Aquinas's own application to clerics and religious does. The
English delivery is restricted, and only short phrases are quoted.

## 7. Material disagreement and complementary difference

| Question | Positions | Kind |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who are the two sons? | Jerome: the first son is the Gentile people, who refused God known by natural law and repented at the Saviour's coming; the second the Jewish people, who promised at Sinai and killed the heir. He reports others who read them as sinners and the just. Chrysostom: the Gentiles and the Jews, and in the application the publicans and harlots against the chief priests. Aquinas: the two peoples, or those who profess justice and those who do penance, or clerics and laity. **Hilary: the elder son, who refused and repented, is the people of the Pharisees, who believed under the apostles after the resurrection; the younger, who promised and did not go, is the people of publicans and sinners, who believed John but could not yet do the Gospel's work** | **Real disagreement** between Hilary and the other three, tied to his text's answer "the younger" (section 3.1). Westcott–Hort: the reversed order and "the latter" | Textual, recorded as such; the studies follow the Lectionary |

## 9. Rights

Scripture appears only in the public-domain Douay–Rheims (Challoner), labelled
as a study translation. Orations and antiphons appear by Latin incipit and
original description, with short Latin phrases where an argument turns on
them. The Latin of the Collect is word for word the Hadrianum's (Wilson 1915,
public domain in the United States, compared with the 2002 text on the page
image). The postconciliar Latin rights record
(`postconciliar-latin-rights-v1.toml`, `scope_partition`) says pre-existing
wording "may be assessed from its own earlier witness and rights basis" and
withholds modern or unpartitioned wording; whether the studies print the
Collect's Latin from the Hadrianum is the authoring stage's decision under
that record and the publication policy. The other two orations have no
established antecedent for their whole text and stay at incipit and
description. No
approved English of the Missal or the Lectionary is reproduced. The *Ordo*'s
tituli are short Latin phrases quoted as evidence of its emphasis. NPNF
English, O'Sullivan's Bellarmine (1866), the Dominican *Summa* (Gutenberg) and
Schuster's English (1927 OCR, tracked under its own record) are quoted from
their tracked records. Jerome (Migne, 1845), Hilary (Migne, 1844; CSEL 22,
1891), Augustine (Migne, 1845), Aquinas (Venice 1745; Liège 1857) are quoted
from public-domain printings whose pages are retained in the library; the
Leonine volume is quoted from its remote scan, which the library records with
unresolved rights and does not retain. Gregory's Latin is an ancient text in a
CC BY-SA electronic edition, and II-II q. 30 a restricted electronic
presentation: short phrases only. The Catholic Encyclopedia labels are short
phrases of public-domain articles under the rights record declared in
`review-dependencies.toml`. The NABRE introduction is protected; nothing of it
is quoted beyond the figure the corpus label uses. *Nostra aetate* is quoted
from the restricted Vatican English delivery in short phrases only, and the
USCCB readings page is described, never transcribed. The page-2 Date column
prints the chronology tool's generated display labels, not this prose.
