\section{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\zlabel{triptych:concise:themes:start}

This Mass asks what God judges when he judges a man: the first word or the last
deed. A son says ``I will not'' and afterwards goes; another says ``I go, Sir''
and does not go. The chief priests and ancients who would not believe John are
asked which of the two did the father's will, and they answer against
themselves. Ezekiel has already given the Lord's side of the question, ``Is it
my way that is not right?'' Around the two readings the other texts set a
confession of sin, a prayer to be shown the Lord's ways, the mind of Christ
Jesus, and a table at which the life laid down for us is received.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=0pt,topsep=.3em]
\item \textbf{Confession and mercy:} the Entrance Antiphon and the Collect.
\item \textbf{The complaint answered:} the first reading and the psalm.
\item \textbf{The mind of Christ and the question about John:} second reading,
Alleluia verse, Gospel.
\item \textbf{Offering and table:} Prayer over the Offerings, Communion
antiphons, Prayer after Communion.
\end{itemize}

The antiphons and orations are the Missal's for the Twenty-sixth Week in
Ordinary Time and serve all three years; the readings,
psalm and acclamation are Year~A's, Lectionary no.~136. The books state three
relations: the first reading is chosen for the Gospel, the psalm responds to
it, and the acclamation greets the Gospel. Philippians and Matthew each follow
a course of their own.

The \work{Ordo lectionum Missae} sets a title over each reading: \latin{Cum averterit se impius ab impietate sua, animam suam
vivificabit}, when the wicked man turns from his wickedness he gives his soul
life, condensed from Ezekiel 18:27; \latin{Paenitentia motus abiit.
Publicani et meretrices praecedunt vos in regnum Dei}, moved by repentance he
went; the publicans and harlots go before you into the kingdom of God, from
Matthew 21:29 and 31; and \latin{Sentite in vobis quod et in Christo Iesu},
have in you the mind that was in Christ Jesus, from Philippians 2:5.
The two correlated titles both fall on repentance.

\subsection{A confession, and a power shown in sparing}

The Entrance Antiphon, \latin{Omnia, quae fecisti nobis, Domine}, is made from
five verses of the prayer of Azarias in Nabuchodonosor's furnace, in the part
of Daniel 3 that the Greek Bible and the Vulgate carry and the Hebrew does not.
It confesses that all the Lord has done to his people he did in true judgment,
because they sinned and did not obey, and it asks glory for his name and mercy
for them. The men who pray it are in the fire because they refused to sin, and
still they say ``we have sinned''.

The Collect, \latin{Deus, qui omnipotentiam tuam}, names the God to whom that
confession is made. His almighty power is shown above all in sparing and in
mercy, and his grace is asked for those who run toward his promises, that they
may share the goods of heaven.

\subsection{The complaint about the Lord's way}

Ezekiel 18 answers a proverb of the exiles, that the fathers ate sour grapes
and the children's teeth are set on edge. Every soul is the Lord's and each
answers for itself: the wicked man who does penance lives, and the just man who
turns away dies. The appointed verses begin with the complaint the Lord
reports, ``And you have said: The way of the Lord is not right''. He turns it
back, ``are not rather your ways perverse?'', and restates the two cases. A
little before them stands the chapter's question: ``Is it my will that a sinner should
die \ldots\ and not that he should be converted from his ways, and live?''
(18:23).

The psalm answers as the prayer of one who wants to walk that way. It asks to
be shown the Lord's ways and taught his paths, begs him to remember his
compassion and not the sins of youth, and confesses that he ``will give a law
to sinners in the way'' and ``teach the meek his ways''. Three families of
words run through these texts: the Lord's way, remembering and not
remembering, and humility. That is an observation about their wording.

\subsection{The mind of Christ and the question about John}

Paul's appeal begins with a ``therefore'' that joins it to the conflict the
Philippians share with him. He asks for one mind and one charity, nothing done
``through contention'' or ``vain glory'', each to ``esteem others better than
themselves''; and he names its ground, ``let this mind be in you, which was
also in Christ Jesus''. The longer form goes on with the hymn: Christ emptied
himself, became ``obedient unto death, even to the death of the cross'', and
was given ``a name which is above all names''. The shorter form ends at v.~5,
and there only the appeal and the name of Christ's mind are read.

The acclamation sings ``My sheep hear my voice'', which John places at the
feast of the Dedication just after ``you do not believe, because you are not of
my sheep'' (Jn 10:26). The Gospel has its own setting in the temple. The chief
priests and ancients have asked Jesus by what authority he acts; he has asked
them whether John's baptism was from heaven, and they would not say. The
parable follows at once, and after it the parable of the wicked husbandmen,
which the chief priests and Pharisees knew ``he spoke of them'' (21:45).

Asked which son did the father's will, they answer ``The first'', and Jesus
turns the first son's words on them in the negative: they ``did not even
afterwards repent'', and the publicans and harlots who believed John go before
them. Not every witness to the text has this order of the sons and this answer; the
Lectionary's text has both.

\subsection{An offering and a table}

The Prayer over the Offerings, \latin{Concede nobis, misericors Deus}, asks
that the offering be accepted and open the fountain of every blessing. In the
prayer from which the Entrance Antiphon comes, Azarias, with no sacrifice left
to offer, asks that the people be accepted ``in a contrite heart and humble
spirit'' (Dan 3:39). At Communion the Missal gives two antiphons. Its rubric
for Ordinary Time prefers the one that agrees with the Gospel; neither of these
is drawn from a Gospel, and the books do not say whether either agrees with
this one.

The servant of Psalm 118 asks God to remember the word in which he gave hope,
his comfort in his lowliness. The verse of John's letter says that God's
charity is known because ``he hath laid down his life for us'' and that ``we
ought to lay down our lives for the brethren'', just before it sets word
against deed, ``not in word nor in tongue, but in deed and in truth'' (1~Jn
3:18). The Prayer after Communion, \latin{Sit nobis, Domine, reparatio mentis et
corporis}, asks that the heavenly mystery repair mind and body, and it joins
sharing the Lord's sufferings to a share in his glory as fellow heirs, the
pairing of Romans 8:17.

\subsection{Three answers to one question}

The Fathers and Doctors who expounded these passages answer the Sunday's
question along three lines, and each line, carried through every text of the
Mass, yields an interpretation of the whole. The first hears in the prophecy
and the parable one law of judgment for every person: God judges each where he
is found, so that no sinner may despair of turning and no just man may rest on
his past. The second follows Jerome, Chrysostom and Aquinas in reading the two
sons as two peoples, keeps the parable's first hearers in view, and meets
Hilary of Poitiers, who assigns the sons the other way round.

The third begins from the second reading: Christ is the Son who obeyed unto
death, and his humble obedience is both pattern and power for the disciple's.
The first two rest on the one correlation the books state, whose titles fall on
repentance, and they are complementary, since they ask who the sons were in the
history of salvation and who they are in every life. The third reads the Mass
from the second reading, under the title \latin{Sentite in vobis}, and from the
Communion antiphons, and it differs in kind; where the shorter form is read, it
keeps only Paul's appeal and the name of Christ's mind. None of these witnesses
expounds this Mass as a whole.
