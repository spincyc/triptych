\section*{Note on Sources and Delivery}

{\small

\textbf{Audience and occasion.} An adult parish assembly, at the Mass of the
Twenty-fifth Sunday in Ordinary Time, Year~A: \latin{Dominica XXV ``per
annum''} of the \work{Roman Missal}, Third Edition, for the dioceses of the
United States, with no.~133 of the \work{Lectionary for Mass}, for the
occurrence of 20~September 2026.

\textbf{Length and pace.} The spoken body runs to 1,482 words: about eleven
and a half minutes at an unhurried 130 words a minute, a little under twelve
at 125, and a little over twelve and a quarter at 120. That is arithmetic on the
word count; no speaker has delivered these words and no delivery has been
timed. The prose was read through in full for sense, sentence length and
ease of speech, silently and not aloud.

\textbf{Relation to the reviewed interpretations.} The argument preached is
the third of the expansive study's three interpretations of this Mass,
\textit{Because I Am Good: The Wage as Gift and the Giver as Reward}, joined
to the second, \textit{Seek the Lord While He May Be Found: The Day as a
Human Life}, from which come Chrysostom's two kinds of hearer and
Augustine's answer to delay. Both take the householder as God, the denarius
as eternal life, and ``my thoughts are not your thoughts'' as a word about
mercy. Where they differ the speech keeps the difference: for Chrysostom no
one murmurs in the kingdom and the complaint is a device of the story, so
the murmur is applied to the hearers and not to the saved. Augustine's
sentence that the saints differ in brightness and are equal in living for
ever stands beside the claim that the wage cannot be divided. Each Father is
credited only with what he says of his own passage. That the psalm's two
words describe the householder's two dealings, that Paul adds hours where
the murmurers reckon theirs, that in the Collect the keeping and the
arriving both stand inside a prayer, and that those who come to Communion
were hired at different hours and receive the same food, are the study's
connections, drawn from the wording of the texts; no Father makes them. Left
to the studies are the first interpretation, the ages of the world; the
saying on first and last, on which Augustine, Chrysostom and Jerome differ;
the Alleluia verse, with what Augustine and Chrysostom each say of Acts
16:14; the Prayer over the Offerings; and the two Communion antiphons.

\textbf{Texts quoted, and their route.} Scripture is quoted in the
Douay--Rheims version as revised by Bishop Challoner, a public-domain study
translation, at Mt 20:12, 13--14 and~15, Isa 55:7--8, Ps 144:8, 17 and~18
(Psalm 145 as the Lectionary numbers it) and Phil 1:21, 23 and~24. It is not
the English proclaimed at Mass, which is that of the \work{Lectionary for
Mass for Use in the Dioceses of the United States of America} and is not
reproduced. The Entrance Antiphon, the Collect and the Prayer after Communion are described
after the study's descriptions of the Latin of the \work{Missale Romanum};
the descriptions are not translations and are not the prayers' words.
Augustine's \work{Sermo} 87 and Chrysostom's homily are quoted in the English
of the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers. Jerome, Aquinas and Augustine's
\work{Enarrationes} are reported without quotation marks, in English that
renders the Latin the study prints; Augustine's sentence on the psalm rests
on the electronic Latin text of augustinus.it alone.

\textbf{Exact loci.} Augustine, ``I have paid thee what I agreed for \ldots\
to bestow a gift'': \work{Sermo} 87.4; the denarius as eternal life, and the
saints unequal in brightness and equal in living for ever: 87.6; against
delay, ``certain as thou art of the reward, but uncertain of the day'':
87.8. Augustine on calling in truth,
\latin{quanto beatior eris, cum seipsum tibi dederit?}: \work{Enarrationes
in Psalmos} 144.22. Chrysostom, ``that place is pure from envy and
jealousy'': \work{Homilies on Matthew} 64.3; the two kinds of hearer: 64.4.
Jerome, \latin{Denarius figuram regis habet \ldots\ imaginem et
similitudinem meam}, and \latin{non tam ipse plus accipere, quam alium nihil
accipere desideras}: \work{Commentarii in Matthaeum} III, PL 26, col.~142.
Aquinas, \latin{nequam est proprie qui de bonitate dolet}: \work{Super
Matthaeum} c.~20, lect.~1, p.~262; \latin{vos ultionem cogitatis, ego
misericordiam}: \work{Super Isaiam} c.~55, p.~556. That the first reading is
chosen in relation to the Gospel: \work{Ordo lectionum Missae},
\work{Praenotanda} 106. Paul a prisoner: Phil 1:7, 13.
