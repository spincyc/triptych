A man had two sons, and a vineyard with work waiting in it. In the words of
the old Douay Bible, he came to the first and said, ``Son, go work to day in
my vineyard.'' And the son said to his face, ``I will not.'' He came to the
second and said the same, and the second gave the answer every father hopes to
hear: ``I go, Sir.''

But the story does not end with the words. ``Afterwards, being moved with
repentance'', the first son ``went''. And the second, the one with the
beautiful answer, ``went not''.

``Which of the two did the father's will?'' A child could answer that. The
first to answer it were the chief priests and the elders of the people, in the
temple. They had asked Jesus by what authority he acted, and he had asked them
in return whether John's baptism was from heaven or from men. They would not
say. So he told them about the two sons and let them be the judges, and they
said, ``The first.'' They had judged themselves. ``Amen I say to you'', Jesus
answers, ``that the publicans and the harlots shall go into the kingdom of God
before you.'' The publicans and the harlots believed John; ``but you, seeing
it, did not even afterwards repent, that you might believe him.''

Listen to that little word, \emph{afterwards}. The story used it of the first
son: afterwards he repented, and went. Now Jesus turns it round on his
hearers: not even afterwards.

Saint John Chrysostom, preaching on this Gospel, states the charge exactly:
``For an evil thing it is not at the first to choose the good, but it is a
heavier charge not even to be brought round.'' These men are not condemned for
once having doubted John, but because they watched the tax collectors and the
prostitutes, the very people they had written off, believe John and change
their lives, and even then would not move.

Yet even this hard saying has a door in it. Chrysostom explains: ``the word,
`go before you,' is not as though these were following, but as having a hope,
if they were willing.'' The chief priests may still follow.

\bigskip

The first reading gives the same answer through the prophet Ezekiel. He lived
among the captives, far from home, and he answered a proverb going round the
land of Israel: ``The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the teeth of the
children are set on edge.'' We are paying for our parents' sins, they meant,
and nothing we do can change it. When the Lord told them otherwise, they
complained: ``The way of the Lord is not right.''

The Lord answers with two cases. The just man who turns away from his justice
and commits iniquity shall die in it. The wicked man who turns away from his
wickedness, and does judgment and justice, ``shall save his soul alive''.
Saint Jerome puts the whole chapter in one sentence: each will be judged in
that in which he is found. Not by his first word. Not by his old record. By
where he is standing when the Lord finds him. And the Lord says why: ``Is it
my will that a sinner should die, saith the Lord God, and not that he should
be converted from his ways, and live?''

The Lord's way cuts in both directions, and Chrysostom, on the parable, gives
two warnings in one breath: ``Let no man then of them that live in vice
despair; let no man who lives in virtue slumber.''

\bigskip

So which son are we?

Most of us here have given the second son's answer: at the font, in our
promises and our vows, and every time we answer Amen. We are the people of ``I
go, Sir.'' Saint Thomas Aquinas turned the second son on the Church's own, on
clerics and religious who profess and do not do: a double sin, he says, of
disobedience and of a broken vow. It is not a long step from the cloister to
the pew.

But there is a first son in every one of us as well: the part of us that has
said, in one way or another, I will not. For him this Gospel is good news,
because afterwards is still possible. Chrysostom tells us how God receives the
one who turns: ``God is not as man, for He reproaches us not with the past,
neither doth He say, Why wast thou absent so long a time? when we repent.''
That is what today's psalm asks: ``The sins of my youth and my ignorances do
not remember.'' And Saint Augustine hears in that psalm the voice of one who
cannot find the way back alone: ``I cannot by myself return, unless Thou
meetest the wanderer.''

There is one danger in all this mercy, and Augustine names it. A man says to
himself: God has promised to forgive me whenever I turn, so I can turn later.
Augustine grants him the promise, and asks, ``who hath promised thee a
to-morrow?'' In the same sermon he says: ``\,`To-morrow, To-morrow;' is the
raven's croak. Moan plaintively as the dove, and beat thy breast.'' Aquinas
noticed the small word in the father's command, go work \emph{to day}: today,
he says, as if to say, through the whole time of your life. The call stands
over the whole of a life, and no one has told you how long your life will be.

\bigskip

But that is only half of this Mass. Look once more at the two sons. Neither of
them gave the father both the word and the deed. Is there a son who says yes,
and goes?

Saint Paul answers in the second reading. He asks the Philippians for
humility, and gives the reason: ``let this mind be in you, which was also in
Christ Jesus.'' Then he goes straight on to say what that mind did. Christ,
``being in the form of God'', ``emptied himself, taking the form of a
servant''. ``He humbled himself, becoming obedient unto death, even to the
death of the cross.'' ``He became obedient as a Son to His Father'', says
Chrysostom, and in that very act he was greatly honouring the Father.

Here is the Son whose yes was done, all the way to the cross.

He is not only an example to copy. He is God, who came down to where we were.
Augustine says that ``proud man would have perished eternally, had he not been
found by the lowly God''. The one who said yes came out to find those who had
said no.

That is why the prayer at the start of this Mass could say something so bold:
that God shows his almighty power above all in sparing and in mercy. Blessed
Ildefonso Schuster, Archbishop of Milan, wrote that to bring back a sinner
asks of God ``a~condescension and powerful energy \ldots\ such as the creation
of the world itself never once called for''.

\bigskip

That is the Son we meet at this altar. When we bring our gifts and ask God to
accept them, we bring the turned heart of the first son, and the Son who
obeyed unto death takes it into his own offering.

One of the two verses the Missal gives for Communion today is John's: ``he
hath laid down his life for us: and we ought to lay down our lives for the
brethren.'' Augustine, reading a proverb about sitting at a ruler's table,
asks: ``what is the table of the ruler, but that from which we take the body
and blood of Him who laid down His life for us? And what is it to sit thereat,
but to approach in humility?''

That sounds far beyond us. ``Do not too soon despair of thyself'', Augustine
told his people. It begins close at hand: ``If thou art not yet equal to the
dying for thy brother, be thou even now equal to the giving of thy means to
thy brother.'' To one who claims the Christian name without its work he says,
``Thou hast the name, and hast not the deeds.'' Set that beside today's
Gospel, and you have the second son.

And after Communion we will ask that this mystery from heaven repair us, mind
and body, and make us fellow heirs of his glory. A turning that has begun
still needs mending, and he mends it here.

\bigskip

So carry one thing out of this church today. Somewhere in your life there is
an ``I go, Sir'' that you said and have not kept: a promise to God, a promise
to someone you love, a confession you keep meaning to make, a brother in need
you keep meaning to help. Do not answer it with the raven's tomorrow. Go
today. The Father will not ask why you were absent so long. You will find him
already coming out to meet you, in the Son who said yes for you, all the way
to the cross.

The call is spoken to every one of us, daughter and son alike, for the whole
of our lives: ``Son, go work to day in my vineyard.''
