\runninghead{One Wage across the Ages}
\properlane{economy}{One Wage across the Ages: The Day as the History of Salvation}
\label{sec:one-wage-across-the-ages}

Heard in its widest sense, the householder's day is the whole history of the
world. God has been calling labourers into his vineyard since the first just
man, Israel through the long day and the nations at its end, and at evening he
gives them all the one reward, which is eternal life. The parable then declares
that the late comers' equal share is God's faithfulness to his own purpose and
no wrong done to those who came before.

\subsection{Five hours, five ages}

Gregory the Great preached on this Gospel to the people in the basilica of
St~Lawrence, and his opening move is to identify the vineyard. The householder is the Creator, and
his vineyard is the universal Church, \latin{ab Abel iusto usque ad ultimum
electum}, from Abel the just to the last of the elect who will be born at the
end of the world; every saint she has brought forth is a branch she has put
out. The hours are then the ages in which God has never ceased to send
workers to tend it: morning from Adam to Noah, the third hour from Noah to
Abraham, the sixth from Abraham to Moses, the ninth from Moses to the coming of
the Lord, and the eleventh \latin{ab adventu Domini usque ad finem mundi}, from
the Lord's coming to the end of the world, the hour in which the apostles were
sent. God worked his vineyard first through the fathers, then through the
teachers of the law and the prophets, and last through the apostles. The
workers of the first four hours Gregory takes together as the ancient Hebrew
people, who in their elect strove from the world's beginning to worship God
with right faith. At the eleventh hour the Gentiles are called, and it is they
who answer that no man has hired them: \latin{Nullus quippe ad eos patriarcha,
nullus propheta venerat}, for no patriarch and no prophet had come to them
(\work{Homiliae in Evangelia} 19.1).

Augustine divides the day in nearly the same way and for the same purpose.
``The first righteous men, as Abel, and Noe, called as it were at the first
hour'', then Abraham, Isaac and Jacob at the third, Moses and Aaron at the
sixth, the holy prophets at the ninth, and ``in the end of the world all
Christians, called as it were at the eleventh hour''. Of each group he repeats
one refrain: they ``will receive together with us the blessedness of the
resurrection'' (\work{Sermo} 87.5). Jerome knows the scheme as the exposition of
others. \latin{Sunt qui hanc parabolam aliter edisserant}, he writes, there
are those who expound this parable differently, and he sets out their hours:
Adam and the patriarchs to Noah, Noah to Abraham and the circumcision given
him, Abraham to Moses and the giving of the Law, Moses himself and the
prophets, and at the eleventh hour \latin{apostolos et gentium populum, quibus
omnes invident}, the apostles and the people of the nations, whom all the
others envy. He adds that the evangelist John understood this when, near
sunset, he wrote, ``Little children, it is the last hour'' (1~Jn 2:18;
\work{Commentarii in Matthaeum} III, PL 26, 141). Aquinas gives the ages of
the world as one of two senses of the day, and at the householder's words ``I
will also give to this last'' he glosses without hesitation: \latin{huic
novissimo, idest gentili}, to this last, that is, to the Gentile, citing
St~Paul: ``What then? Do we excel them? No, not so'' (Rom 3:9; \work{Super
Matthaeum} c.~20, lect.~1).

They do not draw the lines in the same places. Gregory and the expositors
Jerome reports begin with Adam; Augustine begins with Abel. Gregory runs the
ninth hour to the coming of the Lord, Augustine gives it to the prophets, and
Jerome's report gives it to Moses and the prophets together; Aquinas divides
at David, giving the sixth hour to the time from Abraham to David and the
ninth to the time from David to Christ. On the workers of
the eleventh hour they differ in emphasis. For Gregory they are the Gentiles,
to whom no prophet had come, and they are also everyone who has come into the
world since the Mediator; for Augustine they are all Christians; for Jerome's
report and for Aquinas they are the people of the nations. None of this
touches what the four hold in common: the one householder has been at work
through every age, and the whole company of the just receives one wage.

\subsection{Why the last are paid first, and what the first complain of}

If all the just rise together, how can the last be paid before the first?
Augustine answers with a calculation. Whoever receives his wage after one hour
has, in a sense, received before the man who waited twelve. The first
righteous have waited through the ages for the resurrection, and Christians
wait a short time; ``though we all receive together, yet we seem to have
received first, because our hire will not tarry long in coming'' (87.5). The
wage itself admits no more and less, for ``that denarius is life eternal, and
in the life eternal all will be equal''. The saints will differ in brightness,
one shining with wedded chastity and another with virginity, one with the
fruit of good works and another with the crown of martyrdom, but ``in respect
to the living for ever, this man will not live more than that, nor that than
this'' (87.6). Aquinas makes the same distinction. Beatitude is one in its object and unequal in the share each has of
it, as many who go to one river carry away more or less according to the
vessel each brings; the soul that charity has enlarged receives more.

The murmuring is harder, since no one who receives the kingdom can grumble at
it. Gregory refers it to the long delay of the just who lived before Christ.
However rightly they had lived, they were not brought to the kingdom until he
came down who opened paradise to men by his death; their murmuring is that
long wait, and they receive the denarius after it when they pass to the joys
of the kingdom. Then he places himself and his hearers in the parable:
\latin{Nos autem qui ad undecimam venimus}, we who have come at the eleventh
hour do not murmur after our labour, because, coming into this world
\latin{post Mediatoris adventum}, after the Mediator's coming, we are led to
the kingdom as soon as we leave the body (19.4). Jerome explains the grievance
as envy, and he observes that the logic of the complaint convicts every hour in
turn. If the householder is unjust, he is unjust not to one but to all, since
the man of the third hour also worked less than the man of the first, and the
man of the sixth less than he. \latin{Omnis itaque retro vocatio gentibus
invidet, et in Evangelii torquetur gratia}: every earlier calling envies the
Gentiles and is tormented by the grace of the Gospel. The closing saying then
means for him that the Jews are turned from head to tail and \latin{nos de
cauda}, we from the tail, to the head (PL 26, 141). Aquinas records both
explanations, Gregory's and the one he attributes to Hilary and Jerome, that
the people of the Jews murmured because the Gentile was made their equal.

Gregory does not let the scheme flatter his congregation. The Gentiles could
plead that no one had hired them. What, he asks, shall we say in our excuse,
who came to the faith almost from our mother's womb and heard the words of
life from the cradle (19.1)? In the history of salvation his hearers belong to
the last hour; in their own lives most of them were called at the first. At
that point the parable passes to the second interpretation of the formulary.

\subsection{The householder goes out}

Augustine gives the parable's verb a meaning that carries this interpretation beyond
the Gospel. The householder ``went out'' to hire. One who is inside his house
is hidden from those outside; when he goes out he is seen. ``So Christ is in
secret, as long as He is not known and acknowledged; but when He is
acknowledged, He hath gone out to hire labourers.'' On the cross he lay hid;
after the resurrection and the sending of the Spirit, when the hundred and
twenty ``began to speak with the tongues of all nations'', then ``was the
calling manifest, now He went out to hire''. Now the Church herself speaks in
the tongues of all nations: ``what tongue has not the Christian religion
reached?'' (87.9). The eleventh hour, on this account, is the hour of the
mission, and the call reaches the idle through the voices of those who are
sent: ``we are servants of his household, we are sent to hire labourers.''

\subsection{A pardon wide enough for the nations}

Jerome reads the first reading against the same horizon. The prophet, he says,
is warning his own people after the \latin{turba gentilium}, the crowd of the
Gentiles, has taken up the mercies of David that they refused: \latin{dum
tempus est, agite poenitentiam}. When the Lord then says that his thoughts are
not our thoughts, Jerome hears an answer to disbelief about the reach of
pardon. Do not think it incredible, the Lord says, that the impious and the
unjust can be saved, \latin{vel populum Iudaeorum, vel omnes qui in gentibus
nesciebant Deum}, whether the people of the Jews or all those among the
nations who did not know God. And on the verses about the rain and the word
that follow the reading he adds: \latin{Non sit incredulus populus nationum},
let not the people of the nations disbelieve that after such crimes the impious
is suddenly saved (\work{In Isaiam} XV, PL 24, 553--555). Jerome does not
mention the parable. The Lectionary sets the two passages side by side, and
what he says of the one is what Gregory and Augustine say of the other: God's
ways are above ours in extending his covenant to those who stood outside it.
The chapter itself points the same way. The verse before the reading
promises, ``thou shalt call a nation, which thou knewest not: and the nations
that knew not thee shall run to thee'' (55:5), and the next chapter brings
``the children of the stranger that adhere to the Lord'' into a house that
``shall be called the house of prayer, for all nations'' (56:6--7).

The psalm gives this interpretation its word, and the word is ``all''. ``The Lord is
sweet to all: and his tender mercies are over all his works''; he is ``just
in all his ways'' and ``nigh unto all them that call upon him''. Bellarmine
insists that the first of these is meant without restriction: ``The
expression, `The Lord is sweet to all,' is absolutely true, because God
`maketh his sun to rise upon the good and the bad'\,''. The psalm's own horizon
is as long as the householder's day. Between the first and second of the
appointed strophes it sings, ``Generation and generation shall praise thy
works'' (v.~4), and between the second and third, ``Thy kingdom is a kingdom
of all ages'' (v.~13).

\subsection{Philippi: a purple-seller and an apostle of the nations}

Two texts of the Mass concern Philippi, the Alleluia verse, whose basis is set
there, and the letter addressed to its church, and both belong to the eleventh
hour as this interpretation understands it. The Alleluia verse is formed from
the conversion of Lydia, a
Gentile who worshipped God, in a Roman colony of Macedonia (Acts 16:12--14).
St~Bede sees in her more than one convert. \latin{Significat namque
Ecclesiam}, he writes, she signifies the Church: a seller of purple, because
the Church gives her blood for Christ to buy eternal life, and one who
constrained the apostle to stay, \latin{quia repulsam a Iudaeis Evangelii
praedicationem Ecclesia interna devotione cordis suscepit}, because the Church
received with the inward devotion of the heart the preaching of the Gospel
that the Jews had driven away (\work{Retractatio in Actus apostolorum} 16, PL
92, 1025). When the assembly asks that its own heart be opened, it takes the
place of the woman in whom the Church of the nations first listened.

The second reading shows the labourer who was sent to that city. No Father
read here connects Paul's dilemma with the parable; the connection lies in the
facts of the letter. Paul, whom Augustine calls ``specially sent as an apostle
to the Gentiles'' (\work{In Iohannis evangelium tractatus} 47.5), writes to a
church of the last hour, and the ``fruit of labour'' for which he consents to
go on living is their progress. He has borne the burden of the day and the
heats as few have, and he does not reckon his hours against anyone.

\subsection{One people at the altar}

In this interpretation the Missal's own texts, prayed with the readings of all
three years, serve this Gospel by what they already say. The Entrance Antiphon speaks of one people and
of one Lord who is theirs for ever: \latin{Salus populi ego sum}. The people whose
salvation he is has no second rank. The Collect rests everything the sacred law
laid down on the love of God and of neighbour. The law given to those who were
hired first is thus not set aside when the wage is given to the last; it is
gathered into its two loves, and those are asked of every labourer at every
hour. The Prayer over the Offerings speaks of the gifts of God's people,
\latin{tuae plebis}, in the singular: one people brings them, and what that
people professes in faith it is to grasp in the sacraments. The first Communion
antiphon puts on the lips of the last-called the ancient word that the
commandments are to be kept ``most diligently''. The Prayer after Communion
names what the labourers have in hand before the evening comes, the effect of
redemption, the one wage already begun in mystery and in conduct.

The second Communion antiphon brings its own witnesses. The verse sung is
John 10:14, and two verses later the shepherd says, ``other sheep I have that
are not of this fold: them also I must bring \ldots\ and there shall be one
fold and one shepherd.'' Gregory hears his own history in that sentence: the
Lord had in view \latin{redemptionem nostram, qui ex gentili populo venimus},
the redemption of us who have come from the Gentile people, and he makes
\latin{ex duobus gregibus unum ovile}, one fold out of two flocks, joining the
Jewish and the Gentile people in his faith, as Paul says, ``he is our peace,
who hath made both one'' (\work{Homiliae in Evangelia} 14.4). Augustine notes
that Christ came in person only to Israel and to the Gentiles ``only sent'',
and he will not let that be a lesser gift: ``He Himself was in those whom He
sent'', and ``it is His voice that is heard in those whom He sends''. Of the
two flocks, ``as of two walls, is the corner-stone formed'' (\work{Tractatus}
47.4--5). Both comments concern verse~16, which is the antiphon's context and
not the appointed verse: the sheep he knows are drawn from both peoples, and
he feeds them at one table.

\subsection{A parable's details, and how far they may be pressed}

Read so, the parable becomes an allegory of its own details, and the
difficulties come from the witnesses themselves. Chrysostom lays down a rule
of method: ``The saying is a parable, wherefore neither is it right to inquire
curiously into all things in parables word by word, but when we have learnt
the object for which it was composed, to reap this, and not to busy one's self
about anything further'' (\work{Homilies on Matthew} 64.3). He states the rule
about the murmuring, and he invokes it a second time about the excuse of the
last-hired: ``And if they say, `No man hath hired us,' in the first place as I
said we must not be curious about all the points in the parables; but here
neither is the good man of the house represented to say this, but they \ldots\
For that He called all, as far as lay in Him, from the first even the parable
shows'' (64.3). He does not apply the rule to the hours, to which he gives a
meaning of his own, the ages of a life, and he does not mention the reading of
them as the ages of the world. His second application nevertheless meets this
interpretation at a point on which it leans. Gregory takes that excuse as the
Gentiles' true plea, that no patriarch and no prophet had come to them, and
Aquinas, where he reads the day as the age of the world, likewise refers the
words to the Gentile people and holds them excused because they had not the
prophets as the Jews had. For Chrysostom the words are the labourers' own and
not the householder's, who leaves them unrefuted because he means to win the
men over, and God called all, as far as lay in him, from the first. That is a
real difference among the witnesses read, and it concerns one sentence of the
parable.

Jerome reports the scheme as the view of others and prefers
to read the hours as the ages of a life. The scheme's own witnesses do not
agree on its details. The ages of the world are therefore a
received allegory of the parable and not its plain sense: the text names no
Abel and no Gentile. What the allegory rests on is the parable's frame, the
saying about first and last, which Chrysostom too takes as ``darkly hinting
at the Jews'', and the larger fact the four expositors see in it, that the God
who called Israel has called the nations to the same life. The Entrance
Antiphon, the orations, the first Communion antiphon and the second reading
take their places in this interpretation by their own wording; the placing is
the editor's, not a commentator's.

\subsection{The four senses of this interpretation}

\begin{fourSenses}
\item[Literal.] A landowner hires workers at five hours of one day, pays the
last as he agreed to pay the first, and defends his right to do so. Isaiah
promises abundant pardon to the wicked who return, because God's ways are not
ours. Paul, imprisoned and uncertain of his sentence, stays at his work for a
young church. Luke records that the Lord opened the heart of a Gentile
woman at Philippi.

\item[Allegorical.] The day is the history of salvation and the vineyard is
the Church from Abel to the last of the elect (Gregory). Christ is the
householder who ``went out'' when he made himself known to the nations after
Pentecost (Augustine). The one denarius is the life he opens to Israel and
to the Gentiles together. Lydia signifies the Church, which took to heart the
preaching the Jews had driven away (Bede), and the two flocks become one fold
under one shepherd (Gregory; Augustine).

\item[Moral.] Those who have long been in the Church do not resent those who
arrive late, and do not count their years of service as a claim on God. Every
earlier calling, Jerome says, envied the Gentiles the grace of the Gospel, and
the same grievance can return in anyone who came before another. Those born
into the faith have less excuse for idleness than those to
whom no prophet came (Gregory). The labourer who has borne the day's burden
stays at his post for the sake of those called late, as Paul does.

\item[Anagogical.] At the evening of the world all the just, from Abel
onward, ``will receive together \ldots\ the blessedness of the resurrection''
(Augustine). They will differ in brightness and be equal in life without end
(Augustine), one beatitude shared according to the measure of each (Aquinas).
The fathers who waited long have already passed, after their delay, to the
joys of the kingdom, and those who come after the Mediator are led to it as
soon as they leave the body (Gregory).
\end{fourSenses}
