\runninghead{Because I Am Good}
\properlane{gift}{Because I Am Good: The Wage as Gift and the Giver as Reward}
\label{sec:because-i-am-good}

The first two interpretations read the parable from its hours. The third reads
it from its evening. The parable turns on the householder's answer to the
complaint, and the answer says three things: the first are wronged in nothing;
what the last receive is no payment but a gift; and the giver's reason is his
own goodness. Four expositors of the Gospel state this of the same verses, and
the rest of the Mass presses the point further, until the wage itself turns out
to be the giver. The \work{Ordo lectionum Missae} marks the same place in the
two readings it correlates. The title it sets over the Gospel is the
householder's question, ``Is thy eye evil, because I am good?'', and the title
it sets over the first reading is the sentence that explains such goodness,
``my thoughts are not your thoughts''. The second reading has a title of its
own, from its own course.

\subsection{Payment to one, gift to another}

Augustine paraphrases the householder's defence at length, and he supplies the
words that the parable leaves unspoken. ``Friend, I do thee no wrong'' means:
``I have paid thee what I agreed for. To this other it is my will not to
render a payment, but to bestow a gift.'' Blame would be just in either of two
cases, if he had taken what was not his or if he had withheld what was owed;
``but when I pay what is due, and give besides to whom I will, neither can he
to whom I owed find fault, and he to whom I gave ought to rejoice the more''
(\work{Sermo} 87.4). His conclusion keeps both halves: ``To the first, it is a
payment; to the other, a free gift; yet the same thing is given alike to
both'' (87.6).

Gregory comes to the same verses by another road and says the same thing in a
sentence. The householder adds, ``is it not lawful for me to do what I
will?'', \latin{quia ipsa regni perceptio eius est bonitas voluntatis},
because the very receiving of the kingdom is the goodness of his will.
\latin{Stulta enim quaestio est hominis contra benignitatem Dei}: it is a
foolish complaint that a man brings against the kindness of God. No one would
have cause to complain if God does not give what he does not owe, but only if
he failed to give what he owed (\work{Homiliae in Evangelia} 19.4).

Aquinas sets out the logic. In his reply the householder shows \latin{iustitiam,
et suam misericordiam}, his justice and his mercy. There is no injury,
\latin{quia quod meum est, do isti, non quod tuum est}: what I give this man
is mine, not yours. If he owed the money to another, or held it under another,
he could not dispose of it; but he is lord. \latin{Balivus enim non potest dare
aliquid nisi secundum merita; Rex autem potest sine meritis: sic Deus, qui est
omnium dominus, potest}. A bailiff can give only according to deserts, a king
can give without them, and so can God, who is lord of all. Nor is the gift
favouritism, for \latin{in eo quod ex misericordia datur, non est acceptio
personarum}: where a thing is given out of mercy there is no respect of
persons, since what is purely mine I may give to whom I will. The murmuring,
then, had no cause in any defect of the master; its only cause was mercy shown
to someone else. Hence the name the householder gives it: \latin{nequam est
proprie qui de bonitate dolet}, he is properly called evil who grieves at
goodness; ``Is thy eye evil'' \latin{eo quod circa te iustitiam ostendi, circa
alterum misericordiam}, because I have shown justice to you and mercy to
another? (\work{Super Matthaeum} c.~20, lect.~1). Chrysostom, whose own
account of the parable belongs to the second interpretation, agrees so far:
the Lord frames the story so as to show ``that the whole is of His love to
man'' (\work{Homilies on Matthew} 64.4).

\subsection{The evil eye}

Jerome describes the grievance with precision. The first-hired accuse the
householder, \latin{non quod minus acceperint quam fuerat constitutum}, not
because they received less than was agreed, but because they wanted to
receive more than those on whom \latin{clementia conductoris}, the mercy of
the one who hired them, had been poured out. The penny answers them by what
it is. \latin{Denarius figuram regis habet}: the denarius bears the figure of
the king. \latin{Recepisti ergo mercedem quam tibi promiseram, hoc est,
imaginem et similitudinem meam: quid quaeris amplius}? You have received the
wage I promised you, that is, my image and likeness; what more do you seek?
And he diagnoses the wish behind the complaint: \latin{non tam ipse plus
accipere, quam alium nihil accipere desideras}, you desire not so much to receive more yourself as that another
should receive nothing, as though the worth of the reward were lessened by
another's sharing it. At the householder's ``Take what is thine, and go thy
way'' he names the terms on which the first-hired are paid: \latin{Iudaeus in
Lege non gratia, sed opere salvatur. Qui enim fecerit eam, vivet in ea}, under
the Law the Jew is saved not by grace but by work, for the one who does it
shall live in it. The second sentence is the Law's own promise to those who
keep it (Lev 18:5, in the form St~Paul cites at Rom 10:5), and Jerome gives it
as the ground of the first. Placed where it is, the remark describes the
contract on which the first-hired are paid. He hears the murmurers' voice
again in Luke's parable, where the elder son envies the younger, will not have
the penitent received, and accuses his father of injustice
(\work{Commentarii in Matthaeum} III, PL 26, 141--142). Chrysostom makes the
same comparison with the elder son, although he will not allow that such a
feeling survives in the kingdom (64.3).

Augustine hears a sentence very like the murmurers' from those who love God
for his gifts and not for himself. When the gifts go elsewhere they ask,
\latin{Quare mihi abstulisti, et illis dedisti?}, why have you taken from me
and given to them?, and in asking it they accuse God of injustice
(\work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 144.22). The evil eye is therefore a way of
looking at God as much as at one's neighbour. It measures him as a paymaster,
and by that measure his generosity to another is a loss to oneself.

\subsection{The wage is the giver}

What then is the denarius, if it cannot be made less by being shared? All the
interpretations take it as eternal life. This one asks what that life
consists in. Of the denarius itself Jerome says that the coin is the king's
own image and likeness, and that likeness is the promised wage; Aquinas
repeats that the denarius bears the king's likeness. Two other witnesses say
what the last reward is, each of his own text and neither of the parable.
Augustine, at the psalm's promise that the Lord is near to all who call upon him in truth, says
that to call in truth is to want the giver more than his gifts: \latin{quanto
beatior eris, cum seipsum tibi dederit?}, how much more blessed will you be
when he gives you himself? (144.22). Gregory, preaching on the Good Shepherd,
names the pasture that the sheep find at last: \latin{Pascua namque electorum
sunt vultus praesens Dei}, the pasture of the elect is the present face of
God, and the mind that beholds it without failing is fed without end on the
food of life (\work{Homiliae in Evangelia} 14.5). A reward of that kind cannot
be divided into a larger and a smaller sum. It can only be received, by each
according to his capacity, and it is the same for the one who bore the heat of
the day and for the one who came at dusk.

\subsection{A justice that wills mercy}

The first reading gives the reason for the householder's conduct before the
Gospel narrates it. ``Let the wicked forsake his way \ldots\ for he is
bountiful to forgive. For my thoughts are not your thoughts.'' Aquinas opens
the contrast in a single antithesis, \latin{Vos impii, ego pius; vos ultionem
cogitatis, ego misericordiam}: you are impious, I am kind; you think of
requital, I of mercy (\work{Super Isaiam} c.~55). Jerome lets the Lord speak in
the first person: \latin{Ego enim clementissimus sum, et multus ad
ignoscendum}, for I am most merciful, and great in forgiving (PL 24, 555). The
labourers who expected more had reasoned correctly by the thoughts of men: more
hours, more pay. The householder's reckoning is as far above theirs as the
heavens above the earth. The chapter has already said what kind of commerce
this is: ``come ye, buy wine and milk without money, and without any price''
(55:1).

The psalm then holds together the two words the murmurers thought
incompatible. It sings that the Lord is ``gracious and merciful'' and also
that he is ``just in all his ways''. Hilary explains how both can be said:
\latin{habet dei iustitia miserandi voluntatem; neque tamen per miserandi
voluntatem ita a se erit demutata, ne iustitia sit. humana autem iustitia solet
in id devenire, ne misericors sit}. God's justice has the will to show mercy,
and yet by that will it is not so changed from itself as to cease to be
justice; human justice, on the other hand, commonly arrives at being
unmerciful (\work{Tractatus super Psalmos} 144.14). Hilary is speaking of the
psalm and says nothing of the parable, but his sentence describes the
householder's two dealings exactly. With the first he keeps his contract to the
penny, which is justice, as Bellarmine glosses the psalm's seventeenth verse:
``he renders to all what is due to them''. With the last he is generous, and
his generosity takes nothing from his justice. The murmurers speak for the
human justice that has no room for mercy. Augustine draws from the same verse
the posture that befits the hearer: praise his justice, and accuse your own
injustice (144.21). And at the psalm's formula of mercy he carries the
principle of gift as far as it will go: consider \latin{quid dederit qui
gratis dedit}, what he has given who gave freely, pardon, the spirit of
justification, charity, and beyond these eternal life and the company of the
angels, \latin{totum de
misericordia}, all of it from mercy. \latin{Merita tua nusquam iactes, quia et
ipsa tua merita illius dona sunt}: nowhere boast of your merits, because your
merits themselves are his gifts (144.11, PL 37, 1876).

\subsection{To live is Christ}

The second reading shows a labourer for whom the wage is a person. ``To me,
to live is Christ: and to die is gain.'' Paul already possesses what he
desires and desires what he already possesses, and his only hesitation is
between having Christ more fully and serving him longer. Aquinas raises the
obvious objection: the desire to be with Christ springs from the love of God,
the desire to remain from the love of neighbour, and the first is the greater;
why then does Paul incline to the second? He answers by distinguishing two
loves of God. \latin{Duplex est dilectio Dei}. By one a man wants to enjoy God
and delight in him, \latin{et hoc est bonum hominis}, and this is the man's
own good. By the other, the love of friendship, a man \latin{praeponit honorem
Dei, etiam huic delectationi, qua fruitur Deo, et haec est perfecta charitas}:
he puts God's honour before even the delight of enjoying God, and this is
perfect charity. Paul chose the more perfect, as when he wished to be
``anathema from Christ, for my brethren'' (Rom 9:3; \work{Super Philippenses}
c.~1, lect.~3). Chrysostom gives the same account: Paul remains ``for Christ's
sake, that I may render more loving unto Him those whom I have made his
servants''; he sought, as he says elsewhere, not ``that which profited
myself'' but his neighbour (\work{Homilies on Philippians}~4).

Neither author mentions the parable, and Paul's place in it is the editor's.
No hour is given him: he is a labourer who has borne the burden of the day
and who, offered his wage, asks for a longer day so that others may come to
the same pay. The murmurers reckon their hours
against the late comers; Paul adds hours for their sake. He can do so because
for him the wage was never a sum. A man who works for Christ's honour loses
nothing when Christ is generous to someone else.

\subsection{Asking for what is commanded}

If the wage is a gift, what of the work? Augustine carries the interpretation a
step further, and the first Communion antiphon is his text. The psalmist has just
said that God commanded his commandments to be kept most diligently, and he
does not go on to say that he has kept them. He says \latin{Utinam}, ``O!
that''. Augustine comments: when you hear \latin{Utinam}, recognise the voice
of one who wishes, and lay down the pride of one who presumes; for who wishes
for what he has in his own power? \latin{Ergo si optat homo quod praecipit
Deus; ut det ipse quod praecipit, rogandus est Deus}: if a man wishes for what
God commands, then God must be asked to give what he commands. He aims the
remark at those who hold that God helps us to justice only by making his
precepts known, leaving us to fulfil them by our own strength
(\work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 118, s.~4.2, PL 37, 1510). Hilary says the same
of the same verse: \latin{nisi a deo dirigamur, infirmes per naturam nostram
erimus. adiuvandi igitur per gratiam eius dirigendique sumus}, unless we are
directed by God we shall be weak by our nature; we need therefore to be helped
and directed by his grace (\work{Tractatus super Psalmos} 118, Aleph~12).
Bellarmine takes the wish first as the king's example of obedience and brings
grace in afterwards: the law makes the just more just, but the first
justification ``cannot be ascribed to the law, but to grace'', and the
psalmist's next prayer is ``withhold not your grace and your assistance,
without which I can do nothing''. The difference is one of emphasis. The
Collect prays in the same manner. Its petition is that those who keep God's
precepts may come to eternal life, so that the keeping and the arriving both
stand inside a prayer.

The Alleluia verse takes the second step, which among the authors read only
Augustine states. Writing on the predestination of the saints, he argues from
St~Paul's request that God ``may open unto us a door of speech'' (Col 4:3) that ``even
the very beginning of faith is the gift of God. For it would not be sought
for from Him in prayer, unless it were believed to be given by Him.'' Lydia is
his example: ``This gift of heavenly grace had descended to that seller of
purple \ldots\ for she was so called that she might believe'' (\work{De
praedestinatione sanctorum} 20.41). The liturgical verse is a prayer of
exactly that kind. Luke narrates that the Lord opened one woman's heart; the
Lectionary has the whole assembly ask him to open theirs; and by Augustine's
argument a Church that asks for the opening believes that the opening is
given. The petition is the Lectionary's adaptation and not Luke's wording, and
Augustine's argument is made from Colossians, with the verse of Acts as its
instance.

Chrysostom comes to the verse as a preacher going through Luke's narrative,
and he reads it differently. At ``Whose heart the Lord opened'' he says:
``Therefore we need God, to open the heart: but God opens the hearts that are
willing: for there are hardened hearts to be seen.'' At ``she attended'' he
divides the sentence: ``The opening, then, was God's work, the attending was
hers: so that it was both God's doing and man's'' (\work{Homilies on
Acts}~35). He does not say where the willingness comes from, and he argues
there against no one; his concern is what the verse gives to God and what it
gives to Lydia. On the parable he speaks in the same way: God called all from
the first, and ``the difference was made by the disposition of them that were
called''. Augustine's sermon on the parable does not take up the question why
some come late; its nearest word is that the householder's ``going out'' is
Christ's making himself known. The two therefore meet only at Acts 16:14, and
the difference between their readings of it is real. Chrysostom speaks of
hearts that are willing and assigns the opening to God and the attending to
Lydia. Augustine counts the very beginning of faith a gift and says that Lydia
was called so that she might believe. Bede, the one later author read on the
verse, passes over the clause in silence. The second step therefore rests on
Augustine's argument, with Chrysostom's different reading of the verse
beside it. It does not reach the first step or the claim with which
this interpretation began, which Chrysostom states for himself: the parable
shows ``that the whole is of His love to man''.

\subsection{The shepherd who feeds with himself}

The second Communion antiphon sets the Gospel's householder beside another
figure who does not calculate. ``I am the good shepherd: and I know mine, and
mine know me.'' Gregory begins from the shepherd's goodness, which is not an
added gift but his essence, and from its proof: he laid down his life for his
sheep \latin{ut in sacramento nostro corpus suum et sanguinem verteret, et
oves quas redemerat, carnis suae alimento satiaret}, that he might turn his
body and blood into our sacrament and satisfy with the food of his own flesh
the sheep he had redeemed (\work{Homiliae in Evangelia} 14.1).
Against him stands the hireling, who feeds the Lord's sheep \latin{non pro
amore intimo \ldots\ sed ad temporales mercedes}, not out of inward love but
for temporal wages (14.2). The contrast is the parable's own. The labourer who
counts his hours against his neighbour's has taken the hireling's view of the
vineyard, and the shepherd gives what no contract could oblige, himself.
Augustine tells his people why they listen to Christ in their preacher:
``Because you are the sheep of Christ, purchased with the blood of Christ. You
acknowledge your own price.'' The ``ruler's table'' of which Scripture speaks, he says, is
the altar: ``you there find the body and blood of Christ; let him who comes to
such a table be ready with similar provision'' (\work{In Iohannis evangelium
tractatus} 47.2). Aquinas explains the possessive in ``mine'': \latin{meae,
inquam, praedestinatione, vocatione, et gratia}, mine, I say, by
predestination, calling and grace, as if to say that they follow him because
they love him (\work{Super Ioannem} c.~10, lect.~4). The sheep do not make
themselves his; his knowing, which is his loving, comes first.

The Missal's remaining texts speak in the same order. At the entrance God
names himself his people's salvation before anything is asked of them. The
Prayer over the Offerings asks him to accept their gifts with favour and then
reverses the direction of giving: what they profess by faith they are to
receive through the sacraments. The Prayer after Communion asks that those
whom he has restored may go on being helped; his aid is continual, not a wage
paid once and done with, and the effect of redemption is something held from
him in mystery and in conduct alike. Those who come to Communion at this Mass
have been hired at many different hours, and all are given the same food.

\subsection{The contract that remains}

The parable's own terms give the strongest difficulty. The first-hired have
a contract and are paid on it, so the parable does not say that no one earns
anything. Augustine keeps this, ``to the first, it is a payment''; Aquinas
keeps it, justice to the one and mercy to the other; and Jerome keeps it when
he sets beside ``Take what is thine'' the Law's own promise that the one who
does it shall live in it. Augustine resolves the tension only in his comment
on the psalm, by calling merits themselves God's gifts, and that goes beyond
what the parable states. The evidence for the furthest step alone gives the
second. That the first opening of the heart is God's gift is Augustine's
statement, and his argument is made from Colossians with Lydia as its
instance. Chrysostom, the other ancient author read on Acts
16:14, speaks of hearts that are willing and gives the opening to God and the
attending to Lydia, and he says nothing of where the willingness comes from.
The step rests on Augustine's argument and on no agreement between the two. On
the claim with which the interpretation began they do agree, for Chrysostom
too finds in the parable that the whole is of God's love to man. The
Entrance Antiphon, the Collect, the Prayer over the Offerings and the Prayer
after Communion take their places in this interpretation by their own
wording, and no commentator assigns them.

\subsection{The four senses of this interpretation}

\begin{fourSenses}
\item[Literal.] A householder keeps his contract with the first, chooses to be
generous to the last, and asks whether an eye is evil because he is good. The
prophet grounds God's abundant pardon in thoughts that are not ours. The psalm
calls the same Lord merciful and just. An apostle for whom to live is Christ
consents to live longer for his people's sake.

\item[Allegorical.] The householder's goodness is the goodness of Christ, the
good shepherd. Of the shepherd of John~10 and his sheep, and not of the
parable, Gregory says that he knows his own by loving them and feeds those he
has redeemed with his own body and blood; Aquinas, that the sheep are his by
predestination, calling and grace; and Augustine, that they acknowledge in his
blood their own price and find his body and blood on the altar. Of the
parable's coin Jerome says that it bears the king's figure and that the wage
promised is the king's own image and likeness, and he does not name Christ
there. The likeness given to each labourer alike is Christ.

\item[Moral.] Work without reckoning, as Paul does; ask for what is commanded
(Augustine; Hilary); boast of no merit, since merits are gifts (Augustine);
rejoice at another's undeserved good. To grieve at goodness is the evil eye
(Aquinas), and the wish that another receive nothing is its mark (Jerome).

\item[Anagogical.] The one denarius is God giving himself. Of the coin itself
Jerome says that the wage promised is the king's own image and likeness.
Augustine and Gregory speak each of his own text and neither of the parable:
of the psalm's promise to those who call in truth Augustine asks, ``how much
more blessed will you be when he gives you himself?'', and of the pasture that
the shepherd's sheep find Gregory says that it is the present face of God. A
reward of that kind cannot in itself be more to one and less to another,
though each receives it according to the measure of his charity.
\end{fourSenses}
