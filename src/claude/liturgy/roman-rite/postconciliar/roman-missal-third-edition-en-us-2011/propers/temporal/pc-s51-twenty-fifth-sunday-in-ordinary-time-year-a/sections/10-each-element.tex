\section{Each Element in Its Setting}
\label{sec:each-element}

The texts of this Mass come from two books with separate histories. The
antiphons and orations belong to the Missal's Twenty-fifth Week in Ordinary
Time and are prayed with the readings of all three years; the readings, psalm
and acclamation belong to the Lectionary's Year~A. Each element has its own
literary setting, textual facts and first commentators.

\subsection{The Lord's oracle at the entrance}

The Entrance Antiphon, \latin{Salus populi ego sum, dicit Dominus}, is spoken
in God's own person. He names himself the salvation of the people; he promises
to hear them from whatever tribulation they cry out of; he declares himself
their Lord for ever. The Missal prints no scriptural reference beside it, in
Latin or in English, and it is not a verse of Scripture. The official
\work{Antiphonary} identifies Ps 37 (36):39--40 as the text's basis, but in the
Clementine Vulgate that psalm shares with
the antiphon only the words \latin{salus} and \latin{tribulationis}. Closer
phrases stand elsewhere in the Psalter: \latin{Salus tua ego sum} (Ps 35
(34):3), \latin{clamaverunt \ldots\ exaudivit \ldots\ ex omnibus
tribulationibus} (Ps 34 (33):18) and \latin{clamabit ad me, et ego exaudiam eum
\ldots\ in tribulatione} (Ps 91 (90):15). None of them is quoted: the antiphon is
a composed oracle, and the Antiphonary's relation is an identified basis rather
than an adaptation. Ildefonso Schuster, who knew it as an Introit of the earlier Roman
Missal sung at the two stational Masses in the basilica of Saints Cosmas and
Damian, thought that it ``emanates perhaps from the Itala'' (\work{The
Sacramentary}, vol.~3, p.~171). The Mass therefore
begins before anyone has asked for anything: God speaks first, and what he
says is a promise to hear.

\subsection{The Collect: the law and its two loves}

The Collect, \latin{Deus, qui sacrae legis omnia constituta}, closes the
Introductory Rites after the Gloria. Its address says that God has made all
that the sacred law lays down rest on the love of himself and of neighbour,
which recalls the Lord's answer about the great commandment: ``On these two
commandments dependeth the whole law and the prophets'' (Mt 22:40). Its
petition is that those who keep God's precepts may come to eternal life. The
prayer thus holds together three things the Gospel of the day will also hold
together: a law to be kept, a love that is the law's whole content, and a life
that is the end of both.

\subsection{Isaiah 55: the summons and its reason}

The first reading stands in the chapter that opens with a free invitation,
``come ye, buy wine and milk without money, and without any price'' (55:1),
and promises ``an everlasting covenant \ldots\ the faithful mercies of David''
(55:3). After the appointed verses the chapter compares God's word to the rain
that ``shall not return to me void'' (55:10--11), and the next chapter admits
the stranger and the eunuch to the house of prayer (56:3--8). Within that
frame the four verses make one movement. Verses 6--7 summon: seek, call,
forsake, return, ``for he is bountiful to forgive''. Verses 8--9 give the
reason, and the word ``for'' binds it to the promise of pardon: God forgives
abundantly because his thoughts are not ours.

St~Jerome and St~Thomas Aquinas both read the contrast of thoughts in that
way, as a statement about mercy and not about divine inscrutability in
general. Jerome paraphrases the Lord: do not think what I promise difficult or
incredible, \latin{impium et iniquum posse salvari} (\work{In Isaiam} XV, PL
24, 554). He applies Romans 11:33 to God's thoughts, ``How incomprehensible are
his judgments'', precisely as the counsel by which the impious are saved.
Aquinas compresses the same reading into one line: \latin{Vos ultionem
cogitatis, ego misericordiam}, you think of requital, I of mercy
(\work{Super Isaiam} c.~55). The three interpretations of the Mass differ over which half of the
passage carries the weight: the reach of the pardon, the shortness of the time,
or the mercy that is higher than requital.

\subsection{Psalm 145 (144): the praise of a king}

The psalm, headed in the Douay--Rheims ``Praise, for David himself'', is an
alphabetical hymn addressed to ``God my king''. The Lectionary takes three
pairs of verses from it: daily praise of a greatness that has no end (2--3),
the formula of mercy (8--9), and the Lord just in all his ways and near to all
who call (17--18). St~Robert
Bellarmine reads the whole psalm as the praise of a king's virtues and finds
them in these very verses: greatness without end in every dimension, mercy
made continuous by patience, justice that ``renders to all what is due to
them'', and ease of access, for the Lord ``comes nigh at once to all that call
upon him, never refusing an audience to anyone'' (\work{Commentary on the
Psalms}, Ps 144).

The last words of the third strophe, ``in truth'', are glossed in three ways.
For St~Augustine those who call in truth are those who want God himself and
not only his gifts (\work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 144.22). For St~Hilary of Poitiers God is near to all by
nature, since ``in him we live and move and are'' (Acts 17:28), and to call
in truth is to call \latin{non ex otio \ldots\ neque ex infidelis ambiguitatis
incerto}, neither idly nor out of the uncertainty of a doubting faith
(\work{Tractatus super Psalmos} 144.21). For Bellarmine the phrase gathers all
the conditions of prayer: faith, hope, at least the beginning of charity,
desire and attention. The appointed
verses stop short of the psalm's next line, ``he will hear their prayer, and
save them'' (v.~19), which says again what the Entrance Antiphon promised.

\subsection{Philippians 1: a prisoner's choice}

Paul writes in chains (Phil 1:7, 13) and does not know how his case will end.
The reading enters at his confidence that Christ shall ``be magnified in my
body, whether it be by life or by death'', follows his dilemma through
verse~24, omits the two verses in which he resolves it (``I know that I shall
abide and continue with you all'', 1:25), and adds the opening command of the
exhortation that runs to 2:4. It is the first of four Sundays given to this
letter, and its place here belongs to that course.

St~John Chrysostom explains the two ways of magnifying: by life, because
Christ delivers Paul from danger; by death, because ``even death itself could
not persuade me to deny Him''. ``To me, to live is Christ'' he reads through
Galatians 2:20: ``I live not, he says, the common life''; as long as Christ is
with him, ``even though death overtake me, still I live''. Death is gain
``because I shall more clearly be present with Him; so that my death is
rather a coming to life'' (\work{Homilies on Philippians}~3). Aquinas gives the
same two ways, \latin{In corpore nostro Christus dupliciter magnificatur}: by
devoting the body to his service, which is by life, and by exposing it for
him, which is by death. He then asks what it means to call anything one's
life. A man's life is what moves him as his end, as hunters call the hunt
their life and friends their friend; Christ is Paul's life \latin{quia solus
Christus movebat eum}, because Christ alone moved him. Death is gain because it
perfects a life that here is imperfect. The dilemma has two movers, \latin{In
homine enim duplex est motus, naturae, sc.\ et gratiae}: nature shrinks from
death, and grace, which is charity, draws in two directions, to be with Christ
and \latin{ad profectum proximi}, toward the neighbour's progress (\work{Super
Philippenses} c.~1, lect.~3). On verse~27 Chrysostom presses the word ``only'':
``this, and nought else, is the only thing we should seek''
(\work{Homilies on Philippians}~4).

\subsection{The Alleluia verse: Lydia's heart made a prayer}

By the acclamation before the Gospel the assembly greets the Lord who is about
to speak to it (\work{General Instruction of the Roman Missal} 62). This
Sunday's verse is formed from Acts 16:14. At Philippi, on the sabbath, by the
riverside where prayer was made, Paul spoke to the women gathered there, and
of Lydia, a seller of purple from Thyatira who worshipped God, Luke says that
the Lord opened her heart ``to attend to those things which were said by
Paul''. She was baptized with her household, and her house became the meeting
place of the brethren (Acts 16:15, 40). The liturgical verse changes Luke's
grammar. What he narrates of one woman the assembly asks for itself, and the
words to be heeded become those of the Son. Acts 16:14 belongs to Luke's
account of the founding of the church to which the second reading is
addressed, so that the verse's basis and the letter both concern Philippi.

\subsection{Matthew 20: the parable and its frame}

The parable is an answer. The rich young man has gone away sad; Peter has
asked, ``Behold we have left all things, and have followed thee: what
therefore shall we have?'' (19:27); the Lord has promised the twelve their
thrones and everyone who has left anything ``an hundredfold'' and ``life
everlasting'', and has added, ``many that are first, shall be last: and the
last shall be first'' (19:30). The parable follows at once, and its last line
returns to that saying. Immediately afterwards Jesus goes up to Jerusalem and
foretells his Passion for the third time (20:17--19). The appointed text ends
with the first sentence of verse~16. The Clementine Vulgate and the
Douay--Rheims continue, ``For many are called but few chosen''; the
\work{Nova Vulgata} published by the Holy See prints the first sentence only.
Chrysostom, Jerome, St~Gregory the Great and Aquinas all had the longer verse
before them. Chrysostom, Gregory and Aquinas expound its second sentence, which
this Mass does not read; Jerome carries it in his lemma and repeats it as the
parable's end, and gives it no comment of its own.

Three questions run through the commentaries. What is the day, with its five
hours? Who are the last? And what does the householder's reply decide? The
three interpretations of the Mass follow the answers. A fourth question cuts across them
all: how the closing saying, ``So shall the last be first and the first
last'', stands to the parable it closes. For St~Augustine it states the
parable's equality. First and last are made equal ``by equality of treatment,
not by inverting their order. For what is the meaning of, `the last were
first, and the first last'? That both the first and last received the same''
(\work{Sermo} 87.4). Chrysostom denies that the saying is drawn from the
parable at all. In the parable ``the first did not become last, but all
received the same contrary to hope and expectation''; the saying foretells
something ``more strange'', that the last shall come before the first, ``so
that that is one thing, and this another''. He takes it as a dark hint at the
Jews and, among believers, at those who shone at first and fell back and those
who have risen from vice (\work{Homilies on Matthew} 64.4). Jerome makes the
saying the key. The parable \latin{ex his quae praemissa sunt, intelligitur},
is understood from what stands before it, namely the same saying at 19:30; the
Lord ranks \latin{non tempori \ldots\ sed fidei}, not by time but by faith;
and \latin{titulus parabolae huius finisque consentiunt}, the heading of the
parable and its end agree (\work{Commentarii in Matthaeum} III, PL 26, 140,
142). Aquinas
records both of the first two senses under Chrysostom's name: the last are
made equal to the first, or the last come before them (\work{Super Matthaeum}
c.~20, lect.~1). Gregory draws only a caution from it: no one should exalt
himself for his work or for his length of service, since no one yet knows how
finely the judge will weigh them, and it is greatly to be rejoiced at to be
even the last in the kingdom of God (\work{Homiliae in Evangelia} 19.4).

\subsection{The Prayer over the Offerings}

After the gifts are prepared the priest prays \latin{Munera, quaesumus,
Domine, tuae plebis}. God is asked to accept his people's gifts with favour,
and the purpose clause joins two verbs: what the people profess by devout faith
they are to lay hold of through the heavenly sacraments. Faith professes and
the sacrament gives possession.

\subsection{The two Communion antiphons}

The Missal gives every Sunday in Ordinary Time two Communion antiphons, the
first from the Psalms and the second for the most part from the Gospel, and
lets either be chosen, with a preference for one that agrees with the Gospel of
the day (rubrics for Ordinary Time, no.~6).

The psalm verses open the long alphabetical psalm of the law. A command is
acknowledged, ``Thou hast commanded thy commandments to be kept most
diligently'', and at once there follows a wish and not a boast: ``O! that my
ways may be directed to keep thy justifications.'' Augustine asks who speaks
and answers, every member of Christ, \latin{vel potius universum corpus
Christi}, or rather the whole body of Christ. He defends the adverb: the
\latin{nimis} of the Latin means \latin{valde}, ``greatly'', and is no excess
(\work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 118, s.~4.1). Hilary hears in verse~4 a rule
against slackness: \latin{nihil igitur dissoluto animo, nihil incurioso
agendum est}, nothing is to be done with a slack or careless mind
(\work{Tractatus super Psalmos} 118, Aleph~11). Bellarmine argues from the
lawgiver: God commands ``not by way of advice, but by strict precept''. What
the wish of verse~5 confesses belongs to the third interpretation.

John 10:14 is spoken to the Pharisees after the healing of the man born blind
(9:39--10:21). The good shepherd, who ``giveth his life for his sheep'', is
set against the hireling, who works for pay and flees when the wolf comes
(10:11--13). The appointed verse is the second ``I am the good shepherd'',
with the mutual knowledge of shepherd and sheep; the next verse grounds that
knowledge in the Father's knowledge of the Son, and verse~16 speaks of ``other
sheep \ldots\ not of this fold''. Gregory glosses the verb: \latin{cognosco
oves meas, hoc est diligo}, I know my sheep, that is, I love them
(\work{Homiliae in Evangelia} 14.3). Aquinas follows him: the knowledge is one
\latin{non solum simplici, sed etiam probationis, et dilectionis}, not of bare
acquaintance but of approval and love (\work{Super Ioannem} c.~10, lect.~4).
Augustine's forty-seventh tractate is headed on John 10:14--21. Its first
five sections, those checked, pass from the shepherd who gives his
life (v.~11) to verses 15--16, and they give the appointed clause no separate
comment.

\subsection{The Prayer after Communion}

The last prayer, \latin{Quos tuis, Domine, reficis sacramentis}, commends
those whom God restores with his sacraments to his continual help. Its purpose
clause names what they are to possess, the effect of redemption,
\latin{redemptionis effectum}, and the two places where they are to possess
it: \latin{et mysteriis \ldots\ et moribus}, in the mysteries and in conduct.
The Mass that opened with God's promise to hear ends by asking that what is
received at the altar be found again in the way its communicants live.
