\section{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\zlabel{triptych:concise:themes:start}

This Mass sets a complaint about fairness inside a promise of mercy. At its
centre a householder pays the labourers of one hour the denarius he agreed
with those of twelve, and he answers their murmur with a question: ``Is thy eye evil, because I am good?'' Before
that Gospel is read a prophet has already given the reason, ``my thoughts are
not your thoughts'', and a psalm has called the same Lord ``gracious and
merciful'' and ``just in all his ways''. Before either, at the entrance, God
has spoken first and promised to hear. The Mass moves from that promise,
through the summons and the parable, to an altar where those hired at every
hour are given the same food, and it ends by asking that what is received be
found again in conduct.

\subsection{Two books and three stated relations}

The texts come from two books. The antiphons and orations belong to the
Missal's Twenty-fifth Week in Ordinary Time and are prayed with the readings
of all three years; the readings, psalm and acclamation belong to the
Lectionary's Year~A. The liturgical books state
three relations among them: the first reading is chosen in relation to the
Gospel, the psalm responds to the first reading, and the acclamation greets the
Gospel. The Apostle and the Gospel are each read in a semi-continuous course
of their own, and Philippians begins on this Sunday a course of four Sundays.
The \work{Ordo lectionum Missae} marks what it singles out in each reading by
the title it sets over it: over the first reading, \latin{Non sunt cogitationes
meae cogitationes vestrae} (Isa 55:8); over the Gospel, \latin{An oculus tuus
nequam est, quia ego bonus sum?} (Mt 20:15); over the second reading, from its
own course, \latin{Mihi vivere Christus est} (Phil 1:21).

\subsection{God speaks first, and a prophet gives the reason}

The Entrance Antiphon, \latin{Salus populi ego sum, dicit Dominus}, is spoken
in God's own person: he names himself the salvation of the people, promises to
hear them from whatever tribulation they cry out of, and declares himself
their Lord for ever. It is a composed oracle, and the Missal prints no
scriptural reference beside it; the official Antiphonary nevertheless
identifies Ps 37 (36):39--40 as its basis, without making the antiphon an
adaptation of those verses. The Mass therefore begins before anyone has asked for anything. The
Collect, \latin{Deus, qui sacrae legis omnia constituta}, rests all that the
sacred law lays down on the love of God and of neighbour, recalling the Lord's
word that on these two commandments ``dependeth the whole law and the
prophets'' (Mt 22:40), and asks that those who keep God's precepts may come to
eternal life.

In the first reading verses 6--7 summon: seek, call, forsake, return, ``for he is bountiful to
forgive''. Verses 8--9 give the reason, and the word ``for'' binds it to the
promise of pardon: God forgives abundantly because his thoughts are not ours.
St~Jerome and St~Thomas Aquinas both read the contrast as a statement about
mercy and not about divine inscrutability in general. Jerome has the Lord say,
do not think it incredible \latin{impium et iniquum posse salvari}, that the
impious and the unjust can be saved (\work{In Isaiam} XV, PL 24, 554); Aquinas
puts it in one line, \latin{Vos ultionem cogitatis, ego misericordiam}, you
think of requital, I of mercy (\work{Super Isaiam} c.~55). The psalm answers in
three pairs of verses: daily praise of a greatness without end, the formula of
mercy, and the Lord just in all his ways and near to all who call. The appointed verses stop short of the psalm's next line, ``he will hear their
prayer, and save them'' (v.~19), which says again what the Entrance Antiphon
promised.

\subsection{Philippi, the parable and the altar}

Two texts of the Mass concern Philippi. Paul writes in chains (Phil 1:7, 13);
the reading follows his dilemma between death and life,
omits the verses in which he resolves it (1:25), and adds the opening command
of the exhortation that follows, ``Only let your conversation be worthy of the
gospel of Christ.'' The Alleluia verse is formed from Acts 16:14, where the
Lord opens the heart of Lydia ``to attend to those things which were said by
Paul''. The liturgical verse changes Luke's grammar: what he narrates of one woman the
assembly asks for itself, and the words to be heeded become those of the Son.

The parable is an answer. Peter has asked, ``what therefore shall we have?''
(19:27); the Lord has promised the hundredfold and life everlasting, and has
added, ``many that are first, shall be last: and the last shall be first''
(19:30). The parable follows at once, and its last line returns to that
saying. The appointed text ends with the first sentence of verse~16. The
Clementine Vulgate and the Douay--Rheims continue, ``For many are called but
few chosen''; the \work{Nova Vulgata} prints the first sentence only, and this
Mass does not read the second.

After the Gospel the Missal's texts speak of receiving. The Prayer over the
Offerings joins two verbs: what the people profess by devout faith they are to
lay hold of through the heavenly sacraments. At Communion the Missal gives two
antiphons and lets either be chosen. The psalm verses acknowledge a command
and at once add a wish and not a boast, ``O! that my ways may be directed to
keep thy justifications''; the verse of John is the second ``I am the good
shepherd'', set against the hireling who works for pay (Jn 10:11--13). The Prayer after Communion names what communicants
are to possess, \latin{redemptionis effectum}, the effect of redemption, and
the two places where they are to possess it, \latin{et mysteriis \ldots\ et
moribus}, in the mysteries and in conduct.

\subsection{Three interpretations of one evening}

Three questions run through the commentaries on the parable: what the day is,
who the last are, and what the householder's reply decides. The Fathers and
Doctors answer along three lines, and each line, carried through every text of
the Mass, yields an interpretation of the whole. The first takes the day as
the history of salvation: God has called labourers from the first just man to
the last, Israel through the long day and the nations at its end, and gives
them all the one wage of eternal life. The second
takes the day as one human life: God calls at every age, the late comer is
truly received, and because no one knows the length of his day no one may put
off the call. The third begins from the evening: what the last receive is a
gift, the first are wronged in nothing, and the wage is finally the giver
himself. The first two are complementary, and St~Augustine, St~Gregory the
Great and Aquinas hold both and say so. The third differs in kind, since it
reads the parable from its ending and is the only one for which the murmuring
is the point and not a problem. None of these witnesses expounds this Mass as
a whole; each speaks of his own passage.
