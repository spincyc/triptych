## How to read this record

Three readings are proposed. Each is a reading of the whole formulary and
each is **editorial synthesis over a compilation**. In Advent the three
readings are chosen for the season and for this Sunday's character
(*Praenotanda* nos. 67 and 93); the *Ordo* pairs none of them officially, and
its tituli single out three things: the plea that God rend the heavens and come
down, the waiting for Christ's revelation, and the watch for the master of the
house. The antiphons, orations, Preface and blessing serve all three cycles
(`propers/verified.md`, Relationship classes). No Father read here comments on
this Mass. Every author below is credited only with what he says of his own
passage, at the locus named; every join between elements is the leaf's and is
labelled **[synthesis]** in the element tables. Where an author's words are
quoted in English, the English is the translator's (scope section 1).

The appointed texts turn on three questions, each with a consequential
difficulty that a witness's reasoning resolves:

| Reading | Exegetical question | Difficulty on which it turns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 `rend-the-heavens` | Has the prophet's cry *rend the heavens and come down* been answered, and if so how does the Church still pray it? | In the *Ordo*'s Latin the cry (63:19b) is followed by *Descendisti*, "you came down" (64:2b). Jerome hears the prayer answered in the Incarnation; Aquinas reads the past tense literally of the Exodus and only secondarily of the Incarnation or the judgment. The United States text does not read *Descendisti* at all (`propers/verified.md` finding 1) |
| 2 `watch-at-the-door` | Why does Christ hide the hour, and to whom is *what I say to you I say to all: Watch* said, when most who hear it die before the end? | Mark 13:32 (just before the reading) says no one knows the day, not even the Son. Jerome (on Matthew 24:36 and 24:42–43, comments that Bede carries over to Mark 13:32–33) and Chrysostom (on Matthew 24:42) answer that the hour is hidden for our good, so that readiness may be continuous, and that the Lord said *you*, not *we*, do not know; Augustine that *all* is the Church through time and the last day comes to each in the day of his death |
| 3 `clay-and-faithful-potter` | If God's people have strayed and hardened, and their justice is a soiled rag, on what can they ground their hope of being found blameless on the day? | In the Latin, Isaiah 63:17 asks why God made them stray (*errare nos fecisti*) and hardened their heart, and 64:4 (Hebrew numbering) says *you were angry, and we sinned*. Jerome and Aquinas refuse to make God the author of sin and call the order inverted; the hope rests on God's fidelity, which Chrysostom and Aquinas find in 1 Corinthians 1:8–9. The United States text renders 63:17 permissively (God *lets* them wander), already on Aquinas's line (*permittendo*), and states anger and sinfulness side by side at 64:4 (scope section 3.3); the studies describe what the assembly hears and teach the hard point as the Latin's |

The three answers do not repeat one application. Reading 1 concerns **God's
coming** (the descent prayed for, given, and awaited again); Reading 2 the
**time** of the coming and the readiness it asks (the hidden hour, the watch
of each); Reading 3 the **ground** on which sinners can await it (not their
justice but God's fidelity and gift). They are complementary, and
"Comparison" says where they press on one another.

Standing (`src/sources/inventories/author-standing-v1.toml`): Augustine,
Jerome, Gregory the Great, Bede, John Chrysostom and Cyril of Jerusalem are
Fathers; Thomas Aquinas and Robert Bellarmine are Doctors. Theophylact is an
ecclesiastical writer and supports only, through the *Catena aurea*.
Hilary's and Pseudo-Jerome's excerpts in the *Catena* are not relied on.

## Reading 1 — `rend-the-heavens`: the cry for God's descent, answered and still prayed

**Controlling claim.** The Mass prays the prophet's cry *O that you would rend
the heavens and come down* as a prayer the Church knows to have been answered
in the Incarnation and still prays toward Christ's second coming. The first
coming is the pledge that the second will come.

**Author evidence.**

- **Jerome** (*In Isaiam* XVII, PL 24 cols. 645–646). The cry *Utinam
  disrumperes caelos, et descenderes* is the voice of those who waited for the
  promised one, *qui semper promitteris*, "that you who are always promised
  might at last fulfil your promises"; it was said "when the Saviour had not
  yet come" and taken our nature from the Virgin's womb. Then, "beautifully
  according to the Hebrew, as if they had been heard", the prophet has them
  say *Descendisti*: the Word made flesh, Emmanuel. Jerome's inference is that
  the past tense following the plea marks the plea as answered.
- **Thomas Aquinas** (*Super Isaiam* 64). *Dirumperes* may be read *exinanires
  temetipsum… et carnem assumeres*, and he cites Psalm 143:5 (*inclina caelos
  tuos, et descende*); the echo of Philippians 2:7 in his words is the
  editor's identification, not his citation. *Descendisti* bears
  three senses: the judgment, the past used for the future; the descent into
  the Virgin's womb; or, "better, according to the literal sense", the memory
  of the descent to free the people from Egypt, as proof of what would happen
  if God came now. Aquinas therefore grants the Christological reading and
  makes it secondary to the historical.
- **Augustine** (*En. in Ps.* 79 §§1–2; 84.9). Psalm 80 "is of the Advent of
  the Lord and of his vineyard"; in it the Lord "is being invoked to come,
  expected until he come, yearned for until he come", and *stir up thy power
  and come* is read of the Crucified who rises in power (Oxford translation).
  On Psalm 85:8, *salutare tuum, Christum tuum*: the salvation asked for is
  Christ.
- **Cyril of Jerusalem** (*Catecheses* 15.1). "We preach not one advent only
  of Christ, but a second also": his descents are twofold, the first unobserved
  like rain on a fleece, the second open; we rest not on the first but look
  also for the second (Gifford's translation).
- **Bellarmine** (*Explanatio in Psalmos* 79, expl. 14–15, 17; 84, expl. 7,
  12). *Visit this vineyard* foretells Christ's coming; the son of man of v.
  16 is "literally" Christ, in what he reports as common agreement; *grant us
  thy salvation* means "send us your Son for a Saviour".

Jerome, Augustine and Cyril are Fathers and Aquinas and Bellarmine Doctors;
each contributes a distinct step (the answered cry; the double sense of the
descent; the psalm as a prayer for Christ's coming; the doctrine of two
comings; the son of man as Christ).

**Four senses.** *Literal:* the people who call the Lord Father and
Redeemer (63:16b) pray that God come down as he once did at Sinai and in Egypt (Aquinas's preferred literal sense); the psalmist prays
that God come and save the ravaged vine. *Allegorical:* the descent is the
Incarnation (Jerome; Aquinas's second sense); the son of man is Christ
(Augustine, Bellarmine). *Moral:* the believer prays the cry as his own,
lifting the soul (antiphon) and asking to be turned (psalm refrain). *Anagogical:*
the second descent in glory (Cyril; Aquinas's first sense, "the past for the
future"; the Preface).

**Limits.** Jerome's Christological reading is his, not the prophet's
historical meaning, which Aquinas places first. The United States
proclamation does not contain *Descendisti*; a study that preaches "the prophet
says *you came down*" must say that the Latin edition reads it and the English
does not. No witness joins Isaiah 63–64 to Mark 13; that join is the leaf's.

## Reading 2 — `watch-at-the-door`: the hidden hour and the watch of all

**Controlling claim.** Christ hides the hour of his coming so that the
readiness of his servants may be constant, and his command *Watch* is
addressed to the whole Church through time, because the day of the Lord
reaches each person in the day of his death.

**Author evidence.**

- **Jerome** (*Commentarii in Matthaeum* IV, on Matthew 24:36 and 24:42–43,
  PL 26 cols. 181–182). Against Arius and Eunomius, who took the Son's not
  knowing for a master's ignorance, he argues that the Word who made all times
  cannot be ignorant of one day in them; the treasures of wisdom are *hidden*
  in him (Col 2:3), and *it is not for you to know the times* (Acts 1:7) shows
  that he knows but that it does not profit the apostles to know, *ut semper
  incerti de adventu Judicis, sic quotidie vivant, quasi die alia judicandi
  sint*. On 24:42–43 he names the reason again: *ut pendulae exspectationis
  incerto semper eum credant esse venturum, quem ignorant quando venturus
  sit*; and the Lord did not say *we* do not know but *you* do not know.
  Jerome comments on Matthew, not on Mark; Bede repeats both comments, nearly
  word for word and without naming him, on Mark 13:32–33. Applying them to
  Mark is Bede's step, not Jerome's.
- **Bede** (*In Marci evangelium expositio* on 13:34–37, his own comment). The
  servants' power over every work is the grace of the Holy Spirit given for
  good works; the porter is the order of pastors and spiritual rulers, charged
  with the care of the Church entrusted to them; but not only the apostles and
  their successors, *omnes vigilare praecipimur*: each is to guard the door
  of his heart against the old enemy's suggestions, and each will give account
  to God for himself. He identifies the man gone abroad with Christ ascended,
  who left the Church in bodily presence but never without the protection of
  his divine presence; the identification and the sentence on the flesh's
  journey are Gregory's (Hom. 9.1), and his closing *vigilat qui…* is Gregory's
  triad (Hom. 13.3). His comments on 13:32–33 are Jerome's (above) and are not
  credited to him.
- **Gregory the Great** (*Hom. in Ev.* 13 §§2–6, on Luke 12:35–40; Hom. 9 §1).
  The source of Bede's *vigilat qui…* (he watches who keeps the mind's eyes open
  to the true light, keeps by works what he believes, and drives off torpor)
  and of his identification and sentence on Christ's journey (Hom. 9.1).
  Gregory adds the knock of sickness before death and the reason for
  concealment: *Horam vero ultimam Dominus noster idcirco voluit nobis esse
  incognitam, ut semper possit esse suspecta*. His three watches are the ages
  of life, so that no one despairs (§5). Gregory comments on Luke, not on Mark;
  the parallel is the leaf's.
- **Augustine** (*Ep.* 199.1.2–1.3). *Quod autem vobis dico, omnibus dico* is
  said to the elect who belong to Christ's body, from the first hearers to
  those who will live at his last coming. Since the day will not find all
  alive, the command belongs to all because *in quo enim quemque invenerit suus
  novissimus dies, in hoc eum comprehendet mundi novissimus dies*. Augustine
  also distinguishes ignorance of the times, which is no fault, from the bad
  servant's *my master delays*.
- **John Chrysostom** (Hom. 77 on Matthew §§2–3, on Matt 24:42–44, parallel).
  He said not "I know not" but "ye know not"; he does not tell them "in order
  that they may watch, that they may be always ready", "continually in virtuous
  action"; men who knew their hour would strive only then (translator's
  English).
- **Thomas Aquinas** (*Super I Cor.* c. 1 lect. 1, on 1:8). *Qui sine crimine
  invenitur in die mortis, sine crimine perveniet ad diem iudicii*: the same
  joining of death and judgment as Augustine's, read in the second reading.

Jerome, Augustine, Gregory, Chrysostom and Bede are Fathers and Aquinas a
Doctor. The reading is carried by Jerome (the reason the hour is hidden; *you*,
not *we*), Augustine (the reach of *all*), Gregory (constancy, the knock, the
ages, the triad of watching) and Chrysostom (*ye know not*, the same remark
in his own homily on Matthew 24:42; readiness as continual virtuous action). Bede adds
the allotment of the watch on Mark 13:34–37 (the servants' grace, the porter,
the door of every heart, each one's account) and is the transmitter, not the
author, of the answer on 13:32–33. Theophylact, through the *Catena*, supports
with the four watches as ages, the same remark on *ye know not*, and the same
reason for concealment.

| Collect | Go out to meet Christ with righteous deeds, to be placed at his right hand: watching as action | [synthesis]; Gregory's *vigilat qui servat operando quod credit* (Hom. 13.3, on Luke 12), which Bede repeats on Mark 13:37 |

**Four senses.** *Literal:* Jesus on the Mount of Olives tells the four and,
through them, all to watch, since the time is unknown, and the time is unknown
so that they live each day as if to be judged on it (Jerome). *Allegorical:*
the man abroad is Christ ascended (Gregory, and Bede after him); the porter the
pastors; the servants the faithful, empowered by the Spirit (Bede). *Moral:*
the door of the heart kept against suggestion (Bede); faith kept by works and
the watches as the ages of life (Gregory). *Anagogical:* the
return of the master at the end, which reaches each person at death
(Augustine).

**Limits.** Jerome, Gregory and Chrysostom comment on parallels (Matthew 24,
Luke 12, Matthew 24); only Augustine and Bede comment on Mark 13:33–37 itself,
and Bede's comment on 13:33 is Jerome's on Matthew 24:42–43. A study credits
the reason for concealment and the *you, not we* remark to Jerome (and
Chrysostom), never to Bede alone. Gregory's three
watches and Theophylact's four are different allegories and are not merged.
Mark 13:32 is not read; a study may explain it as context and must not present
it as appointed. The narrated setting (Olivet, the four disciples) is the
chronology's; the date is Maas's derived year.

## Reading 3 — `clay-and-faithful-potter`: our failure and God's fidelity

**Controlling claim.** The people who pray the cry confess that their own
justice is soiled and that they cannot turn back by themselves; the ground of
their hope is not what they are but who God is, Father and potter, faithful
to the call he has given. Conversion and fruit are his gift.

**Author evidence.**

- **Jerome** (*In Isaiam* XVII cols. 643–650). On 63:17 God is not the cause
  of error and hardness; his patience, waiting for our salvation, seems a
  cause. The petition that follows he reads both as *Convertere… Domine*,
  "turn, Lord", and as *converte nos*, "turn us", the second developed with
  the Septuagint's clause (cols. 643–644): the conversion the people need is
  itself asked of God. On 64:5 (Vulgate) *ordo praeposterus*: not because you were angry did
  we sin, but because we sinned you were angry; we who have always been in sin
  will be saved by your mercy alone. On 64:7–8: if we consider our merits we
  must despair; considering your clemency we dare to pray, for you are our
  Father; we are clay, but knowing we are sons we dare to say *Ne irascaris*.
- **Thomas Aquinas** (*Super Isaiam* 63–64). *Fecisti errare… permittendo, non
  immittens gratiam*; *indurasti, permisisti*. Under *convertere, de ira ad
  misericordiam* he sets Psalm 89:13 *convertere, domine* and Lamentations
  5:21 *converte nos, domine et convertemur* together ([85023]). *Praeposterus ordo: ideo
  iratus, quia peccavimus: et tamen salvabimur, tua misericordia*. *Pater
  noster es tu*: the clay in the potter's hand (Jer 18).
- **Augustine** (*En. in Ps.* 79 §3; 84.9; 84.15). *O God, convert us*: "unless
  Thou convert us, we shall not be converted" (Oxford translation). The one to
  whom God shows mercy cannot be proud, for he sees that whatever good he has is
  from God. On the Communion antiphon's verse: *Tua ergo peccata potes habere;
  fructum bonum non habebis, nisi ille dederit cui confiteris*; God gives the
  sweetness of doing justice; unless God rains, the seed sown does not grow.
- **John Chrysostom** (Hom. 2 on 1 Corinthians §§5, 7–8). The praise of the
  Corinthians belongs "to the grace of God", not to them; *He shall confirm* and
  *unreprovable* imply that they still waver; *God is faithful*, that is, true:
  what he has promised he will perform; this is put early "lest after the
  vehemence of the reproofs they might fall into despair"; unbelief comes from
  those who start away, not from him who calls (translator's English).
- **Thomas Aquinas** (*Super I Cor.* c. 1 lect. 1). The hope is not vain
  because Christ will confirm in grace to the end; and God would not seem
  faithful *si nos vocaret ad societatem filii et nobis denegaret, quantum in
  ipso est, ea, per quae pervenire ad eum possemus*.
- **Bellarmine** (*Explanatio in Psalmos* 79 expl. 3). We cannot turn to God
  without his aid, so the prophet asks for both: that God convert us and show
  his face.

Jerome, Augustine and Chrysostom are Fathers, Aquinas and Bellarmine Doctors.
Jerome and Aquinas address the hardest words of the first reading; Augustine
the psalm, the acclamation and the Communion antiphon; Chrysostom and Aquinas
the second reading. Each step is distinct.

**Four senses.** *Literal:* the people who call the Lord their Father and
Redeemer (63:16b) and whose sanctuary has been trampled (63:18, just outside
the reading) confess their sin and appeal to God as Father and maker (64:7); Paul thanks God for gifts that are God's and rests
the Corinthians' perseverance on God's fidelity. *Allegorical:* fulfilled in Christ
and his body: the face God is asked to show was veiled with "a cloud of
flesh" and acknowledged when Christ rose and ascended (Augustine 79 §3, on
the refrain); those whom God
calls have the fellowship of his Son now *per similitudinem gratiae*
(Aquinas on 1 Cor 1:9, [86234]). The potter and the clay stay in the literal
sense. *Moral:* humility
before the gift; confession as the earth from which truth springs (Augustine
84.15). *Anagogical:* confirmed to the end and found without grave fault on
the day (Aquinas on 1 Cor 1:8).

**Limits.** The reading must not make God the author of sin; both witnesses on
63:17 refuse it, and their accounts (patience; permission and withheld grace)
differ in emphasis. Jerome's "turn us" at 63:17 is his second reading of the
petition, which he develops with the Septuagint's clause; a study must not say
that the Hebrew, the *Nova Vulgata* or the United States text reads "turn us"
there. Jerome's applications of 63:18 and 64:5 to the Jewish
people (scope sections 3.3, 7) are not used. Aquinas's *sine crimine* (without
mortal sin) is his gloss on the Vulgate's *sine crimine*; the studies must not
present it as Paul's wording in another translation.

## Comparison

- **Where they agree.** All three take the first coming as given and the
  second as awaited. Reading 1 states it; Reading 2 draws the moral of the
  interval; Reading 3 says what makes the interval bearable.
- **Where they press on one another.** Reading 1 hears *Descendisti* as the
  answered prayer; Reading 3 hears the same passage as confession of sin and
  appeal to fatherhood. The *Ordo*'s Latin carries both; the United States
  text carries the second more strongly, since the answering *you came down* is
  not read. Reading 2's command to watch could be heard as a demand that the
  servants secure their own readiness; Reading 3 answers that the will to
  meet Christ is itself asked as a gift (the Collect) and that God confirms to
  the end (1 Corinthians 1:8). Reading 2 in turn keeps Reading 3 from
  passivity: the gift is given as *power over every work* (Mark 13:34; Bede).
- **Real disagreement kept.** On the literal sense of *Descendisti* Jerome and
  Aquinas differ. On the reach of the command Augustine extends it to each
  death; Bede keeps it at the return and divides it by office. Both
  disagreements are presented as such.
- **The difference of question.** Reading 1 asks *what* is prayed for (God's
  coming); Reading 2 *when* and *how* to wait (always, by watching); Reading 3
  *on what ground* (God's fidelity). A homily can take one as its argument and
  draw on the others only where its own witnesses carry it.
