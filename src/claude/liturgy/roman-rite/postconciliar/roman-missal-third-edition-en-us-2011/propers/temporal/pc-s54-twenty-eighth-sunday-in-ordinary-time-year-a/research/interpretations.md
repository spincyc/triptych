# Proposed interpretations — `PC-S54-A`

Research stage, 8 October 2026. Three readings of the whole formulary are
proposed from the evidence in [`scope.md`](scope.md). Every locus cited here is
described there with its tier, language, form and limits, and is bound in
[`source-bindings.toml`](source-bindings.toml) or listed in scope section 5.
Each author is entered only for what he says of his own passage.

## How to read this record

**What the authors say and what this leaf says are kept apart.** Each Father
or Doctor below comments on one biblical passage or, in the case of the
Collect, on one prayer or one doctrine. None comments on this Mass. A few join
two of its texts themselves, and those joins are named as theirs: Aquinas cites
Matthew 22:4 for the feast of Isaiah 25:6 and Psalm 22:5 for that feast's
richness (scope 3.2); his sentence on the parable's king echoes the words of 1
John 3:2, though he cites 1 Corinthians 13:12 there (scope 3.1); he reads Psalm 22:6 as subsequent grace, the pair of terms the
Collect uses, without citing the Collect (scope 3.3); Augustine joins Psalm
33:11 to John 6:51 (scope 3.7). A formulary is a compilation; this week's
Missal texts serve all three Lectionary cycles, and the second reading follows
its own semi-continuous course, of which this is the last Sunday. So in every
reading:

- the **Author evidence** table reports only what an author says of his own
  passage; and
- the **Element contributions** table is this leaf's synthesis, with each link
  classed in the profile's vocabulary. `officially correlated`, `responsorial`,
  `acclamatory` and `semi-continuous` come from the Lectionary and the General
  Instruction; `documented reception` is an author's own connection;
  `textual observation` is a verbal fact of the texts; `source-grounded
  synthesis` is a link carried by an author's own statement about his passage;
  `editorial or AI proposal` is a link carried by nothing but the leaf. No link
  claims compositional intent.

Stable lane keys: `wedding-feast`, `wedding-garment`, `farm-and-merchandise`.

**Carrying authors.** Each reading names the authors who carry it, by the
names of `src/sources/inventories/author-standing-v1.toml` (Gregory the Great,
John Chrysostom, Augustine of Hippo, Jerome, Hilary of Poitiers, Irenaeus of
Lyons: father; Thomas Aquinas, Robert Bellarmine: doctor). Ildefonso Schuster
(blessed) and Lucien Fromage (ecclesiastical writer) support where noted and
carry nothing. Cyril of Jerusalem has no standing row and supports only; the
*Mystagogical Catecheses* are cited as attributed to him (scope 3.9).

**Standing limits on all three.**

- No commentary on the Prayer over the Offerings or the Prayer after Communion
  was found. What the tables say of them is this leaf's description after the
  owner's summaries, or doctrinal illumination named as such; it is not the
  approved English or a translation, and no English wording of any oration or
  antiphon stands in quotation marks here. English in quotation marks is
  either an author's, in the tracked translation scope section 3 names, or
  Scripture in the Douay–Rheims study translation, never the approved
  Lectionary or Missal English. Latin words in italics are the Missal's as the
  owner records them, the *Ordo*'s, or Scripture's in the Clementine.
- Latin is quoted under the normalization stated in scope section 3.
- The Gospel has two forms. Every contribution resting on Matthew 22:11–14 is
  marked **(longer form only)** and does not hold for a celebration that uses
  the shorter form, which ends at v. 10.
- The Communion antiphons are alternatives. Every four-senses clause or
  contribution resting on one of them is marked **(Communion antiphon A only)**
  or **(Communion antiphon B only)**.
- Several witnesses were read in English translation, OCR or electronic text
  only (scope section 3 says which), and none was collated with a modern
  critical text. The sweep is bounded (scope sections 1 and 4): no reading may
  be called the tradition's, the earliest, or the common view, and no order of
  priority or dependence among the authors is stated.
- The identification of the first invited with Israel is the reading of the
  Fathers who make it and is presented only under the bound of *Nostra aetate*
  4 (scope 3.11): never in the leaf's own voice, with the literal addressees
  kept as the chief priests and elders, the moral application directed to the
  baptized, and nothing that presents the Jewish people as charged with the
  Passion, rejected, accursed or uncalled.

**The *Ordo*'s own emphasis.** The *Ordo lectionum Missae* sets over the first
reading *Faciet Dominus convivium, et absterget lacrimam ab omni facie*
(Isaiah 25:6, 8) and over the Gospel *Quoscumque inveneritis, vocate ad
nuptias* (Matthew 22:9, a verse in both forms). That is the official
correlation: the feast the Lord makes beside the wedding to which whoever is
found is called. Over the second reading it sets *Omnia possum in eo, qui me
confortat* (Philippians 4:13). Its acclamation verse prays for the hope of
*our* calling. The tituli and the verse are evidence about the readings they
head, not about the Missal texts.

**The appointed texts' most demanding features**, which each reading is tested
against below: (1) the king's anger, the armies and the burnt city (v. 7), and
the passing of the invitation from the first invited to all found on the roads;
(2) the guest gathered from the highways by the king's own servants who is
bound and cast out for lacking a garment (vv. 11–13, longer form only); (3)
"many are called, but few are chosen" (v. 14, longer form only); (4) Isaiah's
promise that death will be cast down and every tear wiped away, set beside a
parable that ends in weeping; (5) that the invited refuse a king's wedding at
all (vv. 3, 5).

## Reading 1 — `wedding-feast`: the king's feast for all peoples

**The exegetical question.** What is the feast the king prepares, and to whom
is it given? (Matthew 22:2, 4, 8–10; Isaiah 25:6–9.)

**Controlling claim.** The king's feast is the wedding the Father makes for
his Son, and it is given to all. The parable's wedding is, for Gregory, the
Church joined to the Son in the mystery of the Incarnation; for Jerome,
Christ's wedding with a Church gathered from Jews and gentiles alike; for
Irenaeus, the marriage the one Father prepared from the beginning and filled
from all nations. Isaiah's feast on the mountain for all peoples is, for
Jerome, the feast Christ gives the nations after his Passion, and Aquinas reads
it with the king's dinner of Matthew 22:4. Its goods are spiritual: the King's
riches signifying spiritual things and teaching (Jerome), the feast of the
Scriptures (Gregory), the table of the altar (Aquinas on the psalm and on
Isaiah). It is spread now, for good and bad alike (Augustine, Gregory), and
completed at the resurrection: Hilary calls the wedding the sacrament of
eternal glory to be received there; Jerome reads Isaiah's death cast down with
Paul's "swallowed up"; Aquinas says the King will be king when he is seen as he
is. Each author is credited only with his clause. Joining the Gospel's wedding
to Isaiah's mountain is Aquinas's own step; joining both to this Mass's
Communion and Prayer after Communion is this leaf's.

**Carrying authors:** Gregory the Great, Jerome, Hilary of Poitiers, Irenaeus
of Lyons, Thomas Aquinas.

**Agreement and a narrower difference.** Gregory, Jerome, Hilary, Irenaeus and
Aquinas each read the king as God the Father, the son as Christ and the wedding
as Christ's union with his Church, and each holds the invitation open beyond the
first invited. They differ on where the feast stands. Gregory places the
wedding in the present Church, where bad and good mingle, and keeps the eternal
banquet for Luke's supper. Hilary makes it the sacrament of eternal glory in the
resurrection. Augustine speaks of two feasts. Aquinas gives both and follows
Gregory for the parable. The difference is one of emphasis and does not break
the claim, because every one who comments places the king's coming in at the
judgment; but no study may say that Gregory reads the parable's wedding as the
final banquet, or that Hilary reads it as the Church on earth.

### Four senses

| Sense | Distillation |
| --- | --- |
| Literal | Isaiah promises a feast for all peoples on the Lord's mountain, where death is cast down and tears wiped away; Jesus tells the chief priests and elders of a king whose wedding the invited refuse and whose hall he fills with whomever his servants find on the roads |
| Allegorical | The king is the Father and the wedding the Son's union with the Church in the Incarnation (Gregory); the Church is gathered from Jews and gentiles (Jerome); Aquinas reads Isaiah's feast on the mountain of the Passion, the feast Matthew 22:4 announces, and the psalm's table as the altar |
| Moral | Come to the feast now spread and receive it worthily, that you may come to the other (Augustine); **(Communion antiphon A only)** hunger for the living bread, not for what leaves the rich empty (Augustine) |
| Anagogical | The wedding is completed in the resurrection (Hilary), when death is swallowed up and Christ's kingdom comes (Jerome on Isaiah 25:8), and the King is seen as he is (Aquinas); **(Communion antiphon B only)** we shall be like him, because we shall see him as he is |

**Strongest difficulty.** The parable is grim where Isaiah is glad: the
invited are destroyed and their city burned (v. 7), and in the longer form a
guest is cast into darkness. Irenaeus, Chrysostom, Jerome and Aquinas read the
first invited as Israel and, in Chrysostom's case and as one option in
Jerome's and Aquinas's, the burnt city as Jerusalem under Titus; Gregory and
Hilary read the armies as angels and the fire as eternal judgment. This reading
must present that history under the bound of *Nostra aetate* 4, as the Fathers'
reading of a parable spoken to the chief priests and elders, with the Council's
reminder that God's gifts and call are without repentance; it must not let
Isaiah's "all peoples" become "not this people", which is Jerome's phrase and
his alone. The Eucharistic reading of Isaiah's feast and the psalm's table is
Aquinas's and the attributed Catechesis's reception, not the texts' literal
sense. Gregory's present-Church reading limits how far the parable's wedding
may be called the last banquet.

## Reading 2 — `wedding-garment`: called by grace, clothed in charity

**The exegetical question.** Why is a guest whom the king's own servants
gathered from the roads bound and cast out, and what is the garment he lacks?
(Matthew 22:10–14, **longer form only** for vv. 11–14.)

**Controlling claim.** The call to the wedding is wholly of grace and excludes
no one: the king invites the unworthy and gathers bad and good (Irenaeus,
Hilary, Chrysostom, Augustine). But the king comes in to see the guests, and a
garment is required of those the call has gathered. The authors below agree
that the garment is something the called must have and keep, and each names it
in his own terms: works of righteousness on which the Spirit rests (Irenaeus);
"life and practice", the clean garment of grace kept by diligence
(Chrysostom); charity, which faith and sacraments alone do not guarantee
(Augustine, Gregory); Christ put on (Aquinas). Those who speak of its source
make it a gift: Chrysostom's guests are cleansed and clothed by grace,
Augustine's Christ "will give you that 'wedding garment'", Gregory's guest must
keep "charitatis gratiam". Aquinas's account of prevenient and subsequent
grace, from Psalm 22:6, states the pair of terms that the Collect asks for:
grace going before, which calls, and following, which brings to glory.
Therefore the called live in humility, not knowing whether they are chosen,
and in hope, since an imperfect garment is not despair (Gregory). Each author
is credited only with his clause; the joins among them, and to the Collect, are
this leaf's.

**Carrying authors:** Augustine of Hippo, Gregory the Great, John Chrysostom,
Irenaeus of Lyons, Thomas Aquinas.

**A real disagreement, to be preserved.** What the garment names divides the
authors. Hilary makes it the glory of the Holy Spirit "bonae interrogationis
confessione susceptus", received at the confession of the good questioning,
and kept unstained. He does not say baptism; that the phrase alludes to the
*conscientiae bonae interrogatio* of 1 Peter 3:21 (Clementine), and so places
the garment's reception at baptism, is this leaf's reading of the allusion,
and the studies must label it so. The *Procatechesis* applies the parable to
baptismal candidates. Augustine and Gregory say expressly that it is **not**
baptism, nor faith, which the bad share, but charity. Chrysostom's clean garment
received by grace and kept by diligence stands between them; Irenaeus names
works of righteousness and Jerome the precepts and works of the new man; Aquinas
gathers sacrament, charity and works as ways of putting on Christ, "et si unum
deficiat, malum". The studies must set Hilary's identification beside
Augustine's and Gregory's, not blend them. Hilary is therefore not a carrying
author of this reading: he carries its first claim (the free call that the will
can refuse) and its requirement that the garment be kept, but not the
identification as charity. All agree that the garment is required of those the
call has gathered and that it is answered for at the judgment.

**A second, narrower difference.** On v. 14 Augustine counts the chosen few
only beside the bad, many in themselves; Hilary locates the rarity in the
chosen, not the called, "per vitium inemendatae voluntatis"; Gregory draws from
it that no one knows whether he is chosen; Aquinas, that some will not come and
some lack the garment. Complementary; none reads the verse as limiting the
call.

### Four senses

| Sense | Distillation |
| --- | --- |
| Literal | **(longer form only)** The king, entering to see the guests his servants gathered, finds one without a wedding garment, who is speechless and is cast out, for many are called and few chosen. Under the shorter form: the servants gather bad and good alike |
| Allegorical | The garment is charity, which the Creator had when he came to the wedding of the Church (Gregory), or Christ himself put on by sacrament, charity and works (Aquinas); grace goes before, calling, and follows, bringing to glory (Aquinas, with Augustine as he quotes him) |
| Moral | Let charity be born, nourished and increased (Augustine); clothe the naked and be clothed (Augustine); keep the garment the call gave (Chrysostom); be humble, not knowing whether you are chosen, and do not despair of an imperfect garment (Gregory) |
| Anagogical | The king's entry is the judgment (Jerome, Gregory), where the end, not the beginning, is sought (Jerome); **(Communion antiphon B only)** the more charity, the fuller the vision of him as he is (Aquinas) |

**Strongest difficulty.** The guest was swept in from the roads by the king's
own servants; how could he be blamed for his clothes, and for so small a fault
so great a punishment? Augustine answers that the fault is not small, since
the garment is charity; Aquinas, that the king did not will the bad to come
unless they prepared themselves; the *Procatechesis*, that he saw the others
in white. None of these answers is in the parable's words, and the studies
must present each as its author's. The common modern supposition that the host
supplied garments was not found in any source read and may not be asserted.
The authors disagree on what the garment is (above), and under the shorter
form the Gospel offers this reading nothing beyond v. 10. "Few are chosen" must
not be turned into a statement about how many are saved: Augustine counts the
few against the bad, Gregory draws only humility from it.

## Reading 3 — `farm-and-merchandise`: why the invited would not come

**The exegetical question.** Why do the invited refuse a king's wedding?
(Matthew 22:3, 5–6, both forms.)

**Controlling claim.** The first refusal is not hostility but preoccupation:
the invited go off, one to his farm and another to his merchandise. Gregory
reads the farm as immoderate earthly labour and the merchandise as greed for
gain; Hilary as the ambition of the world and the desire of money; Aquinas,
reporting Hilary, as the appetite of human glory and of avarice, and says
himself that no temporal thing should keep anyone from coming to God;
Chrysostom says the excuses seem reasonable, but spiritual things must be
prized above all, and that they did not come "from 'making light of it'".
Those who come easily, Gregory says, are often those whom no earthly
prosperity accompanies. The second reading shows the freedom the invited
lacked: Paul knows how to be full and to be hungry, to abound and to
want, because Christ strengthens him, and plenty is as dangerous as want
(Chrysostom, Aquinas). The rich hunger while those who seek the Lord lack no
good, because goods cannot fill desire and God can (Augustine, Bellarmine);
God supplies all want from his riches in glory (Aquinas on Philippians 4:19).
Joining the parable's excuses to Paul and to the psalm verse is this leaf's
synthesis; each author speaks only of his own passage.

**Carrying authors:** Gregory the Great, John Chrysostom, Thomas Aquinas,
Augustine of Hippo.

**Agreement.** Gregory, Chrysostom, Hilary and Aquinas each read the farm and

deny that a temporal cause, however reasonable, excuses. Chrysostom and Aquinas
read Paul's contentment as virtue in plenty and in want, by Christ's strength.
Augustine and Bellarmine read the rich who hunger as those whom goods cannot
fill. **No author read connects the parable's excuses to Philippians 4 or to
Psalm 33:11**; that connection is this leaf's and must be presented as such.

**What they share.** All three read the parable as a call that is wholly God's
gift and can be refused or wasted, and all three read the end of the call as a
feast that fills. `wedding-feast` and `wedding-garment` rest directly on the
Lectionary's one official correlation and on authors who expound the Gospel and
Isaiah; Aquinas's own join of Isaiah 25:6 to Matthew 22:4 belongs to the first.
`farm-and-merchandise` rests on the Gospel's vv. 5–6 and reads the formulary
from the second reading and the first Communion antiphon, whose links to the
parable are the leaf's.

**Where they differ.** They answer different questions about different verses
and reach different consequences. `wedding-feast` asks what God gives and
answers with the Church's present table and the resurrection, so its
consequence is hope and the universal reach of the call; it must carry the
hardest history (v. 7) under the Council's bound. `wedding-garment` asks what
the gathered must have and answers with charity and grace that follows, so its
consequence is humility and the works of love; its authors genuinely disagree
on whether the garment is the gift received at the confession of the good
questioning and kept (Hilary, read by this leaf as baptismal) or the charity
baptism does not guarantee. `farm-and-merchandise` asks what keeps the invited away and
answers with goods that cannot fill, so its consequence is detachment and the
right use of plenty and want. They are complementary: no author read denies
another reading's claim, and Gregory and Aquinas each contribute to all three
from different sentences. The real disputes among the authors read are what
the garment is, whether the wedding is the present Church or the last banquet,
and whether the burnt city is Jerusalem or the eternal fire; each is recorded
in scope section 7 and must survive in the studies.

**Fewer than two?** No. `wedding-feast` stands on five Fathers and a Doctor
writing on the Gospel and Isaiah, one of whom joins the two. `wedding-garment`
stands on five who state it of the Gospel's vv. 11–14 and on Aquinas's doctrine
of grace from the psalm, with a recorded disagreement. `farm-and-merchandise`
stands on four who state it of the Gospel's v. 5 and of the second reading and
the first Communion antiphon; its links among those texts are labelled as the
leaf's.
