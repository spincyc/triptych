**Every appointed Scripture unit now has a checked ancient witness and a
checked medieval, Doctoral or later saintly witness, and the sweep is still
bounded.** The first submission read what the source library already held and
stopped. The re-entry opened one of the ancient Gospel commentaries that had

## 3. Reception matrix

**Quotation check.** At first submission thirty-three quotations were searched
for verbatim in the texts read; thirty-two matched and two of Gregory's were
corrected. On the re-entry the six Latin quotations of Jerome on Isaiah were
checked again, this time on page images of Migne PL 24, and all six stand as
quoted. Every Latin sentence newly quoted below (Jerome on Matthew, Hilary,
Bede, Aquinas on Matthew, John and Philippians) and the sentences quoted from
Augustine's *Enarr.* 118 s. 4.1–2 and 144.11 were read on a page image of the
printed edition named, not only in OCR. Where a witness was read in OCR or in
an English translation only, the row says so and nothing is quoted in Latin
from it. The Latin quoted is normalized in these ways and its wording is not
otherwise altered: *i* is written for consonantal *j*; printed letterforms are
given in modern letters, so the ligatures *æ* and *œ* are written out
(*praemium*, *poenitentiam*); and Hilary is quoted with *v* where CSEL 22
prints consonantal *u*. The pages read *uoluntatem* and *deuenire* (p. 834),
*inuocantibus* and *ueritate* (p. 838) and *adiuuandi* (p. 367), all seen
again on the second re-entry.

Roles: **direct** = the author is expounding this passage; **doctrinal use** =
the author uses the verse in another argument; **history** = liturgical
history of an element; **lead** = located, not relied on. Tier: **A** = ancient
(to Gregory the Great); **L** = medieval, Doctoral or later saintly. No witness
was collated against a modern critical text.

**What the appointment excludes.** Chrysostom (64.3–4), Jerome (col. 142),
Gregory (19.5) and Aquinas (p. 262) all expound "many are called, but few
chosen". This Mass ends at 16a. Their comments on that sentence are reception
of a text this formulary does not appoint and may be reported only as such.

**Gregory's setting.** His homily is headed for Septuagesima Sunday. That is
a fact about the book he preached from. Nothing else of that day is used here.

**Aquinas's attributions are leads.** Where Aquinas names Chrysostom, Gregory,
Hilary or Jerome, the statement is his report. Chrysostom, Gregory and Jerome
were read directly above; Hilary on Matthew was not opened.

**The editor's notes on "Sunt qui" are a lead.** Jerome names no one. At the
foot of PL 26, col. 141, the editor's note *a*, keyed to Jerome's "Prima hora",
says "Imprimis autem Origenes, ex quo et Hilarius desumpsit", and quotes in
Latin a division of the hours that it gives as Origen's: a first order from
Adam, a second from Noah, a third belonging to Abraham and the fathers down to
Moses, a fourth to Moses and the law given in the desert, and the last, about
the eleventh hour, as the coming of Christ. It adds that Origen ("Adamantius")
is not silent either about the reading
Jerome approves, the whole of a human life, "quam et plerique recentiores
amplexati sunt". Notes *b* and *c* refer Jerome's sentence on head and tail,
and his "Legi in cuiusdam libro" at v. 13, to Origen as well. The notes were
read on the page image on the second re-entry; the retained artifact's record
calls them Vallarsi's. They are an editor's identification of Jerome's source
and of Hilary's dependence, not a checked reading of either. Origen and Hilary
on Matthew were not opened, and the Latin the note prints as Origen's was
compared with no text of Origen.

**Observation:** Augustine argues against despair and perverse hope in nearly
the same terms at *Sermo* 87.10–11 and at *Enarr.* 144.11. Placing the two
side by side is this leaf's comparison of two texts by one author; neither
refers to the other. **Difference of gloss:** "in truth" is wanting God
himself (Augustine), undoubting faith in the true God (Hilary), or the full
conditions of prayer (Bellarmine). These are complementary.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | John Chrysostom, *Hom. in Phil.* 3 (to v. 24) and 4 (to v. 28) | NPNF English, tracked | direct | Christ is magnified by life in Paul's deliverance and by death in his readiness. "To me to live is Christ" is Galatians 2:20: not the common life. Life is "the fruit of my work", so the present life is needful "if we bear fruit; since if it be unfruitful, it is no longer life". Paul is the athlete who could be crowned and chooses to go on contending, "that I may make the plot which I have planted bear much fruit". | L | Thomas Aquinas, *Super Philippenses* c. 1 lect. 3 (lect. 4 on v. 27a in OCR only) | Latin, Dessain edition (Liège, 1857), vol. The two agree that Paul chooses to remain for others' fruit and that "to live
is Christ" names a life moved wholly by Christ. Aquinas adds the reason the
choice is the more perfect one: it puts God's honour before one's own
enjoyment of God. Augustine's Romans 14:8 at *Enarr.* 144.5 is a parallel
thought and is not exegesis of Philippians; Aquinas cites the same verse here.

and at v. 8. The difference is one of emphasis.
