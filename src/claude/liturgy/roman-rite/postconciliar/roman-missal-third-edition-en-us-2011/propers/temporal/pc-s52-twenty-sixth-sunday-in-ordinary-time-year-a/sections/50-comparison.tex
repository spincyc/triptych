\section{The Three Interpretations Compared}
\label{sec:comparison}

All three interpretations hear God's way as mercy that is also just, and all
three put the deed before the word: the son who went, the people who obeyed in
works, the Son who obeyed unto death. They differ in the question they put to
the texts, and therefore in the verses on which they rest and in what they ask
of the rest of the Mass.

\begin{comparisontable}{Question}{Afterward he repented}{The publicans go before you}{The mind of Christ Jesus}

penance (Aquinas); the reading Jerome reports as others'. & The people of the
nations and the people of Sinai (Jerome, Chrysostom, Aquinas); Hilary assigns
them the other way. & Not the question asked; Christ set beside them as the
Son whose yes was done is the editor's.\\

directions (Jerome). & God's receiving of the nations, which Israel calls
unequal (Jerome); the mercy to penitents that the self-righteous resent
(Augustine). & The way that heals the humble who confess and humbles the proud
(Augustine); its likeness to Christ's path is the editor's.\\
Where does the weight fall? & Ezek 18:27--28 and Mt 21:29, the verses the
\work{Ordo}'s titles take. & Mt 21:31--32 and Ezek 18:29--31. & Phil
2:5--8.\\

come late share the promises (the editor's). & Sparing and mercy lead men to

The first two interpretations rest on the Lectionary's one official
correlation, the first reading with the Gospel, and the \work{Ordo}'s titles
place that correlation on repentance: the wicked man who turns and lives, the
son who was moved by repentance and went. The third does not rest there. It
reads the formulary from the second reading and the Communion antiphons, and
it reaches the correlated readings only through Augustine's reading of the
complaint in Ezekiel and through the editor's own joining.

The first two are complementary rather than rival. Jerome gives both in his
commentary on Ezekiel 18, the principle at vv.~21--25 and the peoples at
vv.~26--31, and in his commentary on Matthew he reports the personal reading of
the parable beside his own. Chrysostom and Aquinas give the peoples first and
then apply the parable to persons, Chrysostom in his warning against despair
and slumber, Aquinas in his just men and sinners and in his clerics and
religious. The two readings answer different questions, who the sons were in
the history of salvation and who they are in every life, and neither excludes
the other. The second interpretation keeps what the first loses, the parable's
first telling to men who would not believe John; the first keeps what the
second cannot say alone, that the law of the parable binds every hearer,
including those who already count themselves the first son.

The third differs from the other two in kind. It does not identify the sons at
all. It asks what obedience is and where it comes from, and it finds the answer
in the Son who emptied himself and was obedient unto death. Its link to the
parable is the editor's, and its hold on the Mass is strongest exactly where
the other two are weakest: in the second reading, which enters the second
interpretation only by the editor's joining, and in the second Communion
antiphon, which Augustine carries to the altar.

Three real disputes among the witnesses cross these lines. Hilary reverses the
sons, making the repentant son the Pharisees who believed after the
resurrection and the promising son the publicans who believed John; his
reading rests on a text whose answer named the younger son, and Jerome, who
knew that answer, preferred the copies that named the first. This touches the
second interpretation directly and the first only at its edge. Augustine and
Aquinas both read John 10:26 of predestination, but Augustine leaves those
addressed no opening, while Aquinas finds among them some who were to believe
later; the second interpretation's hope for those who then refused can appeal
to Aquinas, for persons in that crowd, and not to Augustine. Jerome and Aquinas differ on whether the good
works of one who falls away help him when he returns; that difference belongs
to the first interpretation and is a development of doctrine, not a quarrel
about what Ezekiel says.

The Missal's prayers are open to all three hearings because they say something
prior to each. The confession of the Entrance Antiphon admits that God's
judgments are true; the Collect finds his power in his mercy; the Prayer over
the Offerings asks that what is offered be accepted; the Prayer after
Communion asks for the repair of mind and body and a share in Christ's glory.
Whichever son the hearer finds himself to be, and whichever people, the Mass
gives him the same words to pray.
