## How to read this record

**What the authors say and what this leaf says are kept apart.** Each Father
or Doctor below comments on one biblical passage or, in the case of the
Collect, on one prayer. None comments on this Mass. One connects two of its
texts: Augustine, expounding Psalm 118:49, cites Ezekiel 18:22 (scope
section 3.7). A formulary is a compilation; this week's Missal texts serve all
three Lectionary cycles, and the second reading follows its own
semi-continuous course. So in every reading:

- the **Author evidence** table reports only what an author says of his own
  passage; and
- the **Element contributions** table is this leaf's synthesis, with each link
  classed in the profile's vocabulary. `officially correlated`, `responsorial`
  and `acclamatory` come from the Lectionary and the General Instruction;
  `documented reception` is an author's own connection; `textual observation`
  is a verbal fact of the texts; `source-grounded synthesis` is a link carried
  by an author's own statement about his passage; `editorial or AI proposal`
  is a link carried by nothing but the leaf. No link claims compositional
  intent.

Stable lane keys: `turning`, `two-peoples`, `obedient-son`.

**Carrying authors.** Each reading names the authors who carry it. All are
Fathers or Doctors in `src/sources/inventories/author-standing-v1.toml`
(Jerome, John Chrysostom, Augustine, Hilary, Gregory the Great: father; Thomas
Aquinas, Robert Bellarmine: doctor). Ildefonso Schuster (blessed) supports
where noted and carries nothing alone.

**Standing limits on all three.**

- No commentary on the Prayer over the Offerings or the Prayer after Communion
  was found. What the tables say of them is this leaf's description after the
  owner's summaries; it is not the approved English or a translation, and no
  English wording of any oration or antiphon stands in quotation marks here.
  English in quotation marks is either an author's, in the tracked
  translation scope section 3 names, or Scripture in the Douay–Rheims study
  translation, never the approved Lectionary or Missal English. Latin words in
  italics are the Missal's as the owner records them, or Scripture's in the
  Clementine.
- Latin is quoted under the normalization stated in scope section 3.
- The second reading has two forms. Every contribution resting on Philippians
  2:6–11 is marked **(longer form only)** and does not hold for a celebration
  that uses the shorter form.
- The Gospel's textual state (scope section 3.1; `propers/verified.md`,
  finding 1) matters here: the Lectionary's text, as the dated USCCB readings
  page gives it, addresses first the son who refuses and then goes, and its
  hearers answer by naming the first son (Douay–Rheims: "The first"), while
  Jerome's and Hilary's texts answered with the last or younger son. Hilary's
  identification rests on his text and must be presented with it.
- Several witnesses were read in English translation or OCR only (scope
  section 3 says which), and none was collated with a modern critical text.
  The sweep is bounded (scope sections 1 and 4): no reading may be called the
  tradition's, the earliest, or the common view, and no order of priority or
  dependence among the authors is stated.

**The *Ordo*'s own emphasis.** The *Ordo lectionum Missae* sets over the first
reading Ezekiel 18:27, the wicked man who turns from his wickedness and gives
his soul life, and over the Gospel Matthew 21:29 and 31, the son moved by
repentance who went and the publicans and harlots who go before
(`propers/verified.md`, Relationship classes). That is the official
correlation, and it falls on repentance. Over both forms of the second reading
it sets Philippians 2:5, *Sentite in vobis quod et in Christo Iesu*. The tituli
are evidence about the readings they head, not about the Missal texts.

## Reading 1 — `turning`: afterward he repented

**Controlling claim.** God judges a person as he is found when his course is
turned, not by his first word or his former record. The wicked who turns from
his wickedness lives; the just who turns from his justice dies; the son who
said no and afterward repented did the father's will, and the one who said yes
and did not go did not. Therefore no one may despair of turning, no one may
rest on a past, and turning is urgent because it is always open and never
finished in this life.

**Carrying authors:** Jerome, John Chrysostom, Augustine, Thomas Aquinas.

**Agreement and a narrower difference.** Jerome, Chrysostom, Augustine and
Aquinas each state the claim of their own passage. They differ on one
consequence: Jerome says old justices do not help the one who falls away;
Aquinas, on the same verse, that works done in charity are deadened and revive
with penance. That is a doctrinal development, not a disagreement about what
Ezekiel asserts, and each position stays with its author.

| Moral | Do not despair of your past and do not rest on it; turn today; let the deed follow the word, for the worst is he who does not repent of what he has done (Aquinas) and a Christian name without deeds profits nothing (Augustine) |
| Anagogical | "Unusquisque in quo invenietur, in eo iudicabitur": the last judgment finds each where he stands (Jerome), so the penitent must persevere in his penance (Aquinas), and the word in which God gave hope is fulfilled (Augustine on Psalm 118:49) |

**Strongest difficulty.** The parable is spoken to the chief priests and
elders about John (Matthew 21:23, 32), and Jerome, Chrysostom and Aquinas all
read it first of peoples; a purely personal reading loses its historical
charge, which `two-peoples` keeps. Ezekiel 18 answers the exiles' fatalism
about inherited guilt and speaks of life and death in the covenant community;
reading it of the last judgment is the Fathers' extension, as Jerome's
*unusquisque in quo invenietur* shows, not the oracle's plain horizon. Augustine
names the practical difficulty: a promise that stays open invites delay, and he
answers it from outside Ezekiel (no one is promised tomorrow).

## Reading 2 — `two-peoples`: the publicans go before you

**Controlling claim.** Jerome, Chrysostom and Aquinas read the two sons as
two peoples, and this reading follows their identification. It is theirs, not
the parable's plain sense and not an assertion in this leaf's own voice. In
it the first son is the Gentile people. It refused, and afterward, at the
Saviour's coming, repented and worked in the vineyard (Jerome); it showed its
obedience in works (Chrysostom). The second son is the Jewish people, which
answered at Sinai that it would do all the Lord said and did not go (all
three). Jerome adds that it did not go "quia, interfecto patrisfamilias
filio, se putavit haeredem". The parable's own application (vv. 31–32) sets
the publicans and harlots who believed John before the chief priests and
elders who did not. Jerome, on Ezekiel 18:26–29, reads God's receiving of the
nations as the just way that Israel calls unequal. On 18:31 he holds the call
to turn and live open "ad utrumque populum", and Chrysostom says the
publicans go before "as having a hope, if they were willing". The reading is
presented under the magisterial bound recorded below and in scope section
3.11.

**Carrying authors:** Jerome, John Chrysostom, Thomas Aquinas.

**A real disagreement, to be preserved.** Hilary (*Comm. in Matt.* 21.11–15,
PL 9, cols. 1039–1041), reading a text in which the Pharisees answer with the
younger son, identifies the sons the other way: the elder, who refused and
repented, is the people of the Pharisees, who after the resurrection repented
and believed under the apostles; the younger, who promised and did not go, is
the people of publicans and sinners, who believed John but could not yet do the
Gospel's work, "obediens professione, licet non efficiens in tempore; quia
fides sola iustificat". Jerome knows the same reading of v. 31 (*Novissimus*),
says true copies read *primum*, and explains *novissimus* as the Jews'
evasion. The disagreement is real and textual as well as exegetical. Hilary is
therefore **not** a carrying author of this reading; the studies must set his
identification beside the claim, not blend it. Hilary and Jerome agree that
what puts the publicans first is their response to John (Hilary: they believed
him; Jerome: they received his baptism of penance). Hilary's reading keeps
the Pharisees' later belief in view, much as Aquinas, on John 10:26, keeps in
view the later belief of some of the crowd then addressed.

**A second difference.** On John 10:26, the verse before the acclamation,
Augustine says those addressed were predestined to everlasting destruction.
His "be ye sheep" in the same section is addressed to his own congregation,
not to them. Aquinas keeps the same gloss (*praesciti ad aeternum interitum*),
but, asking whether it drove its hearers to despair, distinguishes within the
crowd. None were then preordained to believe; some were preordained to
believe later. The difference is narrower than a contradiction, since both
read the saying of predestination, but it is real for this reading. Augustine
closes to those addressed the hope that Aquinas keeps open for some of them.
The acclamation's context therefore does not settle how the second son ends,
and Augustine may not be cited for a call to the second son.

**A magisterial bound on presentation.** *Nostra aetate* 4 (scope section
3.11) is the checked bound on how this reading is presented and applied.
What happened in the Passion "cannot be charged against all the Jews, without
distinction, then alive, nor against the Jews of today", and the Jews "should
not be presented as rejected or accursed by God, as if this followed from the
Holy Scriptures". The same article says that God holds the Jews most dear for
the sake of the fathers and does not repent of his gifts and calls. Its limit:
it governs presentation and application, not exegesis. It does not say what
the parable means, judges no Father's reading, and licenses no softening of
what a Father says. The studies and the homily therefore report the
identification as Jerome's, Chrysostom's and Aquinas's. They keep the literal
addressees the chief priests and elders, and attribute Jerome's harsh
sentences to him beside his *ad utrumque populum*. They present no people,
then or now, as charged with the Passion or as rejected or accursed, and they
direct the moral application to the baptized.

| Sense | Distillation |

**Strongest difficulty.** Hilary's contrary identification and the textual
variant show that the allegory is not the parable's plain sense, and the
studies must present it as the Fathers' reading. The literal addressees are
the chief priests and elders (21:23, 45), not a people as such; Jerome's harsh
sentence on Israel ("Usque hodie Israel blasphemat") must be attributed to him
and set beside his own "ad utrumque populum" and Chrysostom's "as having a
hope, if they were willing". The studies and the homily present the
identification under the bound of *Nostra aetate* 4 set out above. Augustine's
reading of Ezekiel 18:25 names no people, and on John 10:26 he is the contrary
position. The second reading contributes nothing to this reading that any
author read supports.

## Reading 3 — `obedient-son`: the mind that was in Christ Jesus

**Controlling claim.** Christ's humble obedience is both the pattern and the
power of Christian humility and charity. He who was equal to God emptied
himself, obeyed the Father as Son unto death on a cross, and was exalted; the
disciple is to have the same mind, counting others better than himself,
obeying against his own will, and laying down his life for the brethren; and
God's power is shown most in this mercy that stoops to forgive and raise. That
last clause is the leaf's joining of Aquinas on the Collect (power shown most
in sparing and mercy) with Schuster's "condescension"; the stooping is not
Aquinas's word.

**Carrying authors:** John Chrysostom, Thomas Aquinas, Augustine.

**Agreement.** Chrysostom, Aquinas and Augustine each state that Christ's
self-humbling obedience is the example of the humility Christians owe, and
that exaltation follows humility. **No author read connects Philippians 2 to
the parable of the two sons or to Ezekiel 18.** Setting Christ beside the two
sons, as the one whose yes was done, is this leaf's proposal and must be
presented as such.

### Four senses

| Sense | Distillation |
| --- | --- |
| Literal | Paul asks the Philippians for one mind and humility, each regarding the others as better, and sets before them the mind of Christ Jesus; **(longer form only)** who, being in the form of God, emptied himself, obeyed unto death on a cross, and was exalted with the name above every name |
| Allegorical | Christ is the Son who obeyed the Father and so honoured him (Chrysostom); the lowly God who finds proud man (Augustine); the shepherd who lays down his life, whose body and blood are received at the ruler's table (Augustine) |
| Moral | Count others better than yourself by the image of God in them (Aquinas); obey against your own will; begin to lay down your life by giving of your goods (Augustine); do not live so as to insult him you rightly praise (Chrysostom) |
| Anagogical | Exaltation follows humility (Aquinas; Hilary on humility's glory); co-heirs in glory with the one whose death is proclaimed; **(longer form only)** every knee bowing |

**Strongest difficulty.** No author read joins Philippians 2 to either the
parable or Ezekiel, and the second reading follows its own course; the
reading's hold on the whole formulary rests on the leaf's synthesis and on the
Collect and Communion antiphons, where the authors do speak. Chrysostom's
homilies on 2:5–11 are chiefly doctrinal (against heresies), so his moral
application is brief. Christ is not only an example: Chrysostom and Aquinas
both insist that the one who emptied himself kept the form of God (Aquinas:
he did not empty himself of the divinity, "quia quod erat, permansit: et quod
non erat, assumpsit"), and a study that reduces the reading to imitation
misstates them. Under the shorter form the reading keeps
only the exhortation and its titulus.

## Comparison

| Question | `turning` | `two-peoples` | `obedient-son` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who are the two sons? | Sinners who do penance and those who profess justice (Aquinas; Jerome's report; Chrysostom's application) | The nations and the people of Sinai (Jerome, Chrysostom, Aquinas); Hilary reverses them | Not the reading's question; Christ as the obedient Son is the leaf's proposal |
| What is "the way of the Lord"? | Judgment of the present state, both ways (Jerome) | God's receiving the nations, which Israel calls unequal (Jerome) | The way of humility, which the froward call froward (Augustine) |
| Where is the weight? | Ezekiel 18:27–28 and Matthew 21:29, the tituli | Matthew 21:31–32 and Ezekiel 18:29 | Philippians 2:5–8 |
| What does the Collect say? | God freely forgives (Aquinas) | Those who come late share the promises (the leaf's proposal) | God's power is shown most in sparing and mercy, leading men to share the infinite good (Aquinas); the sinner's rehabilitation asks a condescension beyond creation (Schuster). | What do the Communion antiphons say? | A: the promise remembered. Augustine: *Memento* asks God to fulfil his promise, and he cites Ezekiel 18:22 among Scripture's ways of speaking of God's forgetting. Bellarmine, on Psalm 129: God's promise of pardon to penitents. | Principal authors | Jerome, Chrysostom, Augustine, Aquinas | Jerome, Chrysostom, Aquinas; Hilary disagreeing on the sons; Augustine contrary on John 10:26 | Chrysostom, Aquinas, Augustine |
| Does it hold under the shorter second reading? | Yes | Yes | Only in part |

**What they share.** All three read God's way as mercy that is also just, and
all three put deed before word: the son who went, the people who obeyed in
works, the Son who obeyed unto death. `turning` and `two-peoples` rest on the
Lectionary's one official correlation, Ezekiel 18 with Matthew 21, whose tituli
place it on repentance. `obedient-son` does not. It reads the formulary from
the second reading and the Communion antiphons, and it reaches the correlated
readings only through Augustine on Ezekiel 18:25 and the leaf's own
proposal.

**Where they differ.** `turning` and `two-peoples` are complementary. Jerome
gives both in his comment on Ezekiel 18 (the principle at vv. 21–25, the
peoples at vv. 26–31) and reports the personal reading of the parable beside
his own; Chrysostom and Aquinas give the peoples and apply the parable to
persons. They answer different questions and neither excludes the other.
`obedient-son` differs in kind: it reads the formulary from the second reading
and the Communion antiphons rather than from the correlated readings, and its
link to the parable is the leaf's alone. The real disputes among the authors
read are Hilary's reversal of the sons, with its textual basis (a
`two-peoples` matter that touches `turning`), Augustine and Aquinas on what
John 10:26 implies for those addressed (Augustine is the contrary position
to `two-peoples`' hope for the second son), and Jerome and Aquinas on whether
lapsed good works help (a `turning` matter, a development rather than a
contradiction).

**Fewer than two?** No. `turning` stands on four Fathers and Doctors writing on
the first reading, the Gospel and the psalm. `two-peoples` stands on three who
state it of the Gospel, one of whom states it of the first reading too, with a
recorded dissenter. `obedient-son` stands on three who state it of the second
reading and of Communion antiphon B's verse; its links to the correlated
readings are labelled as the leaf's.
