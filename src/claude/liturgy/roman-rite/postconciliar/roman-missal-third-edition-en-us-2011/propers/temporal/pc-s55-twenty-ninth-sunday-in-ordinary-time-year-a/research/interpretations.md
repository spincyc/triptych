## How to read this record

Three readings are proposed. Each is a reading of the whole formulary and
each is **editorial synthesis over a compilation**: the Missal week's
antiphons and orations serve all three cycles, the second reading and the
Gospel run in independent semi-continuous courses, and only the first reading
and the Gospel are officially correlated, by the *Ordo*'s tituli that name
Cyrus and Caesar (`propers/verified.md`, Relationship classes). No Father read
here comments on this Mass. Every author below is credited only with what he
says of his own passage, at the locus named; every join between elements is
the leaf's and is labelled so in the element tables. Augustine's psalm
expositions, Chrysostom's homilies, Bellarmine's commentary and the NPNF and
ANF texts are read in English; Jerome, Hilary, Bede and Aquinas in Latin on
page images; Ambrose and Cassiodorus in tracked electronic Latin; Aquinas on 1
Thessalonians and on Psalms 16 and 32 in the Corpus Thomisticum electronic
text (`scope.md` section 3, quotation check).

The appointed texts' most demanding feature is the Gospel's last sentence:
Jesus answers a trap with a coin and a command that has two objects, and
leaves the second, "the things that are God's", unspecified. Each reading
answers a different question that sentence raises, and each turns on a
consequential difficulty that a witness's reasoning resolves:

| Reading | Exegetical question | Difficulty on which it turns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 `image-and-inscription` | What are "the things that are God's", and why does Jesus ask whose image the coin bears? | Jesus never says "image of God". The reading rests on an inference from the coin's image to man made in God's image (Genesis 1:26), which Augustine himself introduces as what "some understand skilfully", and Jerome reads the phrase literally of tithes, first-fruits, oblations and victims |
| 2 `cyrus-and-caesar` | How can God's people owe anything to a pagan ruler who does not know God, and how far does the debt run? | Isaiah calls Cyrus the Lord's "anointed" and says he has not known the Lord; an early Latin and Greek reading made the verse a word "to Christ my Lord", which Jerome rejects. And the Fathers differ on the ground of Caesar's claim: a just debt limited by godliness (Chrysostom, Augustine) or an obligation that binds only those who keep the world's goods (Hilary, Ambrose) |
| 3 `sincere-heart` | Why does Jesus call them hypocrites when every word of their opening is true? | The questioners' praise is accurate (Jesus is truthful, teaches God's way, regards no man's person), so the defect cannot be falsehood of words. The witnesses locate it in the gap between mouth and heart, and make Christ's reading of that heart the decisive act of the scene |

The three answers do not repeat one application. Reading 1 concerns the
**object** of what is owed to God (the self, as his image); reading 2 the
**order** of the two debts (Caesar's real claim, inside God's sovereignty and
bounded by it); reading 3 the **heart** of the one who renders (single or
divided, seen by God). They are complementary, and section "Comparison" says
where they press against one another.

Standing (`src/sources/inventories/author-standing-v1.toml`): Augustine,
Ambrose, Hilary, Jerome, John Chrysostom and Bede are Fathers; Thomas Aquinas
and Robert Bellarmine are Doctors. Cyril of Alexandria, Cyprian and Justin
Martyr have no row and support only; Cassiodorus and Fromage are
ecclesiastical writers and support only; Schuster is Blessed and supports
only as a commentator. Schuster and Fromage are cited only for what each says
of a text this formulary shares with his Mass, the Gospel and Psalm 16:6 (his
Communion, this formulary's Entrance antiphon), and Fromage also for
Philippians 2:15–16; neither joins those texts to one another, and neither is
cited for any join (`scope.md` section 3.11). Honorius Augustodunensis is an
ecclesiastical writer by the registry and a lead only, not relied on. Novatian and the *Epistle of Barnabas* are structural
witnesses to a variant reading only.

## Reading 1 — `image-and-inscription`: render to God the one who bears his image

**Controlling claim.** The coin belongs to Caesar because it bears his image
and inscription; the human person bears God's image and so belongs to God.
"The things that are God's" are therefore not a second tax beside Caesar's
but the whole self, which the Mass's prayers ask God to receive as a devoted
will and a sincere heart. Where Communion antiphon B is used, the leaf adds
that Christ, the image of God who carries no image of Caesar (Ambrose, *Exp.
Luc.* IX.35), gave himself whole as a ransom (Mark 10:45); that join of
Ambrose's image to the ransom is the leaf's, not Ambrose's, and holds only
with that antiphon.

**The inference, and who makes it.** Jesus makes the questioners produce the
coin and name its image (Matthew 22:19–21). Augustine draws the inference in
five places and states its logic each time: "as Cæsar looks for his own image
on the coin, so God looks for His in man" (*Tract. in Ioh.* 41.2); "We are
God's money: we have wandered away as coin from the treasury. The impression
that was stamped upon us has been rubbed out by our wandering. He has come to
refashion … to Cæsar his money, to God yourselves" (*Tract.* 40.9); "Cæsar
seeketh his image; render it: God seeketh His image; render it" (*En. in Ps.*
57 §8); "let him vow himself, let him pay himself as a vow … his own image is
rendered unto Cæsar: let His image be rendered unto God" (*En. in Ps.* 115
§13). In *En. in Ps.* 4.8 he presents it as "that which some understand
skilfully", grounds it in man's creation "after the image and likeness of
God, which he defaced by sin", and adopts it: "as the tribute money is
rendered to him, so should the soul to God, illumined and stamped with the
light of His countenance". Ambrose, on Luke's form of the same saying, gives
it a christological centre: "alia enim imago Dei, alia imago mundi" (with 1
Corinthians 15:49), and "Imaginem Caesaris non habet Christus, quia imago est
Dei"; Christ paid the census "non de suo", returning to the world what was
the world's (*Exp. Luc.* IX.34–35). Hilary reaches the same conclusion by a
different premise, not the image but origin: what is God's own is "corpus,
animam, voluntatem", because "Ab eo enim haec profecta atque aucta
retinemus: et proinde condignum est, ut ei se totum reddant, cui debere se
recolunt et originem et profectum" (*Comm. in Matt.* 23.2). Aquinas keeps a
literal sense (natural goods such as bread and wine to God, coin to Caesar)
and gives the image reading as the mystical one: "Nos habemus animam quae est
ad imaginem Dei, ideo eam Deo reddere debemus" (*Super Matth.* c. 22, Venice
p. 284).

**What the answer changes.** If the reading holds, the saying does not
divide life into a political half and a religious half of equal standing:
Caesar's claim reaches coin, God's claim reaches the one who carries the coin.
The demand on the hearer is not a larger offering but self-gift, and the
Gospel's last words become the measure of the Mass's own petitions for a
devoted will.

**The strongest difficulty, and the alternative.** Jerome reads the second
half literally: "quae sunt Dei Deo, decimas primitias, et oblationes, ac
victimas sentiamus", adding that Christ himself rendered to God by doing the
Father's will (*Comm. in Matt.* III, PL 26 col. 163). On Jerome's reading the
saying sets the temple's dues beside the emperor's; the image is the coin's
alone. The image reading is not the saying's literal sense; it is an
inference from Genesis 1:26, which none of the witnesses claims Jesus states,
and Augustine himself credits it to "some". The studies must present it as
the Fathers' spiritual reading of the coin, keep Jerome's literal answer
beside it, and note that Jerome's last clause (the Father's will) and
Hilary's "voluntatem" converge on the will without the image. Hilary's
premise (origin and increase) is not Augustine's (image), and the two may not
be merged into one argument. Aquinas holds both senses and is the witness
that they need not compete.

### Four senses

- **Literal.** Asked whether tribute is lawful, Jesus makes his questioners
  show the census coin and name the image and inscription it bears, then
  commands that Caesar be repaid what is Caesar's and God what is God's
  (Matthew 22:19–21). On the literal reading the Fathers differ about the
  second object: tithes, first-fruits, oblations and victims (Jerome), or body,
  soul and will (Hilary).
- **Allegorical.** Christ is the image of God who bears no image of Caesar
  (Ambrose), come to restamp the coin that sin had worn smooth (Augustine,
  *Tract.* 40.9), and the Son of Man who gives his life as the ransom
  (Communion antiphon B, where used).
- **Moral.** Render yourself: not a portion beside the tax, but the will
  devoted and the heart undivided (Collect), the body, soul and will owed to
  their origin (Hilary), the person paying himself as a vow (Augustine, *En.
  in Ps.* 115).
- **Anagogical.** The image restored is seen at the end: Augustine joins the
  saying to "we shall be like Him … for we shall see Him as He is" (1 John
  3:2) and says "then shall the truth be reproduced in us" (*Tract.* 40.9);
  the soul is rendered "stamped with the light of His countenance" (*En. in
  Ps.* 4.8).

**Limits.** The image reading is spiritual, not literal, and is never put in
Jesus' mouth. Jerome's literal reading stays beside it. The Isaiah link rests
on the Clementine *assimilavi* and on Jerome alone. Ambrose comments on Luke,
not Matthew.

## Reading 2 — `cyrus-and-caesar`: the Lord who grasps the hand of kings

**Controlling claim.** The Lord who took Cyrus by the right hand, though Cyrus
did not know him, so that from east to west all might know there is no God
besides him, is the God in whose world Caesar's coin circulates. Tribute is
therefore a real debt, rendered and not merely given, and the Christian pays
it; but the same sovereignty bounds it, because the gods of the nations are
nothing beside the Lord who reigns, and whatever Caesar claims against God is
"no longer Cæsar's tribute, but the devil's". Where Communion antiphon B is
used, its source context adds the measure of all authority: in Mark
10:42–45, as Bede and Chrysostom (on Matthew's parallel) read it, the Son of
Man came to serve and not to lord it over others as the rulers of the
gentiles do. The Missal's antiphon itself keeps only the ransom clause and
omits the contrast of service (`propers/verified.md` finding 8), so this
clause belongs to the antiphon's context, holds only where antiphon B is
sung, and is not credited to the antiphon's words.

**The inference, and who makes it.** Isaiah calls a gentile king the Lord's
anointed. Jerome explains the title: "Iste appellatus est christus, id est,
unctus Domini, quod erat insigne apud Hebraeos regiae potestatis, ut quomodo
apud nos diadema et purpura solis imperatoribus datur"; God gave him
victories and treasures "ut qui prius colebas idola, beneficiis unum sentires
Deum"; and "Ego Dominus, et non est amplius" means that there is no God
beside him (*Comm. in Isa.* XII, PL 24 col. 457–458). Aquinas: "Christo,
idest regi … propter regiam dignitatem"; the fruit God required of Cyrus's
power was "suae cognitionis", and the fruit in others was fulfilled "in hoc
quod Cyrus epistolis suis laudem Dei scripsit ubique; vel quia omnes Deum
collaudaverunt videntes Iudaeorum liberationem" (*Super Isa.* c. 45, Parma p.
539). Chrysostom, on Matthew 6, sets Cyrus beside the Magi as one whom God
moved "disposing him to let the Jews go … not … so as to destroy their free
will". On the Gospel, Chrysostom draws both halves of the claim from the
words: "this is not to give but to render, and this He shows both by the
image, and by the superscription", and "it is possible both to fulfill to men
their claims and to give unto God the things that are due to God from us",
with Romans 13:7; then the limit: "know that He is speaking only of those
things, which are no detriment to godliness; since if it be any such thing as
this, such a thing is no longer Cæsar's tribute, but the devil's" (*Hom. in
Matt.* 70.2). Augustine gives the limit a case: "Julian was an infidel
Emperor … Christian soldiers served an infidel Emperor; when they came to the
cause of Christ, they acknowledged Him only who was in heaven"; "They
distinguished their everlasting from their temporal master" (*En. in Ps.*
124.7), and the Christians, citing this saying, never refused kings their due
(*En. in Ps.* 118, under Schin). Aquinas states the dual belonging: "Vos estis
Dei, et Caesaris", with Baruch's counsel to seek the peace of Babylon
(*Super Matth.* c. 22). On Psalm 95 Augustine and Bellarmine identify who
reigns over the nations: Christ, reigning "from the wood" where "the Roman
empire hath never yet reached" (Augustine, with an Old Latin addition the
Lectionary lacks); God, who "began to reign, through faith, among the
gentiles … where the devil previously reigned" (Bellarmine). Bede and
Chrysostom read Mark 10:45 against its context of the rulers of the gentiles:
"cum ad summitatem virtutum non potentia, sed humilitate veniatur" (Bede);
"being King of the powers above, I was willing to become man … even my life
did I give a ransom" (Chrysostom, *Hom. in Matt.* 65.4, on Matthew's
parallel).

**What the answer changes.** The saying is neither a charter for two
independent realms nor a refusal of civil duty. Caesar's coin is rendered
because it is owed, inside a world where the Lord alone is God, and the
debt's limit is fixed by what is due to God. The first reading supplies what
the Gospel presupposes: a God who uses the power of those who do not know him.

**The strongest difficulties, and the alternatives.** (1) *The text of Isaiah
45:1.* Cyprian's testimonies, Novatian and the *Epistle of Barnabas* read "to
Christ my Lord" and apply the verse to the Son; Jerome calls this the error of
many Latins and Greeks who read Κυρίῳ for Κύρῳ, and refuses to refer "thou
hast not known me" to Christ; the Lectionary's Latin (*de uncto meo Cyro*)
excludes the variant. A study may tell the history of the reading but may not
build on it. (2) *The typology.* Jerome still reads *assimilavi te* of Cyrus
going before "in typum et imaginem" of Christ; Aquinas reads the same word of
the other saints whose names were foretold and sees no type. The reading uses
Cyrus as the Lord's instrument, not as a type, and reports Jerome's typology
as his. (3) *The ground of Caesar's claim.* Hilary and Ambrose make the
obligation depend on possession: "Si enim nihil eius penes nos resederit,
conditione reddendi ei quae sua sunt non tenebimur" (Hilary); "Et tu si vis
non esse obnoxius Caesari, noli habere quae mundi sunt … relinque omnia tua,
et sequere Christum" (Ambrose). Chrysostom and Augustine make it a debt of
justice that holds for every Christian. Both sides agree that the tribute is
lawful and that God's claim comes first; the studies must not merge the two
grounds. (4) *The Herodians and the Pharisees* are named by the Gospel; the
reading speaks of rulers and powers, never of a people (`scope.md` section
3.10).

### Four senses

- **Literal.** The Lord raises Cyrus, a king who does not know him, to subdue
  nations and free Israel, so that all may know there is no other God
  (Isaiah 45:1, 4–6); Jesus declares the census coin owed to Caesar and God's
  due owed to God (Matthew 22:21). Tribute is a debt rendered (Chrysostom).
- **Allegorical.** Christ is the King who reigns over the nations by the
  cross (Augustine on Psalm 95, on his Old Latin text) and, where Communion
  antiphon B is used, the Son of Man who gives his life as a ransom, whom
  the antiphon's context sets against the rulers of the gentiles (Mark
  10:42–45, not sung; Bede, Chrysostom). Jerome's reading of Cyrus as a type of Christ is reported
  as his, not adopted.
- **Moral.** Render civil dues as justice requires and seek the peace of the
  city (Aquinas, with Baruch), but refuse whatever is against God: the soldiers
  who served Julian and would not offer incense (Augustine); the limit at
  godliness (Chrysostom).
- **Anagogical.** The Lord "will judge the peoples with equity" (Psalm 96:10c)
  and comes to judge the earth (95:13, context); the rod of the ungodly is
  "for a season" and not for ever (Augustine, *En. in Ps.* 124.8).

**Limits.** No witness joins Isaiah 45 to Matthew 22; the join is the
Lectionary's official correlation and the leaf's reading of it. Romans 13 and
Acts 17 are not appointed. The reading draws no conclusion about any modern
form of government; the Fathers spoke of emperors and kings.

## Reading 3 — `sincere-heart`: the hypocrites' question and the one who reads hearts

**Controlling claim.** The questioners speak true words with a divided heart;
Christ answers first to their heart and then to their words, and his reading
of the heart is the sign of who he is. The Mass asks for the opposite of their
duplicity, a will devoted and a heart sincere, and sets the Church among a
crooked generation as lights whose word and life agree.

**The inference, and who makes it.** "Master, we know that thou art a true
speaker …" (Matthew 22:16) is accurate praise, so the charge of hypocrisy
(22:18) must concern something other than the words. Jerome defines the word
from the scene: "Hypocrita ergo appellatur, qui aliud est, et aliud simulat,
id est, aliud opere agit, et aliud voce praetendit", calls the question
"Blanda et fraudulenta interrogatio", and makes discernment the first virtue
of the answerer: "Prima virtus est respondentis, interrogantium mentem
cognoscere" (*Comm. in Matt.* III, PL 26 cols. 162–163). Chrysostom: "they
testify the truth unto Him, confessing what was really so, nevertheless, not
with an upright mind, nor willingly"; Christ "gave them proof of His Godhead,
by revealing the secrets of their hearts" (*Hom. in Matt.* 70.1–2). Aquinas
gives the anatomy: "Homines mali ab adulatione incipiunt"; Christ is
"verax" because "Deo coniunctus per unionem"; he "respondet ad mentem eorum;
secundo ad verba … Quia hominis est respondere ad verba, Dei autem respondere
ad mentem"; "hypocritae sunt proprie qui aliud in ore habent, aliud in
corde"; and the lesson, "quod non debemus adulatoribus credere" (*Super
Matth.* c. 22, Venice p. 283). Hilary: Christ "interna cogitationum secreta
cognoscens (Deus enim nihil non eorum quae intra homines sunt absconsa
speculatur)" (*Comm. in Matt.* 23.1). Augustine: "because in one way ye speak,
in another way judge, hypocrites ye are" (*En. in Ps.* 57 §8), and, on Psalm
95, the whited wall that is "hypocrisy, that is, pretence. Without it is
bright, within it is dirt" (§3; the definition is used without its
application in that section to the Donatist party, `scope.md` section 3.10). On the remedy, Aquinas reads the
acclamation's context: "simplices simus sicut filii Dei; quod est, quando
intentio est ad unum" (*Super Phil.* c. 2 lect. 4), and Chrysostom says that God permitted the Thessalonians' persecution
from the first "that no one might say, that the Preaching was established
lightly or by flattery" (*Hom. in 1 Thess.* 1).

**What the answer changes.** The Gospel becomes a scene about the giver
before it is a ruling about the gift. Rendering to God is impossible with a
divided heart, however correct the words; the One who answers is the One who
sees the heart, and that is the ground of the Mass's petition for a sincere
heart.

**How the counter-question works: convergence, not a dispute.** Every
witness read on the counter-question holds that it yields the answer out of
the questioners' own mouths. Augustine: asked whose image the coin bore, they
"gave a kind of answer to themselves in reference to the question they had
asked the Lord: and accordingly from their answer He drew this inference,
'Render therefore unto Cæsar …'", a case he distinguishes from the question
of authority, where the answer was withheld (*De serm. Dom.* II.20.70); and
"He hath compelled them with their mouth against themselves to give
sentence … He said not, 'It is lawful to pay tribute,' or, 'It is not lawful
to pay tribute'" (*En. in Ps.* 57 §8). Chrysostom: "by their tongue He brings
out the decision, and causes them to decide, that it is lawful; which was a
clear and plain victory … not from ignorance doth He ask, but because it is
His will to cause them to be bound by their own answers" (*Hom. in Matt.*
70.2). Jerome: "Sapientia semper sapienter agit, ut suis potissimum
tentatores sermonibus confutentur", and he asks "ut ad sermonem eorum
competenter respondeat" (PL 26 col. 163). Aquinas: "non potest melius
confutare opponentem, quam secundum verba sua. Unde primo ponit quaestionem;
secundo ex responsione elicit veritatem", asking "ex dispensatione" (Venice
pp. 283–284). The reasons differ (the questioner made to answer himself;
the teeth broken in their own mouth; binding by their own answer; wisdom
refuting by the tempters' words), and they strengthen the reading's claim:
the One who reads the heart also makes the hypocrite's own mouth give the
verdict. The studies present this as convergence and may not set any of these
Fathers against another on it (`scope.md` section 7).

**The strongest difficulty, and how the witnesses meet it.** Is Christ's
reading of their heart a sign of who he is, or only the discernment any wise
respondent should have? The words that raise it are Matthew 22:18: "But Jesus knowing their
wickedness" (Douay; Clementine *Cognita autem Jesus nequitia eorum*), the
lemma under which Jerome and Aquinas write. Matthew says that Jesus knew
their purpose; he does not say whether anyone else knew it beforehand (22:15
tells only that the Pharisees "consulted among themselves", Clementine
*consilium inierunt*). The objection draws
first on Jerome, who states the discernment as a rule for every answerer:
"Prima virtus est respondentis, interrogantium mentem cognoscere, et non
discipulos, sed tentatores vocare" (PL 26 col. 163), drawing no conclusion
about Christ from it. It may add the setting: Chrysostom notes, after Luke,
that they asked "in the presence of the multitude, so that the testimony
should be the stronger" (*Hom. in Matt.* 70.1), and a snare laid in public
might seem guessable by any shrewd hearer. (Chrysostom himself draws from
the crowd only that "in a larger body of spectators they afforded the
demonstration of their folly"; the inference to a guessable plot is the
objection's, not his.) On that reading the scene would teach prudence and
prove nothing of Christ. Chrysostom's own premise runs the other way. The
intent was hidden behind words that were true and respectful: "their words
were full of much respect, for they both called Him Master, and bore witness
to His truth, and that He was no respecter of persons; but being God, He was
deceived by none of these things. Wherefore they also ought to have
conjectured, that the rebuke was not the result of conjecture, but a sign of
His knowing their secret thoughts"; and by "revealing the secrets of their
hearts" he "gave them proof of His Godhead" (*Hom. in Matt.* 70.1–2). The
concealment is what makes the rebuke knowledge and not a guess. Hilary
grounds it in God, who sees all that is hidden within men (23.1); Aquinas in
the two natures: "hominis est respondere ad verba, Dei autem respondere ad
mentem; ideo quia Christus erat Deus, et homo, ideo ad utrumque respondet"
(p. 283). **Outcome:** the reading's christological claim rests on
Chrysostom, Hilary and Aquinas, and on the concealment Chrysostom names, not
on Jerome and not on any publicity of the plot; Jerome's sentence, with
Aquinas's "non debemus adulatoribus credere", carries the moral sense (the
hearer must discern flattery). The studies must not cite Jerome for the
Godhead, must not say that the questioners consulted openly, and must not
credit Chrysostom with the argument from the crowd. A second difficulty: the witnesses' language about the questioners
easily passes into polemic against a people; the reading keeps to the agents
the Gospel names and to the vice, which belongs to anyone (`scope.md`
section 3.10).

### Four senses

- **Literal.** The Pharisees' disciples and the Herodians flatter Jesus with
  true words to trap him; he names them hypocrites and answers through the
  coin (Matthew 22:15–21). Hypocrisy is the gap between mouth and heart
  (Jerome, Aquinas, Chrysostom).
- **Allegorical.** Christ is the Truth, "verax" by union with God (Aquinas),
  who reads hearts as only God does (Hilary, Chrysostom); the Church, whose
  gospel came in power and not in word only, shines with a borrowed light in
  a crooked generation (Aquinas on Philippians).
- **Moral.** Simplicity, "quando intentio est ad unum" (Aquinas): a sincere
  heart and a devoted will (Collect), no flattery given or believed (Aquinas),
  words that match deeds (Jerome).
- **Anagogical.** The Lord comes to judge the peoples with equity (Psalm
  96:10c) and, in the psalm's close, the world in his truth (95:13, context),
  when nothing hidden within men will be hidden (Hilary).

**Limits.** No witness reads the Collect or any other oration. The Isaiah
contrast is the leaf's and is weaker than the other joins; Cyrus contributes
by contrast only. Philippians 2:15b is context of the acclamation, not its
text.

## Comparison

| | Reading 1 `image-and-inscription` | Reading 2 `cyrus-and-caesar` | Reading 3 `sincere-heart` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Question answered | What is owed to God? | How is the debt to Caesar related to God's sovereignty, and where does it end? | What is wrong with the questioners, and what does Christ answer to? |
| Decisive words | "Whose image and inscription is this?" (22:20) | "Render … to Caesar … and to God" (22:21) with Isaiah 45:1, 4–6 | "Why do you tempt me, ye hypocrites?" (22:18) with 22:16 |
| Principal inference | Image → owner: man bears God's image, so the self is God's (Augustine, Ambrose; Hilary by origin) | Sovereignty → limit: the God who uses pagan kings makes their due real and bounded (Chrysostom, Jerome, Augustine, Aquinas) | Mouth ≠ heart: true words from a divided heart; Christ answers the heart (Jerome, Chrysostom, Aquinas, Augustine) |
| Consequence | Self-gift, not a second tax | Civic duty inside, and under, worship | Sincerity of the one who renders |
| First reading's role | Weakest: Jerome's *assimilavi* typology only, qualified | Strongest: the official correlation, Cyrus as God's instrument | By contrast: not knowing God and serving, against knowing and tempting |
| Chief disagreement kept | Jerome's literal dues against the image | Hilary and Ambrose (possession) against Chrysostom and Augustine (justice); the "Christ my Lord" variant | Whether Christ's reading of hearts proves his Godhead (Chrysostom, Hilary, Aquinas) or states any answerer's discernment (Jerome): complementary, with the christological claim resting on the first three. On the counter-question the witnesses converge |

**What they share.** All three read the saying as subordinating Caesar's claim
to God's and none reads it as a division into two equal realms. All three rest
on Matthew 22:15–21 read in its full scene, and each uses the Collect's
petition for a devoted will and a sincere heart, but for a different reason:
the will as the self owed (1), the majesty served first (2), the heart
undivided (3).

**Where they differ.** Reading 1 would be satisfied by a hearer who pays every
tax and gives God himself; reading 2 asks in addition where the tax must be
refused; reading 3 asks whether the giver means what he says. Reading 2 is the
only one that needs the first reading as an argument rather than a frame.
Readings 1 and 3 can be preached together (render yourself, with an undivided
heart); reading 2 adds a public dimension the other two leave implicit.

**Difficulties confronted.** The image inference beyond the literal sense (1);
the variant of Isaiah 45:1, Jerome's typology against Aquinas, and the two
grounds of Caesar's claim (2); the accuracy of the hypocrites' praise, and
whether Christ's discernment of their heart is a sign of his Godhead or a
respondent's virtue (3). The counter-question is not a difficulty between the
witnesses: Augustine, Chrysostom, Jerome and Aquinas agree that it draws the
answer from the questioners' own mouths. The Lectionary's psalm response and acclamation are
not used as evidence for any reading where their wording departs from the
Latin (`propers/verified.md` findings 2–3).

**Carrying authors for the manifest.** Reading 1: Augustine, Ambrose,
Hilary of Poitiers, Thomas Aquinas. Reading 2: John Chrysostom, Jerome,
Augustine, Thomas Aquinas (with Bede). Reading 3: John Chrysostom, Jerome,
Thomas Aquinas, Augustine. Each reading has at least two Fathers with a
developed contribution at a checked locus.
