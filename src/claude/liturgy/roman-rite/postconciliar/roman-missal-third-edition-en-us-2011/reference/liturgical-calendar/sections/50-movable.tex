\section{The movable cycle}
\label{sec:movable}

\subsection{Easter, and what the liturgical books do not say}

Every movable date in the Roman calendar reduces to two anchors: Easter, and the Sunday nearest 30
November. The second is given by \loc{nualc}~40 in words. The first is not given anywhere in the
Missal.

This is worth dwelling on, because it is a real gap in the normative apparatus rather than an
oversight of the reader. The 2002 \latin{editio typica tertia} contains no rule for computing
Easter, no golden numbers, no epacts, no dominical-letter table and no perpetual paschal table. What
it contains is a \latin{Tabella temporaria praecipuarum celebrationum anni liturgici} covering the
civil years 2000 to 2023 --- and nothing after. A user of the 2002 book in 2026 has run off the end
of the only chronological apparatus the book supplies. The 2008 \latin{reimpressio emendata} and the
territorial editions extend the table; the norm itself was never restated.

What the Church has said about the date of Easter in the reform period is not a computus but a
willingness to change one. The Second Vatican Council appended to \emph{Sacrosanctum Concilium} a
declaration on the revision of the calendar:

\begin{normtext}{\emph{Sacrosanctum Concilium}, appended \latin{declaratio de calendario recognoscendo}, n.~1}
Sacrosanctum Concilium non obnititur quin festum Paschatis certae dominicae in Calendario Gregoriano
assignetur, assentientibus iis quorum intersit, praesertim fratribus ab Apostolicae Sedis communione
seiunctis.
\end{normtext}

The declaration presupposes the Gregorian calendar and a movable Easter within it, and offers no
objection to fixing Easter to a determined Sunday provided those concerned agree. It is a
conditional willingness, not a change; the condition has not been met, and the Gregorian
computation continues to govern.

That computation is therefore stated here as an inherited rule of the Gregorian calendar rather than
as an article of the liturgical books: Easter is the Sunday following the first ecclesiastical full
moon that falls on or after 21 March, where both the equinox and the full moon are the tabular
constructions of the Gregorian calendar and not astronomical events. This reference does not cite an
ecclesiastical statement of that rule, because none was located; the ceiling is recorded in the
terminal appendix.

\subsection{The offsets}

Once Easter is fixed, the rest of the movable cycle follows by counting. The offsets below are read
off the Norms and the Missal rather than assumed: Ash Wednesday from \loc{nualc}~28 with 30 (Lent
runs from Ash Wednesday, and its sixth Sunday is Palm Sunday); the Ascension from \loc{nualc}~25
(\latin{quadragesima die post Pascha}); Pentecost from \loc{nualc}~22--23 (the fiftieth day, closing
the fifty days); Trinity from \loc{nualc}~6\,c; the Most Sacred Heart and the Immaculate Heart from
their own entries in the General Roman Calendar, which are inscribed by rule and not by date.

\begin{ruletable}
\textbf{Ash Wednesday} & Easter less 46 days. Always a Wednesday.\\
\textbf{Palm Sunday of the Passion} & Easter less 7 days.\\
\textbf{Ascension, Thursday form} & Easter plus 39 days. The fortieth day of Easter counted inclusively.\\
\textbf{Ascension, Sunday form} & Easter plus 42 days, the Seventh Sunday of Easter --- only where the
competent authority has actually made the assignment under \loc{nualc}~7\,b.\\
\textbf{Pentecost} & Easter plus 49 days.\\
\textbf{Blessed Virgin Mary, Mother of the Church} & Easter plus 50 days, the Monday after Pentecost. An
obligatory memorial inscribed by rule, not by date; it therefore always falls on the first weekday
of the resumed Ordinary Time.\\
\textbf{Most Holy Trinity} & Easter plus 56 days.\\
\textbf{Most Holy Body and Blood of Christ, Thursday form} & Easter plus 60 days.\\
\textbf{Most Holy Body and Blood of Christ, Sunday form} & Easter plus 63 days --- only where the competent
authority has made the assignment under \loc{nualc}~7\,c, as it has for the United States.\\
\textbf{Most Sacred Heart of Jesus} & Easter plus 68 days. Inscribed in the General Roman Calendar as
\latin{Feria VI post dominicam secundam post Pentecosten}, a solemnity.\\
\textbf{Immaculate Heart of the Blessed Virgin Mary} & Easter plus 69 days. Inscribed as \latin{Sabbato post
dominicam secundam post Pentecosten}, an obligatory memorial, subject to the 1998 notification
described in Section~\ref{sec:ranks}.\\
\textbf{Christ the King} & The Sunday before the First Sunday of Advent (\loc{nualc}~6\,d).\\
\textbf{First Sunday of Advent} & The Sunday falling on 30 November or nearest to it
(\loc{nualc}~40); equivalently, the fourth Sunday before 25 December. The two formulations agree in
every year from 1900 to 2100.\\
\end{ruletable}

The last row deserves its equivalence stated rather than assumed. \loc{nualc}~40 fixes Advent by
proximity to 30 November; the older and more widely quoted formula counts four Sundays back from
Christmas. Because 25 December and 30 November are twenty-five days apart, the fourth Sunday before
Christmas always falls between 27 November and 3 December, which is exactly the window of Sundays
nearest 30 November, and the tie case --- 30 November itself a Sunday --- is resolved the same way by
both. They are two descriptions of one Sunday, and this reference uses \loc{nualc}~40 because it is
the promulgated one.

\subsection{The Missal's own table, and what checking it discloses}
\label{sec:tabella}

The \latin{Tabella temporaria} of the 2002 edition is not merely a convenience. It is the only place
in the typical edition where the arithmetic of this section and of Section~\ref{sec:ot} is worked out by the
promulgating authority itself, and it is therefore the strongest available control on any published
computation. Its columns are, in order: the civil year; the dominical letters; the Sunday Lectionary
cycles carried by that civil year, given as a pair because the cycle turns at Advent; Ash Wednesday;
Easter; the Ascension; Pentecost; then, under the head \latin{Hebdomadæ per annum}, the last day and
the number of the last week of Ordinary Time before Lent (\latin{ante Quadr.: usque ad d., hebd.}),
and the first day and the number of the first week resumed after Easter Time (\latin{post temp.
pasch.: a die, ab hebd.}); and finally the First Sunday of Advent.

Every one of its twenty-four rows was recomputed for this reference from the Gregorian computus and
the norms, field by field. The results are worth reporting exactly, because they are the evidence
for the rules published here and because one of them is a discrepancy.

\begin{itemize}
\item In all twenty-four rows the Sunday cycle pair agrees with the arithmetic of \loc{olm}~66,
footnote 102; the Easter, Ascension, Pentecost, Monday-after-Pentecost and First-Sunday-of-Advent
columns agree with the computus and the offsets above; and the two week numbers agree with the rule
set out in Section~\ref{sec:ot}. Nineteen rows agree in every field without exception.
\item Five rows disagree, and they disagree in exactly one way. In 2004, 2008, 2012, 2016 and 2020
--- the leap years of the range whose Ash Wednesday falls in February --- the two February dates are
each one day earlier than the norms require. For 2004 the table gives Ash Wednesday as \latin{24
febr.} where Easter of \latin{11 apr.} less forty-six days is 25 February, and gives the last day of
Ordinary Time before Lent as \latin{23 febr.} where \loc{nualc}~44 requires the Tuesday, 24
February. The pattern repeats identically in the other four, and in each of them the printed date
for Ash Wednesday is a Tuesday, which no Ash Wednesday can be. The week numbers in the same rows are
correct, as are the March, April, May, June and November columns.
\item The sixth leap year in the range, 2000, agrees in every field. Its Ash Wednesday falls on 8
March, after the intercalated day.
\end{itemize}

Every affected row is a leap year, every unaffected row is not, and within the leap years only the
dates preceding 29 February are wrong: this is a systematic defect in one arithmetic path, not a
scattering of typographic slips. What cannot be settled from the evidence available here is where in
the chain it arose. The
artifact examined is a digitally typeset secondary reproduction of the 2002 edition, not a page-image
facsimile of a licensed altar book, and in that artifact the table's ruled columns have themselves
been flattened into running lines. The defect may stand in the printed \latin{editio typica}, may
have been corrected in the 2008 \latin{reimpressio emendata}, or may have been introduced in the
reproduction. This reference reports what it read and does not resolve which. The practical
consequence is unaffected either way: a February date taken from that table in a leap year must be
recomputed, and the table's week numbers may be relied on.

\subsection{Movable entries inside the fixed calendar}

The General Roman Calendar is printed as twelve months of dates, and it is easy to describe it as
the fixed half of the year. It is not quite that. Six celebrations are inscribed in it by rule
rather than by date, and they are printed as prose lines appended to the month in which they usually
fall:

\begin{ruletable}
\textbf{Holy Family of Jesus, Mary and Joseph} & \latin{Dominica infra octavam Nativitatis, vel, ea
deficiente, die 30 decembris} --- a feast, printed at the end of December.\\
\textbf{Baptism of the Lord} & \latin{Dominica post diem 6 ianuarii} --- a feast, printed at the end
of January, with the Monday rule appended.\\
\textbf{Most Sacred Heart of Jesus} & \latin{Feria VI post dominicam secundam post Pentecosten} --- a
solemnity, printed at the end of June.\\
\textbf{Immaculate Heart of the Blessed Virgin Mary} & \latin{Sabbato post dominicam secundam post
Pentecosten} --- an obligatory memorial, printed at the end of June.\\
\textbf{Most Holy Trinity; Body and Blood of Christ} & Assigned by \loc{nualc}~6\,c and 7\,c rather
than by a dated entry.\\
\textbf{Christ the King} & Assigned by \loc{nualc}~6\,d.\\
\end{ruletable}

An inventory that transcribes only the dated rows of the General Roman Calendar will therefore be
short by two solemnities, one feast and one obligatory memorial, all four of them of the first
importance. The same trap catches the Blessed Virgin Mary, Mother of the Church, added to the
general calendar after the 2002 printing and inscribed on the Monday after Pentecost; it appears in
no month at all.

\subsection{The Epiphany proclamation}

The Missal preserves one liturgical act by which the year's movable dates are published to a
congregation. At the Mass of the Epiphany, after the Gospel, the rubric reads: \latin{Ubi mos est,
pro opportunitate, publicari possunt post Evangelium festa mobilia anni currentis iuxta formulam
infra positam}, with a cross-reference to the formula \latin{Annuntiatio Paschæ festorumque
mobilium} in the first appendix of the edition. The permission is conditional twice over --- where
the custom exists, and as occasion suggests --- and the announcement is of the current year's movable
feasts, not of the computation that produced them. The formula's own wording could not be read in
the artifact used here, whose first appendix carries only its heading; that omission is recorded in
the terminal appendix and in the source records.
