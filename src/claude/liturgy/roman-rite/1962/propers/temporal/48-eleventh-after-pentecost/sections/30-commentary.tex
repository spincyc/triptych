\subsection*{The text: what two 1962 books actually print}

The Latin above was collated at 300 and 600 dpi --- 1200 dpi for the Gospel's
unaccented opening line --- against the CMAA facsimile of the Vatican
\latin{editio typica} (printed pp.~390--392), and independently against page
images of the Benziger \latin{editio iuxta typicam} of the same year (printed
pp.~385--386). The two books are not two photographs of one page. They are two
editions of one revision, and they differ in nine places. Nothing below is
blended; the typical edition governs every form printed in this guide.

\begin{receptiontable}{Place}{Vatican \latin{editio typica}}{Benziger \latin{iuxta typicam}}
Introit close & \latin{℣.~Glória Patri.} and stop & \latin{℣.~Glória Patri. Deus.} --- the printed cue to repeat the antiphon\\
Introit & \latin{ipse dabit virtútem et fortitúdinem} & \latin{ipse dabit virtútem, et fortitúdinem}\\
Chant headings & \latin{Ant. ad Introitum}, \latin{Ant. ad Offertorium}, \latin{Ant. ad Communionem} & \latin{Antiphona ad \dots} in all three places\\
Collect & \latin{Deus, qui, abundántia pietátis tuæ, et mérita} & \latin{Deus, qui abundántia pietátis tuæ, et mérita} --- the first comma dropped\\
Collect & \latin{et adícias} & \latin{et adiícias}\\
Collect conclusion & \latin{Per Dóminum nostrum.} & \latin{Per Dóminum.}\\
Epistle heading & \latin{ad Corínthios.} with the reference on the same line & reference on its own line\\
Gradual layout & \latin{Graduale} heading, text on a new line & heading, reference and text run in\\
Postcommunion & \latin{ut, in utróque salváti,} & \latin{ut in utróque salváti,}\\
\end{receptiontable}

Two of these bear on the commentary rather than on the eye. The typical
edition's comma pair around \latin{abundántia pietátis tuæ} marks that ablative
as parenthetical to \latin{qui \dots\ excédis}, which is exactly the reading the
argument below makes of the prayer; Benziger's single comma leaves it open
whether the ablative attaches forward or back. And \latin{adícias} against
\latin{adiícias} is the same contracted/uncontracted class that separates the
two editions elsewhere; the 1862 Pustet hand missal prints \latin{adjicias}, so
three books spell one verb three ways. The Secret's conclusion is the short
\latin{Per Dóminum.} in both witnesses. No reading in either was unclear enough
to need a third witness, and no substitution was made in either direction.

\subsubsection*{Eleven places where the appointed text is not the Bible's text}

The consequential collation is not between the two 1962 books but between the
appointed text and the Clementine Vulgate the Douay--Rheims renders. Eleven
divergences were verified directly and are textual observations, not
inferences. They are gathered once here; the commentary returns to each where
it bears on an argument. This is the point at which the Latin has to be on the
page, because an argument about wording cannot be conducted in translation.

\begin{receptiontable}{Proper and locus}{Appointed text}{Clementine Vulgate}
1. \cue{Int.} Ps. 67:6--7, 36 & one continuous antiphon & a centonisation of v.~6b, v.~7a and v.~36b, declared by the missal's own triple reference\\
2. \cue{Int.} Ps. 67:7 & \latin{unánimes in domo} & \latin{unius moris in domo} --- a different reading, not a spelling; see below\\
3. \cue{Ep.} 1 Cor. 15:1 & \latin{Fratres: Notum vobis fácio} & \latin{Notum autem vobis fácio, fratres} --- the address moves forward, \latin{autem} goes\\
4. \cue{Ep.} 1 Cor. 15:10 & ends \latin{in me vácua non fuit.} & the verse continues \latin{sed abundántius illis ómnibus laborávi: non ego autem, sed grátia Dei mecum}\\
5. \cue{Grad.} Ps. 27:7 & \latin{In Deo sperávit cor meum} & \latin{in ipso sperávit cor meum}, after an opening \latin{Dóminus adjútor meus et protéctor meus} the chant does not sing\\
6. \cue{Grad.} Ps. 27:7 & \latin{confitébor illi} & \latin{confitébor ei}\\
7. \cue{Grad.} Ps. 27:1 & \latin{clamávi \dots\ ne síleas, ne discédas a me} & \latin{clamábo \dots\ ne síleas a me: nequándo táceas a me, et assimilábor descendéntibus in lacum}\\
8. \cue{All.} Ps. 80:3 & \latin{súmite psalmum iucúndum cum cíthara} & \latin{Súmite psalmum, et date tympanum; psaltérium iucúndum, cum cíthara}\\

10. \cue{Off.} Ps. 29:3 & \latin{Dómine, clamávi ad te} & \latin{Dómine Deus meus, clamávi ad te}\\
11. \cue{Comm.} Prov. 3:9--10 & \latin{de primítiis frugum tuárum \dots\ torculária redundábunt} & \latin{de primítiis ómnium frugum tuárum da ei \dots\ torculária tua redundábunt}\\
\end{receptiontable}

Negative results belong with that table and are as much a finding as the
divergences. The Alleluia's first verse (Ps.~80:2) and the Offertory's first
verse (Ps.~29:2) match the Clementine word for word; so do the Gospel from
v.~32 onward and the Epistle from v.~2 to the cut in v.~10, orthography aside.
One further question is deliberately \textit{not} published as a divergence: at
Mk.~7:34 the missal and the 1862 Pustet print \latin{suspíciens in cælum},
while the two Clementine transcriptions consulted print \latin{suscípiens}.
Those two transcriptions may not be independent of each other, and no printed
Clementine facsimile was collated for the verse, so the guide records an
unresolved lead rather than a finding.

\subsubsection*{Three consequences the collation forces on this commentary}

\textit{First}, at Ps.~67:7 the chant's \latin{unánimes} is not a corrupt
\latin{unius moris}. It is the reading of an older Latin psalter, and one
checked expositor --- Cassiodorus --- lemmatises exactly that word. The Introit
therefore sings a text that Augustine, Hilary, Jerome, Peter Lombard and
Bellarmine did not have in front of them; what they have instead is
\latin{unius moris} or \latin{unius modi} with \latin{unanimes} supplied as a
\textit{gloss}. The Introit sings the gloss. That is argued at the element.

\textit{Second}, the Gradual's versicle ends \latin{ne discédas a me}, and no
checked witness expounds those words, because no checked witness has them: all
of them expound \latin{ne síleas} and \latin{nequándo táceas}. Any reception
attached to the Gradual's closing plea is reception of a different clause, and
this guide says so where it uses it.

\textit{Third}, the Epistle stops mid-verse, at \latin{et grátia eius in me
vácua non fuit}. The words the missal does not read are the words on which the
entire classical Latin argument about grace and free will turns. That is not a
curiosity; it changes what may honestly be claimed about this lesson's
reception, and it is treated at length at the Epistle.

\subsection*{Introit \cue{Int.}}

\elementid{Deus in loco sancto suo}{\latin{Ant. ad Introitum} --- Ps. 67, 6-7 et 36, with \latin{Ps. ibid.,} 2}{1542}

\latinpolicynote

\textbf{The whole psalm, and what the antiphon takes from it.} Psalm 67 is a
long processional: God arises, his enemies scatter, he rides through the desert
at the head of his people, Sinai trembles, rain falls on an inheritance, kings
flee, a train of captives is led up to a mountain God chose for his dwelling,
and the psalm ends in a summons to sing to him who mounts above the heaven of
heavens. The antiphon takes three clauses out of that and welds them: the last
words of v.~6 (\latin{Deus in loco sancto suo}), the first words of v.~7
(\latin{Deus qui inhabitáre facit \dots\ in domo}), and the middle of v.~36
(\latin{ipse dabit virtútem et fortitúdinem plebi suæ}). What is dropped is
instructive. Verse 6 begins with God as father of orphans and judge of widows;
v.~7 continues with prisoners brought out and rebels left in a waterless place;
v.~36 begins \latin{Mirábilis Deus in sanctis suis} and ends
\latin{benedíctus Deus}. The centonisation keeps the housing and the
strengthening and lets the orphans, the prisoners and the benediction go.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Hilary of Poitiers, \work{Tractatus super Psalmum} 67, §§2, 8, 37 (CSEL 22)}
The earliest Latin exposition of the psalm, and it reads the whole thing as
prophecy of Christ. On the psalm verse the missal sings --- \latin{Exsúrgat
Deus} --- Hilary is explicit that the rising asked for is the Resurrection:
\latin{resurrectionis enim nunc in deo tempus optatur}. At vv.~6--7 his lemma
is \latin{unimores}, a hyper-literal Latin for the Greek, and he is the first
witness to weld the verse to the Jerusalem community of Acts: \latin{ubi unius
moris ac uoluntatis pacificus unanimisque conuentus --- erat enim secundum
Actus apostolorum credentium anima et cor unum}. At v.~36 he takes the giving
of \latin{virtus} and \latin{fortitudo} as the resurrection of the body: God is
wonderful in his saints \latin{quos in uitae resurrectionem excitabit
aeternos}, and gives strength to a people \latin{quae inmortalis effecta intra
se uitam uirtutemque contineat}.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Augustine, \work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 67, §§2--3 (v.~2), §7 (vv.~6--7), §44 (v.~36; NPNF sec.~40)}
Augustine's lemma at v.~7 is \latin{unius modi}, and he glosses it at once with
the word the Introit sings: \latin{unius modi habitare facit in domo}, that is,
\latin{unanimes, unum sentientes: iste est locus sanctus Domini}. He then makes
the philological observation that the Greek admits either \latin{modi} or
\latin{mores}, and notices that the Greek carries no prefix answering the
\textit{in} of \latin{inhabitare}. The doctrinal point is the one the Introit's first two
clauses now make in sequence: God is contained by no place, so the holy place
he is in is the one he makes --- \latin{ne quaeramus eum absque nobis; sed
potius unius modi habitantes in domo, mereamur ut habitare etiam ipse dignetur
in nobis} --- and that place is built by grace and not by merits. On v.~2 he
regards the petition as already answered: in the public-domain English of the
NPNF translation, ``Already this has come to pass: Christ has risen up \dots\
and His enemies have been dispersed through all nations.'' On v.~36 the
Introit's last limb becomes resurrection doctrine, and he proves it out of the
chapter the day's own Epistle comes from: \latin{Seminatur enim hoc corpus in
infirmitate; surget in virtute} (1 Cor.~15:43), \latin{virtutem resurrectionis
eius} (Phil.~3:10), and \latin{fortitudinem autem, qua inimica destruetur
mors}. \textit{Material negative, checked in the full Latin:} Augustine does
\textbf{not} cite Acts 4:32 here. The Acts link that later readers assume is
Augustinian is Hilary's and Cassiodorus's.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Jerome, \work{Tractatus de Psalmo} 67 (Morin)}
A third track. Jerome's lemma is \latin{unius moris}, and he records what the
Hebrew has: \latin{In hebraeo autem habet ``Dominus inhabitare facit monachos
in domo'': id est, in quibus non cohabitat peccatum}. The monk hears
\textit{monks} in the verse. His exposition is accordingly moral rather than
ecclesial --- \latin{Qui unius moris sunt, et non mutantur \dots\ Peccator autem
mutatur}; \latin{ubicumque sanctitas est, ibi habitaculum Dei est}. On v.~2 he
states a double key openly: \latin{specialiter} of the Lord, \latin{ut resurgat
a mortuis, et dispergat inimicos suos, hoc est diabolum et exercitum eius}, and
\latin{generaliter} whenever we are in tribulation. \textit{Negative:} the
homily breaks off at v.~31 and never reaches v.~36; the \work{Commentarioli} on
this psalm begin only at v.~10.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Cassiodorus, \work{Expositio Psalmorum} 67, ad vv.~6, 7, 36}
The finding that governs this element. Cassiodorus is the one checked
expositor whose lemma is the Introit's own text: \latin{Deus, qui habitare
facit unanimes in domo}. With that word in front of him he restores the Acts
citation Augustine had dropped --- \latin{Multitudinis autem credentium erat
cor unum et anima una} --- adds Ps.~132:1, and identifies the house
ecclesially: \latin{In domo vero Ecclesiam significat}. At v.~36, however, he
parts from Hilary and Augustine and takes the strength given to God's people
without any resurrection at all: \latin{Dominus dabit fidelibus suis virtutem
patientiae, et fidei fortitudinem, ut possint ad aeterna praemia pervenire}.
The eschatology survives only as the \latin{praemia}; the gift itself has been
moralised into patience and faith.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Theodoret of Cyrus, \work{Interpretatio in Psalmos} 67, ad vv.~7 and 36 (PG 80, cols.\ 1380, 1400) --- Greek-tradition sample}
Theodoret reads the Septuagint's ``single-mannered'' as those who hold one aim
and choose to care for virtue only. There is no Acts citation, no monastic
reading, and at v.~36 no resurrection: the saints are lovers of virtue whose
lives make God be praised, on the model of Matthew 5:16, and the strengthening
is providential. \textit{Source limit:} the only English of this commentary is
in copyright, so Theodoret is summarised here and nowhere quoted.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Peter Lombard, \work{Commentarium in Psalmos} 67, ad v.~6 (PL 191); St.\ Robert Bellarmine, \work{Explanatio in Psalmos} 67, tr.\ John O'Sullivan (Dublin, 1866), pp.~196--201}
The conveyor belt and its terminus. Lombard's exposition is Augustine with the
gloss embedded and tagged --- \latin{unius moris \dots\ id est unanimes, unum
sentientes \dots\ Hunc autem locum gratia aedificat, non meritis} --- which is
how the schools received both the word and the anti-Pelagian point. By
Bellarmine the Acts reading is simply standard: the dwellers are ``of one mind,
one will,'' and he quotes ``One soul, one heart.'' At vv.~35--36 he follows
Augustine rather than Cassiodorus: God ``will endow his elect with true and
real immortality.''
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{What the chant did to the tradition.} Set those lemmata side by side and
the Introit's second clause turns out to be a patristic gloss that became a
liturgical text. Augustine reads \latin{unius modi} and explains it by
\latin{unanimes}; Lombard transmits the explanation; Bellarmine reads the
explanation back into the verse. Cassiodorus alone has \latin{unanimes} as
text. The Roman chant sings it. This guide does not assert a line of descent
from Cassiodorus's psalter to the Introit --- no \work{Psalterium Romanum}
witness was collated for it --- but the coincidence is exact and is the reason
the Douay cannot be quoted for the sung word.

\textbf{And a disagreement the formulary does not resolve.} The Introit's last
clause is read two ways by witnesses of equal standing. Hilary, Augustine and
Bellarmine hear the resurrection of the body in it; Cassiodorus hears patience
and faith; Theodoret hears providential strengthening. The day's Epistle is
1~Corinthians 15, and Augustine reaches his reading by quoting that very
chapter --- but he is expounding a psalm, not this Mass, and nothing documents
that anyone paired them. The disagreement is left standing.

\textbf{One material negative.} St.~Thomas's psalms commentary stops at Psalm
54; there is no Thomistic exposition of Psalm 67 or Psalm 80, and the guide
does not supply one from elsewhere in his corpus.

\subsection*{Collect \cue{Coll.}}

\elementid{Omnípotens sempitérne Deus, qui, abundántia pietátis tuæ}{\latin{Oratio}, composed oration}{1543}

\textbf{Where the prayer comes from.} All three orations of this Mass travel
together as a set, and they are old. The set stands complete in the Old
Gelasian sacramentary (Vat.~Reg.~lat.~316, ed.\ H.~A. Wilson, Oxford 1894), at
p.~228, as the seventh of the sixteen unassigned Sunday masses of Book~III ---
\latin{Item alia Missa}, tied to no numbered Sunday at all. Wilson's own
footnote records that the Frankish Gelasians assign the mass to the
\textit{thirteenth} Sunday after Pentecost, and that Pamelius's Gregorian
prints it at the twelfth. The Gregorian Hadrianum (ed.\ Wilson, HBS 49, 1915)
does assign it: at p.~172, chapter XXX, \latin{DOMINICA .XII. POST
PENTECOSTEN}. Through this whole stretch the Gregorian series runs one Sunday
ahead of 1962, so the Hadrianum's twelfth is the 1962 eleventh.

\textbf{A bounded negative that matters.} The Secret and the Postcommunion are
older still and stand in the Veronese (``Leonine'') collection. The Collect is
\textbf{not there}. The whole of Feltoe's text was searched for
\latin{abundantia pietatis}, \latin{merita suppli-}, \latin{adicias} and
\latin{adjicias}, \latin{excedis}, and all seven occurrences of
\latin{praesumit}; none is this prayer, and Wilson's Gelasian margins cite no
Leonine parallel for it either. The Collect's oldest verified witness is the
Gelasian. That is a bounded result about the books actually searched, not a
claim about a prayer's absolute age.

\textbf{The chant cues, and why no design argument follows.} Wilson's apparatus
to the Hadrianum reports the marginal chant cues of the Ottobonianus manuscript
(Vat.~Ottob.~lat.~313). The antiphon set of this 1962 Mass --- \latin{Deus in
loco sancto suo}, \latin{Exaltabo te Domine}, \latin{Honora Dominum} --- is
cued there against \textit{Dominica X post Pentecosten}, two masses before the
orations it now accompanies; Gregorian Dominica XII itself carries the cues
\latin{Respice \dots\ in testamentum} and \latin{Panem de caelo}, which are the
1962 Thirteenth Sunday's chants. The pairing of the \latin{Honora Dominum}
Communion with the \latin{abundantia pietatis} Collect is therefore a product
of a later realignment of two independently moving cycles. Anything this guide
observes about how the day's chants and orations illuminate each other is an
observation about the book as it now stands, never a recovered intention.

\textbf{The grammar, which is the prayer.} \latin{Omnípotens sempitérne Deus,
qui, abundántia pietátis tuæ, et mérita súpplicum excédis et vota.} The two
objects are not a doublet. \latin{Merita} are what a suppliant has;
\latin{vota} are what a suppliant asks. God is addressed as exceeding both, and
the ablative \latin{abundántia pietátis tuæ} --- set off by a comma pair in the
typical edition --- names the reason. Then the petition, and its two purpose
clauses: \latin{ut dimíttas quæ consciéntia métuit, et adícias quod orátio non
præsúmit}. The first asks remission of what conscience fears; the second asks
God to \textit{add} what prayer does not presume. The subject of
\latin{præsúmit} is \latin{orátio}: it is the prayer, not the petitioner, that
does not presume. The registered 1861 English makes the petitioners the ones
who ``dare not presume to ask,'' which is a fair devotional rendering and a
different sentence; the guide quotes the witness and argues on the Latin, as
declared at the element above. The structure is therefore symmetrical and
complete: below what we deserve, God does not exact; above what we ask, God
supplies.

\textbf{An echo, labelled as an echo.} The Vulgate of Ephesians 3:20 addresses
God as \latin{qui potens est omnia facere superabundánter quam pétimus aut
intellégimus}. The two clauses map onto the Collect's two --- giving beyond
what we ask, giving beyond what we understand to ask. There is no quotation and
no documented dependence; it is reported as a verbal echo and nothing more.

\textbf{Doctrinal illumination, not a source.} No patristic or early-medieval
commentary on the \textit{wording} of these three orations was found, which is
the expected result for composed Roman prayers of this age; the earliest
wording-level commentary located is modern. Two Thomistic loci nevertheless
illuminate the second petition exactly. \work{Summa theologiae} II--II q.~83
a.~2 ad~3 states the principle the Collect prays: \latin{Deus multa nobis
praestat ex sua liberalitate etiam non petita} --- God grants us much unasked,
out of his liberality. And q.~83 a.~16 treats whether the prayer of a sinner is
heard, concluding that it is heard \latin{non quasi ex iustitia \dots\ sed ex
pura misericordia}, with a \latin{sed contra} that quotes Augustine on the
publican: \latin{si peccatores non exaudiret Deus, frustra publicanus dixisset:
Domine, propitius esto mihi peccatori}. Neither article is about this Collect.
Both are about what the Collect asks.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Dom Prosper Guéranger, \work{The Liturgical Year} \dots\ \work{Time after Pentecost}, vol.~II, 2nd ed., tr.\ Laurence Shepherd (1909), Eleventh Sunday, pp.~272--274}
Guéranger keys the Collect to the publican --- and does so on historical
grounds, citing Rupert of Deutz, \work{De divinis officiis} XII, 11, for an
older arrangement that read Luke 18 on this Sunday. On the prayer itself:
the Church, ``with motherly tenderness, comes and takes up his faltering
prayer, and gives it her own eloquence \dots\ granting them what they, poor
sinners, are too afraid to presume to ask for.'' That is reception of the
second petition, and it reads \latin{orátio} the way the 1861 missal does.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Blessed Ildefonso Schuster, \work{The Sacramentary} (\work{Liber Sacramentorum}), tr.\ Levelis-Marke, vol.~III (1927), pp.~124--125}
Schuster calls the Collect ``a perfect liturgical gem'' and says that ``this
short petition from the Sunday Liturgy contains a complete treatise on
prayer.'' The judgment is worth recording because it is a judgment about
compression: three clauses that between them state who prays, what prayer
cannot reach, and what God does about the shortfall.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{Afterlife in the reformed books.} The prayer did not stop in 1962. It
is used verbatim, with the same contracted \latin{adícias}, as the Collect of
\latin{Dominica XXVII per annum} in the postconciliar \work{Missale Romanum}.
That reuse was verified in two independent unofficial Latin transcriptions; no
free scan of the official \latin{editio typica tertia} was located, so the
official locus remains a lead and the postconciliar book is not quoted here.
The guide reports the reuse at that strength and no higher.

\elementguard
\subsection*{Epistle \cue{Ep.}}

\elementid{Fratres: Notum vobis fácio Evangélium}{\latin{Léctio Epístolæ beáti Pauli Apóstoli ad Corínthios} --- 1 Cor. 15, 1-10}{1544}

\textbf{The complete literary context.} First Corinthians 15 is the last of the
letter's problem chapters and the only one that argues a doctrine rather than
settling a practice. Some at Corinth were saying \latin{resurréctio mortuórum
non est} (v.~12). Paul answers in three movements: he restates the gospel he
delivered and the witnesses who saw the risen Christ (vv.~1--11); he shows what
follows if the dead are not raised --- empty preaching, empty faith, false
witness, the dead perished (vv.~12--19); and he expounds Christ as firstfruits,
the order of the resurrection, the manner of the risen body, and the swallowing
up of death (vv.~20--58). The appointed lesson is the first movement, minus its
last verse and minus the last clause of v.~10. It is therefore the
\textit{premiss} of the chapter read without the argument it was laid down to
support --- which is precisely what makes it usable as a Sunday lesson: the
assembly hears the creed and the witness list, not the polemic.

\textbf{The oldest layer of reception is not about grace.} Long before anyone
argued about merit from v.~10, this passage was the anti-docetic proof text.
St.~Irenaeus quotes the formula verbatim inside the argument that Christ truly
suffered (\work{Adversus haereses} 3.18.3). Tertullian presses it further, in
the public-domain English of the Ante-Nicene Fathers: ``since you are told that
He died according to the Scriptures \dots\ no otherwise than in the flesh, you
will also allow that it was in the flesh that He was raised from the dead. For
the very same body which fell in death, and which lay in the sepulchre, did
also rise again'' (\work{De resurrectione carnis} 48). \textit{Bounded note:}
\work{Adversus Marcionem} 5 engages this chapter only from v.~12, so it is not
a witness to the appearance list and is not cited as one.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ John Chrysostom, \work{Homiliae in epistulam I ad Corinthios} 38, §§2--3, 5, 7 (NPNF 1/12, tr.\ Chambers, 1889)}
Chrysostom's first observation is grammatical and it controls everything after
it: Paul says \textit{delivered} and \textit{received}, not \textit{taught} ---
``neither here does he say, I said to you, nor, I taught you, but uses the same
expression again, saying, \textit{I delivered unto you that which also I
received}.'' The gospel is transmission, not invention. The centre of the
homily is anti-Manichaean: because Christ was sinless, his dying ``for our
sins'' can only be the body's death --- ``if being without sin He died, He died
--- not the death of sin \dots\ but the death of the body'' --- and
\latin{secúndum Scriptúras} pins that sense, with the burial clinching it. The
appearance list he reads as a chain ordered by credibility, Cephas ``the most
credible of all''; and he notices that Paul writes \textit{fallen asleep}, not
\textit{dead}, so that even the verb preaches resurrection. On v.~8 the
self-abasement is calculated: ``This is rather an expression of modesty than
anything else.'' On v.~10 --- the lesson's last words --- he catches the
reserve exactly: ``he said not, I have displayed a diligence worthy of His
grace, but, \textit{it was not found vain}.'' Chrysostom's instinct throughout
is \textbf{moral}. Augustine's, on the same words, will be \textbf{dogmatic}.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Ambrosiaster, \work{Commentaria in epistulam ad Corinthios primam}, ad 15:1--11 (PL 17)}
The earliest Latin commentary on the lesson, and the one with a text-critical
surprise in it. On vv.~1--2 the stakes are total: those who desert the
tradition \latin{perdiderunt quod crediderunt; omnis enim spes credentium in
hoc sensu est, quia mortui resurgent}. \latin{Secundum Scripturas} he refers to
the Old Testament, chiefly Isaiah 53, with an excursus on prophetic past
tenses; the third day he takes from Hosea 6:3 read \latin{sub persona populi},
adding \latin{In Christo enim omnes resurrexisse, sicut in Adam omnes mortuos
esse, nemo fidelium denegat}. James is singled out \latin{ut multifaria
apparentia fidem resurrectionis firmaret}. \latin{Abortivus} he explains as
\latin{extra tempus natus} --- Paul received the apostolate when Christ had
already been received into heaven with his flesh. On v.~9, \latin{tempore enim
minor est, non dignitate}; on v.~10, \latin{Ne se extulisse jactanter putaretur,
statim se humiliat}. \textbf{And his lemma for the lesson's last clause is not
the Vulgate's.} He reads \latin{Et gratia ejus \textbf{pauper} in me non fuit}
--- ``his grace was not poor in me'' --- an Old Latin variant that Cornelius a
Lapide still knew, a millennium later, as ``St.~Ambrose's'' reading. The
missal's \latin{vácua} is not the word the first Latin commentator had.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Theodoret of Cyrus, \work{Interpretatio in xiv epistulas S.\ Pauli}, in 1 Cor. 15 (PG 82, cols.\ 347--352; Migne's Latin column quoted)}
Theodoret states the chapter's occasion first: impostors were denying the
bodily resurrection, and cross, passion and resurrection all happened
\latin{propter resurrectionem communem}; hence \latin{si enim non creditur
resurrectio corporum, supervacanea est praedicatio Evangelii}. He builds the
witness list as an ascending argument from testimony --- the five hundred are
\latin{a suspicione alienum} and checkable, since \latin{multi manent usque
adhuc}. His reading of \latin{abortivus} is his own and is the harshest in the
tradition: Paul \latin{abortivo fetui seipsum comparat, qui in hominum catalogo
non recensetur} --- the aborted foetus is not entered in the register of men.
On v.~10 he catches Paul's double bind: \latin{Divitias occultare conatur, et
aperire cogitur \dots\ Occultat enim miracula, et solum laborem ostendit, atque
hunc quoque dicit esse divinae gratiae}. \textit{Rights limit:} the only modern
English of this commentary is in copyright; the Latin column is quoted instead.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Thomas Aquinas, \work{Super I ad Corinthios}, cap.~15, lectio 1, nn.~888--911}
\textbf{Authenticity first, because this corpus has a seam in it.} The
\latin{lectura} on 1 Corinthians survives with a gap filled from Peter of
Tarantaise; that supplement covers 7:15--10:33, and the authentic
\latin{reportatio vulgata} resumes at chapter 11. Chapter 15 is therefore
Thomas's own. Lectio 1 covers vv.~1--11 entire and lectio 2 begins at v.~12, so
the whole 1962 pericope sits inside one lecture. Thomas divides the four things
delivered --- death, burial, resurrection, appearance --- gives three reasons
the burial is named at all, and explains the omission of the women's
appearances with a principle of forensic economy: \latin{noluit ponere
testimonia nisi authentica}. He preserves Augustine's judgment that the true
reading at v.~5 is \latin{duodecim} and that \latin{undecim} is \latin{corruptum
\dots\ vitio scriptorum} --- the missal prints \latin{úndecim}, and the guide
prints what the missal prints while recording that the tradition mostly defends
the harder number. On \latin{abortivus} he does what a scholastic does and
distinguishes three senses at once: \latin{vel quia nascitur extra tempus
debitum, vel cum violentia educitur, vel quia non perducitur ad debitam
quantitatem} --- and applies all three to Paul, including, remarkably, the
second: converted by coercion, which he turns into an argument
\latin{contra haereticos, qui dicunt quod nullus debet cogi ad fidem, quia
Paulus coactus fuit}. On the lesson's own last words: \latin{ex me nihil sum,
sed id quod sum, gratia Dei sum \dots\ Et dicit ``id quod sum,'' quia homo sine
gratia nihil est}. \textit{Rights limit:} Larcher's English is modern and in
copyright; the Latin is quoted instead.
\end{witnessnote}

\subsubsection*{The lesson stops one clause short of the argument the tradition built on it}

This is the finding that most changes how the Epistle may be preached from,
and it is a textual observation before it is anything else. The missal reads to
\latin{et grátia eius in me vácua non fuit} and stops. The Clementine verse
continues: \latin{sed abundántius illis ómnibus laborávi: non ego autem, sed
grátia Dei mecum}. Now consider what hangs on the words that are not read.

\begin{receptiontable}{Witness}{The argument}{Which words it needs}
St.\ Augustine, \work{De gratia et libero arbitrio} 5.12 & Paul's merit was real but \textit{evil} --- \latin{Meritum enim fuit quidem in apostolo Paulo, sed malum}; then the balance: \latin{nec gratia Dei sola, nec ipse solus, sed gratia Dei cum illo} & the proof of free will is \latin{plus omnibus illis laboravi}; the correction is \latin{non ego autem, sed gratia Dei mecum}\\
St.\ Gregory the Great, \work{Moralia in Iob} 16.25 & prevenient and subsequent grace: \latin{Superna ergo pietas prius agit in nobis aliquid sine nobis, ut, subsequente quoque nostro libero arbitrio, bonum quod iam appetimus agat nobiscum} & the whole schema hangs on one word: \latin{Non enim diceret \textbf{mecum} si \dots\ subsequens liberum arbitrium non haberet}\\
St.\ Thomas Aquinas, \work{Super I Cor.} c.~15 lect.~1 n.~909 & \latin{gratia cooperans} is derived here: God \latin{non solum infundit gratiam \dots\ sed etiam movet ad bene utendum gratia infusa} & \latin{non autem ego, sed gratia Dei mecum}\\
St.\ John Chrysostom, Hom.~38 §7 & the humility ``with reserve'' turns on a seesaw of boast and retraction & ``laboured more abundantly \dots\ yet not I''\\
Ambrosiaster; Cornelius a Lapide & separate lemmata and comments; a Lapide's anti-Reformation argument rests on \textit{with} me, not \textit{in} me & both omitted clauses\\
\end{receptiontable}

Two conclusions follow, and the second is the one worth having. \textit{First},
a guide may not say that the Fathers expound this lesson's teaching on grace,
because their classic expositions quote past its last word. What they expound
is verse 10 entire. \textit{Second}, the truncation is not a 1962 decision.
The same mid-verse ending stands in the Venice Missal of 1570 and in the Pustet
Missal of 1862, so it is the received lectionary boundary of the Roman rite, of
undocumented motive and considerable age. What the Church actually reads on
this Sunday ends on \latin{grátia \dots\ vácua non fuit}: grace not made void,
with the labour and the \latin{mecum} left unread. The guide notes, and refuses
to press, the fact that the very tradition whose key clause is cut ---
Augustine, Gregory, Thomas --- affirms human cooperation; a lection boundary is
not a doctrinal statement, and this one cannot be made into a denial of what
those authors teach.

\subsubsection*{One word, three readings, and a sixfold catalogue}

\latin{Tamquam abortívo} is where this pericope's reception genuinely
disagrees with itself, and the disagreement is substantive rather than verbal.
Ambrosiaster and Chrysostom read it temporally: born out of time, that is,
brought to apostolic birth after the ascension and Pentecost. Theodoret reads
it juridically: a thing not entered in the register of men, the language of
worthlessness rather than of chronology. Aquinas adds the violent sense ---
\latin{cum violentia educitur} --- and makes Paul's conversion on the Damascus
road a case of it. Cornelius a Lapide, writing in the seventeenth century and
read here in the public-domain English of Cobb (1908), catalogues six readings
and rejects at least one of them (Baronius's theory that Paul was a
supernumerary member of the Sanhedrin). The Douay's ``as by one born out of due
time'' takes the first sense only; the guide notes that the Latin will bear all
three and does not choose among them.

\textbf{Two further material negatives.} The \work{Summa theologiae}'s treatise
on grace, I--II qq.~106--114, never cites 1 Cor.~15:10 verbatim: the files were
searched for \latin{gratia Dei sum id quod sum}, \latin{gratia Dei mecum},
\latin{vacua}, and \latin{abundantius}, and q.~111 a.~2's \latin{sed contra}
rests on Augustine's treatise instead. The verse enters Thomas's doctrine of
grace through the \latin{lectura}, not through the \work{Summa}. And no
Augustinian exegesis of \latin{abortivus} said of Paul was found in
site-scoped searches of his Latin corpus; what Augustine does with the
adjacent verses is homiletic --- \work{Sermo} 279, 5--6 calls Paul
\latin{modicus}, ``least and last, like the hem of the Lord's garment'' that
healed the woman with the issue of blood, and \work{Enarratio in Ps.~70}, sermo
1, shows him preaching on an Apostle-reading that contained v.~9 in his own
day's liturgy.

\textbf{Where the Summa does use the lesson.} \work{Summa theologiae} III
q.~55 a.~3 cites vv.~6--7 as appearances of which the Gospels make no mention,
and III q.~57 a.~6 ad~3 presses v.~8 dogmatically: the Damascus vision
\latin{veritatem resurrectionis non probaret nisi ipsum verum corpus visum
fuisset ab eo}. The appearance-list is doing evidentiary work about the risen
body, which is what Paul laid it down to do.

\subsection*{Gradual \cue{Grad.}}

\elementid{In Deo sperávit cor meum}{\latin{Graduale} --- Ps. 27, 7 et 1}{1545}

\textbf{The chant inverts the psalm.} Psalm 27 opens with a cry of a man afraid
of being ignored --- \latin{Ad te, Dómine, clamábo: Deus meus, ne síleas a me:
nequándo táceas a me, et assimilábor descendéntibus in lacum} --- and reaches
its thanksgiving only at v.~7, after the imprecation of vv.~3--5. The Gradual
prints \latin{7 et 1}: the body of the chant is the thanksgiving and the
versicle is the cry that historically came first. As at the Introit, the
liturgy sings the arrival and then the approach.

\textbf{And it alters the words at four points.} \latin{In Deo} for the psalm's
\latin{in ipso} (the pronoun looks back to an opening \latin{Dóminus adjútor
meus} that the chant does not sing, so the chant must supply a name);
\latin{confitébor illi} for \latin{confitébor ei}; \latin{clamávi}, past, for
\latin{clamábo}, future; and, at the very end, \latin{ne discédas a me}, which
has no counterpart anywhere in the Clementine verse. Of these the last is
decisive for reception, and negatively: \textbf{no checked witness expounds
\latin{ne discédas a me}, because no checked witness has it.} Every one of them
--- Augustine, Cassiodorus, Jerome, Aquinas, Lombard, Bellarmine --- expounds
\latin{ne síleas} and \latin{nequándo táceas}. What the tradition supplies for
the chant's closing plea is therefore a sense, not a lemma, and the guide says
so wherever it uses that sense.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Augustine, \work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 27, §§1, 7}
Augustine assigns the whole psalm to Christ: \latin{Ipsius mediatoris vox est,
manu fortis in conflictu passionis}. On the versicle's ``be not silent from
me'' he reads a Christological danger --- that the Word be separated from the
assumed manhood. And at v.~7 he supplies the flattest and most consequential
gloss in the whole tradition of this chant: \latin{Et refloruit caro mea: id
est, et resurrexit caro mea}. The flourishing is the Resurrection. He then
notices the psalm's own \latin{ex voluntáte mea}: those freed from the fear of
death confess him by free will.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Cassiodorus, \work{Expositio Psalmorum} 27, ad vv.~1, 7}
Cassiodorus's lemma at v.~7 is the chant's \latin{confitébor illi}. He follows
Augustine on the speaker --- \latin{Christus Dominus clamat ad Patrem tempore
passionis} --- and then develops the flower-figure into a two-stage history
that Augustine had left implicit: \latin{Refloruit \dots\ quia et primo
floruit: quippe quae ex Virgine sine peccato tanquam pulcherrimi floris
singulare decus emicuit}, with Isaias 11:1 behind it. The flesh flowered in the
virginal conception and re-flowered in the Resurrection. \latin{Refloruit ergo
significat resurrexit}: the prefix carries the doctrine.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Jerome, \work{Commentarioli in Psalmos} 27 (Morin)}
Two short notes, both useful. On v.~1, \latin{Lacus significat infernum} --- and
his lemma is the Gallican one, without \latin{ne discédas}. On v.~7 he records
a variant and then a second reading: \latin{Pro carne, Theodotion ``cor''
interpretatus est. Aliter: quippe consurreximus Christo}. Theodotion read
``heart'' where the Church's psalter reads ``flesh''; and the flourishing may
be read not of Christ's flesh but of ours, risen with him. \textit{Negative:}
there is no Jerome homily on Psalm 27; the \work{Tractatus} series jumps from
Psalm 14 to Psalm 66.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Thomas Aquinas, \work{Super Psalmo} 27, nn.~1, 7}
Thomas divides the psalm scholastically and then, following the Gloss, gives
the versicle to the man Christ praying not to be abandoned by the Godhead:
\latin{ne sileas subtrahendo mihi in beneficium resurrectionis, et ne taceas,
idest ne deseras in passione}. He also records Jerome's rendering of the
Hebrew, \latin{ne te tacente assimiler}. On v.~7 he gives the flower a
salvation-historical span: \latin{In primo homine floruit caro eius per
innocentiae puritatem, sed peccando inquinata est. Sed in Christo refloruit in
resurrectione}. His lemma for the last clause is \latin{confitébor tibi}, not
the chant's \latin{illi}, and he reads it of the converted will praising
freely; he adds that if the verse is said of Christ, \latin{refertur ad membra
sua}.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Theodoret of Cyrus, \work{Interpretatio in Psalmos} 27, ad v.~7 (PG 80, col.\ 1060) --- the dissent}
Theodoret keeps the psalm where the Antiochene school keeps it: with David in
flight from Saul, the Ziphites and Doeg. On v.~7 he understands a man released
from the hardships of flight, sprouting and blooming again --- bodily vigour
recovered because his spirits have recovered. \textbf{No resurrection.} He is
the one major witness who declines the Christological reading of
\latin{reflóruit caro mea}, and the chant is therefore sung under a real
disagreement, not a consensus. \textit{Rights limit:} summarised, not quoted;
the only English is in copyright.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Peter Lombard, \work{Commentarium in Psalmos} 27, ad v.~7 (PL 191); St.\ Robert Bellarmine, \work{Explanatio in Psalmos} 27, tr.\ O'Sullivan (1866), pp.~68--69}
Lombard transmits both readings with their tags: the flesh \latin{quae in Adam
defloruit per peccatum} from the interlinear Gloss, and \latin{Vel, caro mea
refloruit, id est iterum in resurrectione floruit} correctly attributed to
Cassiodorus. Bellarmine popularises the result --- ``He now passes to foretell
the glory of the Lord's resurrection'' --- and gives the flower its most
concrete form: ``My flesh, that had withered up in death, is not only restored
to life, but to the bloom of youth, health, joy, and beauty.''
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{A one-line development map.} Augustine's equation
(\latin{refloruit~=~resurrexit}) is taken up by Cassiodorus, who adds the
conception-flower; Lombard transmits both with attributions intact; Aquinas
recasts them as Adam and Christ, flesh and soul; Bellarmine turns them into
devotional description. Jerome supports from the side with Theodotion's variant
and the co-resurrection reading. Theodoret dissents. Hilary is simply absent:
the extant \work{Tractatus super Psalmos} covers Psalms 1, 2, 9, 13, 14, 51--69
and 118--150 with fragments, and Psalm 27 is not among them.

\subsection*{Alleluia \cue{All.}}

\elementid{Exsultáte Deo adiutóri nostro}{Alleluia, no separate heading --- Ps. 80, 2-3}{1546}

\textbf{The psalm and the compression.} Psalm 80 is a feast-day psalm: a
summons to shout, a recital of the exodus from the burden of Egypt, and then a
divine oracle of complaint --- my people did not hear me, so I let them go
after the desires of their heart. The chant takes only the summons, and
condenses even that. The Clementine v.~3 reads \latin{Súmite psalmum, et date
tympanum; psaltérium iucúndum, cum cíthara}; the Alleluia sings \latin{súmite
psalmum iucúndum cum cíthara}. Two instruments vanish and one adjective moves:
\latin{iucúndum} belongs to the psaltery in the Bible and to the psalm in the
chant. The result is a line that says, in effect, take a pleasant psalm with
the harp --- which is a description of what the schola is at that moment doing.

\textbf{The reception problem this creates, stated plainly.} Every checked
expositor of this verse builds part of his exposition on the timbrel that the
chant removes. That is not an objection to the chant; it is a limit on the
reception. What follows illuminates Psalm 80:2--3; it does not comment on the
Alleluia's line, which is nobody's lemma.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Augustine, \work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 80, §§3--5}
Augustine takes Asaph as \latin{congregatio} and the title's winepresses as the
Church's press, separating oil from dregs. On v.~2 he is anti-spectacle:
\latin{Exsultate alii circo, vos Deo} --- let others shout in the circus; you,
to God our helper. On v.~3 he builds an exchange out of the two instruments:
\latin{Psalmus spiritalis est, tympanum carnale \dots\ accipite spiritalia, et
date carnalia}. Receive the spiritual word, give back carnal things --- support
and mercy to the poor. The psaltery, which sounds from above, is heavenly
preaching; the cithara, played below, is earthly works; word and deed have to
sound together.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Cassiodorus, \work{Expositio Psalmorum} 80, ad vv.~2--3}
Cassiodorus first presses \latin{adiutóri}: an idol \latin{fictitius deus non
est adiutor, sed elisor; non sanans, sed vulnerans} --- not healing but
wounding. The true God heals, and the verb is the one this day's Offertory
sings four elements later. He then etymologises the shout --- \latin{Iubilate
dictum est a iuvando, id est delectando} --- and re-tools Augustine's exchange
ascetically: the drum is a stretched hide, therefore the mortified body,
\latin{sic hominum corpus, dum pro Domino tribulatione quatitur, ad superna
mandata dulcius temperatur; tunc Deo bene damus tympanum dum eleemosynas
facimus, cum ieiuniis corpus affligimus}. Psaltery and cithara --- God's words
and human acts --- \latin{in unam societatem debeant convenire}.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Theodoret of Cyrus, \work{Interpretatio in Psalmos} 80, ad vv.~2--3 (PG 80, cols.\ 1520--1521); Bellarmine, \work{Explanatio in Psalmos} 80, tr.\ O'Sullivan, pp.~246--247}
The literal wing. Theodoret notes the hexaplaric alternatives for the two
verbs, takes the jubilation as a victory-shout, and keeps the instruments as
instruments, appealing to the account of the temple orchestra in Chronicles.
Bellarmine sides with him against Augustine on the occasion --- a new-moon
festival --- and adds, with characteristic bluntness, that ``he who sings
moodily \dots\ gives to understand that he has very little affection for him
whose praises he chants.'' Against Augustine's and Cassiodorus's allegory this
is a genuine and unresolved divergence about what the verse is for.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{Two negatives worth printing.} St.~Jerome does treat Psalm 80, but not
these verses: his homily is a riff on the title, on the three
\latin{pro torcularibus} psalms (8, 80, 83) and on the number eighty ---
\latin{Octavus dies resurrectionis est \dots\ in octavo ergo vincimus, et in
octogesimo cum Domino congregamur} --- and then leaps from the title to v.~5.
His famous timbrel allegory (\latin{nos sumus tympanum \dots\ quod non habet
carnes, nisi quod tenditur}) belongs to his homily on Psalm 149, not here, and
is not cited for this chant. And Aquinas, again, is absent: his psalms
commentary ends at Psalm 54.

\subsection*{Gospel \cue{Gosp.}}

\elementid{In illo témpore: Exiens Iesus de fínibus Tyri}{\latin{✠ Sequéntia sancti Evangélii secúndum Marcum} --- Marc. 7, 31-37}{1547}

\textbf{Where the pericope sits in Mark, and what the incipit removes.} The
healing follows the Syrophoenician woman (7:24--30) and precedes the feeding of
the four thousand (8:1--9). The itinerary --- Tyre, then Sidon, then the Sea of
Galilee by way of the Decapolis --- keeps Jesus in largely Gentile territory
from beginning to end, and it is a long way round: Sidon lies north of Tyre, so
the route to the lake is described as a detour. Mark's own first words are
\latin{Et íterum éxiens}: \textit{again} going out, a backward link to the
earlier Tyre episode. The liturgical incipit replaces that with \latin{In illo
témpore: Exiens Iesus}, supplying a subject Mark does not name at v.~31 and
dropping the \latin{íterum}. The lesson therefore begins as a fresh journey
where the evangelist wrote a resumption.

\textbf{There is no synoptic parallel.} Matthew 15:29--31 is a summary notice
of many healings on a mountain: no individual deaf-mute, no fingers, no
spittle, no sigh, no \latin{Ephphetha}. That is why the scene surfaces in
St.~Augustine's \work{De consensu evangelistarum} at Book IV --- his sweep of
Mark-only material --- and not in the harmonising Books II--III. Any reception
of ``the healing of the deaf-mute'' is reception of Mark alone.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Ps-Jerome, \work{Commentarius in evangelium secundum Marcum}, cap.\ VII (PL 30, cols.\ 611C--612B)}
Transmitted under Jerome's name and not his: the 1842 Oxford editors of the
\work{Catena} already called it spurious, and modern scholarship treats it as
an anonymous seventh-century work of the Irish milieu --- in any case earlier
than Bede. Its reading is corporate from the first line: Tyre is
\latin{angustia} and stands for Judaea; \latin{Sidon vero venatio
interpretatur, quae fera indomitaque nostra natio est}; the Decapolis is
\latin{decalogi mandata}. The itinerary \textit{is} the passage of salvation
from Judaea to the Gentiles. The deaf-mute is accordingly not an individual but
the race: \latin{Genus humanum per multa membra, ac si unus homo varia peste
absumptus enumeratur: in protoplasto caecatur}. The fingers are
\latin{verba spiritus}; the spittle is \latin{divina sapientia: quae solvit
vinculum labiorum humani generis} --- and the author names what the loosed
mouth then says: \latin{Credo in Deum Patrem omnipotentem}. The opened ears
open \latin{ad hymnos, cantica, et psalmos}. The sigh teaches: \latin{nos
gemere docuit, et in coelum thesaurum cordis nostri erigere}.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Bede, \work{In Marci evangelium expositio} II, on 7:31--37 (PL 92, cols.\ 203--206; his lemma spells \latin{Effeta})}
The fullest Latin exposition, and the one the later tradition quarries. Six
moves. \textit{(a)} Being taken apart from the crowd is where salvation starts:
\latin{Prima salutis janua est infirmum apprehendente Domino de turba seorsum
educi}, the sick mind drawn away \latin{a consuetis humanae conversationis
moribus}. \textit{(b)} The fingers are the Holy Spirit, and he proves it from
Scripture rather than asserting it: \latin{Mittit digitos suos in auriculas,
cum per dona sancti Spiritus aures cordis \dots\ aperit. Namque digitum Dei
appellari Spiritum sanctum testatur ipse Dominus}, comparing Luke 11:20 with
Matthew 12:28. \textit{(c)} The spittle is divine wisdom --- \latin{Sputum
namque Domini saporem designat sapientiae} --- and the tongue it touches is a
catechumen's: the Lord acts \latin{cum ad confessionem fidei ora
catechizandorum instruit}. \textit{(d)} The look upward names where hearing and
speech must be sought; the sigh is not need but instruction: \latin{Ingemuit
autem, non quia ipsi opus esset cum gemitu aliquid petere a Patre \dots\ sed ut
nobis gemendi daret exemplum}. \textit{(e)} Verses 34--35 hold both natures at
once: \latin{Suspiciens in caelum quasi homo Deum precaturus ingemuit, sed mox
uno sermone quasi divina potens maiestate curavit}. \textit{(f)} Morally the
deaf-mute is anyone \latin{qui nec aures audiendis Dei verbis, nec os aperit
proloquendis}, and he is carried by those already taught. \textit{One
correction this guide makes:} Bede's closing paragraph on v.~36 is Augustine's
\work{De consensu evangelistarum} 4.4.5 almost verbatim. Here Bede depends on
Augustine --- not, as is sometimes assumed for Marcan pericopes, on Gregory.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{The Victor-of-Antioch tradition, quoted as ``Chrysostomus'' in the \work{Catena aurea in Marcum}, cap.~7, lectio 4}
Chrysostom wrote no homilies on Mark. The excerpts the \work{Catena} labels
``Chrysostomus'' for this pericope belong to the Greek Mark-catena tradition
associated with Victor of Antioch; the 1842 Oxford editors say so in the
margin and print ``Pseudo-Chrys.'' The guide names the source that way and does
not credit Chrysostom. The move is worth having on its own account: the healing
instrument is Christ's own assumed body, \latin{quod divina virtute ditatum
erat corpus divinitati unitum \dots\ veniens Christus in seipso perfectionem
demonstravit humanae naturae}; and the sigh is \latin{nostram causam suscipiens
in seipso, et naturae misertus humanae}. Where Bede makes the sigh an example
for us, this tradition makes it a taking-on of our case.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Theophylact, \work{Enarratio in evangelium Marci}, on 7:31--37 (PG 123, cols.\ 565--568; Migne's Latin quoted)}
The Greek witness, read with Migne's facing Latin because the only English is
in copyright. He takes the affliction as demonic in origin, which is why it
follows the exorcism of the Syrophoenician's daughter, and notes that Jesus
leaves Gentile territory promptly lest he be called a transgressor of the law.
On the taking aside: \latin{non erat arrogantiae sectator, qui se humiliarat
usque ad nostram inopiam}. On the spittle he makes the strongest sacramental
point in the tradition: it was done \latin{ut ostenderet omnes partes sanctae
carnis suae sanctas ac divinas esse \dots\ tametsi superfluitas sit omne
sputum, sed in Domino admirabilia erant ac divina omnia}. On the sigh he is
openly undecided --- twice \latin{fortassis} --- between prayer to the Father
and pity for human nature under tyranny. His coda, which both the \work{Catena}
and a Lapide lift, converts the command of silence into an ethic: benefactors
must not seek applause, and beneficiaries must praise even the unwilling
benefactor.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Augustine, \work{De consensu evangelistarum} 4.4.5 (NPNF 1/6, tr.\ Salmond, 1888)}
Augustine treats only vv.~36--37, and only one difficulty: if Christ knew they
would publish it the more, why forbid it? His answer, in the public-domain
English, is that ``he desired to give backward ones to understand how much more
zealously and fervently they ought to preach on whom He lays the commission to
preach, if even men who were interdicted were unable to keep silent.'' No
allegory of fingers, spittle or sigh anywhere; this is the one place the
Latin tradition's greatest exegete touches the pericope, and it is a note on
prescience and preaching.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Cornelius a Lapide, on Mark 7:31--37, \work{The Great Commentary}, tr.\ T.~W.\ Mossman, vol.~3 (1887; 1891 printing), pp.~408--410}
Philology first. The Greek adjective at v.~32, he says, means ``speaking with
difficulty \dots\ stammering,'' not born mute; and \latin{Ephphetha} he derives
through the Hebrew root with the note that ``for which the Syrians use
Ephpheta.'' He minds decorum at v.~33 (Christ spat on his own finger). Then he
absorbs the tradition --- Bede twice, including ``Where, says Bede, the two
natures of the one and the same Mediator between God and man are plainly set
forth''; Theophylact on praising benefactors; Augustine on the silence. His own
novelty is scholastic and dissenting: the command of v.~36 ``was not properly a
command, involving a fault if disobeyed, but merely a token of urbanity and
modesty \dots\ Wherefore they did not commit sin who nevertheless divulged
them.'' On v.~37 he presses the adverb: it means ``beautifully, becomingly,
harmoniously \dots\ Christ's whole life was one continuous beneficence.''
\end{witnessnote}

\subsubsection*{The word left the Gospel and became a rite}

This pericope did something no other text in the formulary did: it generated a
liturgical ceremony that was still being performed in 1962. St.~Ambrose is the
earliest witness, and he says outright where the ceremony came from --- in the
public-domain English of the NPNF translation: ``celebrating the mystery of the
opening, we said, Epphatha, which is, Be opened \dots\ Christ made use of this
mystery in the Gospel, as we read, when He healed him who was deaf and dumb''
(\work{De mysteriis} 1.3--4). \work{De sacramentis} I.1.2--3 describes the
gesture from the other side, as a memory of what was done: the priest touched
ears and nostrils, ``It is a Hebrew word, which rendered into Latin is
adaperire, that is, Be opened'' --- and explains the move from mouth to nostrils
on grounds of propriety toward women.

Fifteen centuries later the \work{Rituale Romanum} still prints it. In the
Pustet printing of 1872, at the baptism of infants (pp.~11--12): \latin{Postea
Sacerdos digito accipiat de saliva oris sui, et tangat aures et nares infantis:
tangendo vero aurem dexteram, et sinistram, dicat: Ephpheta, quod est,
Adaperire: deinde tangit nares, dicens: In odorem suavitatis.} The saliva and
the ears are the Gospel's own gestures, carried into the rite; the same
ceremony stands in the order for adults at p.~33. Ambrose's line runs from this
pericope into the sacramental books independently of the whole exegetical
chain, and it is the reason a reader is more likely to have met the word
\latin{Ephphetha} at a font than in Mark.

\subsubsection*{A large negative result, deliberately printed}

\textbf{St.~Gregory the Great does not expound this Gospel.} The complete OCR
of PL 75 and PL 76 --- the \work{Moralia} entire, the \work{Homiliae in
Ezechielem}, and the forty \work{Homiliae in Evangelia} with their indices ---
was searched case-insensitively and OCR-tolerantly for the word in its three
spellings, for \latin{surdum et mutum} and its case-forms, and every ``Marc.''
citation in both volumes was reviewed (sixty-six of them in PL 76). There is no
exposition of Mark 7:31--37 anywhere, and no Gospel homily on the pericope; the
one near hit is Herod and the Baptist. Two independent corroborations: the
\work{Catena aurea} gathers no Gregory for this lection, and Bede, who
elsewhere leans on Gregory heavily, here transcribes Augustine instead.
\textit{Bounds:} the search was OCR-based, and PL 77 --- the \work{Regula
pastoralis}, the \work{Dialogi}, the \work{Registrum} --- was not searched.
Two smaller negatives belong beside it: the authentic Jerome \work{Tractatus in
Marci evangelium} jumps from 5:30--43 to 8:1--9 and has no tractate here; and
Chrysostom's Homily 52 on Matthew, which reaches the mountain summary, has no
deaf-mute, no spittle and no reference to Mark.

\subsection*{Offertory \cue{Off.}}

\elementid{Exaltábo te, Dómine}{\latin{Ant. ad Offertorium} --- Ps. 29, 2-3}{1548}

\textbf{The title does the work.} Psalm 29 carries the superscription
\latin{Psalmus cantici in dedicatione domus David}, and the whole Latin
tradition reads the psalm through it. The antiphon takes vv.~2--3: extolling,
being upheld, enemies not allowed to gloat, a cry, and a healing. It drops
\latin{Deus meus} from the opening vocative of v.~3, which every checked lemma
carries. What is left is the shortest possible statement of the day's Gospel in
the first person: \latin{clamávi ad te, et sanásti me}.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Augustine, \work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 29 --- two expositions (Enarr.\ I §§1--3; Enarr.\ II §§1, 6, 11--12)}
The first exposition calls the psalm one ``of the joy of the Resurrection \dots\
not only of the Lord, but also of the whole Church,'' and reads v.~2 as praise
of God's own height: \latin{Exaltabo \dots\ suscepisti me: sublimitatem tuam
laudabo}. The healing at v.~3 is healing from mortality itself. The second
exposition --- which exists in Latin only, the NPNF series carrying just the
first --- solves the title's puzzle: \latin{Modo enim fabricatur domus, id est
Ecclesia, postea dedicabitur. In dedicatione apparebit gloria populi
christiani, quae modo latet}. The house is being built now and dedicated later;
Christ's risen body is the pattern. And then, on the antiphon's own last words,
the question that the whole later tradition repeats: \latin{Quem sanavit, qui
nunquam aegrotavit \dots? Non, sed mortem carnis portabat, vulnus tuum
portabat, sanaturus te de vulnere tuo. Sanata est autem caro. Quando? Cum
resurrexit} --- with 1 Cor.~15:54 quoted, which is again the day's Epistle
chapter.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Cassiodorus, \work{Expositio Psalmorum} 29, title and vv.~2--3}
Cassiodorus compresses the title into a formula: \latin{Domus significat
dominici corporis templum; dedicatio vero resurrectionem}. On v.~2 he makes a
rhetorician's note on the chant's own verb: \latin{Delectasti vero posuit, pro
delectare fecisti. Quae figura dicitur hypallage}. On v.~3 he reruns
Augustine's paradox almost verbatim --- \latin{Quemadmodum sanatus est, qui non
legitur aegrotasse? Sed occulte ille aegrotabat, quando universum hominem sua
incarnatione suscepit \dots\ quia in illo sanati sumus, sanatum se ipse
profitetur} --- a textbook demonstration of how much of Cassiodorus is
Augustine reorganised.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Jerome, \work{Commentarioli in Psalmos} 29, ad titulum; St.\ Thomas Aquinas, \work{Super Psalmo} 29, nn.~1--2}
Jerome gives the tradition its tersest form in a single sentence:
\latin{Dedicatio domus David, resurrectio Salvatoris intellegitur, in qua omnia
vitae corpora dedicantur}. Aquinas weighs the letter first --- David never
dedicated the Lord's house, and 2 Samuel 5 may supply his own --- then decides:
\latin{Tamen melius intelligitur quod referatur ad mysterium domus David, idest
Christi \dots\ dedicationem, quae fuit in resurrectione, quando corpus Christi
indutum est gloria immortalitatis. Super corpus Christi continue construitur; et
per conversionem fidelium dedicabitur.} That fuses Cassiodorus's body and
Augustine's Church into a single two-stage scheme. On v.~3 he distinguishes:
God heals the whole man, but \latin{Christum vero sanavit a corporali solum
passibilitate}.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Theodoret of Cyrus, \work{Interpretatio in Psalmos} 29, title and vv.~2--3 (PG 80, cols.\ 1069--1072); Bellarmine, tr.\ O'Sullivan, pp.~73--74}
Theodoret argues from the letter that the title cannot be David's temple, since
David neither built it nor fits the words, and concludes that the dedication of
the house is the re-making of human nature accomplished when Christ accepted
and destroyed death. On v.~2 he has the Master take our firstfruits and refuse
to let nature become the demons' prize. On v.~3 Antiochene precision returns:
the cry and the healing fit Hezekiah in the obvious sense, while human nature
received its resurrection unasked, out of sheer love of humankind. Even the
soberest reader of the four, then, reaches death-destroyed-and-nature-renewed;
only the mechanics differ. Bellarmine states the consensus flatly: ``In a
prophetic sense, it applies literally to Christ. `Thou hast healed me' of the
wounds I suffered on the cross \dots\ `saved me' by my resurrection,'' and adds
that all the saints will say it ``most completely, in soul and body'' on the
last day.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{What the convergence is and is not.} On this psalm the Latin and Greek
traditions agree, which they did not at the Gradual or the Alleluia: Jerome,
Augustine, Cassiodorus, Aquinas, Theodoret and Bellarmine all read the healing
of v.~3 as the Resurrection. That convergence is the reason for a caution the
guide repeats in the interpretive section: to hear the Offertory as the healed
deaf-mute's own thanksgiving is to read it against, not with, the checked
tradition, which puts the words in the mouth of the risen Christ or of the
Church in him. \textit{Negative:} there is no Hilary on Psalm 29.

\subsection*{Secret \cue{Sec.}}

\elementid{Réspice, Dómine, quǽsumus, nostram propítius servitútem}{\latin{Secreta}, composed oration}{1549}

\textbf{The oldest text in the formulary, and it began somewhere else.} This
prayer stands in the Veronese (``Leonine'') collection, ed.\ Feltoe, p.~142 ---
inside the October mass \latin{DE SICCITATE TEMPORIS}, a mass against drought,
whose preface begs for rain. It is not a Sunday prayer there and has nothing to
do with the harvest imagery this Sunday's Communion carries; it became a Sunday
Secret only when the Gelasian gathered the three orations into one mass
(Wilson, p.~228), and it was fixed to a numbered Sunday only later still. Three
verified variants, recorded and not blended:

\begin{receptiontable}{Witness}{Reading}{Character}
Veronese, Feltoe p.~142 & \latin{Respice quaesumus Dne nostram propitius servitutem \dots\ sit nostrae \textbf{fragilitati} subsidium} & dative; the older word order \latin{quaesumus Domine}\\
Old Gelasian, Wilson p.~228 & \latin{fragilitatis} in V, R, S and Gerbert's text (Wilson's n.~7); \textbf{no} \latin{et} before the second \latin{sit} & the 1962 genitive is already Gelasian; the 1962 \latin{et} is not\\
Gregorian Hadrianum, Wilson p.~172 & \latin{fragilitati} in the base text, \textbf{fragilitatis} in Ottob.~313 & the manuscript that carries the 1962 reading is the same one whose chant cues sit two masses away\\
Venice Missal, 1570 & \latin{ad nostram \dots\ servitutem} & that edition's own preposition; not the 1962 text and not adopted here\\
\end{receptiontable}

\textbf{The grammar.} \latin{Servitus} is the object of God's looking, and the
prayer asks two things of one gift: \latin{sit tibi munus accéptum, et sit
nostræ fragilitátis subsídium}. The first clause is directed at God, the second
at the offerers, and the genitive is doing real work --- the support is
\textit{of our frailty}, that is, support supplied to a weakness that is
admitted in the same breath in which the gift is offered. The dative of the
older books (\latin{fragilitati subsidium}, ``a support for frailty'') says
something very slightly different, and the two readings are printed above
rather than harmonised.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Blessed Ildefonso Schuster, \work{The Sacramentary}, vol.~III (1927), p.~127}
Schuster fixes the register of the first noun. The \latin{servitus} of which
the Missal speaks, he says, is precisely what the Greeks called
\latin{leitourgia} --- in his own words, ``the sacerdotal ministry.'' On that reading the
prayer does not ask God to look at a general attitude of subjection but at the
liturgical act being performed as the words are said, and the frailty supported
is the frailty of those performing it.
\end{witnessnote}

\subsection*{Communion \cue{Comm.}}

\elementid{Honóra Dóminum de tua substántia}{\latin{Ant. ad Communionem} --- Prov. 3, 9-10}{1550}

\textbf{The one wisdom text of the day, and the missal trims it.} Proverbs 3 is
a father's instruction: trust the Lord and lean not on your own prudence, honour
him with your substance, do not despise his correction. Verses 9--10 are the
single promise of material increase in the chapter, and they are conditioned on
a prior giving away. The antiphon compresses at three points: v.~9 loses
\latin{ómnium} and the closing imperative \latin{da ei}, and v.~10 loses the
second \latin{tua}. Losing \latin{da ei} is the interesting one, because it
removes the verb of donation and leaves the two verbs of filling: \textit{honour
\dots\ and thy barns shall be filled}. Schuster notices that the antiphon
``departs from the usual rule of the classical antiphons'' at all --- it is not
a psalm --- and connects it to ``these summer Masses, during the time of
harvest,'' and to the old blessing of firstfruits at \latin{Per quem hæc omnia}.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Bede, \work{In Proverbia Salomonis} I, on Prov.~3:9--10 (PL 91, cols.\ 950--952)}
Bede reads the two verses on three levels and the second is the one that
matters for this Mass. \textit{Literally}, the alms must come from one's own
and honestly gotten substance, and be given for God's honour rather than for
applause: \latin{Multi multa pauperibus, sed de aliena substantia \dots\
tribuunt}. \textit{Spiritually}, the firstfruits are the firstfruits of
\textit{every} good work and of every grace received; a man honours the Lord
\latin{de sua substantia} who credits all the good he has to grace and not to
his own powers and merits, \latin{memor illius verbi: sine me nihil potestis
facere} (John 15:5). \textit{Eschatologically}, the filled barns are heavenly
satiety --- he quotes \latin{satiabor \dots\ dum manifestabitur gloria tua} ---
and the overflowing presses the \latin{interna suavitas aeternorum}; the giver
of earthly help is repaid \latin{caelesti remuneratione, ditior dando}, richer
for the giving.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Rabanus Maurus, \work{Expositio in Proverbia Salomonis} I (PL 111, cols.\ c.\ 688--690); Salvian of Marseilles, \work{Ad Ecclesiam} I (PL 53, cols.\ 214--216)}
Rabanus prints the full Vulgate lemma and then reproduces Bede's exposition
verbatim, minus a clause: the Carolingian canonisation of Bede's reading and
the route by which it reached the schools. Salvian, writing a good three
centuries before Bede, is entirely literal and much sharper. Since everything
we have is God's gift, God calls his own property ``ours'' precisely so that
our giving can earn something: \latin{Cum totum suum sit quod ab eo accepimus,
nostrum esse dicit ut demus \dots\ Si devotus es, da quasi tuum; si indevotus,
redde quasi non tuum.} Give it as yours if you are devout; hand it back as not
yours if you are not.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{Bede's second level and the day's Epistle say the same thing twice.}
\latin{Grátia autem Dei sum id quod sum} is the first-person form of ``credit
all your good to grace, not to your own powers.'' This guide records the
convergence as a textual and reception observation and claims nothing about
why the two texts stand on one page; the chant-cue evidence at the Collect
shows that the Communion and the orations of this Mass reached each other
late.

\textbf{Negatives, and one lead left open.} Salonius of Geneva's mystical
exposition of Proverbs was searched entire for \latin{Honora},
\latin{substantia}, \latin{primiti-}, \latin{horrea} and \latin{torcular-} and
treats these verses nowhere. St.~Cyprian, the obvious candidate on almsgiving,
quotes Proverbs 20:9, 21:13, 10:3 and 20:7, Sirach 29:12 and Tobias 4 in
\work{De opere et eleemosynis}, and Isaias 58, Job, Tobias and Micheas in
\work{Ad Quirinum} III.1 --- but not Proverbs 3:9. Tobias 4:7, ``give alms of
thy substance,'' is the near-twin that the Latin tradition tends to quote
instead. Greek patristic use of Proverbs 3:9 was not verified: the Septuagint's
wording differs enough from the Vulgate's that a phrase search cannot reach it,
and the question is recorded as a lead rather than closed.

\subsection*{Postcommunion \cue{Postcomm.}}

\elementid{Sentiámus, quǽsumus, Dómine, tui perceptióne sacraménti}{\latin{Postcommunio}, composed oration}{1551}

\textbf{Transmission.} Like the Secret, this prayer predates its Sunday. It
stands in the Veronese collection at Feltoe p.~80, in a July mass, in a form
with one word the later books drop: \latin{ut in utroque salvati \textbf{de}
caelestis remedii plenitudine gloriemur}. The Gelasian (Wilson p.~228) has the
older word order \latin{Sentiamus, Domine, quaesumus}; the Hadrianum
(p.~172) and 1962 read \latin{quǽsumus, Dómine}. The 1962 form was verified
against the facsimile; nothing is blended.

\textbf{The grammar, and the two things it refuses to separate.}
\latin{Tui \dots\ sacraménti} is a split genitive governed by
\latin{perceptióne}; the subject of \latin{sentiámus} is the assembly, and the
verb is deliberately experiential --- may we \textit{feel} the help. What is
asked for is \latin{subsídium mentis et córporis}, and \latin{in utróque}
resumes exactly those two. The prayer will not let the sacrament be a
spiritual benefit with bodily side effects, nor the reverse: it names both,
saves both, and then asks for something more than benefit. \latin{Gloriémur} is
a verb of boasting. The 1861 English renders it ``enjoy the full effect,''
which keeps the petition and loses the boast; the divergence was declared at
the element above and the argument here is conducted on the Latin.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Schuster, \work{The Sacramentary}, vol.~III (1927), pp.~127--128}
Schuster draws the consequence the prayer's second noun invites: the body's
contact with the Body of Jesus confers ``the grace of the final
resurrection.'' That is reception of \latin{córporis} and of \latin{in utróque
salváti} together, and it is the point at which this Postcommunion joins the
resurrection reading that the Introit's last clause, the Gradual's
\latin{reflóruit}, the Offertory's title and the Epistle's kerygma have each
carried in their own way. The convergence is a fact about the checked
reception of five separate texts. It is not evidence that anyone assembled
them for that reason, and the guide does not make it into evidence.
\end{witnessnote}
