# Proposed interpretations: Twenty-fourth and Last Sunday after Pentecost (1962)

Research stage, iteration 0, of `proper-study` v19 run `11c5af05fe374203`,
16 November 2026. Three readings of the whole formulary, for the expansive
study. Every locus cited is in research/scope.md, section 5, where the bytes
read are named; the textual difficulties are in scope section 7 and the
disagreements in section 8. The formulary is a compilation: each reading's
joining of elements is the editor's synthesis, marked below as such, and each
Father is credited only with what he says of his own passage. No Father
comments on this Mass as a Mass; the liturgical commentators (scope section 6)
are ecclesiastical writers and support, never carry.

The appointed elements and their keys: `introit` (Jer 29:11-12, 14 with Ps
84:2), `collect` (*Excita*), `epistle` (Col 1:9-14), `gradual` (Ps 43:8-9),
`alleluia` (Ps 129:1-2), `gospel` (Mt 24:15-35), `offertory` (Ps 129:1-2),
`secret` (*Propitius esto*), `communion` (Mk 11:24), `postcommunion`
(*Concede … vitiosum*).

## The question each reading answers

The Gospel is the formulary's most demanding text: a prophecy of desolation,
flight, deception and cosmic signs that ends in a gathering, a parable and an
oath about words that do not pass. Around it the chants speak of captivity
turned back and deliverance done, and the orations ask that wills be stirred,
hearts turned and minds healed. Three distinct questions arise from the
Gospel's own words, and the Fathers answer each by a different inference.

| Reading | Exegetical question | The words that raise it | Principal inference | Consequence that distinguishes it |

The three are not three titles for one application. Reading 1 answers
*where* the end leads and is christological and anagogical; reading 2 answers
*how* to live under the warning and is moral; reading 3 answers *why* the
warning and the promise can be trusted and turns on the theology of the word
and of prayer. They share the Gospel and differ in which of its verses
governs and which Fathers carry the inference.

## 1. `where-the-body-is` — The scattered gathered to the crucified body

### Controlling claim

The Gospel's end is a gathering: *where the body is, there the eagles will be
gathered*, and the angels *will gather his elect from the four winds*. Read
with Jerome and Hilary, the body is the crucified Christ and the gathering is
to his passion; read with Augustine, the captivity from which the scattered
are brought back is sin, and the body to which they come is the risen one who
feeds them. Set beside the Introit's *I will bring back your captivity from
all places* and the Epistle's *who rescued us from the power of darkness and
transferred us into the kingdom of the Son*, the formulary reads (editorial
synthesis) as one assembly: the exiles of the Introit are the elect of the
Gospel, and the Epistle names their passage as already begun.

### Carrying authors and their reasoning

- **Jerome** (*In Mt.* IV, col. 179-180; *In Hier.* V, cols. 893-894). On 24:28
  he moves from the natural proverb (eagles sense a carcass across seas) to the
  inference *quanto magis nos … debet festinare ad eum*. He then reads *corpus*
  through the Greek πτῶμα, *cadaver*, *"ab eo quod per mortem cadat"*: the word
  itself names a body that has fallen in death, so the body is *passionem
  Christi*, *"ad quam provocamur; ut ubicumque in Scripturis legitur,
  congregemur"*. The eagles are *sancti, quibus innovata est juventus ut
  aquilae* (Ps 102:5), who take wings *"ut ad Christi veniant passionem"* (Is
  40:31). On Jer 29 his anagogy makes the return from Babylon the return
  *"ad locum nostrum Ecclesiam"*, after *saeculi hujus confusione*, when God
  fulfils what he promised. Two of his own loci, on two of this Mass's texts,
  each read the end as a coming home.
- **Hilary** (*In Mt.* 25.8, 26.1). He calls the saints eagles *de volatu
  spiritalis corporis* and says the angels gather them *in loco passionis*;
  his reason: *"digne illic claritatis adventus exspectabitur, ubi nobis
  gloriam aeternitatis passione corporeae humilitatis operatus est"*: the glory
  returns where it was earned. In 26.1 the cosmic signs show *claritatis
  reditum*, and *"sic ex humilitate mortis Dei gloria"*, the time of our
  redemption in which we are carried *ex corruptione corporum in honorem
  spiritalis substantiae*.
- **Augustine** (*En. in Ps.* 84.3-5; *Quaest. Ev.* I.42). On the Introit's
  verse he asks which captivity is turned back, since *neminem nostrum modo
  esse apud Barbaros*; he answers with Rom 7:23, the captivity of the law of
  sin, said to Christ, *"liberando nos ab operibus malis, a peccatis nostris,
  per quae nobis satanas dominabatur"*; *"remissa iniquitate liberaris"*. On
  24:28 the body is the risen body taken to heaven, *cadaver* because he spoke
  as one about to die, and the eagles are the spiritual who *"passionem
  humilitatemque imitando, tanquam de ejus corpore saturantur"*.

Supporting: **Chrysostom** (*Hom. in Mt.* 76.3-4: the eagles *"the multitude of
the angels, of the martyrs, of all the saints"*; the sign *"the cross being
brighter than the sun"*; the angels gather the risen; the trumpets *"for
arousing, for gladness"*), **Aquinas** (*Super Mt.* c. 24: Christ comes
*ostendens signa passionis*; he reports Jerome's gathering in the memory of the
passion), **Chrysostom** on Col 1:12-13 (translated *"to the same honor with
Him"*), and **Fromage**, an ecclesiastical writer, whose eagles fly to *"the
sacred prey of thy divine Body"*.

The authors agree that the body is Christ's and the eagles the saints. They
differ on the place: the passion as read and remembered (Jerome), the place of
the passion (Hilary), heaven (Augustine); Chrysostom counts angels among the
eagles. The reading keeps all three places and does not choose.

### What each element contributes

- `introit`: *redúcam captivitátem vestram de cunctis locis*, a return from all
  places; the verse *avertísti captivitátem Iacob*, which Augustine reads of
  sin's captivity turned back by Christ. The antiphon omits Jeremiah's
  *congregabo vos de universis gentibus*; the gathering it leaves unsaid is the
  Gospel's *a quátuor ventis* (editorial juxtaposition).
- `collect`: touched lightly; the faithful whose wills are stirred are those
  being gathered.
- `epistle`: *qui erípuit nos de potestáte tenebrárum, et tránstulit in regnum
  Fílii dilectiónis suæ*; Chrysostom: delivered, *"showing our great misery"*,
  and translated so *"that it is both of us and of Him"*; *in partem sortis
  sanctórum in lúmine*, the lot given freely. Aquinas reads *potestas
  tenebrarum* with Is 49:25, *captivitas a forti tollitur*: his join of
  captivity to Col 1:13, which the editor extends to the Introit.
- `gradual`: *Liberásti nos* as the thanks of the brought-back.
- `alleluia` and `offertory`: *De profúndis*: Augustine, *this mortal life is
  our deep*; the cry of those not yet gathered.
- `gospel`: vv. 27-31, the lightning, the body and eagles, the sign, the
  gathering (the reading's centre); vv. 15-22 as the dispersal that precedes it
  (Chrysostom: the elect for whose sake the days were shortened).
- `secret`: *ómnium nostrum ad te corda convérte*, hearts turned to the one
  centre; *transeámus*.
- `communion`: lightly; asking and receiving at the place of gathering.
- `postcommunion`: *per hæc sacraménta quæ súmpsimus*: the editor sets
  Augustine's *de ejus corpore saturantur* and Fromage's Eucharistic eagles
  beside the sacrament received. No Father here names the Eucharist at 24:28;
  the link is the editor's and Fromage's.

### The difficulty and the disagreement

The difficulty is 24:28 itself (scope 7.2): a proverb of carrion read of
Christ. Jerome's inference turns on the word *cadaver*, Augustine's on the
speaker's coming death, Hilary's on the fitness of glory returning where
humility was. If accepted, the verse that sounds most like a threat becomes
the Gospel's promise of assembly. The disagreement on place stays. A second
difficulty: the Introit's Jeremiah is a historical promise to exiles in
Babylon after seventy years, with words the antiphon omits; Jerome's anagogy
is what carries it to the Church, and his comment on *finem et patientiam*
concerns words not in the chant.

### Four senses

- **Literal.** Jeremiah promises the exiles return from all places; Paul
  thanks the Father who has rescued the Colossians from darkness into the
  Son's kingdom; Jesus tells his disciples that after the tribulation the Son
  of Man will come visibly and the angels will gather the elect, as eagles
  gather where the body lies.
- **Allegorical.** The body is Christ crucified and risen; the eagles are the
  saints renewed, gathered to his passion and fed from his body (Jerome,
  Hilary, Augustine); the captivity turned back is sin forgiven (Augustine on
  Ps 84).
- **Moral.** To come to where the body is: to be gathered wherever the passion
  is read and remembered (Jerome), to imitate his passion and humility
  (Augustine), and to confess the captivity one is in, *ut dignus sis liberari*
  (Augustine).
- **Anagogical.** The last assembly of the elect from the four winds, where
  the glory returns to the place of the passion (Hilary) and the exiles reach
  *locum nostrum* (Jerome).

### Limits

Jerome's *ubicumque in Scripturis legitur* concerns Scripture and the memory
of the passion, not the Mass; Hilary's *locus passionis* is a place, not a
sacrament; Augustine's eagles are fed *tanquam* from his body. The Eucharistic
reading is the editor's and Fromage's. Chrysostom's angels among the eagles
must not be dropped. The Introit's *de cunctis locis* is Jeremiah's word to
Babylon's exiles, and Jerome's anagogy is his own.

## 2. `flight-without-turning-back` — The commands of flight as a rule of life

### Controlling claim

The Gospel's commands, *let those in Judaea flee to the mountains; let him on
the roof not come down; let him in the field not turn back for his coat; pray
that your flight be not in winter or on the sabbath*, are read by Hilary and
Jerome as words to every hearer: once one has gone up, one must not go back
down to what one left. Chrysostom reads them of the Roman war and the
believers of Jerusalem; the disagreement is kept. The formulary's orations,
asking that wills be stirred to the fruit of the divine work and that hearts,
freed from earthly desires, pass over to heavenly ones, are set beside the
spiritual reading (editorial synthesis); the Epistle's *walk worthily … in all
patience and long-suffering with joy* is Paul's version of the same steadiness.

### Carrying authors and their reasoning

- **Hilary** (*In Mt.* 25.5-7). His argument starts from an objection to the
  letter: *"praeceptum istud secundum humanam intelligentiam rationem dicti
  factique non recipit"*. A man on the roof cannot leave the house without
  coming down, and there is no reason to stand on the roof rather than stay
  inside. So the words must be weighed *ut momenta praeceptorum coelestium
  consequamur*: the roof is the house's height and perfection; the one who
  stands *in corporis sui perfectione*, *regeneratione novus, spiritu celsus*,
  must not descend *in humiliora rerum saecularium cupiditate*. The field
  worker must not go back for the tunic of former sins. The woe to the
  pregnant cannot be a curse on a condition of nature, *"absit … ut quidquam
  sit, quod homini nisi sua culpa malum fiat"*; it is souls heavy with sin, and
  the nursing are souls still on milk who *nec peccata effugerint, nec cibum
  veri panis acceperint*. Winter and sabbath: *"ne aut in peccatorum frigore,
  aut in otio bonorum operum reperiamur"*.
- **Jerome** (*In Mt.* IV, cols. 177-178). He sets out the literal options
  (Antichrist; Caesar's image in the temple; Hadrian's statue; the siege) and
  then *"et omne dogma perversum"*: when we see it standing in the holy place,
  that is, the Church, *"debemus fugere de Judaea ad montes, hoc est, dimissa
  occidente littera … appropinquare montibus aeternis"*, stay on the roof where
  the devil's fiery darts do not reach, not go down *"de domo conversationis
  pristinae"*. On winter and sabbath, if of the end: *"ut non refrigescat fides
  nostra et in Christum charitas, neque ut otiosi in opere Dei torpeamus
  virtutum sabbato"*. The days are shortened *"ne temporum mora, fides
  concutiatur credentium"*.

Supporting: **Augustine** (*Quaest. Ev.* I.37: *"ne in tristitia aut laetitia
rerum temporalium quis inveniatur a die illa"*); **Bede** on the Marcan parallel
(the roof is the one who *excedens animo carnalia* lives spiritually; ascend
higher as more follow the wandering ways of vice); **Gregory** (*Hom.* 1.5:
*"Nolite … diligere quem videtis diu stare non posse"*); and, for the Epistle,
**Chrysostom** (*Hom. in Col.* 2: *strengthened* concerns trials; patience
towards outsiders, longsuffering towards one another; *"with faith he always
couples conduct"*) and **Aquinas** (*Super Col.* c. 1 l. 3: patience against
adversity, *longanimitas* against the delay of the reward, *cum gaudio*
against sadness).

**Chrysostom** (*Hom. in Mt.* 76.1-2) and **Aquinas** (*Super Mt.* c. 24) hold the
literal sense: the discourse is *"addressed to the Jews"*, the apostles would
not be there, the elect are the believers in the besieged city, Josephus
confirms the tribulation. This is the real disagreement (scope 8.1). The
reading does not deny Chrysostom: it takes Hilary's and Jerome's step from the
letter to the hearer, and it keeps the historical sense as the first one.

### What each element contributes

- `gospel`: vv. 15-26: the abomination, the flight, the shortened days, the
  false christs in the desert and the inner rooms (Jerome: the desert of the
  philosophers and the chambers of heretics; Augustine: schisms in a corner and
  hidden conventicles).
- `collect`: *Excita … voluntátes … propénsius exsequéntes*: wills stirred so as
  not to be found idle (editorial juxtaposition with Jerome's *otiosi in opere
  Dei*).
- `epistle`: *ut ambulétis digne Deo … in omni ópere bono fructificántes … in
  omni patiéntia, et longanimitáte cum gáudio*: Paul's prayer for steadiness
  under trial (Chrysostom, Aquinas).
- `gradual`: *Liberásti nos ex affligéntibus nos*: Aquinas, deliverance *vel in
  praesenti*, since the afflicters cannot harm spiritually.
- `alleluia` and `offertory`: *De profúndis*: Hilary, the cry *de profundo
  humanarum calamitatum*, from the heart's secret.
- `secret`: *a terrénis cupiditátibus liberáti, ad cæléstia desidéria
  transeámus*: the passing over the reading asks for; set beside Hilary's roof
  (editorial).
- `communion`: lightly; Aquinas on Mt 24:20: *in talibus ad Deum solum est
  recurrendum*.
- `postcommunion`: *quidquid in nostra mente vitiósum est … curétur*: beside
  Hilary's souls heavy with sin (editorial).
- `introit`: lightly; the exiles who must not settle in Babylon. Jerome on Jer
  29 warns against prophets who promise a quick return; the editor does not
  extend this.

### The difficulty and the disagreement

Scope 7.1. Hilary's answer turns on a contradiction in the letter and so
moves to the soul; Chrysostom refuses to leave the letter. If Hilary's and
Jerome's answer is accepted, the Gospel addresses this congregation directly
and its prayer is the Secret's; if Chrysostom's alone, it addresses them
through a fulfilled history. The study must present both and say which it
follows.

### Four senses

- **Literal.** Jesus warns that when the sign Daniel spoke of stands in the
  holy place, those in Judaea must flee at once, without going back for
  anything, and pray that the flight not fall in winter or on the sabbath
  (Chrysostom and Aquinas: the Roman war).
- **Allegorical.** The abomination is every perverse doctrine standing in the
  Church; the mountains are the eternal mountains; Judaea the killing letter
  (Jerome); the roof is the perfection of the regenerated (Hilary).
- **Moral.** Not to go down from the roof into worldly desire nor go back for
  the tunic of old sins (Hilary); not to let faith and love grow cold nor rest
  idle (Jerome); not to be found absorbed in temporal joy or sorrow
  (Augustine).
- **Anagogical.** The flight ends on the eternal mountains; the days are
  shortened for the elect, and those who did not turn back are found ready on
  the day.

### Limits

Hilary refers the abomination to Antichrist and Jerome to several referents;
neither speaks of the Collect or the Secret. Chrysostom's literal answer is not
a minority to be dismissed: he is the fullest commentator on this Gospel read
here. The reading must not present the spiritual sense as the only one.

## 3. `words-that-do-not-pass` — What God has said is as good as done

### Controlling claim

The Gospel ends with two sayings: *this generation shall not pass till all
these things be done* and *heaven and earth shall pass, but my words shall not
pass*. The Fathers read neither as a date: Chrysostom makes *this generation*
the faithful whom nothing overcomes and grounds certainty in the speaker who
made heaven and earth; Gregory contrasts the durable heavens with the passing
word whose sentences do not change; Hilary, the created from nothing with the
words from eternity. Augustine, on the psalms this Mass sings, explains why the
prophets speak of the future in the past: *quia tam certum est quasi factum
sit*. The Mass's past tenses (*avertísti*, *Liberásti*, *erípuit … tránstulit*)
and the Communion's *crédite quia accipiétis* are set beside these readings
(editorial synthesis): faith treats what God has said as already given, and
prays accordingly.

### Carrying authors and their reasoning

- **John Chrysostom** (*Hom. in Mt.* 77.1). The fig tree answers *when*: the
  interval is not great; but more, it confirms that the thing will surely
  happen, *"For as this of the fig tree is of necessity, so that too"*, as the
  grain of wheat proves the resurrection. *This generation* cannot be the
  generation then living, so it is *"that of the believers"*, which *"nothing
  shall prevail"* over. Heaven and earth are named because *"it were more easy
  for these firm, fixed, and immoveable bodies to be blotted out, than for ought
  of my words to fall to the ground"*, and to show him *"maker of all"*: so
  *"let him test His sayings … from what is past, let him believe also the
  things to come"*.
- **Gregory the Great** (*Hom. in Ev.* I, on the Lukan parallel). *"Sequentium
  rerum certitudo est praeteritarum exhibitio"*: what has been foretold and
  fulfilled guarantees the rest. On the fig: as fruit shows summer near, *"ex
  ruina mundi prope esse agnoscitur regnum Dei"*; the world *"crescit, ut
  cadat"*; the kingdom is summer, *"quia tunc moeroris nostri nubila
  transeunt"*. On heaven and earth: nothing more durable in nature, nothing
  quicker to pass than a word, and yet *"sine mutabilitate manentes sententias
  exprimit meus sermo qui transit"*. Hence *levate capita*: *"exhilarate
  corda"*, for those who love God rejoice at the world's end.
- **Augustine** (*En. in Ps.* 43.8-9; 84.3). On the Gradual's verse: *"tanquam
  praeteritum dicitur, quia tam certum est quasi factum sit"*; *"Da prophetam
  cui tam certum sit futurum quam tibi praeteritum"*; and the psalm *"miscet
  etiam verba futuri temporis"*. On the Introit's verse: *"apud Deum et quod
  futurum est, jam factum est"*.

Supporting: **Hilary** (*In Mt.* 26.3: heaven and earth *de nihilo* carry the
necessity of not being, his words *ex aeternitate* the power to remain; 26.4:
the day hidden to keep us watchful); **Jerome** (col. 180: the signs are
*prooemia et praecursores*, not the end; heaven and earth pass *immutatione, non
abolitione sui*; *In Hier.* V: future things known to God alone, and prayer
commanded *ut quod promissum est, mereantur accipere*); and, for the Communion,
**Bede** (*distinctio deprecantium*), **Hilary** on Ps 129 (*naturae suae est ut
audiat; sed fidei nostrae est ut precemur audiri*) and **Aquinas** on Col 1:9
(prayer *non ut eum flectamus, sed ut nos erigamus in eum*).

The carrying authors agree that certainty rests on God's word and not on a
date. They differ in where the evidence lies: what has already happened
(Chrysostom, Gregory) or what God has already decided (Augustine). Hilary and
Jerome differ on heaven and earth (necessity of not being, against change
without abolition); Chrysostom and Jerome on *generation* (the faithful, against
the human race or the Jews). These stay (scope 8.3).

### What each element contributes

- `introit`: *Ego cógito cogitatiónes pacis*: God's thoughts, which Jerome says
  God alone knows; *invocábitis me, et ego exáudiam vos*: prayer commanded though
  God could act without it (Jerome); the verse's past tense *avertísti*
  (Augustine).
- `collect`: *pietátis tuæ remédia maióra percípiant*: a petition for what is
  promised but not yet received.
- `epistle`: *grátias agéntes … qui dignos nos fecit … qui erípuit … et
  tránstulit*: thanks for what is already given, which Chrysostom says Paul
  names *"for these are the causes of the other"*; *in omni patiéntia, et
  longanimitáte*: Aquinas's *longanimitas* against the delay of the reward (Hab
  2:3).
- `gradual`: *Liberásti … confudísti* in the past and *laudábimur …
  confitébimur* in the future: Augustine's text for the mixing of tenses.
- `alleluia` and `offertory`: *De profúndis … exáudi*: Hilary: God hears by
  nature; faith asks to be heard.
- `gospel`: vv. 32-35, the fig, the generation, heaven and earth (centre); vv.
  23-26, the deceivers who say *here* and *there*, against whom the lightning
  is unmistakable.
- `secret`: *ad cæléstia desidéria transeámus*: desire turned to what remains.
- `communion`: *crédite quia accipiétis, et fiet vobis*: belief that treats the
  promised as received (editorial join of Augustine's past-for-future to the
  antiphon); the conditions in Bede, Hilary, Jerome, Aquinas (scope 7.5).
- `postcommunion`: lightly; the remedy received now.

### The difficulty and the disagreement

Scope 7.3 and 7.5. *This generation* read of the living would be a failed
prediction; none of the witnesses reads it so, and their alternatives differ.
The Communion read literally would promise any object; every witness puts the
condition in the one who prays. If accepted, the Gospel's sternest verses
become grounds of confidence, and the Communion a lesson in how to pray, not a
guarantee of objects.

### Four senses

- **Literal.** Jesus says the signs will come as surely as summer follows the
  fig's leaves, that this generation will not pass before all is done, and that
  his words outlast heaven and earth; the psalms speak of deliverance done; the
  Lord promises that what is asked in faith will be given.
- **Allegorical.** The generation that does not pass is the faithful, the Church
  more honoured than heaven and earth (Chrysostom); the Word who made the
  world speaks the sentence that stands (Gregory, Hilary); the prophet's past
  tense is Christ's work certain with God (Augustine).
- **Moral.** To believe the future from the fulfilled past (Chrysostom,
  Gregory); not to love what cannot stand (Gregory); to pray as one who has
  received, forgiving as one asks forgiveness (Bede).
- **Anagogical.** The summer after the tempest for the just (Chrysostom), when
  the clouds of grief pass (Gregory), and what was said is done.

### Limits

Gregory comments on Luke, not Matthew; cite him at his locus as the parallel.
Augustine's sentences are on Ps 43 and 84, not on the Gospel or the Communion.
The join of the chants' tenses to the Communion's *accipiétis* is the editor's.
Chrysostom's *generation of believers* and Jerome's *human race or Jews* are
different answers and must both be given if the verse is discussed.

## Comparison of the three readings

- **Shared.** All three read the Gospel with the same Fathers and take its end as
  Christ's visible coming; all three keep Chrysostom's literal sense of vv.
  15-22 beside any spiritual one; all three treat the chants' captivity and
  deliverance as more than Babylon.
- **Different questions.** Reading 1 asks where the end leads (to the body);
  reading 2, how to live before it (without turning back); reading 3, why to
  trust it (the word that does not pass).
- **Different governing verses.** 24:28-31 (reading 1); 24:16-20 (reading 2);
  24:32-35 (reading 3).
- **Different carrying authors.** Jerome, Hilary and Augustine (1); Hilary and
  Jerome (2); Chrysostom, Gregory and Augustine (3). Jerome and Hilary are used
  in two readings on different loci; Augustine on different texts.
- **Different use of the same elements.** The Introit is a return (1), an
  exile not to be mistaken for home (2, lightly), a promise known to God alone
  (3). The Secret's *transeámus* is passage to the centre (1), departure without
  return (2), desire for what remains (3). The Gradual's *Liberásti* is the thanks
  of the gathered (1), present deliverance under affliction (2), the past tense
  of certainty (3).
- **Tensions.** Reading 2 rests on Hilary's rejection of the letter, which
  Chrysostom, carrying reading 3, would not grant; the study should say so
  where both appear. Hilary's fig-branch as Antichrist (scope 8.4) runs against
  reading 3's fig as pledge; reading 3 uses Chrysostom's and Gregory's fig and
  should not cite Hilary for it.
