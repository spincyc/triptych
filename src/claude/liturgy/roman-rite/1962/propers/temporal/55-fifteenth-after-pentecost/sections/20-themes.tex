\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\label{triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

The three prayers of this Mass deny the Church and the communicant any standing of
their own, twice over: she cannot stand safe without God, and what must go before
in us is not our own sense but the working of a heavenly gift. The Secret between
them asks only that the sacraments guard and defend, so that not one of the three
credits the assembly with anything of its own. Everything the day
sings and reads is the motion that answers that denial --- mercy moving first in
four grammars, a harvest whose seed was given, a dead man handed back alive at
a town gate, and flesh given at the end of it for the life of the world.

\subsection*{1. Mercy moves first, and the formulary says so four times in four grammars}

The word is a petition at the Introit, \latin{miserére mihi, Dómine, quóniam ad
te clamávi tota die}; a divine attribute at the Collect, \latin{miserátio
continuáta mundet et múniat}; the thing to be proclaimed at the Gradual,
\latin{ad annuntiándum mane misericórdiam tuam}; and at the Gospel the motion in
Christ out of which the miracle comes, \latin{misericórdia motus super eam}.

\textbf{At the Gospel the initiative is Christ's, and Cyril says so.} Cyril of
Alexandria sets Naim against Capharnaum: there Christ was present by invitation,
here nobody summons him and he comes to raise the dead man on his own motion,
\ifdefined\TriptychSynthesisEdition
as the fifth unit of the commentary sets out in Cyril's own words%
\else
as the Gospel subsection sets out in Cyril's own words%
\fi{} (Payne Smith, vol.~1,
pp.~132--133). The widow of the pericope asks
nothing, says nothing, and is credited with no faith; everything that happens at
the gate begins on the other side. What the scene draws from those who watch it is
a verdict about God and not about the young man, \latin{Deus visitávit plebem
suam}, and the appointed text leaves that sentence with them and not with the
Evangelist.

\textbf{The first and last orations of the Mass say the same thing negatively,
and their grammar says it too.} The Collect's semicolon puts the Church's
incapacity, \latin{quia sine te non potest salva consístere}, ahead of the
petition it grounds; the Postcommunion makes the same denial of the communicant
in the first person plural, \latin{ut non noster sensus in nobis, sed iúgiter
eius prævéniat efféctus}, and puts its own subject last of all, both grammars
being set out
\ifdefined\TriptychSynthesisEdition
at the third unit of the commentary.%
\else
at the Collect and Postcommunion subsections.%
\fi{}
Augustine, on the Introit's own psalm, makes the incapacity the ground on which
the petition is heard --- the ear bends to the neck that is not stiffened --- in
the sentence
\ifdefined\TriptychSynthesisEdition
the same unit prints%
\else
the Introit subsection prints%
\fi{} (\work{En.\ Ps.}~85.2).
\latin{Misericordia} and \latin{miseratio} are different nouns, so what runs
through the four elements is one sense and not one word, and \latin{praeveniat}
is a plain verb of precedence. The Gradual pairs the mercy with truth,
\latin{misericórdiam tuam et veritátem tuam}, the fixed collocation whose
psalms
\ifdefined\TriptychSynthesisEdition
the same unit counts through the psalter.%
\else
the Gradual subsection counts through the psalter.%
\fi{} Four grammars, and in
none of them does the motion begin on the assembly's side.

\subsection*{2. The lesson runs an economy, and its currency is itself given}

The Epistle is unembarrassedly commercial --- \latin{Opus autem suum probet
unusquísque}, \latin{Quæ enim semináverit homo, hæc et metet}, \latin{témpore enim
suo metémus} --- while the orations allow the Church nothing of her own. The
tradition's resolution is not that the economy is figurative but that its currency
is a gift.

Augustine, expounding the Gradual's own psalm, draws the consequence for any
progress at all: \latin{quia munera ipsius sunt, non merita tua} (\work{En.\
Ps.}~91.3); the Collect asks that the Church be governed \latin{tuo semper
múnere}. \textbf{Aquinas finds the same doctrine in the lesson's own grammar},
reading the asymmetry of the possessives at Gal.\ 6:8: \latin{cum agit de
seminatione carnis, dicit in carne sua \dots\ sed cum loquitur de semine spiritus,
non dicit suo, quia spiritus non est nobis a nobis, sed a Deo} (\work{Super
Galatas} cap.~6 lect.~2) --- and the asymmetry is in the appointed Latin,
\latin{qui séminat in carne sua} against \latin{qui autem séminat in spíritu}, a
possessive at the flesh and none at the spirit. At 6:5 he makes even the sheaves
measured grace, the burden each man carries to judgement being \latin{mensuram
gratiae suae \dots\ tamquam manipulos bonorum operum}. \latin{Munere} in
the Collect and \latin{munera} in Augustine do similar work, so the lesson's
commerce and the orations' denial are one doctrine and not two: what the day
tells the assembly it will reap was first given it to sow.

\subsection*{3. Flesh sown, flesh carried out, flesh given}

\latin{Caro} turns twice inside one Mass. The Epistle makes flesh the ground that
yields corruption, \latin{de carne et metet corruptiónem}; the Gospel carries a
corpse out through a gate; the Communion gives flesh as the world's life,
\latin{caro mea est pro sǽculi vita}. The lesson carries its burdens with the
bier-bearers' own verb, \latin{ónera portáte} at 6:2 and \latin{onus suum portábit} at
6:5 standing in the same Mass as \latin{hi autem, qui portábant} at the gate of
Naim. And the lesson's other half reaps out of the spirit what the flesh cannot
yield --- \latin{qui autem séminat in spíritu, de spíritu metet vitam ætérnam} ---
so the turn the noun makes across the three elements is already answered inside
the element that begins it.

\textbf{One Father turns the noun in both places, and he does it from the Gospel
side.} Asked why Christ touched the bier when a word would have sufficed, Cyril
answers that the touch is itself the teaching: what the flesh of the Word
touches it makes alive, because that flesh is the Word's own, and
\ifdefined\TriptychSynthesisEdition
the fifth unit of the commentary prints his sentence%
\else
the Gospel subsection prints his sentence%
\fi{} (Payne Smith, vol.~1, p.~135). Aquinas
draws the same inference at the Communion's verse, naming John Damascene for it
and closing on Christ's healing of the sick by his own touch (\work{Super
Ioannem} cap.~6 lect.~6), so the bier and
the bread stand side by side on a checked witness's own sentence; Augustine
supplies the ecclesial half at the same verse, where none but the body of Christ
lives by Christ's Spirit (\work{Tract.\ in Io.}~26.13), so that the life the
antiphon offers is had by being made a member of the one whose flesh it sings. Cyril's Luke commentary
reaches this guide through
an 1859 English of an ancient Syriac version, and Augustine's and Aquinas's
lemmata at that verse read \latin{dabo} and \latin{pro mundi vita}, so their
expositions belong to the verse and not to the antiphon as it is sung.

\subsection*{4. One voice through every change of person}

The formulary changes person constantly: first singular at the Introit and the
Offertory, first and second plural in the Epistle and its second singular at
\latin{consíderans teípsum} in Gal.\ 6:1, no speaking person in the
Gradual or the Alleluia, Christ's own singular at the Communion, first plural in
the Secret and the Postcommunion. \textbf{Augustine
expounds both of this Mass's psalm-antiphons on a single principle.} At the
Introit's psalm the one Christ is head and members together, and
\ifdefined\TriptychSynthesisEdition
the third unit of the commentary prints his sentence%
\else
the Introit subsection prints his sentence%
\fi{} (\work{En.\ Ps.}~85.1); at the Offertory's psalm
he settles the question of its speaker on the same principle, and
\ifdefined\TriptychSynthesisEdition
that unit carries his answer%
\else
the Offertory subsection carries his answer%
\fi{} (\work{En.\ Ps.}~39.5). Cassiodorus expounds both
psalms on the same principle in his own words, so two Latin commentators state
the reading: at Ps.~85 the speaker is Christ in the form of a servant and at
Ps.~39 the Church he speaks in,
\ifdefined\TriptychSynthesisEdition
that unit printing his Latin at both%
\else
each subsection printing his Latin%
\fi{} (PL~70). Head
at the one antiphon and body at the other, and the Mass sings both of them in
the first person singular.

\textbf{The orations move with it.} Their objects narrow from \latin{Ecclésiam
tuam} to \latin{nos} to \latin{mentes nostras et córpora}, while their verbs go
from cleansing and fortifying to guarding and defending to possessing and going
before; so the singular of the chants and the plural of the prayers are not two
speakers alternating but one body addressed at closer range as the rite proceeds.
Objects narrowing across a Mass is a common euchological shape, and this formulary
is one instance of it rather than its inventor. And at five places in this
Mass's two psalm-antiphons Augustine's lemma is not the chant's, three at Ps.~85
and two at Ps.~39, so there what he expounds is the psalm and not the sung text;
Cassiodorus's lemma departs from them at \latin{miserere mei} alone.

\subsection*{5. The day's subject is an interval to be endured}

The vocabulary of duration is unusually dense --- \latin{tota die} (Int.),
\latin{mane \dots\ per noctem} (Grad.), \latin{exspéctans exspectávi} (Off.),
\latin{témpore enim suo metémus} and \latin{dum tempus habémus} (Ep.), and the
stock euchological \latin{continuáta}, \latin{semper} and \latin{iúgiter} in the
three orations, which carry little on their own. \textbf{Every load-bearing one of
the scriptural markers has a reading behind it that stretches it to a lifetime.}
Augustine reads \latin{tota die} as the whole age in which the body of Christ
groans under pressure, running to the end of the world, and Cassiodorus as the
span of one life, both
\ifdefined\TriptychSynthesisEdition
at the third unit of the commentary%
\else
at the Introit subsection%
\fi{} (\work{En.\ Ps.}~85.5; PL~70).
At the Gradual's versicle Augustine divides the day between the time when it
goes well with us and the time of tribulation, giving the psalm's mercy to the
one and its truth to the other (\work{En.\ Ps.}~91.4); Cassiodorus on the same
versicle takes the
two words for the whole of a life, \latin{Mirabiliter vita nostra duobus est
sermonibus conclusa}. Ambrose on the Offertory's doubled verb makes perfection
not the waiting but the having waited (\work{In Ps.\ XXXIX}~§1), on the strength
of Matt.\ 10:22, the verse Jerome and Augustine likewise reach for where the
lesson says \latin{non deficiámus}; and Aquinas glosses \latin{dum tempus habémus} as
this life, which is the time of sowing. Chrysostom, on
the same verse, makes the interval the measure of mercy itself: ``As it is not
always in our power to sow, so neither is it to show mercy.''

\textbf{The one element that could have said \latin{today} is the one whose today
is left unsung.} The Alleluia sings Ps.~94 from inside the summons to worship and
leaves unsung the oracle of warning in which the psalm's own \latin{hodie}
stands,
\ifdefined\TriptychSynthesisEdition
as the second unit of the commentary sets out.%
\else
as the Alleluia subsection of the commentary sets out.%
\fi{}
What this Mass sings is the waiting; the \latin{hodie} that would cut the waiting
short is the half the chant does not reach.
