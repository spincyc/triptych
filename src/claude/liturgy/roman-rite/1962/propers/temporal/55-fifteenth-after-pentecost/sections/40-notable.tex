\section*{The Propers: Notable and Quotable}

Five afterlives of wording from the scriptural propers, each a use that moves the
phrase into a register its writer did not occupy. Two elements supply all five:
Gal.\ 6:2, 6:5, 6:7 and 6:9 have each become
free-standing English proverbs, and the Introit, the Gradual, the Alleluia and the
Communion have not.

\begin{afterlife}{Epistle, Gal. 6:7 --- the verse as the operative sentence of a money judgment}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} Epistle, marginal no.~1584, printed p.~396:
\latin{Nolíte erráre: Deus non irridétur. Quæ enim semináverit homo, hæc et
metet.}

\textbf{Later text and locus.} \work{Benton v. Bakker (In re Heritage Village
Church \& Missionary Fellowship, Inc.)}, 92 B.R.\ 1000, \textbf{1022} (Bankr.\
D.S.C., 9 November 1988), Reynolds, Bankruptcy Judge. The final paragraph of the
opinion, under the heading CONCLUSION: ``In conforming with the religious overture
of this case, this Court observes that James Bakker either overlooked or ignored
parts of the Bible including I Timothy 6:10 \dots\ Now, however, we must apply
Galatians 6:7 --- `Be not deceived; God is not mocked: for whatsoever a man
soweth, that shall he also reap'. Let judgments be entered pursuant to this
Memorandum Opinion.''

\textbf{The turn.} In the lesson the verse is a warning inside a passage about
supporting one's catechist and doing good to the household of the faith. Here a
federal bankruptcy court, having found that the president of a religious
broadcasting corporation took excessive compensation from it, uses the verse as
the pronouncement of civil liability: it does not illustrate the judgment, it
delivers it, and the judge frames the borrowing as ``conforming with the religious
overture of this case.'' The sentence a congregation hears read as exhortation
becomes the state's sanction against a preacher. The court quotes the Authorised
Version exactly and names chapter and verse, so the dependence is explicit, and
Bakker's 1989 federal fraud conviction is a different case.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Epistle, Gal. 6:7--8 --- the title and the mechanism of a 1917 War Office film}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} Epistle, marginal no.~1584, printed p.~396:
\latin{Quæ enim semináverit homo, hæc et metet. Quóniam qui séminat in carne sua,
de carne et metet corruptiónem.}

\textbf{Later work and locus.} \work{Whatsoever A Man Soweth} (1917), silent film,
35mm, black and white, 2256 feet; director, producer and writer Joseph Best;
sponsor, the War Office. Catalogued by the British Film Institute at Screenonline
film id 1358391, with a curatorial analysis signed Bryony Dixon; corroborated in
the Wellcome Collection catalogue, work id \texttt{n4b7rac9}, which lists it as
the 1917 title in a British Film Institute collection of British sex-education
films.

\textbf{The turn.} The title is the King James wording of Gal.\ 6:7 verbatim,
truncated where the proverb is usually truncated, and the BFI's own curator states
that the title is biblical and the film built as a sermon: ``As the biblical title
suggests, it is essentially a straight sermon, a form that its target audience
would have found familiar both from church at home and during their military
service.'' \textbf{The film enacts the next appointed verse.} The synopsis
records a Canadian soldier accosted by a prostitute outside the National Gallery, taken round hospital wards ``occupied
by victims of venereal disease at every stage, including rotting legs and hands,''
a school for the blind where ``more than half the children there contracted
blindness as a result of hereditary venereal disease,'' and the soldier's brother
infecting his wife and finding their child born blind. Gal.\ 6:8's \latin{de carne
et metet corruptiónem} is staged as clinical corruption of the flesh, and the
BFI's own clip title for the temptation sequence is ``Sowing wild oats.'' Paul's
warning about self-deception in the moral life becomes a state hygiene instrument
for keeping soldiers fit for duty, with the reaping specified as syphilis,
congenital blindness and a ruined family. The sponsorship is the BFI record's own.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Offertory, Ps. 39:2 --- the incipit as the title of a soldier's poem, and the choir taking it back}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} Offertory, \latin{Ant.\ ad Offertorium},
Ps.~39, 2, 3 \latin{et} 4, marginal no.~1588, printed p.~397: \latin{Exspéctans
exspectávi Dóminum \dots} The uncorrected optical layers of the Pustet Ratisbon
1862, the Venice 1570 and the 1604 \latin{typica} show \latin{Expectans
expectavi}, the 1570 with that book's \emph{u} for \emph{v}.

\textbf{Later texts and loci.} Charles Hamilton Sorley, ``Expectans expectavi,''
poem XXV in \work{Marlborough and Other Poems} (Cambridge: Cambridge University
Press, 1916), pp.~65--66, dated at its foot ``May 1915.'' The poem opens ``From
morn to midnight, all day through, / I laugh and play as others do, / I sin and
chatter, just the same / As others with a different name'' and ends ``With parted
lips and outstretched hands / And listening ears Thy servant stands, / Call Thou
early, call Thou late, / To Thy great service dedicate.'' Sorley was killed at
Loos on 13 October 1915, aged twenty, five months after the date under the poem,
and the volume was published posthumously. Then Charles Wood, ``Expectans
Expectavi,'' alternative title ``This sanctuary of my soul,'' for mixed chorus and
organ, first published Boston: C.~C.\ Birchard, plate no.~1147, 1919, with Sorley
named as librettist.

\textbf{The turn, twice.} Ps.\ 39:2 says the speaker waited and was answered;
Sorley's speaker is the one who does not wait --- ``From morn to midnight \dots\ I
sin and chatter'' --- and the waiting is transferred to an inner shrine kept
``Unlatched and lit'' on the chance that God might ``care / To enter or to tarry
there,'' with ``Call Thou early, call Thou late'' handing the initiative back. The
confident Latin \latin{exspectavi} stands over a poem about not having been paying
attention. The second turn is on top of the first: Wood's 1919 anthem sets only
the last two stanzas and is catalogued under their first line, so a poem whose
point is a distracted young man is sung in choir as a straightforward act of
devotion under a psalm title, with the confession cut. \textbf{Nothing in the
poem or in its printing ties Sorley's Latin to this Sunday's Offertory rather
than to the Psalter or the Vulgate at large}; the words are the same words, in
the older spelling those three layers show, and they are the poem's only Latin.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Offertory, Ps. 39:2--4 --- the psalm closing a rock album, with the ending changed to a complaint}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} Offertory, marginal no.~1588, printed p.~397.
Douay--Rheims at Ps.\ 39:2--4: ``With expectation I have waited for the Lord \dots\
and he put a new canticle into my mouth, a song to our God.''

\textbf{Later work and loci.} U2, \work{War}, Island Records ILPS 9733, released
28 February 1983 in the United Kingdom; track 10 of 10 is ``40,'' 2:35. The
album's opening track is ``Sunday Bloody Sunday.'' Bono describes the adaptation
in his own introduction to \work{Selections from the Book of Psalms} (Pocket
Canons, 1999): ``lost for words and forty minutes of recording time left
before the end of our studio time, we were still looking for a song to close our
third album, War. We wanted to put something explicitly spiritual on the record to
balance the politics and the romance of it \dots\ We thought about the psalms
\dots\ `Psalm 40' \dots\ `40' became the closing song at U2 shows and on hundreds
of occasions, literally hundreds of thousands of people \dots\ have shouted back
the refrain, pinched from `Psalm 6': ``How long'' (to sing this song)''.''

\textbf{The turn.} Ps.\ 39(40) is a thanksgiving: the waiting is over, the cry has
been heard, the new song is already in the mouth. The song keeps the thanksgiving
and appends a question from a different psalm --- Ps.\ 6:4, \latin{usquequo},
``how long,'' a lament --- so it ends not in the new song but in the question, and
the crowd rather than the band is left singing it. On a record whose first track
is ``Sunday Bloody Sunday'' the unanswered question lands on the Troubles.
\textbf{The overlap with the appointed wording is the waiting-and-being-heard
clause and the new-song clause and not the whole song}: the song follows the
Authorised Version of Ps.\ 40:1--3, while the antiphon is the Roman chant's Latin,
which departs from the Vulgate at three places here and cuts the whole of v.~3 ---
the pit, the rock and the established goings --- which the song keeps. The
statement about the refrain's origin and the crowd's response is the adapter's own
testimony.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Epistle, Gal. 6:2 and 6:5 --- the lesson's own internal tension argued out in wartime Parliament}
\textbf{Appointed text and loci.} Epistle, marginal no.~1584, printed p.~396,
containing both \latin{Alter altérius ónera portáte, et sic adimplébitis legem
Christi} (6:2) and \latin{Unusquísque enim onus suum portábit} (6:5).

\textbf{Later text and locus.} United Kingdom, House of Commons, Official Report,
debate ``National Scheme of Family Allowances,'' HC Deb 23 June 1942, vol.~380,
cc1853--944, at \textbf{c1895}. Mr Maurice Hely-Hutchinson, Member for Hastings,
speaking against the Motion: ``It is far simpler to base one's whole attitude
towards this as indeed towards all forms of social service on the words in the
second verse of the 6th Chapter of St.\ Paul's Epistle to the Galatians--- Bear ye
one another's burdens than it is to struggle on to the fifth verse of the same
chapter and there find the striking contradiction--- For every man shall bear his
own burden.'' The interruption, at the same column: ``Mr.\ George Griffiths
(Hemsworth): That is not a contradiction but a confirmation.'' And the reply: ``It
was the late Sir Arnold Wilson who called my attention to that apparent
contradiction \dots\ The two verses reflect the use of the same English word
`burden' to represent two entirely different words in the original Greek \dots\ So
the instruction appears to be that a man ought to carry his pack, and the rest of
us ought to help him if it turns out to be so overwhelming a burden as to be a
calamity.''

\textbf{The turn.} The appointed lesson is treated as a self-contradicting statute
to be construed. Paul sets the two sentences three verses apart in one paragraph
about correcting a brother and testing one's own work; in the Commons they become
the two rival principles of the welfare state, in the debate whose subject-matter
became the Family Allowances Act 1945. The reply is a lexical argument on the floor of the House, used to limit
the state's duty to what is ``so overwhelming a burden as to be a calamity''; the
retort from the Labour benches is the counter-reading. \textbf{The Greek argument
in that speech is Hely-Hutchinson's, and he attributes it to the late Sir Arnold
Wilson}: his renderings ``calamities'' and ``pack'' are his own and are not
established. Paul does write two different Greek nouns at 6:2 and 6:5,
\emph{baros} and \emph{phortion}, which the Greek New Testament carries whatever
was said in the House. The two phrases stand in twelve and in two spoken
contributions across the whole Hansard corpus, several of them in the same class
of debate, so this exchange is one attestation of a standing parliamentary
habit.
\end{afterlife}
