\section*{The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities}

\begin{studyframe}
Everything below is exploratory editorial and AI proposal. None of it is sourced
historical intent, attributed teaching or documented reception, and none of it
should be quoted as any of those. Each proposal joins at least two precisely named
appointed elements and ends on the strongest thing that stands against it.
\textbf{No connection here is claimed to be unknown, unprecedented, first or
authored by the model}; where a proposal and a checked witness conflict, the
witness wins. The precedent search behind each, its corpus and its bounds, is
recorded in \texttt{research/scope.md} and summarised in the terminal appendix.
\end{studyframe}

\begin{proposal}{P1. Incapacity stated twice in one Mass, once of the Church and once of the person}
\pfield{Anchors}{Collect \cue{Coll.}, \latin{et quia sine te non potest salva
consístere}; Epistle \cue{Ep.}, Gal.\ 6:1 \latin{consíderans teípsum, ne et tu
tentéris} and Gal.\ 6:3 \latin{Nam si quis exístimat se áliquid esse, cum nihil
sit, ipse se sedúcit}}
\pfield{Mechanism}{The same denial made at two scales inside one formulary. The
Church cannot stand safe of herself; the man who takes his own measure deceives
himself. The Collect's own grammar states the incapacity before it asks anything:
the semicolon after \latin{consístere} divides the causal clause from the single
petition it grounds, so the prayer establishes that the Church has no standing
before it asks that she be governed. The lesson does the same thing to the
individual corrector, and Jerome, Aquinas and Augustine read it that way ---
Jerome noting that Paul wrote \latin{ne et tu tenteris} and not \latin{ne tu cadas},
because \latin{tentari, in potestate tentantis est}; Aquinas that nothing so
breaks a man's severity in correcting as the fear of his own fall; and Augustine
requiring the corrector to answer God first that he acts from love.}
\pfield{Fruit}{A single doctrine of dependence preached at both scales in one
hour: the ecclesiology of the Collect and the moral counsel of the lesson turn out
to be the same sentence said twice, which is why the Mass can command correction
of a brother without licensing severity.}
\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses}{Read alone, the Collect is a
conventional prayer for the Church and Gal.\ 6:1--3 is a rule of pastoral
practice. Read together, the rule of practice is the Collect's ecclesiology
applied to one man, and the shared move is from an admission of nothing to an
instruction about what to do next.}
\pfield{Strongest limit}{\latin{Salva consístere} and \latin{exístimat se áliquid
esse} share no Latin root, so this is a doctrinal join throughout and not a
word-play, and the shift from the ecclesial scale to the personal one is the
proposal rather than an observation in the text. \textbf{Whether any checked
expositor reads Gal.\ 6:3 ecclesially was not reached}, so the second half of the
join has no reception behind it.}
\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{P2. The great King over all the earth, and the town he visits}
\pfield{Anchors}{Alleluia \cue{All.}, \latin{et Rex magnus super omnem terram},
Ps.\ 94:3 as the chant sings it; Gospel \cue{Gosp.}, Lk.\ 7:16 \latin{Quia
prophéta magnus surréxit in nobis: et quia Deus visitávit plebem suam}}
\pfield{Mechanism}{The chant's substituted predicate is a claim of universal
scope, and the Gospel that follows it directly puts God at one town's gate. Two
\latin{magnus} predications stand across those two elements, one of God as king
over the whole earth and one of a prophet risen among us, and the crowd's second
clause names the visit as God's own. The substitution is not an invention of the
chant's: \latin{rex magnus super omnem terram} is the Latin Bible's own phrase at
Ps.\ 46:3, the one place in the tracked Clementine where \latin{rex magnus} and
\latin{super omnem terram} collocate.}
\pfield{Fruit}{The Mass's widest predication of God and its narrowest scene of him
stand within a few minutes of each other, so that the universality the Alleluia
sings is what makes the visit at Naim more than a local wonder --- and the
Gospel's own crowd, saying \latin{Deus visitávit plebem suam}, is the one that
draws the inference.}
\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses}{Treated on its own the
Alleluia is the least integrated element of the ten, a verse of general praise
with no reception at all attaching to its own wording. Set against the Gospel it
turns out to be the element that states the scope of what the Gospel then
narrates.}
\pfield{Strongest limit}{Severe and threefold. \textbf{No checked witness expounds
the chant's predicate at all}: Augustine and Cassiodorus both read \latin{super
omnes deos} and build their expositions on the very word the chant removes, so the
proposal has no reception behind its key phrase. Nothing shows the chant took the
phrase from Ps.\ 46:3 rather than from a psalter not opened here, no psalter of
the older type having been collated at all. And the reading is
inherited chant wording, standing in the uncorrected optical layers of the Pustet
1862, the Venice 1570 and the 1604 \latin{typica}, so nothing about a 1962
compiler may be claimed from it.}
\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{P3. The ear the Mass asks God to bend, and the visitation the crowd reports}
\pfield{Anchors}{Introit \cue{Int.}, \latin{Inclína, Dómine, aurem tuam ad me},
with the missal's own \latin{ad me}; Gospel \cue{Gosp.}, Lk.\ 7:16 \latin{et quia
Deus visitávit plebem suam}}
\pfield{Mechanism}{The Mass opens by asking God to bend down toward one speaker
and closes its Gospel with a crowd reporting that he has come. What makes it more
than a theme is the added word: the tracked Clementine reads \latin{Inclina,
Domine, aurem tuam et exaudi me}, and the antiphon supplies a direction the Bible
verse does not have, while the Gospel supplies the arrival. \latin{Visitare}
with God as subject stands in Luke exactly four times, in the Latin stem and in
the Greek lemma alike, so the word
enters twice in the Benedictus, is spoken once by the crowd at Naim, and returns
once over Jerusalem.}
\pfield{Fruit}{A shape for the whole Mass that is neither allegory nor
coincidence: the first thing asked is a movement of God toward one voice, and the
last thing narrated is a crowd of witnesses saying it happened. It gives the
communicant a way of hearing the Introit as a petition the day's own Gospel
answers.}
\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses}{The Introit's \latin{ad me} is
otherwise only a textual curiosity, recorded in the divergence apparatus and
dropped. Set against the Gospel's \latin{visitávit}, it is the element that makes
the petition directional.}
\pfield{Strongest limit}{The addition is inherited chant wording and not a 1962
act, so nothing about a compiler may be claimed; \latin{inclina aurem} is common
psalter idiom and its recurrence proves nothing on its own; and \textbf{the
appointed Gospel prints \latin{Deus visitávit plebem suam} in the mouth of the
crowd and not of the Evangelist}, so a reading that makes it Luke's own verdict is
an interpretation and not the text. Cassiodorus's lemma does carry \latin{ad me},
so the addition is older than the Missal.}
\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{P4. Four different operations on the extent of what is read or sung}
\pfield{Anchors}{Introit \cue{Int.}, Offertory \cue{Off.}, Communion \cue{Comm.}
and Gospel \cue{Gosp.}}
\pfield{Mechanism}{Four different operations on extent, one to each element. The
Introit is a three-segment centonisation, v.~1a to \latin{exáudi me}, then v.~2b
from \latin{salvum fac}, then v.~3 entire, with \latin{ad me} added and
\latin{mihi} for \latin{mei}. The Offertory is a single passage with its middle
removed, the whole of Ps.\ 39:3 from \latin{et eduxit me de lacu miseriæ} to
\latin{direxit gressus meos} cut away, and v.~4b with it. The Communion is a
half-verse, dropping \latin{Si quis manducaverit ex hoc pane, vivet in æternum}.
The Gospel is a substituted opening, \latin{In illo témpore: Ibat Iesus} for
\latin{Et factum est: deinceps ibat}. Cento, excision, halving, substitution ---
four operations, each of them a departure from the Clementine word by word, and
each with a literary consequence of its own.}
\pfield{Fruit}{A reader who has seen the four together stops treating a liturgical
excerpt as a shortened quotation. The appointed text is a made thing, and the four
ways of making it are visible in one formulary, which is a better instruction in
how to read any Mass than a note attached to any one of them.}
\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses}{Each operation looks like an
accident of length where it stands. Only the set shows that four distinct
editorial procedures are at work in ten elements, and that three of the four fall
on chants and one on a pericope.}
\pfield{Strongest limit}{\textbf{Antiphon and pericope extents are inherited chant
and lectionary tradition, and nothing in the corpus establishes that anything was
cut in order to say something.} The chant repertories that would show whether
these extents are stable across the tradition are not held in this repository, so
the pattern is a description of what the 1962 book prints and not evidence of an
act of composition. A reader who took the four as deliberate would be claiming a
compiler this evidence does not reach.}
\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{P5. What lies immediately past the cuts}
\pfield{Anchors}{Offertory \cue{Off.}, ending at \latin{hymnum Deo nostro};
Introit \cue{Int.}, \latin{sperántem in te} and the half-verses it passes over;
Secret \cue{Sec.}, \latin{Tua nos, Dómine, sacraménta custódiant}}
\pfield{Mechanism}{Two cuts in one Mass stop one clause short of the same theme,
and a prayer of the same Mass then asks for a word one of them withheld. The
Offertory ends at \latin{hymnum Deo nostro}, so Ps.\ 39:4b \latin{Videbunt multi,
et timebunt, et sperabunt in Domino} is cut mid-verse and Ps.\ 39:5 \latin{Beatus
vir cujus est nomen Domini spes ejus} is never reached --- while the Introit's own
antiphon already prays \latin{salvum fac servum tuum \dots\ sperántem in te}, so
the hope the Offertory stops before is the disposition under which the Mass began.
And the Introit's centonisation passes over Ps.\ 85:2a \latin{Custodi animam meam},
while the Secret asks \latin{Tua nos, Dómine, sacraménta custódiant}: the verb the
unsung half-verse used of the soul is the verb the Secret asks of the sacraments.}
\pfield{Fruit}{The Mass keeps saying, in prayers of its own, what its chants stop
just short of singing. That is a way of hearing the orations as continuous with
the chants rather than as a separate genre laid over them, and it gives the Secret
--- otherwise the least remarkable of the three prayers --- a precise place in the
formulary.}
\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses}{A cut is normally noticed only
as an absence. Here the absences are answered elsewhere in the same Mass, which no
element-by-element reading can see, because the answer is never in the element
where the cut is.}
\pfield{Strongest limit}{\textbf{The unsung matter is not appointed text, and a
proposal that leans on it presses verses the assembly does not hear.} The Ps.~39
hope-formula is also a different construction from the beatitude formula
\latin{beatus \dots\ qui sperat}: Ps.\ 39:5 reads \latin{spes ejus}, so it is a
hope-beatitude in another grammar and must not be counted as a further instance of
that formula. And \latin{custodíre} is among the commonest verbs in the Roman
euchology, so the Introit-and-Secret leg rests on a coincidence of a very ordinary
word.}
\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{P6. Let us walk, and Jesus was going}
\pfield{Anchors}{Epistle \cue{Ep.}, Gal.\ 5:25 \latin{Si spíritu vívimus, spíritu
et ambulémus}, the lesson's first imperative; Gospel \cue{Gosp.}, \latin{In illo
témpore: Ibat Iesus in civitátem}, the pericope's first finite verb as the missal
prints it}
\pfield{Mechanism}{The appointed lesson's first imperative and the appointed
Gospel's first finite verb are the same act, and the missal's own arrangement puts
them one after the other in the rite, with only a Gradual and an Alleluia between.
The incipit is the missal's own: it replaces \latin{Et factum est: deinceps ibat}
and supplies the subject the pericope would otherwise lack, so that the first
thing said of Christ in this Mass's Gospel is that he was walking, immediately
after the assembly has been told to walk.}
\pfield{Fruit}{A reading of the day's two lessons as one instruction and its
example, in the plainest possible form: the Epistle says walk, and the Gospel
shows where the walking goes --- to a gate, a bier and a widow. It also gives the
Epistle's opening imperative, which is otherwise a conclusion drawn from premises
the Mass does not read, something in this Mass to attach to.}
\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses}{Read separately, Gal.\ 5:25 is
the tail of an argument about flesh and spirit and Lk.\ 7:11 is a scene-setting
line. Read in the order the book prints them, they are an imperative and its
enactment, and the enactment ends in mercy rather than in moral effort.}
\pfield{Strongest limit}{Obvious and severe. \textbf{\latin{In illo témpore} is
the standard Roman Gospel incipit formula throughout the book}, and \latin{Ibat}
is inherited from the Bible text's own \latin{deinceps ibat}, so the substitution
moves the verb to the head of the pericope rather than creating it. A proposal
that overstated this would be claiming a compiler's wit where there is a formula.
How many other 1962 Gospel pericopes have their incipit reshaped the same way is a
whole-Missal count nobody has run, and the optical layers available would not
support it.}
\end{proposal}
