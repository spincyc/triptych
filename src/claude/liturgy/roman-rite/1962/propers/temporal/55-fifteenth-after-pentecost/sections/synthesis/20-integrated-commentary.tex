\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

\subsection*{1. Nearly every place the sung text leaves the Bible was already in print long before 1962}

The Vatican \latin{editio typica} governs every word of this formulary, and
the collation that decides how much of the wording belongs to 1962 is not the
one against the Bible but the one against the books that had printed the same
readings earlier. \textbf{Set beside the Benziger \latin{editio iuxta typicam}
of the same year, read only in an uncorrected optical layer and allowing for
that layer's accents, the two 1962 books part at a single word.} At Lk.\ 7:14
the Vatican book spells \latin{Aduléscens} and Benziger \latin{Adolescens};
everything else that separates them is pointing, abbreviated conclusions and
the length of a repetition cue. Since the Clementine spells the \emph{o}, and
so do the layers of the Venice 1570 and of the 1604 \latin{typica}, that vowel
is a form the 1962 book prints where its predecessors do not.

Against the tracked Clementine the sung texts part company at eight places at
the level of the word, and at a ninth in the Introit's psalm verse, where
\latin{servi tui: quia ad te} answers \latin{servi tui, quoniam ad te}.

\begin{divergencetable}
Int., Ps. 85:1 & \latin{aurem tuam ad me, et exáudi me:} & \latin{aurem tuam et exaudi me,} --- the antiphon \textbf{adds} \latin{ad me}\\
Int., Ps. 85:3 & \latin{miserére mihi, Dómine,} & \latin{Miserere mei, Domine,}\\
All., Ps. 94:3 & \latin{super omnem terram.} & \latin{super omnes deos.} --- a different predicate\\
Off., Ps. 39:2 & \latin{et respéxit me:} & \latin{et intendit mihi.}\\
Off., Ps. 39:3 & \latin{et exaudívit deprecatiónem meam:} & \latin{Et exaudivit preces meas,}\\
Off., Ps. 39:4 & \latin{hymnum Deo nostro.} & \latin{carmen Deo nostro.}\\
Comm., Jn. 6:52 & \latin{quem ego dédero} & \latin{quem ego dabo}\\
Comm., Jn. 6:52 & \latin{pro sǽculi vita.} & \latin{pro mundi vita.}\\
\end{divergencetable}

\textbf{Every one of those eight is legible in the optical layers of the
Pustet Ratisbon 1862, of the Venice 1570 and of the Vatican \latin{typica} of
1604}, which puts all eight in print centuries before the book that now
carries them and leaves 1962 inheriting rather than making them.

At two of the eight it is the chant that renders the Greek more exactly and
the Bible text that renders it loosely. Swete's Greek has a singular
\emph{deeseos} at Ps.\ 39:3 that \latin{deprecatiónem meam} answers and
\latin{preces meas} does not, and \emph{hymnon} at v.~4 is a hymn and not a
\latin{carmen}; both readings sit in Cassiodorus's lemma (\work{Exp.\ Ps.}\ on
Ps.~39; PL~70), and \latin{hymnum} in Augustine's (\work{En.\ Ps.}~39 §4;
PL~36, 433--450) and Ambrose's (\work{In Ps.\ XXXIX}~§3; PL~14, 1109--1110)
besides. The third of the Offertory's departures has the opposite standing:
\latin{respéxit me} corresponds to no Greek reading collated for this Mass,
and yet the lemma Cassiodorus expounds carries it too (\work{Exp.\ Ps.}\ on
Ps.~39; PL~70), so it is attested without being derivable.

Everything else that divides the appointed wording from the Bible is of a
different order --- four extents cut away, the address \latin{Fratres} put at
the head of the lesson, a substituted Gospel incipit, capitals and stops at
the joins. Two elements barely move at all: after its address the Epistle runs
through Gal.\ 5:25--6:10 word for word with the Clementine, and the Gradual's
only departure from it is a comma the Clementine prints where the missal has
none.

\subsection*{2. Five sung texts, five cuts, and expositors whose weight falls where the knife went}

Not one of this Mass's five sung texts gives its psalm or its verse entire,
and \textbf{at four of the five the surviving exposition belongs to words the
choir never reaches.} Whoever follows this Mass's reception back is repeatedly
sent to a clause the books have taken out of it.

The Introit is a cento in three segments --- v.~1a to \latin{exáudi me}, then
v.~2b from \latin{salvum fac}, then v.~3 whole, with v.~4 for the psalm verse.
What the centonist drops first is the psalm's title, and it is the one title
of its kind: across Vulgate Pss.\ 72--88, the block the tracked Clementine
closes with the doxologies at Ps.\ 71:19--20 and Ps.\ 88:53, the headings name
Asaph, the sons of Core, Eman and Ethan, and Ps.~85 alone stands as
\latin{Oratio ipsi David.} Stripped of it, the chant is the prayer of a
servant whose name has gone. Augustine hangs the second and third sections of
his exposition on \latin{quoniam egenus et inops ego sum} (\work{En.\ Ps.}~85
§§2--3; PL~37, 1081--1090), which is among the clauses the cento leaves out,
and comes at the sung words through it.

The Offertory cuts the middle out of Ps.\ 39:2--4: the whole of v.~3 from
\latin{et eduxit me de lacu miseriæ} through \latin{direxit gressus meos}
goes, and v.~4b with it, so that \latin{sperabunt in Domino} is severed
mid-verse. Augustine's reading of the psalm's central image belongs to the
verse that has gone --- the pit is \latin{Profunditas iniquitatis, ex
carnalibus concupiscentiis} and the rock underfoot is Christ (\work{En.\ Ps.}~39
§3; PL~36, 433--450) --- and the sung remainder keeps the deliverance without
the place delivered from.

The Gradual takes Ps.\ 91:2--3 and not the inscription \latin{Psalmus cantici,
in die sabbati} that stands as its v.~1. All that survives of Hilary of
Poitiers on this psalm is a treatise on that very inscription, and he says
nothing about either clause the chant sings (\work{Tract.\ de titulo ps.\ XCI}~§§1--3;
CSEL~22, pp.~339ff.). His argument is that the rest the title announces cannot
be the seventh day of Genesis, since \latin{nulla hic diei septimi requies,
nullum humanorum operum otium decantatur}, and must be looked for instead at
Ps.\ 94:11 --- a verse of the psalm the Alleluia sings from. His Latin comes
through a poor optical layer of a scan of Zingerle's edition, whose running
heads and apparatus are mangled and whose page order cannot be relied on. The
one clause of the psalm on which a Latin expositor spends a whole treatise is
thus the clause the choir never opens its mouth on; and Augustine, who does
reach the sung verses, turns the same title inward before he gets to them ---
\latin{Intus est, in corde est sabbatum nostrum} (\work{En.\ Ps.}~91 §§1--4;
PL~37, 1171--1178).

At the Alleluia the loss is not a cut but a substitution, and it costs more.
The chant sings \latin{super omnem terram} where every Bible witness collated
reads \latin{super omnes deos}, and both Latin expositors of Ps.\ 94:3 build
on the word that is gone: Augustine takes the gods first for the demons of the
nations, \latin{Parum enim erat quia terribilis super omnia daemonia Deus:
quid magnum, esse super omnia daemonia?}, and then for men deified by grace,
\latin{Deos dixit participatione, non natura} (\work{En.\ Ps.}~94 §§5--6;
PL~37, 1217--1234), while Cassiodorus divides the same word between gods men
invent and men God appoints, \latin{Deos frequenter legimus ab hominibus
confictos \dots\ Legimus etiam deos, sanctos viros a Domino constitutos}
(\work{Exp.\ Ps.}\ on Ps.~94; PL~70). \textbf{And the substitution is not all
this chant leaves behind.} Ps.~94 summons to worship in vv.~1--7 and warns in
vv.~8--11, and the appointed verse is the first of the summons's reason
clauses, four verses before that turn, so the psalm's own \latin{Hodie si
vocem ejus audieritis} is never sung here. It is the unsung half that Hebrews
quotes at length --- Heb.\ 3:7--11, 3:15, 4:3, 4:5 and 4:7, three of the five
coming back to the \latin{hodie}. What
the chant does sing is not an invention either: \latin{rex magnus} and
\latin{super omnem terram} collocate at Ps.\ 46:3 in the tracked Clementine
and nowhere else, and outside the Bibles there is an identified English of
what the chant itself reads, the 1861 Philadelphia hand missal setting the
versicle at its p.~428 as ``a great King over all the earth.''

The Communion sings the second half of Jn.\ 6:52 and not the first, so the
half it leaves out, \latin{Si quis manducaverit ex hoc pane, vivet in
æternum}, is carried in unsung; and the discourse's own refrain of
resurrection at the last day --- three times in the same words at vv.~40, 44
and 55 of the missal's own Clementine numbering and once in others at v.~39
--- is the element the excerpt carries in and cannot say. The Gospel is cut at
the head in the same manner: \latin{In illo témpore: Ibat Iesus} replaces
Luke's \latin{Et factum est: deinceps ibat}, and with the connective goes the
pair the Evangelist had made --- a servant healed at a distance for a Gentile
who asks, and then a son raised at close quarters for a widow who does not.

\subsection*{3. Who is speaking, and what the singer is told he is doing while he sings}

The Mass sings in the first person singular at the Introit and the Offertory
and prays in the first person plural at the Secret and the Postcommunion,
while the Collect makes its petition for \latin{Ecclésiam tuam} in the third
person and puts no first person in it at all. Its two Latin commentators, who
expound both psalms, do not put the same speaker behind them.

At Ps.~85 Augustine's answer is the whole Christ. Head and members make one
who \latin{et oret pro nobis, et oret in nobis, et oretur a nobis} (§1), so that
\latin{Inclina, Domine, aurem tuam} is uttered \latin{ex forma servi} and by
the entire Body, and \latin{clamavi tota die} covers not a day but \latin{omni
tempore \dots\ Unus homo usque in finem saeculi extenditur}; the petition is
granted on the ground of the petitioner's own lowness, \latin{Inclinat aurem,
si tu non erigas cervicem: humiliato enim appropinquat; ab exaltato longe
discedit} (\work{En.\ Ps.}~85 §2; PL~37, 1081--1090). Cassiodorus arrives at
the same reading by his own route --- \latin{quod dicit: Inclina, ostendit
quia se ad ipsum extendere non poterat humana conditio} --- glosses
\latin{tota die} as \latin{totius vitae tempus}, and at the psalm verse
assigns the gladness asked for to the servant's part in the speaker, \latin{ab
illa scilicet parte qua servus est} (\work{Exp.\ Ps.}\ on Ps.~85; PL~70, read
on an optical layer of Migne whose recognition is poor). His verse numbering
runs level with the Vulgate's at this psalm, the inscription being counted
inside v.~1.

\textbf{At the Offertory the two part.} Augustine settles the question
outright: the speaker is Christ, sounding now in the name of the Head and now
in ours who are his members (\work{En.\ Ps.}~39 §5; PL~36, 433--450).
Cassiodorus gives the psalm to the Church instead --- \latin{Ecclesia
catholica, quae fuerat de totius mundi partibus congreganda, patientiae
virtutem praedicat} --- and supplies the philology neither of the others does,
on a lemma that is the antiphon's word for word: the doubled verb is
\latin{sermo geminatus, quia superflua non est tam decora repetitio},
\latin{Respexit: quia praevenit omne bonum nostrum}, \latin{Deprecatio quoque
significat frequentissimam precem}, and \latin{hymnus} is a Greek word for
praise put together under the law of song, which is why the psalm must add
\latin{Deo nostro}, the pagans having sung hymns to their idols (\work{Exp.\ Ps.}\ on
Ps.~39; PL~70). Ambrose reads the same psalm as an announcement of the New
Testament, \latin{immisisti in os meum canticum novum, quod est Novum
Testamentum}, and draws a practical consequence from it, \latin{ut nostra
omnia relinquamus, Christum sequentes, et nostros diligamus inimicos}
(\work{In Ps.\ XXXIX}~§3; PL~14, 1109--1110, read on page images with no text
layer and transcribed by eye). So the servant of the one antiphon and the
patient Church of the other are two answers from one commentator, and the Mass
sings both of them with the same pronoun.

Where the lemmata differ the difference reaches the Missal's own text.
Augustine's Ps.~85 has no \latin{ad me}, reads \latin{Miserere mei} at v.~3
and \latin{Iucunda} at v.~4 where the missal prints \latin{Lætífica}
(\work{En.\ Ps.}~85 §§1--6; PL~37, 1081--1090); Cassiodorus reads \latin{ad
me} and \latin{Laetifica} with the missal and \latin{miserere mei} against it
(\work{Exp.\ Ps.}\ on Ps.~85; PL~70). \textbf{The addition the antiphon makes
is therefore older than the Missal and stands in a sixth-century Latin lemma,
while its \latin{miserére mihi} stands in neither Latin commentator.}

The Gradual tells the singer what singing is. Confession, for Augustine, faces
two ways at once --- \latin{In utraque re, et in peccato tuo, quia tu fecisti,
et in bono facto, confitere Domino, quia ipse fecit} --- and \latin{psallere}
is not vocal at all: \latin{Opus nostrum, psalterium nostrum est \dots\ Canta
ore, psalle operibus}, the singer to seek \latin{gloriam Dei, non tuam; nomen
ipsius, non tuum} (\work{En.\ Ps.}~91 §§1--4; PL~37, 1171--1178), which is in
the matter of praise the very thing this Mass's own lesson prohibits at Gal.\ 5:26.
Cassiodorus reaches both halves in his own sentences, refusing merit ---
\latin{nec meritorum nostrorum esse dicimus quod ipsius miseratione
praestatur} --- and making the psaltery an image of works: \latin{Psallere
enim significat piis operibus Domini mandata peragere, ut sicut psalterium de
superioribus sonat, ita operatio nostra ad aures Divinitatis ascendat}
(\work{Exp.\ Ps.}\ on Ps.~91; PL~70). What the chant sings is also an answer
to the lesson that has just ended: the lesson's last \latin{bonum} stands
three words before the \latin{máxime autem ad domésticos fidei} on which
Gal.\ 6:10 closes, and the responsory printed under it on p.~396 opens
\latin{Bonum est confitéri Dómino} --- though the Greek uses \emph{kalon} at 6:9 and
\emph{agathon} at 6:10, so the Latin is doing part of the repeating, and the
palm and cedar that would have answered the Epistle's harvest lie in verses
this chant never reaches. Its own \latin{misericórdiam tuam et veritátem tuam}
is a fixed pair, which the tracked Clementine collocates at three places only
--- Ps.\ 39:11, Ps.\ 87:12 and Ps.\ 91:3 --- two of them in psalms this Mass
sings from.

Against all of this the three orations put a grammar that claims nothing: the
first person plural at the Secret and the Postcommunion, and at the Collect no
first person whatever, its petition made for the Church in the third. The
Collect turns on a semicolon, which sets the Church's incapacity on one side
and the single petition it grounds on the other; the Secret sets two verbs of
defence over one object; and the Postcommunion keeps its subject back to the
end of the clause, so that what is asked to possess is \latin{doni cæléstis
operátio} and what is asked to go before is its \latin{efféctus} and not
\latin{noster sensus}.

\subsection*{4. One verb of carrying, two burdens, and four expositors who do not agree}

The appointed Latin prints \latin{portáte} at Gal.\ 6:2, \latin{portábit} at
6:5 and \latin{qui portábant} at Lk.\ 7:14, and a single Greek verb stands
behind all three. The New Testament has \emph{bastazo} twenty-seven times
over, four of those in Galatians and two of the four inside this very lesson;
it is also the word Luke gives the men carrying the bier through the gate of
Naim. \textbf{One Latin verb renders all three occurrences and the Douay
follows the Latin}, so that in neither column can a reader see that the
command laid on the Galatians and the work of the bearers are the same word.
The two burdens run the other way about: \emph{baros} at 6:2 and
\emph{phortion} at 6:5 are two Greek nouns under one Latin one, and the Douay
again keeps to the Latin with ``burdens'' and ``burden.'' A single Mass thus
holds an order to take up someone else's load, a sentence saying that one load
can be transferred to nobody, and a procession carrying a body out of a town.

\textbf{Both ends of the lesson stop just short of the cross.} It opens one
verse past 5:24 \latin{Qui autem sunt Christi, carnem suam crucifixerunt} and
closes four verses before 6:14 \latin{Mihi autem absit gloriari, nisi in cruce
Domini nostri Jesu Christi}, so vainglory is raised as the problem and Paul's
answer to it is not read.

How the two burdens can both be true is the question the Latin tradition puts
to this lesson, and it comes back with four answers. Jerome makes 6:1 a
discipline of the corrector before it is a remedy for the corrected: Paul
calls the offender \latin{homo} and withholds the word from the man correcting
him, and he wrote \latin{ne et tu tenteris} rather than \latin{ne tu cadas},
because \latin{Vinci quippe vel vincere, nonnumquam in nostra est potestate:
caeterum tentari, in potestate tentantis est} (col.~426); at 6:5 he removes
the unshareable burden to the judgement seat, where \latin{non Job, non
Daniel, nec Noe rogare posse pro quoquam} (col.~429) --- \work{Comm.\ in
Gal.}\ lib.~III, on Gal.\ 5:26--6:10 at PL~26, cols.\ 423--432, transcribed by
eye from page images that carry no text layer. Since his lemmata agree with
the missal where the argument turns, and the Vulgate Galatians is his own
revision, the text he expounds and the text this Mass reads are one text.
Augustine makes correction the single proof of a spiritual man, to be
attempted only after the conscience has been questioned before God ---
\latin{Dilige et dic, quod voles} (§§56--57) --- and identifies the burden
nobody can lift with the weight of conscience, which admiration cannot lighten
and may increase: \latin{Non ergo laudatores nostri minuunt onera conscientiae
nostrae; atque utinam non etiam accumulent} (§59), at \work{Exp.\ Gal.}~§§52--61,
PL~35, 2145--2148. His Galatians is pre-Vulgate, and his lemmata depart from
the missal's at nearly every appointed verse, 6:5 reading \latin{proprium onus
portabit}. \textbf{Chrysostom declines the whole frame.} For him the passage
is not a discipline of anyone's conscience but an economy of complementary
weaknesses inside one body, the Church a building whose stones occupy
different positions, and 6:2 an instruction to make the thing up in common out
of what each bears with in the others (\work{In Gal.}\ cap.~6; PG~61,
673--680). He is read in nineteenth-century English, so where he separates
``fulfil'' from ``complete'' the distinction being reported is his about the
Greek. Aquinas turns 6:5 into the rendering of an account and adds a third way
of carrying another's burden that the others do not have, \latin{pro poena
sibi debita satisfaciendo, orationibus et bonis operibus}, which is his own
development; his second exposition of 6:7 repeats Jerome's argument almost
word for word, so those two make one continuous line and not two independent
witnesses (\work{Super Gal.}\ cap.~6 lect.~1--2). Four expositors, four
answers to one verse.

At the gate the verb finally takes a body for its object and the carrying
halts, \latin{Hi autem, qui portábant, stetérunt}, and what is being carried
is a man who can neither share a burden nor shoulder his own. \textbf{Neither
Latin expositor of the scene lets the bearers be helpers.} Ambrose makes them
the mortal fluxes of the body's own nature, \latin{hi sunt nostri funeris
portitores} (\work{Exp.\ Luc.}~V.90; CSEL~32 pars~4, pp.~216--218; PL~15,
1376B--1377A); Bede makes them \latin{vel immunda desideria \dots\ vel
lenocinia blandientium \dots\ venenata sociorum} and the bier itself
\latin{male secura desperati peccatoris conscientia} (\work{In Luc.}\ lib.~II;
PL~92, cols.\ 417--420, from a Migne optical layer whose two columns
interleave badly).

The Secret then asks for defence from what the lesson has just finished
naming. Chrysostom, on Gal.\ 6:1, lays the man's fall to ``the malice of the
devil rather than the remissness of the soul'' (\work{In Gal.}\ cap.~6; PG~61,
673--680), and \latin{contra diabólicos \dots\ incúrsus} is what the prayer
over the gifts asks to be guarded against. He is expounding Paul and not the
prayer.

\subsection*{5. Where the expositors put the Church in the scene, and the doctrine that joins the bier to the bread}

The three prayers name the Church once and then narrow away from her ---
\latin{Ecclésiam tuam} at the head of the Collect, \latin{nos} at the Secret,
\latin{mentes nostras et córpora} at the Postcommunion --- and the clause that
follows her there, \latin{quia sine te non potest salva consístere}, refuses
her any standing of her own before a petition is made at all. The lesson ends
on the same body under another name, \latin{máxime autem
ad domésticos fidei}, which is the household of Eph.\ 2:19, \latin{domestici
Dei}; Chrysostom takes that clause for a preference inside a charity that
excludes nobody, grace ``invites both land and sea to the board of charity,''
showing only a greater care for its own household (\work{In Gal.}\ cap.~6;
PG~61, 673--680). \textbf{The commentators on the day's Gospel find the same
body inside the scene at Naim, all three at the same figure and each to a
different end --- and two further expositors do not place her there at all.}

Ambrose reads the widow as holy Church (\work{Exp.\ Luc.}~V.88--92; CSEL~32
pars~4, pp.~216--218; PL~15, 1376B--1377A). She calls the
younger people back from the funeral, \latin{quae flere prohibetur eum cui
resurrectio debeatur} (§89), and ringed by the crowd she seems \latin{plus
\dots\ esse quam feminam}; and she weeps notwithstanding, \latin{fleat pro te
mater ecclesia, quae pro singulis tamquam pro unicis filiis uidua mater
interuenit} (§§91--92). He is also the one witness here who names the cross,
and he names it out of the timber the bier is made of: the dead man
\latin{spem resurgendi habebat, quia ferebatur in ligno} (§90). Bede reads the same
widowed mother as mother Church (\work{In Luc.}\ lib.~II; PL~92, cols.\
417--420), \latin{licet e multis collecta personis, una sit perfecta et
immaculata virgo}, with each of the faithful for her only son and Gal.\ 4:19
cited for the motherhood, and works the rest of the scene's furniture through
against her: the city gate is one of the bodily senses
through which death gets out; the handing back to the mother is sacramental
reconciliation, \latin{Redditur matri, cum \dots\ per sacerdotalis decreta
judicii communioni sociatur Ecclesiae}; and the miracle is turned against
Novatian, who \latin{veramque matrem Ecclesiam \dots\ negat consolari debere}.
He is also the only witness located who says anything about v.~16. Augustine
puts her at the same figure and gives her the affect opposite Ambrose's
(\work{Sermo}~98; PL~38, 591--595): his widowed mother is mother Church
rejoicing at every raising (§2), and the three raisings are three degrees of
sin --- the girl indoors is consent in the heart, the young man carried
through the gate is the deed done, and Lazarus under the stone is habit,
\latin{vis dura consuetudinis} (§5). That sermon stops at v.~15, one verse
short of the appointed pericope.

Cyril of Alexandria and Gregory of Nyssa read the scene without the Church in
it at all. Cyril takes the raisings for ``a pledge of the hope prepared for us
of a resurrection of the dead'' (\work{Comm.\ in Luc.}, Sermon~XXXVI; Payne
Smith, Oxford 1859, vol.~1, pp.~132--135); Gregory of Nyssa, at \work{De
hominis opificio}~25.6--11 (PG~44, 213--217), takes the whole sequence for a
graded schooling by which human weakness was accustomed to credit
resurrection, and calls the widow's son ``the stock of her race, the shoot of
its succession, the staff of her old age'' --- his words reaching this page in
the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers' nineteenth-century English, second series
vol.~5. Neither of them denies the ecclesial reading and neither is answering
its question. \textbf{So the one Church weeps in Ambrose and rejoices in
Augustine, and what this pericope hands on is a set of readings rather than
one doctrine with variants.}

Cyril supplies two things the Latin readings have not got. The first is the
initiative, and he states it himself: at Capharnaum Christ was there on an
invitation, whereas at Naim ``He draws near without being invited. For no one
summoned Him \dots\ but He comes to do so of His own accord.'' The second is
the touching of the bier, which he makes the
entire instruction, arguing that the flesh of the Word is ``the body of life''
and that what it touches it quickens, with iron heated in fire for the
comparison (Sermon XXXVI; Payne Smith, vol.~1, pp.~132--135). \textbf{That
doctrine is where the bier and the bread meet, and the join is not a
resemblance but a sentence a witness wrote.} Expounding the Communion's own
verse, Aquinas calls the flesh \latin{organum divinitatis suae} and states the
principle that an instrument works by the power of the agent using it:
\latin{cum instrumentum agat virtute agentis \dots\ ita ut Damascenus dicit et
caro virtute verbi adiuncti vivificat: unde Christus tactu suo sanabat
infirmos} (\work{Super Io.}\ cap.~6 lect.~6). The closing words of that
quotation are the Gospel of this same Mass. Cyril argues the identical
doctrine at length on the Fourth Gospel, under a chapter heading that states
it, \emph{That the Holy Body of Christ is Life-giving} (\work{Comm.\ in
Io.}~IV.2; Pusey, Library of the Fathers 43, Oxford 1874, vol.~1,
pp.~409--412).

Over the Communion's verse the expositors then divide about what sort of
eating is in question. Augustine drives it inward --- \latin{qui manducat
intus, non foris; qui manducat in corde, non qui premit dente} (§12) --- and
makes the sacrament's \latin{res} the \latin{societatem corporis et membrorum
suorum, quod est sancta Ecclesia}, which is \latin{omni homini ad vitam, nulli
ad exitium} where the sacrament itself is \latin{quibusdam ad vitam, quibusdam
ad exitium} (§15), at \work{Tract.\ in Io.}~26 §§12--18, PL~35, 1607--1616.
Chrysostom lets the clause carry two references at once, ``either His saving
doctrines and the faith which is in Him, or His own Body,'' and treats the
quarrel it provokes in the next verse as the pattern of a permanent failure,
unbelief arriving in company with the question how (\work{Hom.\ in Io.}~46;
PG~59, 257--260, in nineteenth-century English). \textbf{A limit travels with
the whole of it.} Augustine and Aquinas both read \latin{dabo} and \latin{pro
mundi vita} at this verse (\work{Tract.\ in Io.}~26 §§12--18; \work{Super
Io.}\ cap.~6 lect.~6), and Aquinas's gloss on the tense turns on the simple
future, \latin{dicit dabo, quia nondum institutum erat hoc sacramentum}, so
neither that gloss nor the exposition of how far the effect reaches can be
carried over to the antiphon's \latin{dédero} and \latin{sǽculi}. What the two
of them expound at that place is the Fourth Gospel's verse and not the
antiphon as this book sings it.

Two results belong to the appointed wording on its own. \latin{Visitare} with
God for its subject occurs in Luke at 1:68, 1:78, 7:16 and 19:44 and nowhere
else, so the crowd's \latin{Deus visitávit plebem suam} is one of four --- and
the appointed text leaves that verdict in the crowd's mouth and not the
Evangelist's. And four names the \work{Catena aurea} attaches to this chapter
have no located text behind them: the eight-resurrections scheme it gives to
\latin{Ambrosius} is absent from Ambrose's Luke commentary, a word search of
the complete critical text returning V.86 and VI.64 with the scheme in
neither, and it may belong to another work of his, or to Ps.-Ambrose, or to a
Glossa attribution; Theophylact's allegory of the widow as the soul, Titus of
Bostra's contrast with Elias and Eliseus, and the unnamed Chrysostom fragment
on consolation are the other three, and all three are leads rather than
witnesses. The same catena also puts one of its headings over the wrong book:
what it labels \latin{Cyrillus in Ioannem} at this chapter is matter from
Cyril's Luke commentary, and Payne Smith's footnote at vol.~1, p.~135 reports
that Aquinas and the Cramer catena manuscript give the fragment beside it to
Cyril too. A negative of a different kind stands beside them: Gregory the
Great has nothing on this pericope --- inside Luke~7 the \work{Catena} cites
him only at the sinful woman of vv.~36--50, and on the standard account of
their contents his \work{Homiliae in Evangelia} reach the chapter only at
Homily~33, on that same passage.

\subsection*{6. What this Mass inherited, where its oldest text has shifted, and where its Gospel comes back}

\textbf{The three prayers descend as a unit.} All three stand together, as one Mass and in liturgical order, in
the Gregorian Hadrianum at Wilson's section XXXIIII, under the heading
\latin{DOMINICA .XVI. POST PENTECOSTEN.} at printed p.~174; and all three in
the Old Gelasian at Wilson's Book~III sect.~XI, printed p.~230, headed
\latin{Item alia Missa} and carrying no Sunday number whatever. Their wording
has shifted twice since, and the two eighth-century books do not shift
together: both of them put the adverb where 1962 does not, reading
\latin{tueantur semper incursus} against the Secret's \latin{semper tueántur
incúrsus}, while at the Postcommunion the vocative is placed differently in
the Old Gelasian alone. So the Hadrianum differs from 1962 in one place and
the Gelasian in two, and at the second the two ancient books also differ from
each other.

\textbf{Of the prayers' own text there is no exposition anywhere the sources
reach.} Not one of the three is commented on by any Father or Doctor, in a
tracked source or in any patristic or medieval work that was opened for this
formulary; none of the three turns up in the Veronense, nor in Férotin's
\work{Liber mozarabicus sacramentorum}, Bannister's \work{Missale Gothicum} or
Lowe's \work{Bobbio Missal}; and nothing opened here dates or attributes any
of them, so what is attested of their history begins with the two
eighth-century books. The nearest approach to an exposition is Honorius
Augustodunensis, who has this Collect at his own Fifteenth Sunday and
summarises it as \latin{quam ut Deus mundet, muniat, rogat} (\work{Gemma
animae} IV cap.~LXXVIII; PL~172, cols.\ 719--720).

\textbf{What the sources disagree about is which Sunday this is.} The Old
Gelasian numbers it not at all, and Wilson's apparatus there reports Rheinau,
S.~Gallen, Gerbert and Menard putting the Mass two Sundays later than 1962 and
Pamelius one later; the same split recurs at the adjoining section, which
makes the offset a numeration base rather than a local slip, and the three
prayers all five of those books carry are exactly the three 1962 keeps. None
of the five was opened here, so the split is Wilson's report of them. The
Hadrianum's own heading numbers the section that carries these orations
\latin{DOMINICA .XVI.} Honorius calls the day the Fifteenth. The \work{Liber
Comitis} heads it \latin{Dominica mensis vi} (PL~30, cols.\ 521--522), its
count from the feast of St~Lawrence having stopped a Sunday earlier, where
Schuster's reckoning makes this the fifth after St~Lawrence and the last of
that series (\work{The Sacramentary} vol.~III, printed pp.~139--141) --- two
counts differing by one. \textbf{That heading carries a limit the others do
not.} What Migne prints there under Jerome's name is unsettled in its date,
its provenance and its textual authority, and it is unsettled too whether the
\latin{mensis vi} and \latin{mensis viii} headings, with no \latin{mensis vii}
standing between them, are the book's own arrangement or Migne's; so the
\latin{mensis vi} is a witness to this Sunday's number only so far as that
question is open.

\textbf{And the chants did not travel with the prayers.} Across five
consecutive sections of the Ottobonianus 313 margins, the series of chants and
the series of orations stand two Sundays apart, and the four-element Mass
written under this Sunday's Introit there --- with the respond \latin{Bonum
est confidere} and the communion \latin{Qui manducat} --- is not the 1962 four
(Wilson, printed p.~173). Honorius agrees with that margin at three slots out
of four, and the three Alleluias he records at his own Fifteenth are none of
them this one. Whether the respond standing under this Introit is a chant
shared between two consecutive Sundays or a copyist's error is unsettled, and
the doubling on Wilson's page is real, his own index printing ``173 (bis).''
In the gregorien.info index of the six earliest Mass antiphonaries Hesbert
collected, this Introit, Gradual and Offertory are already at \latin{Dominica
XV post Pentecosten}, but that Mass takes \latin{Qui manducat carnem meam} for
its Communion and fixes no Alleluia at all, and \latin{Panis quem ego dedero}
stands there a Sunday earlier. Measured against that record the movement runs
one way only: this Mass's Communion has come forward from the Fourteenth to
the Fifteenth, and the antiphon it displaced has not gone back to fill the 1962
Fourteenth, which the tracked registry gives \latin{Primum quaerite}, Mt.\ 6:33.
Every antiphonary figure here is the gregorien.info database's report of
Hesbert's printed pages.

\textbf{One change in the formulary is recent, and a single instrument made
it.} The Mass prints one Collect, one Secret and one Postcommunion; inside the
1960 code that follows from RG 434~b with RG 435, while the seasonal second
and third orations were themselves abolished by a separate instrument,
\work{Variationes in Breviario et Missali Romano} cap.~IV n.~18,
\latin{Orationes pro diversitate Temporum abolentur}, printed at \work{Acta
Apostolicae Sedis}~52 (1960), p.~709. Guéranger's 1909 volume shows the state
before the change, printing the three seasonal orations by cross-reference,
and calls the day ``the Sunday of the widow of Naim'' (\work{The Liturgical
Year: Time after Pentecost} vol.~II, printed pp.~344--356).

\textbf{This Mass's Gospel is read again on the Thursday of Lent's fourth
week, and that day is attested outside the registry.} Schuster names it
(\work{The Sacramentary} vol.~III, printed pp.~139--141) and so, independently,
does Guéranger in his 1909 volume just cited; and the Pustet 1862 prints no Gospel
at this Sunday at all but sends the reader across to that Thursday, which is
why the Gospel's presence in a public-domain witness rests on the Venice 1570
and the 1604 \latin{typica}.
