\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

\subsection*{1. Where the appointed wording is not the Bible's, and the older books that already carried it}

The Vatican \latin{editio typica} controls every word of the appointed
formulary. Against the Benziger \latin{editio iuxta typicam} of the same year,
whose text reaches this guide only in an uncorrected optical layer, and allowing
for that layer's accent substitutions, the two agree --- apart from the
differences set out here --- through the Introit, the Collect, the body of the
Epistle, the Alleluia, the Offertory, the body of the Secret and the Communion.
\textbf{Where they differ, one difference is a word and the rest are pointing,
abbreviation and cue length.} At Lk.\ 7:14 the typical edition prints
\latin{Aduléscens} with \emph{u} and Benziger \latin{Adolescens} with \emph{o};
the Clementine reads the \emph{o}, and so do the uncorrected optical layers of
the Venice 1570 and the 1604 \latin{typica}, so the \emph{u} is the 1962 typical
edition's own form.

Against the tracked Clementine the sung texts part company at eight places at
the level of the word, and at a ninth in the Introit's psalm verse, where
\latin{servi tui: quia ad te} answers \latin{servi tui, quoniam ad te}.

\begin{divergencetable}
Int., Ps. 85:1 & \latin{aurem tuam ad me, et exáudi me:} & \latin{aurem tuam et exaudi me,} --- the antiphon \textbf{adds} \latin{ad me}\\
Int., Ps. 85:3 & \latin{miserére mihi, Dómine,} & \latin{Miserere mei, Domine,}\\
All., Ps. 94:3 & \latin{super omnem terram.} & \latin{super omnes deos.} --- a different predicate\\
Off., Ps. 39:2 & \latin{et respéxit me:} & \latin{et intendit mihi.}\\
Off., Ps. 39:3 & \latin{et exaudívit deprecatiónem meam:} & \latin{Et exaudivit preces meas,}\\
Off., Ps. 39:4 & \latin{hymnum Deo nostro.} & \latin{carmen Deo nostro.}\\
Comm., Jn. 6:52 & \latin{quem ego dédero} & \latin{quem ego dabo}\\
Comm., Jn. 6:52 & \latin{pro sǽculi vita.} & \latin{pro mundi vita.}\\
\end{divergencetable}

\textbf{All eight stand also in the uncorrected optical layers of the Pustet
Ratisbon 1862, the Venice 1570 and the Vatican \latin{typica} of 1604, so none
of the eight is a 1962 act.} What those three layers can carry is bounded: no
page image of any of them was opened here; the source records of the 1604 and
the 1570 classify their layers degraded and forbid resting any negative search
on them, the Pustet's classifies its layer prose Latin and bounds that
prohibition to searches over its accented text, and all three admit quotation
only through a checked passage. What is reported of them is what the layer
shows, and no negative result rests on them.

At two of the eight the chant is the closer rendering and the Bible text the
looser one. Against Swete, \latin{deprecatiónem meam} renders the singular
\emph{deeseos} exactly where \latin{preces meas} does not, and \emph{hymnon} is
\latin{hymnum} and not \latin{carmen}; both stand in Cassiodorus's lemma at
Ps.~39, and \latin{hymnum} in Augustine's and Ambrose's as well.
\latin{Respéxit me} answers neither Swete's \emph{proseschen moi} nor any Greek
variant collated here, and yet it too stands in Cassiodorus's lemma, so it has a
witness even though it has no derivation.

The rest of what separates the appointed text from the Bible is matter of
another kind: four extents cut, the lesson's prefixed \latin{Fratres}, the
Gospel's substituted incipit, capitalisation, and pointing at the joins.
\textbf{The Epistle is remarkably clean} --- after its liturgical address the
whole of Gal.\ 5:25--6:10 matches the tracked Clementine word for word --- and
the Gradual departs only in pointing.

\subsection*{2. The two chants in which the assembly says \latin{I} are cut, and their expositors put their weight on the verses the cuts remove}

The Introit is a three-segment centonisation --- v.~1a as far as \latin{exáudi
me}, then v.~2b from \latin{salvum fac}, then v.~3 entire, with v.~4 as the
psalm verse. \textbf{The clause it drops first is the psalm's ascription, and
that ascription is unique.} Among Vulgate Pss.\ 72--88 the tracked Clementine
gives Asaph at 72--82, \latin{filiis Core} at 83, 84, 86 and 87, Eman the
Ezrahite at 87 as well, Ethan the Ezrahite at 88, and \latin{Oratio ipsi David.}
at 85 alone: Ps.~85 is the single Davidic prayer in the whole of Book~III, which
the tracked text bounds by the doxologies at Ps.\ 71:19--20 and Ps.\ 88:53. What
the cento sings after that omission is a psalm addressed by a servant to his God
with no name attached to it.

Augustine's exposition of that psalm is the locus classicus of the
\latin{totus Christus}, and the whole of the Introit's reception runs through
it: the Word is head of men and men his members, so that there is one Christ
\latin{qui et oret pro nobis, et oret in nobis, et oretur a nobis},
\latin{Inclina, Domine, aurem tuam} being spoken \latin{ex forma servi} and by
the whole Body. The ground on which the petition is heard is the speaker's own
lowness --- \latin{Inclinat aurem, si tu non erigas cervicem: humiliato enim
appropinquat; ab exaltato longe discedit} --- and \latin{clamavi tota die} is
not one day but \latin{omni tempore \dots\ Unus homo usque in finem saeculi
extenditur}. \textbf{His second and third paragraphs hang on \latin{quoniam
egenus et inops ego sum}}, the clause the cento does not sing, and he comes at
the sung words through it. Cassiodorus states the same reading in his own words
--- \latin{quod dicit: Inclina, ostendit quia se ad ipsum extendere non poterat
humana conditio} --- takes \latin{tota die} for \latin{totius vitae tempus}, and
at the psalm verse assigns the joy asked for to the servant's own part in the
speaker, \latin{ab illa scilicet parte qua servus est}. His numbering runs level
with the Vulgate's here, Ps.~85 counting its inscription inside v.~1, so his
\latin{Vers.~1} is the Vulgate's v.~1; his text was read on an optical layer of
Migne whose recognition is poor.

Neither commentator's lemma is the antiphon's at every point. Augustine's v.~1
has no \latin{ad me}, his v.~3 reads \latin{Miserere mei}, and his v.~4 reads
\latin{Iucunda} where the missal prints \latin{Lætífica}; Cassiodorus reads
\latin{ad me} and \latin{Laetifica} with the missal and \latin{miserere mei}
against it. So the \latin{ad me} the antiphon adds is older than the Missal and
stands in a sixth-century Latin lemma, while \latin{miserére mihi} stands in
neither Latin lemma.

\textbf{At the Offertory the same thing happens more sharply.} The antiphon
takes vv.~2, 3 and 4 of Ps.~39 and cuts the middle out of them: the whole of
v.~3 from \latin{et eduxit me de lacu miseriæ} to \latin{direxit gressus meos}
goes, and v.~4b with it, so that \latin{sperabunt in Domino} is cut mid-verse.
\textbf{Augustine's fullest paragraph is an exposition of the verse the antiphon
removes}: the pit of misery is \latin{Profunditas iniquitatis, ex carnalibus
concupiscentiis}, and the rock on which the feet are set is Christ. Of the words
the chant does sing he says that the new song belongs to the new man ---
\latin{novus homo, dicat canticum novum \dots\ Dicimus ergo hymnum Deo nostro, et
ipse hymnus liberat nos} --- and at his fifth paragraph he answers the question
of the psalm's speaker outright: it is Christ, speaking now in the name of our
Head and now of us who are his members. His lemma is not the antiphon's at
either of its first two verbs, reading \latin{Sustinens sustinui} and
\latin{attendit mihi}, and the dative is his point: \latin{Attendit mihi,
inquit, id est, consolando attendit, ut mihi prodesset.}

Ambrose reads the doubled verb as the psalm's point --- \latin{non est perfecti
exspectare, sed exspectasse} --- and the new canticle as the New Testament
itself, \latin{immisisti in os meum canticum novum, quod est Novum Testamentum},
drawing its practical consequence, \latin{ut nostra omnia relinquamus, Christum
sequentes, et nostros diligamus inimicos}. His Latin was read on page images
with no text layer and transcribed by eye. Cassiodorus supplies the philology
the other two do not, on a lemma that is the antiphon's text word for word:
\latin{considerandus est hic sermo geminatus, quia superflua non est tam decora
repetitio}; \latin{Respexit: quia praevenit omne bonum nostrum};
\latin{Deprecatio quoque significat frequentissimam precem}; and \latin{hymnus}
is a Greek word for praise composed under the law of song, which is why the
psalm adds \latin{Deo nostro}, the pagans having had hymns for their idols.
\textbf{For him the speaker of this psalm is the Church} ---
\latin{Ecclesia catholica, quae fuerat de totius mundi partibus congreganda,
patientiae virtutem praedicat} --- where at Ps.~85 he had made it Christ in the
form of a servant.

\subsection*{3. A chant that drops its title and a chant that changes its predicate leave their receptions behind}

Two of the day's chants put the question directly. The Gradual sings Ps.\
91:2--3 and not the inscription \latin{Psalmus cantici, in die sabbati} that
stands as Vulgate v.~1; the Alleluia sings Ps.\ 94:3 with a predicate the Latin
Bible does not have.

What the Gradual leaves unsung runs from that title through the wicked springing
up as grass to \latin{Justus ut palma florebit} and \latin{Plantati in domo
Domini \dots\ florebunt} --- the harvest imagery that would have answered the
Epistle's sowing, and that nobody hears. What it does sing answers the lesson's
last word: Gal.\ 6:10 closes \latin{operémur bonum ad omnes, máxime autem ad
domésticos fidei}, and the responsory printed directly beneath it on p.~396
begins \latin{Bonum est confitéri Dómino}. Two bounds hold that catchword down
--- in the Greek the lesson uses \emph{kalon} at 6:9 and \emph{agathon} at 6:10,
so the triple repetition is partly the Latin's and the chant answers only the
second, and the further pairing with the psalm's palm and cedar is drawn from
verses the chant does not sing. Its own \latin{misericórdiam tuam et veritátem
tuam} is a fixed collocation, standing in the tracked Clementine at Ps.\ 39:11,
Ps.\ 87:12 and Ps.\ 91:3 and nowhere else --- two of the three in psalms this
Mass sings from.

\textbf{At the Gradual the reception is anchored in the title the chant does not
sing.} Augustine makes the sabbath interior --- \latin{Intus est, in corde est
sabbatum nostrum \dots\ Cui autem bona est conscientia, tranquillus est} --- and
sets it against a merely bodily observance, \latin{Melius est enim arare, quam
saltare}. Hilary of Poitiers goes further: what survives of him on this psalm is
a tractate on its title and not a commentary on its verses, and he has nothing
on either clause the Gradual sings. What he argues is that the rest the title
names cannot be the seventh day of Genesis, \latin{nulla hic diei septimi
requies, nullum humanorum operum otium decantatur}, so that it must be sought at
Ps.\ 94:11 --- a verse of the Alleluia's own psalm. \textbf{His Latin reaches
this guide through a poor optical layer of a scan of Zingerle's critical
edition}, whose running heads and apparatus are badly mangled and whose page
sequence is unreliable.

\textbf{What the Gradual does sing, both Latin commentators read as a double
confession.} Augustine: \latin{In utraque re, et in peccato tuo, quia tu
fecisti, et in bono facto, confitere Domino, quia ipse fecit}; and
\latin{psallere} is not singing but doing, \latin{Opus nostrum, psalterium
nostrum est \dots\ Canta ore, psalle operibus}, the singer to seek \latin{gloriam
Dei, non tuam; nomen ipsius, non tuum} --- which is in praise exactly what this
Mass's own lesson forbids at Gal.\ 5:26. On the versicle he divides the day
between the time when it goes well with us and the time of tribulation, giving
the psalm's mercy to the one and its truth to the other. Cassiodorus reaches
both halves in his own words: \latin{nec meritorum nostrorum esse dicimus quod
ipsius miseratione praestatur}, and \latin{Psallere enim significat piis
operibus Domini mandata peragere, ut sicut psalterium de superioribus sonat, ita
operatio nostra ad aures Divinitatis ascendat}.

Ps.~94 is a summons to worship in vv.~1--7 and an oracle of warning in
vv.~8--11, the turn falling at v.~7/8. The chant's verse stands inside the
summons as the first of its reason clauses, four verses before the turn, so what
it carries in is the summons and not the warning --- and the warning half is the
one Hebrews quotes at length, three of its five citations returning to
\latin{Hodie si vocem ejus audieritis}. \textbf{At the Alleluia the substituted
predicate costs the element its whole reception.} Augustine's lemma is
\latin{et rex magnus super omnes deos}, and he builds two developments on the
word the chant removes: with 1~Cor.\ 8:5--6 the gods of the nations are demons,
over whom it would be small praise for God to be great --- \latin{Parum enim erat
quia terribilis super omnia daemonia Deus: quid magnum, esse super omnia
daemonia?} --- and then the gods are men made gods by participation,
\latin{Deos dixit participatione, non natura; gratia, qua voluit facere deos
\dots\ Deus verus facit deos credentes in se.} Cassiodorus, read afterwards on
the same verse, also has \latin{super omnes deos} and expounds the gods the same
two ways, \latin{Deos frequenter legimus ab hominibus confictos \dots\ Legimus
etiam deos, sanctos viros a Domino constitutos}. Neither development survives
into what the choir sings.

\textbf{The chant's predicate is nevertheless the Latin Bible's own phrase.} It
stands verbatim at Ps.\ 46:3, the one place in the tracked Clementine where
\latin{rex magnus} and \latin{super omnem terram} collocate. Neither phrase the
English Bibles carry renders the chant --- ``a great King above all gods'' is the
Bible text the missal departs from, and ``a great king over all the earth'' is
the Douay's Ps.\ 46:3 --- but an identified English of the chant's own reading
exists outside the Bibles: the 1861 Philadelphia hand missal prints the versicle
at its printed p.~428 as ``a great King over all the earth,'' beside the Latin
the missal sings.

\subsection*{4. One Latin verb of carrying, four expositors of the lesson who do not agree, and bearers at the gate whom neither Latin witness calls help}

The appointed text prints \latin{portáte} at Gal.\ 6:2, \latin{portábit} at 6:5
and \latin{qui portábant} at Lk.\ 7:14, and one Greek verb stands behind all
three. \emph{Bastazo} occurs twenty-seven times in the New Testament, four of
them in Galatians and two of those inside this lesson, and it is the verb Luke
gives the men carrying the bier out of the gate of Naim; neither the Latin nor
the Douay distinguishes it from the other verbs of bearing. The two burdens
under it are one Latin noun and two Greek words, \emph{baros} at 6:2 and
\emph{phortion} at 6:5, and the Douay follows the Latin and says ``burdens'' and
``burden.'' \textbf{So one Mass holds a command to carry another's load, a
sentence saying that one load can be handed to nobody, and a procession actually
carrying a body out of a gate.} The lesson argues over the carrying; the Gospel
performs it.

\textbf{Both of the lesson's boundaries fall just clear of the cross.} It begins
one verse after 5:24 \latin{Qui autem sunt Christi, carnem suam crucifixerunt},
and ends four verses before 6:14 \latin{Mihi autem absit gloriari, nisi in cruce
Domini nostri Jesu Christi}, so the Mass raises vainglory as a problem and does
not read Paul's answer to it.

\textbf{The apparent self-contradiction between the two burdens is what the
Latin tradition on this lesson argues about, and it does not reach one answer.}
Jerome reads 6:1 as a discipline of the corrector before it is a remedy for the
corrected: Paul calls the offender \latin{homo} and does not call the corrector
one, and he wrote \latin{ne et tu tenteris} and not \latin{ne tu cadas}, because
\latin{Vinci quippe vel vincere, nonnumquam in nostra est potestate: caeterum
tentari, in potestate tentantis est}; and at 6:5 he moves the unshareable burden
to the tribunal, where \latin{non Job, non Daniel, nec Noe rogare posse pro
quoquam}. His lemmata agree with the missal at the points that matter, and the
Vulgate Galatians is his own revision, so his lemma and the appointed text are
one text; the Latin was read on page images with no text layer and transcribed
by eye. Augustine makes correction the one test of a spiritual man, to be
undertaken only after examining one's own conscience before God ---
\latin{Dilige et dic, quod voles} --- and identifies the unshareable burden with
the weight of conscience, which no admirer can lighten: \latin{Non ergo
laudatores nostri minuunt onera conscientiae nostrae; atque utinam non etiam
accumulent.} He reads a pre-Vulgate Latin Galatians whose lemmata differ from
the missal's at nearly every appointed verse, his own at 6:5 reading
\latin{proprium onus portabit}. \textbf{Chrysostom takes the passage the other
way}: not a discipline of the corrector's conscience but an economy of
complementary weaknesses inside one body, the Church a building whose stones
hold different positions and 6:2 asking the members to ``make it up all of you
in common, by the things wherein you bear with one another.'' He is read in
nineteenth-century English, so his distinction between ``fulfil'' and
``complete'' is his argument about the Greek. Aquinas makes 6:5 the rendering of
account and adds a third mode of bearing another's burden, \latin{pro poena sibi
debita satisfaciendo, orationibus et bonis operibus}, which is his own
development and not the tradition's; his second exposition of 6:7 is Jerome's
argument almost word for word, so those two are one continuous tradition and not
two independent witnesses. Four expositors, four answers to one verse.

\textbf{At the gate of Naim the verb takes a body for its object, and the
carrying stops.} \latin{Hi autem, qui portábant, stetérunt}, and what is being
carried is a man who can neither share his burden nor bear his own. Neither
Latin expositor of the scene reads the bearers as help: Ambrose makes them the
mortal fluxes of material nature, \latin{cum uel ignis inmodicae cupiditatis
exaestuat uel frigidus umor exundat \dots\ hi sunt nostri funeris portitores},
and Bede makes them \latin{vel immunda desideria \dots\ vel lenocinia
blandientium \dots\ venenata sociorum} and the bier itself \latin{male secura
desperati peccatoris conscientia}. Bede's Latin reaches this guide through an
optical layer of Migne whose two-column setting interleaves badly.

\textbf{The Secret names what the lesson told the corrector to fear.}
Chrysostom, on Gal.\ 6:1, arraigns ``the malice of the devil rather than the
remissness of the soul'' in the man overtaken in a fault, and \latin{contra
diabólicos \dots\ incúrsus} is what the Secret asks to be defended from, the
lesson it follows having just named the assailant. He is commenting on Paul and
not on the prayer.

\subsection*{5. The Church, the bier and the bread: three things the expositors of one scene supply, and one they do not agree about}

\latin{Ecclésiam} stands once in the whole formulary, in the first prayer of the
Mass, with \latin{quia sine te non potest salva consístere} denying her any
standing of her own before anything at all is asked. The lesson closes on the
same body under another name, \latin{máxime autem ad domésticos fidei}, which is
the household metaphor of Eph.\ 2:19, \latin{domestici Dei}; Chrysostom reads
that clause as a preference inside a universal charity, grace ``invites both
land and sea to the board of charity, only it shows a greater care for its own
household.'' \textbf{The expositors of the day's Gospel find that body inside
the scene at Naim as well, and they do not put her in the same part of it ---
and two of them do not put her there at all.}

\textbf{Ambrose puts the Church beside the bier.} The widow is holy Church, who
calls the younger people back from the pomp of the funeral, \latin{quae flere
prohibetur eum cui resurrectio debeatur}, and ringed by the crowd of peoples
seems \latin{plus \dots\ esse quam feminam}; and she weeps after all,
\latin{fleat pro te mater ecclesia, quae pro singulis tamquam pro unicis filiis
uidua mater interuenit}. He is also the one witness who names the cross here,
and he names it from the wood: the dead man \latin{spem resurgendi habebat, quia
ferebatur in ligno \dots\ ut esset indicio salutem populo per crucis patibulum
refundendam}. \textbf{Bede puts her on the bier.} The only son is mother Church,
\latin{licet e multis collecta personis, una sit perfecta et immaculata virgo},
each of the faithful her only son with Gal.\ 4:19 quoted for her motherhood, and
the city gate is one of the bodily senses through which death goes out. The
restoration of the young man to his mother is sacramental reconciliation ---
\latin{Redditur matri, cum \dots\ per sacerdotalis decreta judicii communioni
sociatur Ecclesiae} --- and the raising is an argument against Novatian, who
\latin{veramque matrem Ecclesiam \dots\ negat consolari debere}. Bede is the only
witness located who comments on v.~16. \textbf{Augustine puts her where Ambrose
does and gives her the opposite affect.} His widowed mother is mother Church
rejoicing at each raising, and his three raisings are three grades of sin: the
girl within the house is sin consented to in the heart, the young man carried
out of the gate is sin gone out into the deed, and Lazarus under the stone is
habit, \latin{vis dura consuetudinis}. His sermon stops at v.~15, one verse
short of the appointed pericope. So the one Church weeps in Ambrose and rejoices
in Augustine, and neither reading is a variant of the other.

\textbf{Cyril and Gregory of Nyssa do not read the Church into the scene at
all.} Cyril reads the three raisings eschatologically, as ``a pledge of the hope
prepared for us of a resurrection of the dead''; Gregory of Nyssa reads the
sequence as a graded pedagogy by which Christ accustomed human weakness to
believe in resurrection, and the widow's only son is ``the stock of her race,
the shoot of its succession, the staff of her old age.'' Neither denies the
ecclesial readings and neither is answering their question, so what the
tradition hands on at this pericope is a set of readings and not one doctrine
with variants.

Cyril supplies two things the Latin readings do not. The first is the
initiative: at Capharnaum Christ was present by invitation, but at Naim ``He
draws near without being invited. For no one summoned Him to restore the dead
man to life, but He comes to do so of His own accord.'' The second is the
touching of the bier, which he makes the whole instruction --- it was done
``that thou mightest learn that the holy body of Christ is effectual for the
salvation of man. For the flesh of the Almighty Word is the body of life \dots\
For consider, that iron, when brought into contact with fire, produces the
effects of fire.'' \textbf{That doctrine is where the bier and the bread meet,
and the join is not a resemblance: Aquinas makes it in his own sentence.}
Expounding the Communion's verse he calls the flesh \latin{organum divinitatis
suae} and states the principle that an instrument acts by the power of its
agent: \latin{cum instrumentum agat virtute agentis \dots\ ita ut Damascenus
dicit et caro virtute verbi adiuncti vivificat: unde Christus tactu suo sanabat
infirmos.} That last clause is the Gospel of this same Mass. Cyril argues the
same doctrine at length on the Fourth Gospel under the chapter heading
\emph{That the Holy Body of Christ is Life-giving}.

\textbf{The expositors of the Communion's verse then divide over what kind of
eating is meant.} Augustine drives it inward --- \latin{qui manducat intus, non
foris; qui manducat in corde, non qui premit dente} --- and makes the \latin{res}
of the sacrament \latin{societatem corporis et membrorum suorum, quod est sancta
Ecclesia}, which is \latin{omni homini ad vitam, nulli ad exitium} where the
sacrament itself is \latin{quibusdam ad vitam, quibusdam ad exitium}. Chrysostom
allows the clause two references at once --- ``By bread He means here either His
saving doctrines and the faith which is in Him, or His own Body'' --- and reads
the discourse as a pedagogy whose next verse is the type of a standing failure:
``when questioning concerning the how comes in, there comes in with it
unbelief.'' \textbf{A bound travels with all of this.} Augustine's and Aquinas's
lemmata at that verse read \latin{dabo} and \latin{pro mundi vita}, and
Aquinas's gloss on the tense turns on the simple future, \latin{dicit dabo, quia
nondum institutum erat hoc sacramentum}, so neither the gloss nor the exposition
of the universality of the effect transfers to the antiphon's \latin{dédero} and
\latin{sǽculi}: what these witnesses expound is the verse of the Fourth Gospel
and not the antiphon as this book sings it. Cyril's Fourth Gospel commentary is
read here in Pusey's English of the Greek, his Luke commentary in Payne Smith's
English of an ancient Syriac version, and Gregory of Nyssa in the
nineteenth-century English of the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers.

Two results belong to the appointed wording itself. \latin{Visitare} with God as
its subject stands in Luke at 1:68, 1:78, 7:16 and 19:44 and nowhere else, so
the crowd's \latin{Deus visitávit plebem suam} is one of four --- and the
appointed text leaves that sentence in the mouth of the crowd and not of the
Evangelist. And the missal's incipit replaces Luke's \latin{Et factum est:
deinceps ibat}, which makes the raising the next thing in order after the
centurion's servant, so the pair Luke builds --- a servant healed at a distance
for a Gentile who asks, then a son raised at close quarters for a widow who does
not --- is severed.

\textbf{Four attributions in the \work{Catena aurea} at this chapter have no
located text behind them.} The eight-resurrections scheme it gives to
\latin{Ambrosius} is nowhere in Ambrose's Luke commentary, a word search of the
complete critical text returning V.86 and VI.64 and neither carrying it; it may
stand in another work of his, or be Ps.-Ambrose, or be a Glossa attribution.
Theophylact's widow-as-soul allegory, Titus of Bostra's contrast with Elias and
Eliseus, and the unnamed Chrysostom fragment on consolation are the other three,
and those three are leads and not witnesses. A different kind of result stands
beside them: Gregory the Great has nothing on this pericope --- within Luke~7 the
\work{Catena} cites him only on the sinful woman of vv.~36--50, and on the
standard account of their contents his \work{Homiliae in Evangelia} treat the
chapter only at Homily~33, on that same passage.

\subsection*{6. What this Mass inherited, where its oldest text has moved, and how little of it is sung here alone}

\textbf{The three prayers descend as a unit, and they are the oldest thing in
the formulary.} All three stand together, as one Mass and in liturgical order,
in the Gregorian Hadrianum under the heading \latin{DOMINICA .XVI. POST
PENTECOSTEN.}, and all three stand in the Old Gelasian under \latin{Item alia
Missa} with no Sunday number at all. Between those two eighth-century books and
1962 the wording moves in two places, and the two books do not move together:
both of them read \latin{contra diabolicos tueantur semper incursus} where 1962
reads \latin{semper tueántur incúrsus}, and at the Postcommunion the Old
Gelasian sets the vocative differently, \latin{possideat, Domine, quaesumus},
where the Hadrianum and 1962 read \latin{possídeat, quǽsumus, Dómine}. So the
Hadrianum stands apart from 1962 at one place and the Old Gelasian at two, and
the two ancient witnesses differ from each other at the second.

\textbf{Of the three prayers' own text there is no exposition anywhere the
sources reach.} No Father or Doctor comments on \latin{Ecclésiam tuam, Dómine,
miserátio continuáta}, \latin{Tua nos, Dómine, sacraménta custódiant} or
\latin{Mentes nostras et córpora possídeat} in any tracked source, or in any
patristic or medieval work opened for this formulary; none of the three occurs
in the Veronense, in Férotin's \work{Liber mozarabicus sacramentorum}, in
Bannister's \work{Missale Gothicum} or in Lowe's \work{Bobbio Missal}; and no
source opened for this formulary dates or attributes any of them, so their
attested history begins at the Old Gelasian and the Hadrianum. Honorius
Augustodunensis, who has this Collect at his own Fifteenth Sunday and writes
\latin{quam ut Deus mundet, muniat, rogat}, is the nearest thing to an
exposition of it that stands anywhere.

\textbf{What is left to read in the three prayers is their grammar, and it is
load-bearing.} The Collect's hinge is its semicolon, which divides the causal
clause from the single petition it grounds, so that the two verbs of the first
limb stand on one side of it and the one verb of the second on the other; the
uncorrected optical layer of the Benziger 1962 shows that semicolon too, and
those of the Pustet 1862, the Venice 1570 and the 1604 \latin{typica} show a
comma in its place. The Secret sets two verbs of defence over one object, with an
adverb that has moved. And the Postcommunion holds its
subject back to the end of its clause, behind two objects and a vocative: it is
\latin{doni cæléstis operátio} that is asked to possess, and its
\latin{efféctus} --- not \latin{noster sensus} --- that is asked to go before,
the pointing carrying the grammar, with no comma after \latin{nostras} and a
colon dividing the petition from the purpose clause.

\textbf{What the sources disagree about is which Sunday this is.} The Old
Gelasian gives it no number. Wilson's apparatus reports Rheinau, S.~Gallen,
Gerbert and Menard assigning the Mass two Sundays later than 1962 and Pamelius
one, and the same split reproduces at the adjoining section, so the offset is a
numeration base and not a local accident; the three prayers all five of those
witnesses carry are exactly the three the 1962 formulary keeps. None of the five
was opened here, so that split is Wilson's report of those books. The Hadrianum
heads the section carrying these orations as the Sixteenth while its own margin
carries the chant set 1962 gives the Seventeenth. Honorius calls the day the
Fifteenth. The \work{Liber Comitis} heads it \latin{Dominica mensis vi}, its
count from the feast of St~Lawrence having stopped one Sunday earlier, where
Schuster calls this one the fifth after St~Lawrence and the last of that series.

\textbf{And the chants did not travel with the prayers.} In the margins of
Ottobonianus 313 the chant series and the oration series run two Sundays apart
across five consecutive sections, and the four-element Mass standing under this
Sunday's Introit there --- with the respond \latin{Bonum est confidere} and the
communion \latin{Qui manducat} --- is not the 1962 four; Honorius agrees with
that margin at three slots of four, and his own Fifteenth records three
Alleluias, none of them this one. In the gregorien.info index of the six
earliest Mass antiphonaries Hesbert collected, this Introit, Gradual and
Offertory already stand at \latin{Dominica XV post Pentecosten}, but that Mass
has the Communion \latin{Qui manducat carnem meam} and no fixed Alleluia at all,
so against that record the move runs one way: \latin{Panis quem ego dedero}
stands at the Fourteenth Sunday there and at the Fifteenth in 1962, while the
Communion that record gives the Fifteenth has not taken its place at the 1962
Fourteenth, which sings \latin{Primum quaerite} at Mt.\ 6:33. Every antiphonary
figure here is the gregorien.info database's report of Hesbert's printed pages.
Whether the Ottobonianus respond under this Introit is a chant shared across two
consecutive Sundays or a copying error is unsettled, and the doubling on
Wilson's page is real, his own index printing ``173 (bis).''

\textbf{One change is recent, and one instrument made it.} The formulary prints
one Collect, one Secret and one Postcommunion. Inside the 1960 code that
single-oration result follows from RG 434~b with RG 435, but the instrument that
abolished the seasonal second and third orations themselves is
\work{Variationes in Breviario et Missali Romano} cap.~IV n.~18,
\latin{Orationes pro diversitate Temporum abolentur}. Guéranger's 1909 volume
shows the state before that change, printing the three seasonal orations by
cross-reference, and names the day ``the Sunday of the widow of Naim.''

\textbf{Almost nothing this Mass sings is sung here alone.} Within the tracked
1962 registry --- a repository derivative and not the printed book --- the
Gradual's antiphon and verse range stands at the Saturday of the second week of
Lent, the Offertory at the Tuesday of the fourth and the Communion at the
Thursday of the first, and Schuster states all three identities independently at
printed pp.~139--141 of volume~III of \work{The Sacramentary}; Ps.~85:2, the
Introit's own \latin{salvum fac servum tuum}, is the Gradual of the Ember Friday
in the first week of Lent, and Ps.~91:2 returns as a Septuagesima Offertory. The
same Gospel is appointed at the Thursday of the fourth week of Lent, where the
Missal pairs it with 4~Kings 4:25--38, Eliseus and the Sunamitess's son; Schuster
and Guéranger both name that Lenten Thursday independently, and the Pustet 1862
does not print this Sunday's Gospel at all but cross-references it there. Within
the same registry the Alleluia's verse is sung at this Mass and at no other.
