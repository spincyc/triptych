\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

\subsection*{1. The expositors of the two first-person chants put their weight on the verses the chants do not sing}

The Introit and the Offertory are the two elements in which the assembly says
\latin{I}, and both are cut. The Introit is a three-segment cento --- v.~1a as far
as \latin{exáudi me}, then v.~2b from \latin{salvum fac}, then v.~3 entire ---
with the psalm's ascription and the half-verses about need, poverty and holiness
left unsung. The Offertory takes Ps.~39:2, 3 and 4 and removes the middle of them,
the whole of v.~3 from \latin{et eduxit me de lacu miseriæ} to \latin{direxit
gressus meos}, and v.~4b with it. \textbf{The three Latin commentators who expound
these two psalms put much of their weight on exactly what the chants pass over.}

Augustine's exposition of Ps.~85 hangs its second and third paragraphs on
\latin{quoniam egenus et inops ego sum}, the clause the cento does not sing, and
comes at the sung words through it; at the psalm verse he turns the petition into
an exhortation to the Body, \latin{Certe recte admonet membra sua ut sursum cor
habeant \dots\ cor ut levetur, voluntatem mutat}. Cassiodorus takes the antiphon's
first word for the whole distance the prayer has to cross --- \latin{quod dicit:
Inclina, ostendit quia se ad ipsum extendere non poterat humana conditio} --- and
at the psalm verse assigns the joy asked for to the servant's own part in the
speaker, \latin{ab illa scilicet parte qua servus est}.

At the Offertory the same thing happens more sharply. \textbf{Augustine's fullest
paragraph is an exposition of the verse the antiphon removes}: the pit of misery
is \latin{Profunditas iniquitatis, ex carnalibus concupiscentiis}, and the rock on
which the feet are set is Christ. Of the words the chant does sing he says that
the new song belongs to the new man, \latin{novus homo, dicat canticum novum:
innovatus amet nova quibus renovatur \dots\ Dicimus ergo hymnum Deo nostro, et
ipse hymnus liberat nos}. Ambrose reads that same clause as the New Testament
itself --- \latin{immisisti in os meum canticum novum, quod est Novum Testamentum}
--- and draws its practical consequence, \latin{ut nostra omnia relinquamus,
Christum sequentes, et nostros diligamus inimicos}. Cassiodorus supplies the
philology Augustine and Ambrose do not: the doubled verb is deliberate,
\latin{considerandus est hic sermo geminatus, quia superflua non est tam decora
repetitio}; \latin{Respexit: quia praevenit omne bonum nostrum}; \latin{Deprecatio
quoque significat frequentissimam precem}; and \latin{hymnus} is a Greek word for
praise composed under the law of song, which is why the psalm adds \latin{Deo
nostro}, the pagans having had hymns for their idols.

Cassiodorus's verse numbering runs one lower than the Vulgate's in psalms carrying
an inscription, so his \latin{Vers.~1} at a titled psalm is the Vulgate's v.~2,
and his text reaches this guide through an optical layer of Migne whose
recognition is poor and which mangles individual words.

\subsection*{2. A chant that drops a title and a chant that changes a predicate leave their receptions behind}

Two of the day's chants put the question directly. The Gradual sings Ps.~91:2--3
and not the inscription \latin{Psalmus cantici, in die sabbati} that stands as
Vulgate v.~1; the Alleluia sings Ps.~94:3 with \latin{super omnem terram} where
the Latin Bible reads \latin{super omnes deos}.

\textbf{At the Gradual the reception is anchored in the title the chant does not
sing.} Augustine makes the sabbath interior --- \latin{Intus est, in corde est
sabbatum nostrum \dots\ Cui autem bona est conscientia, tranquillus est} --- and
sets it against a merely bodily observance, \latin{Melius est enim arare, quam
saltare}. Hilary of Poitiers goes further: what survives of him on this psalm is a
tractate on its title and not a commentary on its verses, and he argues that the
rest the title names cannot be the seventh day of Genesis, \latin{nulla hic diei
septimi requies, nullum humanorum operum otium decantatur}, so that it must be
sought at Ps.\ 94:11 --- a verse of the Alleluia's own psalm. \textbf{Hilary's
Latin reaches this guide through a poor optical layer of a scan of Zingerle's
critical edition}, whose running heads and apparatus are badly mangled and whose
page sequence is unreliable.

\textbf{What the Gradual does sing, both Latin commentators read as a double
confession.} Augustine: \latin{In utraque re, et in peccato tuo, quia tu fecisti,
et in bono facto, confitere Domino, quia ipse fecit}; and \latin{psallere} is not
singing but doing, \latin{Opus nostrum, psalterium nostrum est \dots\ Canta ore,
psalle operibus}, the singer to seek \latin{gloriam Dei, non tuam; nomen ipsius,
non tuum} --- which is in praise exactly what this Mass's own lesson forbids at
Gal.\ 5:26. Cassiodorus reaches both halves independently: \latin{nec meritorum
nostrorum esse dicimus quod ipsius miseratione praestatur}, and \latin{Psallere
enim significat piis operibus Domini mandata peragere, ut sicut psalterium de
superioribus sonat, ita operatio nostra ad aures Divinitatis ascendat}.

\textbf{At the Alleluia the substituted predicate costs the element its whole
reception.} Augustine's lemma is \latin{et rex magnus super omnes deos}, and he
builds two developments on the word the chant removes. With 1~Cor.\ 8:5--6 the
gods of the nations are demons, over whom it would be small praise for God to be
great: \latin{Parum enim erat quia terribilis super omnia daemonia Deus: quid
magnum, esse super omnia daemonia?} Then the gods are men made gods by
participation: \latin{Deos dixit participatione, non natura; gratia, qua voluit
facere deos \dots\ Deus verus facit deos credentes in se.} Cassiodorus, read
afterwards on the same verse, also has \latin{super omnes deos} and expounds the
gods the same two ways, \latin{Deos frequenter legimus ab hominibus confictos, ut
Iovem, Martem, Saturnum \dots\ Legimus etiam deos, sanctos viros a Domino
constitutos}. Neither development survives into what the choir sings, so of the seven
scriptural elements this is the one whose appointed wording no witness located
expounds; the three composed orations have no exposition of their text at all.

\subsection*{3. The verb of carrying runs from the two burdens of the lesson to the bier at Naim, and its four expositors disagree who carries what}

The appointed text prints \latin{portáte} at Gal.\ 6:2, \latin{portábit} at 6:5 and
\latin{qui portábant} at Lk.\ 7:14, and one Greek verb stands behind all three.
\emph{Bastazo} occurs twenty-seven times in Nestle's 1904 text, four of them in
Galatians and two of those inside this lesson, and it is the verb Luke gives the
men carrying the bier out of the gate of Naim. Neither the Latin nor the Douay
distinguishes it from the other verbs of bearing. \textbf{So one Mass holds a
command to carry another's load, a sentence saying that one load can be handed to
nobody, and a procession actually carrying a body out of a gate.} The lesson argues
over the carrying; the Gospel performs it.

\latin{Alter altérius ónera portáte} at 6:2 and \latin{Unusquísque enim onus suum
portábit} at 6:5 are one Latin noun used twice; Paul writes two different Greek
words, \emph{baros} and \emph{phortion}, and the Douay follows the Latin and says
``burdens'' and ``burden.'' \textbf{The apparent self-contradiction is what the
Latin tradition on this lesson argues about, and it does not reach one answer.}
Jerome moves the unshareable burden to the tribunal, where \latin{non Job, non
Daniel, nec Noe rogare posse pro quoquam}, and reads 6:1 as a discipline of the
corrector before it is a remedy for the corrected: Paul calls the offender
\latin{homo} and does not call the corrector one, and he wrote \latin{ne et tu
tenteris} and not \latin{ne tu cadas}, because \latin{Vinci quippe vel vincere,
nonnumquam in nostra est potestate: caeterum tentari, in potestate tentantis est}.
Augustine identifies the unshareable burden with the weight of conscience, which no
admirer can lighten --- \latin{Non ergo laudatores nostri minuunt onera
conscientiae nostrae; atque utinam non etiam accumulent} --- and requires the
corrector to answer God first that he acts out of love. \textbf{Chrysostom takes
the passage the other way}: not a discipline of the corrector's conscience but an
economy of complementary weaknesses inside one body, the Church a building whose
stones hold different positions and 6:2 asking the members to ``make it up all of
you in common, by the things wherein you bear with one another.'' Aquinas makes 6:5 the rendering of
account and adds a third mode of bearing another's burden, \latin{pro poena sibi
debita satisfaciendo, orationibus et bonis operibus}, which is his own development
and not the tradition's.

\textbf{At the gate of Naim the verb takes a body for its object, and the carrying
stops.} \latin{Hi autem, qui portábant, stetérunt}, and what is being carried is a
man who can neither share his burden nor bear his own. Neither Latin expositor of
the scene reads the bearers as help: Ambrose makes them the mortal fluxes of
material nature, \latin{cum uel ignis inmodicae cupiditatis exaestuat uel frigidus
umor exundat \dots\ hi sunt nostri funeris portitores}, and Bede makes them
\latin{immunda desideria \dots\ lenocinia blandientium \dots\ venenata sociorum}
and the bier itself \latin{male secura desperati peccatoris conscientia}. Bede's
bier is a conscience --- the very burden Augustine says at 6:5 that no admirer can
lighten --- and at Naim other men are carrying it. Bede's Latin reaches this guide through an optical layer of Migne
whose two-column setting interleaves badly.

\textbf{The Secret names what the lesson told the corrector to fear.} Chrysostom,
on Gal.\ 6:1, arraigns ``the malice of the devil rather than the remissness of the
soul'' in the man overtaken in a fault, and \latin{contra diabólicos \dots\
incúrsus} is what the Secret asks to be defended from, the lesson it follows having
just named the assailant. Augustine reads a pre-Vulgate Latin Galatians whose
lemmata differ from the missal's at nearly every appointed verse, his own at 6:5
reading \latin{proprium onus portabit}.

\subsection*{4. The bier of the Gospel is set beside the bread of the Communion in Aquinas's own sentence}

\latin{Caro} stands in two of this Mass's elements, and the flesh the noun is not
used of stands in a third: at Gal.\ 6:8 \latin{caro} is what yields corruption to
the man who sows in it; at the gate of Naim the flesh is what is being carried out
dead, though the appointed Gospel calls it \latin{defúnctus}, \latin{lóculum} and
\latin{qui erat mórtuus} and never uses the noun; and at the Communion
\latin{caro} is what is given for the life of the world. \textbf{The join between
the second and the third is not a resemblance: Aquinas makes it in his own
sentence.}

Aquinas, expounding the Communion's verse, calls the flesh \latin{organum
divinitatis suae} and states the principle that an instrument acts by the power of
its agent: \latin{cum instrumentum agat virtute agentis \dots\ ita ut Damascenus
dicit et caro virtute verbi adiuncti vivificat: unde Christus tactu suo sanabat
infirmos.} That last clause is the Gospel of this same Mass, where Christ touches
the bier. Cyril argues the same doctrine at length on the Fourth Gospel under the
chapter heading \emph{That the Holy Body of Christ is Life-giving}: the indwelling
Word ``transformed it into His Own proper good, that is life \dots\ rendered It
life-giving, as Himself is by Nature.''

\textbf{The expositors of the Communion's verse then divide over what kind of
eating is meant.} Augustine drives it inward --- \latin{qui manducat intus, non
foris; qui manducat in corde, non qui premit dente} --- and makes the \latin{res}
of the sacrament \latin{societatem corporis et membrorum suorum, quod est sancta
Ecclesia}, which is \latin{omni homini ad vitam, nulli ad exitium} where the
sacrament itself is \latin{quibusdam ad vitam, quibusdam ad exitium}. Chrysostom
allows the clause two references at once --- ``By bread He means here either His
saving doctrines and the faith which is in Him, or His own Body; for both nerve
the soul'' --- and reads the discourse as a pedagogy, ``observe how little by
little He led them up to Himself. Here He says that Himself gives, not the
Father,'' with the quarrelling that follows in the next verse as the type of a
standing failure: ``when questioning concerning the how comes in, there comes in
with it unbelief.''

Aquinas's gloss on the tense turns on the simple future, \latin{dicit dabo, quia
nondum institutum erat hoc sacramentum}, and his lemma at that verse reads
\latin{pro mundi vita}, so neither the gloss nor the exposition of the
universality of the effect transfers to the antiphon's \latin{dédero} and
\latin{sǽculi}: what these witnesses expound is the verse of the Fourth Gospel and
not the antiphon as this book sings it. Cyril's Fourth Gospel commentary is read
here in Pusey's English of the Greek.

\subsection*{5. The word for the Church stands once in this Mass, and the Gospel's expositors do not put her in one place at Naim}

\latin{Ecclésiam} stands once in the whole formulary, in the first prayer of the
Mass: \latin{Ecclésiam tuam, Dómine, miserátio continuáta mundet et múniat}, with
\latin{quia sine te non potest salva consístere} denying her any standing of her
own before anything at all is asked. The lesson closes on the same body under
another name, \latin{máxime autem ad domésticos fidei} at Gal.\ 6:10, which is the
household metaphor of Eph.\ 2:19, \latin{domestici Dei}; Chrysostom reads that
clause as a preference inside a universal charity, grace ``invites both land and
sea to the board of charity, only it shows a greater care for its own household.''
\textbf{The expositors of the day's Gospel find that body inside the scene at Naim
as well, and they do not put her in the same part of it --- and two of them do not
put her there at all.}

\textbf{Ambrose puts the Church beside the bier.} The widow is holy Church, who
calls the younger people back from the pomp of the funeral, \latin{quae flere
prohibetur eum cui resurrectio debeatur}, and ringed by the crowd of peoples seems
\latin{plus \dots\ esse quam feminam}; and she weeps after all, \latin{fleat pro te
mater ecclesia, quae pro singulis tamquam pro unicis filiis uidua mater
interuenit}.

\textbf{Bede puts her on it.} The only son is mother Church, \latin{licet e multis
collecta personis, una sit perfecta et immaculata virgo}, and the city gate is one
of the bodily senses through which death goes out. The restoration of the young man
to his mother is sacramental reconciliation --- \latin{Redditur matri, cum \dots\
per sacerdotalis decreta judicii communioni sociatur Ecclesiae} --- and the raising
is an argument against the Novatianists, who deny that the true mother Church ought
to console. What the Collect asks for the Church, that mercy cleanse and fortify
her, is at Bede's v.~15 a thing done through the priest's judgement.

\textbf{Augustine puts her where Ambrose does and gives her the opposite affect.}
His widowed mother is mother Church rejoicing at each raising, and his three
raisings are three grades of sin: the girl within the house is sin consented to in
the heart, the young man carried out of the gate is sin gone out into the deed, and
Lazarus under the stone is habit, \latin{vis dura consuetudinis}. So the one Church
weeps in Ambrose and rejoices in Augustine, and neither reading is a variant of the
other. Augustine's sermon stops at v.~15, one verse short of the appointed
pericope, and Bede is the only witness located who reaches v.~16 at all.

\textbf{Cyril and Gregory of Nyssa do not read the Church into the scene at all.}
Cyril reads the three raisings eschatologically, as ``a pledge of the hope prepared
for us of a resurrection of the dead''; Gregory of Nyssa reads the sequence as a
graded pedagogy by which Christ accustomed human weakness to believe in
resurrection --- the fever, the nobleman's son, the ruler's daughter, the young man
on his way to the tomb, four-day Lazarus --- and the widow's only son is ``the
stock of her race, the shoot of its succession, the staff of her old age.'' Neither
denies the ecclesial readings and neither is answering their question, so what the
tradition hands on at this pericope is a set of readings and not one doctrine with
variants. Gregory's words reach this page in the nineteenth-century English of the
Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, second series vol.~5, and Cyril's in Payne Smith's
English of an ancient Syriac version.

Four attributions in the \work{Catena aurea} at this chapter have no located text
behind them. The eight-resurrections scheme it gives to \latin{Ambrosius} is
nowhere in Ambrose's Luke commentary, whose complete critical text carries the
raisings at V.86 and VI.64 and the scheme at neither; it may stand in another work
of his, or be Ps.-Ambrose, or be a Glossa attribution. Theophylact's widow-as-soul
allegory, Titus of Bostra's contrast with Elias and Eliseus, and the unnamed
Chrysostom fragment on consolation are the other three, and those three are leads
and not witnesses. A different kind of result stands beside them: Gregory the Great has
nothing on this pericope --- within Luke~7 the \work{Catena} cites him only on the
sinful woman of vv.~36--50, and his \work{Homiliae in Evangelia} treat the chapter
only at Homily~33, on that same passage.

\subsection*{6. What this Mass inherited, and the two places its oldest text has moved}

\textbf{The three prayers descend as a unit, and they are the oldest thing in the
formulary.} All three stand together, as one Mass and in liturgical order, in the
Gregorian Hadrianum under the heading \latin{DOMINICA .XVI. POST PENTECOSTEN.},
and all three stand in the Old Gelasian under \latin{Item alia Missa} with no
Sunday number at all. Between those two eighth-century books and 1962 the wording
moves in two places, and the two books do not move together. Both of them read
\latin{contra diabolicos tueantur semper incursus} where 1962 reads \latin{semper
tueántur}; and at the Postcommunion the Old Gelasian sets the vocative
differently, \latin{possideat, Domine, quaesumus}, where the Hadrianum and 1962
read \latin{possídeat, quǽsumus, Dómine}. So the Hadrianum stands apart from 1962
at one place and the Old Gelasian at two, and the two ancient witnesses differ
from each other at the second.

\textbf{What the sources disagree about is which Sunday this is.} The Old Gelasian
gives it no number. Wilson's apparatus reports Rheinau, S.~Gallen, Gerbert and
Menard assigning the Mass two Sundays later than 1962 and Pamelius one, and the
same split reproduces at the adjoining section, so the offset is a numeration base
and not a local accident; the three prayers all five of those witnesses carry are
exactly the three the 1962 formulary keeps. The Hadrianum heads the section
carrying these orations as the Sixteenth while its own margin carries the chant
set 1962 gives the Seventeenth. Honorius Augustodunensis calls the day the
Fifteenth. The \work{Liber Comitis} heads it \latin{Dominica mensis vi}, its count
from the feast of St~Lawrence having stopped one Sunday earlier, where Schuster
calls this one the fifth after St~Lawrence and the last of that series.

\textbf{And the chants did not travel with the prayers.} In the margins of
Ottobonianus 313 the chant series and the oration series run two Sundays apart
across five consecutive sections, and the four-element Mass standing under this
Sunday's Introit there --- with the respond \latin{Bonum est confidere} and the
communion \latin{Qui manducat} --- is not the 1962 four; Honorius agrees with that
margin at three slots of four, and his own Fifteenth records three Alleluias, none
of them this one. In the gregorien.info index of the six earliest Mass
antiphonaries Hesbert collected, this Introit, Gradual and Offertory already stand
at \latin{Dominica XV post Pentecosten}, but that Mass has the Communion
\latin{Qui manducat carnem meam} and no fixed Alleluia at all, so against that
record the move runs one way: \latin{Panis quem ego dedero} stands at the
Fourteenth Sunday there and at the Fifteenth in 1962, while the Communion that
record gives the Fifteenth has not taken its place at the 1962 Fourteenth, which
sings \latin{Primum quaerite} at Mt.\ 6:33. Whether the Ottobonianus respond is a
chant shared across two consecutive Sundays or a copying error is unsettled, and the doubling on Wilson's page is
real, his own index printing ``173 (bis).''

\textbf{One change is recent, and one instrument made it.} The formulary prints
one Collect, one Secret and one Postcommunion, where three of the
four older Missals tracked here print seasonal second and third orations at this
Mass. Inside the 1960 code the single-oration result follows from RG 434~b with RG
435, but the instrument that abolished the seasonal orations themselves is
\work{Variationes in Breviario et Missali Romano} cap.~IV n.~18, \latin{Orationes
pro diversitate Temporum abolentur}. Guéranger's 1900 volume shows the state
before that change, printing the three seasonal orations by cross-reference.

\textbf{Of the three prayers' own text there is no exposition anywhere the
sources reach.} No Father or Doctor comments on \latin{Ecclésiam tuam, Dómine,
miserátio continuáta}, \latin{Tua nos, Dómine, sacraménta custódiant} or
\latin{Mentes nostras et córpora possídeat} in any tracked source, or in any
patristic or medieval work opened for this formulary. Exegesis of a composed
euchological prayer is not a
genre the Fathers and Doctors wrote in. None of the three occurs in the
Veronense, in Férotin's \work{Liber mozarabicus sacramentorum}, in Bannister's
\work{Missale Gothicum} or in Lowe's \work{Bobbio Missal}, and no source opened
for this formulary dates or attributes any of them: their attested history
begins at the Old Gelasian and the Hadrianum. Every antiphonary figure above is
the gregorien.info database's report of Hesbert's printed pages, and Honorius's
reading of the whole Mass off the September historia of King Josiah is his own
scheme applied to a twelfth-century German use.
