## 1. The brief

### 1.1 What the research establishes about this formulary

Three results govern everything else the lanes returned, and the author should read them
before the element-by-element material.

**The orations are the oldest thing in the Mass and the chants are not fixed to them.**
All three composed orations stand together, as one Mass and in liturgical order, in the
Gregorian Hadrianum as Wilson prints it, section CXXXIIII, heading `DOMINICA .XVI. POST
PENTECOSTEN.`, printed p. 174, manuscript folio mark `[fo. 168v]` (LIT-001, read on the
page image of Internet Archive leaf n231 of item `gregoriansacrame00cath`, and
independently in the hash-verified optical layer at physical lines 14188-14201). The same
three stand in the Old Gelasian (Vat. Reg. lat. 316) at Wilson 1894 Book III sect. XI,
printed p. 230, under the heading `Item alia Missa` and with no Sunday number at all
(LIT-003, COV-007). Between the eighth-century book and 1962 the wording changed in
exactly one place: the Secret's adverb moved, both ancient Roman witnesses reading `contra
diabolicos tueantur semper incursus` against the 1962 `contra diabolicos semper tueantur
incursus` (LIT-002). Against that stability, the chant set is not stable. In the
Ottobonianus 313 margins the chant series and the oration series run two Sundays apart
across five consecutive sections, uniformly (LIT-005, LIT-007); the four-element Mass that
stands under this Sunday's Introit in that margin — Introit `Inclina domine aurem`, Respond
`Bonum est confidere`, Offertory `Expectans expectaui`, Communion `Qui manducat` — is
agreed to, three slots out of four, by Honorius Augustodunensis writing c. 1120-1130
(LIT-015), and it is not the 1962 four. Hesbert's six earliest Mass antiphonaries, as
indexed by gregorien.info, already have this Sunday's Introit, Gradual and Offertory at
`Dominica XV post Pentecosten` (AMS 187) but give it the Communion `Qui manducat carnem
meam` and no fixed Alleluia at all (LIT-017). Against that record the movement is
one-directional: the 1962 book has moved `Panis, quem ego dedero` from the Fourteenth Sunday
to the Fifteenth, and the Communion AMS 187 gives the Fifteenth, `Qui manducat carnem meam`,
has not taken its place at the 1962 Fourteenth, which sings `Primum quaerite` at Mt. 6:33;
the 1962 book's own `Qui manducat meam carnem`, Jn. 6:57, stands at the Ninth Sunday
(LIT-017 for the AMS placements, the tracked registry keyed at §3.3 for the 1962 ones; see
§2.7 and §6.3).

**The chant's psalter is older than the Bible the guide prints beside it.** The appointed
chants depart from the tracked Clementine at eight word-level places — `ad me` and `miserere
mihi` at the Introit, `super omnem terram` at the Alleluia, `respexit me`, `deprecationem
meam` and `hymnum` at the Offertory, `dedero` and `saeculi` at the Communion — and the lanes
between them have now attested four of those eight in sixth-century Latin. Cassiodorus's
lemmata at the Offertory are the antiphon's text word for word — `Exspectans exspectavi
Dominum, et respexit me: et exaudivit deprecationem meam` — and he reads `hymnum` for the
Clementine's `carmen`; at the Introit he reads `Inclina, Domine, aurem tuam ad me` with the
`ad me` the Clementine lacks, and `Laetifica animam servi tui` where Augustine reads
`Iucunda` (PAT-024). Ambrose reads the Offertory's exact incipit and `hymnum` (PAT-023);
Augustine carries `deprecationem meam` and `hymnum` but reads `Sustinens sustinui` and
`attendit mihi` (PAT-014). Two of the three Offertory departures are, on the Greek,
the literal renderings where the Clementine is the freer one: Swete's `dee:seo:s` is a
singular and `deprecationem meam` renders it exactly where `preces meas` does not, and
`humnon` is `hymnum` and not `carmen` (SCR-018). What no lane established, and what the
guide must not assert, is a derivation: no Psalterium Romanum, no Psalterium iuxta Hebraeos
and no Old Latin psalter was collated anywhere in this production, the repository registers
none, and `propers/verified.md` likewise declines the derivation (SCR-026, PAT-024, THE-016,
COV-008). What is publishable is the dating consequence: these readings were current in
Latin before the Clementine and are not chant alterations.

**The Alleluia is the one element with no reception at all, and the reason is textual.**
The chant reads `et Rex magnus super omnem terram` where every Bible witness any lane opened
reads `over all gods` — Swete's `epi pantas tous theous`, the Clementine's `super omnes
deos`, the Douay's `above all gods` (SCR-009). Augustine's Enarratio in Ps. 94 §§5-6 builds
two whole developments on the word `gods` — that the gods of the nations are demons, over
whom it would be small praise for God to be great, and that the `gods` are men made gods by
participation, `Deus verus facit deos credentes in se` — and neither development survives
the chant's substitution (PAT-013). Cassiodorus, opened afterwards on the same verse, reads
`super omnes deos` too and expounds the `gods` the same two ways, which strengthens the
negative rather than relieving it (PAT-024). The consequence reaches four sections at once:
the commentary may not quote Augustine's exposition as commentary on the Alleluia as sung;
the Douay cannot render the line and `propers/verified.md` has already declared that under
Rights; and no English corpus can even be searched for the appointed wording, because
`a great King above all gods` is the Bible text the missal departs from and `a great King
over all the earth` belongs to Ps. 46:3, so any hit under either form is a false positive
(CUL-012). The one positive result is mechanical and exact: the chant's predicate is the
Latin Bible's own phrase, standing verbatim at Ps. 46:3, `quoniam Dominus excelsus,
terribilis, rex magnus super omnem terram`, which an exhaustive sweep of the tracked
Clementine returns as the only place where `rex magnus` and `super omnem terram` collocate
(SCR-009). That is a fact about the Latin Bible and not a route of derivation.

### 1.2 Cross-proper claims settled for the synthesis commentary

Six claims. Each draws together several ritual moments, several scriptural contexts and
several checked reception witnesses; none is a procession through the propers. Evidence
classes follow `guidance/liturgy/roman-1962-propers.md` "Evidence and Claim Discipline".

**C1 — Mercy is what moves first, and the formulary says so four times in four
grammars.** Anchors: Introit, Collect, Gradual, Gospel, Postcommunion. The word is a
petition at the Introit (`miserere mihi, Domine, quoniam ad te clamavi tota die`), a divine
attribute at the Collect (`miseratio continuata mundet et muniat`), the thing to be
proclaimed at the Gradual (`ad annuntiandum mane misericordiam tuam`), and the motion in
Christ that produces the miracle at the Gospel (`misericordia motus super eam`) (THE-002).
The Gospel's initiative is documented and not inferred: Cyril of Alexandria, expounding
this pericope, observes that at Capharnaum Christ was present by invitation but at Naim
`He draws near without being invited. For no one summoned Him to restore the dead man to
life, but He comes to do so of His own accord` (Payne Smith vol. 1, pp. 132-133; THE-001,
PAT-004). The Collect and the Postcommunion deny the Church and the communicant any
standing of their own — `quia sine te non potest salva consistere`, `ut non noster sensus
in nobis, sed iugiter eius praeveniat effectus` — and Augustine's exposition of the
Introit's own psalm makes the same denial the ground of the petition: `Humilem respicit
Deus, superbum repellit` (En. Ps. 85.2; THE-001). Class 1 for the four occurrences, class 3
for Cyril and Augustine, class 4 for the movement. The real competitor is C2, and it is not
dissolved by this claim; two limits belong beside it — `misericordia` and `miseratio` are
different nouns, so the chain is one of sense and not of a repeated word, and `praeveniat`
in the Postcommunion is a plain verb of precedence whose doctrinal freight must be carried
by the witnesses rather than by the verb (THE-002, THE-001).

**C2 — The Epistle's economy of sowing and reaping runs on a currency that is itself
God's gift.** Anchors: Epistle, Collect, Gradual, Postcommunion. The lesson runs an
economy — `Opus autem suum probet unusquisque`, `Quae enim seminaverit homo, haec et metet`,
`tempore enim suo metemus, non deficientes` — while the orations deny any standing of one's
own, and the resolution the tradition supplies is not that the economy is figurative but
that its currency is given. Augustine, expounding the Gradual's own psalm, makes confession
two-sided and says of progress `quia munera ipsius sunt, non merita tua` (En. Ps. 91.3);
the Collect asks that the Church be governed `tuo semper munere` (THE-004, PAT-012).
Aquinas reads the grammatical asymmetry of Gal. 6:8 the same way: `cum agit de seminatione
carnis, dicit in carne sua, quia caro est nobis de natura nostra, sed cum loquitur de
semine spiritus, non dicit suo, quia spiritus non est nobis a nobis, sed a Deo` — and the
asymmetry he reads is present in the appointed Latin as this leaf collated it, `qui seminat
in carne sua` against `qui autem seminat in spiritu`, with the possessive at the flesh and
none at the spirit (THE-004, Super Galatas cap. 6 lect. 2). At Gal. 6:5 he adds that the
burden each carries to judgement is `mensuram gratiae suae ... tamquam manipulos bonorum
operum`. Class 3 for each witness, class 4 for the synthesis. Two limits: `munere` in the
Collect and `munera` in Augustine are the same word doing similar work but the Collect is
not quoting Augustine and no dependence is evidenced, so this is convergence and not
derivation; and the lesson's own resolution, if it has one, is at Gal. 6:8's contrast
rather than in any of these witnesses (THE-004).

**C3 — Flesh sown, flesh carried out, flesh given: the word turns twice inside one
Mass, and one Father turns it in both places.** Anchors: Epistle, Gospel, Communion. The
Epistle makes flesh the ground that yields corruption (`de carne et metet corruptionem`),
the Gospel carries a corpse out of the gate (`ecce defunctus efferebatur filius unicus
matris suae`), and the Communion gives flesh as the world's life (`caro mea est pro saeculi
vita`) (THE-007). The joint is Cyril's and is made from the Gospel side, not the Eucharistic
one: asked why Christ touched the bier when a word would have sufficed, he answers `that
thou mightest learn that the holy body of Christ is effectual for the salvation of man. For
the flesh of the Almighty Word is the body of life ... as iron, when brought into contact
with fire, produces the effects of fire`, and it `annihilates the influence of death and
corruption` (Payne Smith vol. 1, p. 135; PAT-004). The same doctrine is argued at length in
his John commentary under the chapter heading `That the Holy Body of Christ is Life-giving`:
the indwelling Word `transformed it into His Own proper good, that is life ... rendered It
life-giving, as Himself is by Nature` (Pusey vol. 1, pp. 409-412; PAT-016). Aquinas makes
the same inference in his own words and names John Damascene for it — `cum instrumentum
agat virtute agentis ... ita ut Damascenus dicit et caro virtute verbi adiuncti vivificat:
unde Christus tactu suo sanabat infirmos` (Super Ioannem cap. 6 lect. 6; PAT-022) — so the
Gospel's `tetigit loculum` may be set beside the Communion's `caro mea est` on a checked
witness's own sentence. Augustine's Tract. 26 supplies the ecclesial half: `De Spiritu
Christi non vivit, nisi corpus Christi ... Accedat, credat ut incorporetur, ut vivificetur`
(PAT-015). Class 3 four times, class 4 for the movement. Limits: Cyril is read in an 1859
English translation from an ancient Syriac version and no Greek was collated; Augustine's
and Aquinas's lemmata read `panis quem ego dabo` and `pro mundi vita`, not the antiphon's
`dedero` and `saeculi`, so their expositions are of the verse and not of the antiphon as
sung (PAT-015, PAT-022, THE-007).

**C4 — One voice: the singular of the chants and the plural of the orations are the same
speaker.** Anchors: Introit, Offertory, Collect, Secret, Postcommunion. The formulary
shifts person throughout — first person singular at Introit and Offertory, first plural,
second plural and second singular (Gal. 6:1 `considerans teipsum, ne et tu tenteris`) in the
Epistle, no person at all in the Gradual, Christ's own singular at the Communion, first
plural in Secret and Postcommunion (THE-003). Augustine expounds both of
this Mass's psalm-antiphons on one principle. At the Introit's psalm: `sitque ipse unus
salvator corporis sui Dominus noster Iesus Christus Filius Dei, qui et oret pro nobis, et
oret in nobis, et oretur a nobis. Orat pro nobis, ut sacerdos noster; orat in nobis, ut
caput nostrum, oratur a nobis, ut Deus noster` (En. Ps. 85.1), and on `clamavi tota die`,
`non una die. Tota die, omni tempore intellege ... Unus homo usque in finem saeculi
extenditur` (En. Ps. 85.5). At the Offertory's psalm he answers the question directly: `It
is Christ ... sometimes ... in the name of our Head; sometimes also He speaks of us who are
His members` (En. Ps. 39.5) (THE-009, PAT-011, PAT-014). Cassiodorus also expounds both
psalms on the same principle, in his own words — at Ps. 85 `Ex forma servi ... Christus
omnipotenti supplicat Patri`, at Ps. 39 `Ecclesia catholica, quae fuerat de totius mundi
partibus congreganda, patientiae virtutem praedicat` (PAT-024) — so two Latin commentators
state the reading. No independence of Augustine is claimed for him: PAT-024 records only
what Cassiodorus says, and his exposition of Ps. 85 itself opens on the head-and-members
division, `Secunda pro membris suis, quorum ipse caput est`, and cites `doctor Augustinus`
within the same exposition (tracked text, `cassiodorus-ps85-latin.txt`). The orations' own
objects narrow across the Mass while their verbs move from
defence to possession: `Ecclesiam tuam` / `mundet et muniat`, then `nos` / `custodiant ...
tueantur`, then `mentes nostras et corpora` / `possideat ... praeveniat` (THE-003). Class 3
for the principle at both psalms and in both commentators, class 4 for the inference about
the formulary's voices. Two limits: Augustine's lemma at Ps. 85:1 lacks the antiphon's `ad
me` and reads `Miserere mei`, and at Ps. 39:2 reads `Sustinens sustinui` and `Attendit
mihi`, so his exposition is of the psalm and not of the sung words at those places
(THE-016); and the narrowing of the orations' objects may be a property of the euchological
genre rather than of this formulary, a comparison across neighbouring Sundays that no lane
made (THE-003).

**C5 — What the choir sings is not what the Bible on the facing page reads, at eight
places, and the difference is a fact about the Latin psalter and not about 1962.** Anchors:
Introit, Alleluia, Offertory, Communion. `propers/verified.md` records the eight
substantive departures and records that each stands also in the Pustet Ratisbon 1862, the
Venice 1570 and the Vatican typica 1604 — so none of them is a 1962 act. Set against the
witnesses the lanes opened, they fall into three groups. Attested in sixth-century Latin,
four of the eight: `ad me` at Ps. 85:1 (Cassiodorus), and `respexit me`, `deprecationem
meam` and `hymnum` at Ps. 39:2-4 (Cassiodorus, with Ambrose for the incipit and `hymnum`,
and Augustine for `deprecationem meam` and `hymnum`) (PAT-023, PAT-024, PAT-014). Beside
them, and not itself a departure the audit records, stands `Laetifica` at Ps. 85:4, where
Cassiodorus reads with the missal and Augustine reads `Iucunda` (PAT-011, PAT-024). Closer to the Greek than the Clementine is: `deprecationem meam` for `preces
meas` and `hymnum` for `carmen` (SCR-018). Unattested in any witness any lane opened:
`miserere mihi` for `mei` at Ps. 85:3, which Augustine and Cassiodorus both read `mei`
(PAT-011, PAT-024), and `super omnem terram` at Ps. 94:3 (SCR-009, PAT-013, PAT-024). The
Communion's two departures, `dedero` for `dabo` and `pro saeculi vita` for `pro mundi
vita`, answer nothing in the Greek — Nestle 1904 has a simple future `do:so:` and `kosmou`
— and neither is Augustine's or Aquinas's lemma (SCR-020, PAT-015, PAT-022). Class 1 for
the collation, class 2 for the dating consequence, class 4 for the grouping. The controlling
limit is the one C5 must never overstep: `departs from the Clementine` and `departs from the
Psalms` are not the same statement, and no derivation from a named psalter may be asserted
by anyone (SCR-018, PAT-024, THE-016, COV-008).

**C6 — The day's subject is an interval to be endured, and the checked reception reads
every one of its markers as the whole of the present life.** Anchors: Introit, Collect,
Epistle, Gradual, Offertory, Secret, Postcommunion. The vocabulary is unusually dense:
`tota die` (Int.), `mane ... per noctem` (Grad.), `exspectans exspectavi` (Off.), `tempore
enim suo metemus, non deficientes` and `dum tempus habemus` (Ep.), `continuata` and `semper`
(Coll.), `semper` (Sec.), `iugiter` (Postcomm.) (THE-011). Each of the load-bearing ones has
a checked reading that stretches it to a lifetime. Augustine on `tota die`: `omni tempore
intellege: ex quo corpus Christi gemit in pressuris, usque in finem saeculi` (En. Ps. 85.5),
and Cassiodorus, in his own words: `cum dicitur, tota die, totius vitae tempus ostenditur,
ut per multa tempora annorumque curricula quasi unius diei continuus clamor esse monstretur`
(PAT-024). Augustine on the Gradual's versicle: `Mane dicitur, quando nobis bene est: nox
dicitur, quando tristitia tribulationis est ... quando bene est, lauda misericordiam; quando
male, lauda veritatem: quia peccata flagellat, non est iniquus` (En. Ps. 91.4), and
Cassiodorus: `Mirabiliter vita nostra duobus est sermonibus conclusa` (PAT-024). Ambrose on
the Offertory's doubled verb: `non est perfecti exspectare, sed exspectasse; nemo enim nisi
qui perseveraverit usque in finem, salvus erit` (In Ps. XXXIX enarr. §1; PAT-023) — the same
Matt. 10:22 that Jerome and Augustine both reach for on the lesson's `non deficiamus ... non
deficientes` (PAT-008, PAT-010). Aquinas on Gal. 6:10: `dum tempus habemus, id est in hac
vita, quae est tempus seminandi`; Chrysostom on the same verse: `As it is not always in our
power to sow, so neither is it to show mercy` (THE-011, PAT-009). Class 1 for the
vocabulary, class 3 for each reading, class 4 for the synthesis. Two limits: `semper`,
`iugiter` and `continuata` are stock euchological adverbs and carry little on their own, so
the weight belongs to the scriptural markers; and the interior-sabbath reading available at
the Gradual rests on the psalm's inscription `Psalmus cantici, in die sabbati`, which stands
as Vulgate v. 1 and is not sung, so it is available from the psalm and not from the chant
(THE-011, SCR-007).

### 1.3 What the evidence does not support

Recorded here because the constraint is on what may be asserted, and the author needs the
list before drafting rather than after.

- **No compiler's intention, anywhere.** Every lane reached this independently and declined
  it: whether the Introit was cut from the one Davidic psalm in Book III for that reason
  (SCR-001), whether the lesson stops at 6:10 to keep clear of the letter's peroration
  (SCR-003), whether the Alleluia omits the psalm's warning half deliberately (SCR-008),
  whether the Epistle and Gospel were chosen for their shared verb (SCR-021), whether the
  Offertory stands at the offering of the gifts because of Hebrews (SCR-017), whether the
  Gradual and Offertory were chosen for their shared `misericordiam tuam et veritatem tuam`
  (SCR-023), whether the interlock of the Fifteenth and Sixteenth Sundays' Introits was
  arranged (LIT-012, PRE-021). Honorius's reading of the whole Mass off the September
  historia of King Josiah is his own scheme applied to a twelfth-century German use and is
  not evidence about why anything was composed (LIT-016).
- **No derivation of any chant reading from a named psalter.** See C5.
- **No Greek word on the patristic lane's authority.** Everything that lane checked in
  Greek was read only in nineteenth-century English translation — Cyril on Luke from an
  ancient Syriac version, Cyril on John, Chrysostom on Galatians and on John, Gregory of
  Nyssa — and no Greek text was collated anywhere in that sweep (PAT-021). Greek words from
  Nestle 1904 and Swete are a different matter and are printable on the scripture-context
  lane's authority, which did collate them.
- **No unverified catena lead.** Theophylact's widow-as-soul allegory, Titus of Bostra's
  contrast with Elias and Eliseus, and the Chrysostom fragment on consolation are leads and
  not witnesses (PAT-007). The eight-resurrections scheme the Catena aurea attributes to
  `Ambrosius` is **not** in Ambrose's Expositio Evangelii secundum Lucam: a word search of
  the complete Schenkl CSEL 32/4 text for `resurrectiones`, `Sunamit-`, `archisynagog-` and
  `sarept-` returns only V.86 and VI.64, neither of which contains it (PAT-007). It is
  attractive material and it may not be printed as Ambrose's.
- **No quotation from any witness read as Migne OCR or as a page image transcribed by eye,
  without re-reading first.** See §3.2 for which witnesses those are.
- **No claim that Gregory the Great is silent on this pericope, in print, without its
  bound.** The negative rests on the Catena aurea as an index of the Latin tradition and on
  the standard account of Gregory's forty homilies; PL 76 and CCSL 141 were not opened,
  because two attempts to retrieve that Migne volume from Internet Archive returned
  mislabelled items (PAT-019).
- **No AMS datum as Hesbert's own.** See §11.

## 2. Passage-by-passage reception matrix

One entry per distinct appointed passage, then the three composed orations. Each entry names
the propers of this formulary that use it, the direct ancient exegesis checked at its own
locus, the medieval and later saintly reception checked, the works, loci, languages and
corpora searched, how each retained witness is used, and the material negative results.
Where more than one lane reached the same passage the accounts are joined here.

### 2.1 Ps. 85 (Vulg.) 1, 2-3 and 4 — Introit

**Used by:** Introit only, in this formulary. Ps. 85:3 is also the Sixteenth Sunday's Introit
incipit and Ps. 85:1 its psalm verse; Ps. 85:2 `Salvum fac servum tuum` is the Gradual of
lent-1-ember-friday (LIT-012, PRE-004, PRE-021, all against the tracked registry
`src/sources/calendars/roman-1962/propers.yaml`, a repository derivative and not the printed
book).

**Text.** A three-segment centonisation: v. 1a to `exaudi me`, then v. 2b from `salvum fac`,
then v. 3 entire, with v. 4 as the psalm verse (SCR-001, from `propers/verified.md` §1). The
psalm is the single Davidic prayer in the whole of Book III of the Psalter: an exhaustive
reading of the ascriptions of Vulgate Pss. 72-88 in the tracked Clementine returns Asaph at
72-82, `filiis Core` at 83, 84 and 86, Eman Ezrahita at 87, Ethan Ezrahita at 88, and
`Oratio ipsi David.` at 85 alone — and that inscription is precisely the clause the sung
text drops. Book III is bounded by the doxologies the tracked text prints at Ps. 71:19-20
and Ps. 88:53; the tracked file marks no book headings of its own (SCR-001).

**Direct ancient exegesis checked.** Augustine, *Enarratio in Psalmum 85* §§1-6, on
Ps. 85:1-4 (CCSL 39; PL 37, 1081-1090), Latin read at www.augustinus.it, file
`esposizione_salmo_104_testo.htm`. This is the locus classicus of the *totus Christus*: the
Word made head of men and men his members, so that the one Christ `oret pro nobis, et oret
in nobis, et oretur a nobis`; `Inclina, Domine, aurem tuam` spoken `ex forma servi` and by
the whole Body; `clamavi tota die` stretched to the whole age; and at v. 4 `Certe recte
admonet membra sua ut sursum cor habeant ... cor ut levetur, voluntatem mutat` (PAT-011).
Cassiodorus, *Expositio Psalmorum* on Ps. 85 (PL 70), read on the OCR of the Internet Archive
scan of that Migne volume, item `patrologiaecurs27goog`: `Ex forma servi (sicut praefati
sumus) Christus omnipotenti supplicat Patri ... quod dicit: Inclina, ostendit quia se ad
ipsum extendere non poterat humana conditio`; on `tota die`, `totius vitae tempus
ostenditur`; at v. 4, `Iucundari petit animam suam fons hilaritatis et origo laetitiae, ab
illa scilicet parte qua servus est` (PAT-024).

**Medieval, Doctoral and later saintly reception checked.** None beyond Cassiodorus. The
repository's own commentary index ranks Theodoret, Jerome (Commentarioli / Tractatus in
Psalmos), Arnobius the Younger, Bellarmine, Peter Lombard, Nicholas of Lyra, Euthymius
Zigabenus and Bruno the Carthusian for this psalm; none was opened (PAT-021).

**How the retained witnesses are used.** Augustine at C1, C4 and C6; Cassiodorus at C4, C5
and C6, and as the second Latin witness that makes the *totus Christus* reading rest on more
than one commentator.

**Textual cautions that reach the citation.** Augustine's lemma at v. 1 has no `ad me` and
runs on into `quoniam egenus et inops ego sum`, which the cento does not sing and on which
§§2-3 hang; his v. 3 reads `Miserere mei` where the missal prints `mihi`; his v. 4 reads
`Iucunda` where the missal prints `Laetifica` and §6 comments on `iucunda` (PAT-011).
Cassiodorus reads `ad me` and `Laetifica` with the missal but `miserere mei` against it
(PAT-024). Neither of the antiphon's two word-level departures has support in Swete's Greek:
LXX Ps. 85:1 has no pronoun answering `ad me`, and LXX Ps. 85:3 `eleeson me` is a bare
accusative that cannot decide between a Latin genitive and dative complement of `miserere`,
so `mihi` for `mei` is a question of Latin construction and not of what the psalm says
(SCR-002). `propers/verified.md` records that the Douay's `Have mercy on me` answers both.

**Material negatives.** No witness collated anywhere reads `miserere mihi` (SCR-002,
PAT-011, PAT-024). The library holds no registered passage record in any psalm commentary
at Ps. 85 — Augustine's Enarrationes are registered at Psalms 17, 22, 25, 51, 55, 65, 69,
77, 90 and 144 only, Cassiodorus at 16, 24, 64 and 88 — although two already-registered
public-domain facsimiles, PG 80 (Theodoret) and the O'Sullivan Bellarmine, span all four of
this formulary's psalms (COV-004).

### 2.2 Gal. 5:25-26; 6:1-10 — Epistle

**Used by:** Epistle. The lesson is appointed at `roman-1962::pentecost-15` and nowhere else
in either calendar (PRE-007, from `src/sources/commentary/mass-commentary-corpus.yaml`).

**Text and literary context.** The lesson opens on a conclusion whose premises it does not
print: Gal. 5:25 `Si spiritu vivimus, spiritu et ambulemus` draws together the
flesh-and-Spirit antithesis of 5:16-24, and the pericope closes the same figure at 6:8 in
agricultural terms, so the excerpt is bracketed at both ends by an argument only one end of
which is heard stated (SCR-003). Three subsidiary threads carry in: the `invicem` chain
(5:13, 5:15, and the lesson's own 5:26), the law thread (5:14, 5:18, 5:23, and the lesson's
6:2 `adimplebitis legem Christi`), and the stop at 6:10, one verse before Paul takes the pen
at 6:11 (SCR-003). The lesson also begins one verse after 5:24 `Qui autem sunt Christi,
carnem suam crucifixerunt` and ends four verses before 6:14 `Mihi autem absit gloriari, nisi
in cruce Domini nostri Iesu Christi`, so the Mass raises vainglory as a problem and does not
read Paul's answer to it, and opposes flesh to spirit without reading the crucifixion of the
flesh that grounds the opposition. The cross is nowhere in the appointed text of this Mass
(THE-015 — arithmetic on the printed reference and the tracked Bible, class 1).

**Four lexical results, all mechanically checked.** (i) The apparent self-contradiction
between `Alter alterius onera portate` (6:2) and `Unusquisque enim onus suum portabit` (6:5)
is an artefact of the Latin: Nestle 1904 reads BAROS at 6:2 and PHORTION at 6:5, and the
Vulgate renders both by one noun, `onera` and `onus` (SCR-004). (ii) The sowing axiom is a
commonplace of the Latin Bible — 2 Cor. 9:6, Prov. 22:8, Jer. 12:13, Eccles. 11:4 — and its
verse is numbered differently in the Clementine and the Greek, the sowing clause standing at
the end of Greek v. 7 and the start of Clementine v. 8 (SCR-005). (iii) `catechizatur` and
`catechizat` at 6:6 transliterate the Greek verb rather than render it, and the Douay does
not carry the loan over, so the Latin and English columns part company on the one word in
this lesson that names a church office; `domesticos fidei` at 6:10 is the household
metaphor of Eph. 2:19 `domestici Dei` (SCR-006). (iv) The lesson's `portare` is the same
verb, in Greek and in Latin, as the bier-bearers' at Lk. 7:14: BASTAZO: occurs 27 times in
Nestle 1904, four of them in Galatians, two of those inside the appointed lesson, and the
appointed text prints `portate`, `portabit` and `qui portabant` within one Mass (SCR-021).

**Direct ancient exegesis checked, four witnesses at their own loci.**

- **Jerome**, *Commentariorum in Epistolam ad Galatas libri tres*, lib. III, on Gal. 5:26-6:10,
  PL 26, cols. 423-432, read on the page images of the Documenta Catholica Omnia PDF at PDF
  pp. 59-63. At 5:26 he confesses that vainglory infects almsgiving, long prayer, the pallor
  of fasting, chastity in every state and martyrdom itself — `martyrium ipsum, si ideo fiat
  ut admirationi et laudi habeamur a fratribus, frustra sanguis effusus est` (col. 424). At
  6:1 he observes that Paul calls the offender `homo` and does not call the corrector one
  (col. 425), uses the verse against dualist heresy (col. 425), and notes that Paul wrote
  `ne et tu tenteris` and not `ne tu cadas`, `Vinci quippe vel vincere, nonnumquam in nostra
  est potestate: caeterum tentari, in potestate tentantis est` (col. 426). At 6:2 he grounds
  the burden on Ps. 37:5 and closes `Quae Christi lex est? Hoc est mandatum meum, ut
  diligatis invicem` (col. 427). At 6:5 he reconciles the two burdens by moving one of them
  to the tribunal: in this life we may help each other by prayers and counsel, but before
  the judgement seat `non Job, non Daniel, nec Noe rogare posse pro quoquam, sed unumquemque
  portare opus suum` (Ezech. 14) (col. 429). At 6:6 he refutes Marcion's reading of the verse
  as being about prayer (cols. 429-430); at 6:7 he ties `Deus non irridetur` directly to v. 6
  against the disciple who pleads a failed harvest, `Excusatio verisimilis hominem potest
  utcumque placare, Deum non potest fallere` (col. 430); at 6:8 he answers the Encratite
  Julius Cassianus, who read sowing in the flesh as a condemnation of marriage; at 6:10 he
  makes the time of sowing the present life (col. 432) (PAT-010).
- **Augustine**, *Expositio epistulae ad Galatas* §§52-61, PL 35, 2145-2148, Latin read at
  www.augustinus.it. Correction of a brother is the one test of a spiritual man and may be
  undertaken only after examining one's own conscience — `Nihil autem sic probat spiritalem
  virum quam peccati alieni tractatio`; `Numquam itaque alieni peccati obiurgandi
  suscipiendum est negotium, nisi cum internis interrogationibus examinantes nostram
  conscientiam liquido nobis coram Deo responderimus dilectione nos facere ... Dilige et dic,
  quod voles` (§§56-57). At 6:2 the law of Christ is the law of charity, whence `Eadem igitur
  Scriptura et idem mandatum, cum bonis terrenis inhiantes premit servos, Testamentum Vetus;
  cum in bona aeterna flagrantes erigit liberos, Testamentum Novum vocatur` (§58). At 6:3-5
  the burden nobody can share is the burden of conscience: `Non ergo laudatores nostri
  minuunt onera conscientiae nostrae; atque utinam non etiam accumulent` (§59). At 6:7-10,
  `Vident enim seminationem operum suorum, sed messem non vident ... Bene autem seminare, id
  est bene operari, facilius est quam in eo perseverare` (§61). §52 distinguishes the two
  vices of 5:26, `aemulatio` healed by peace and `invidia` by meekness (PAT-008).
- **Chrysostom**, *Commentary on the Epistle to the Galatians*, chapter 6 (PG 61, 673-680),
  read in NPNF1/13 English at www.newadvent.org/fathers/23106.htm. `He says not, in meekness,
  but, in a spirit of meekness, signifying thereby ... that to be able to administer
  correction with mildness is a spiritual gift`; at 6:1 he arraigns `the malice of the devil
  rather than the remissness of the soul`; at 6:2 the Church is a building whose stones hold
  different positions and `fulfil` is really `complete` — `make it up all of you in common,
  by the things wherein you bear with one another`; at 6:6 Christ imposed on the teacher the
  necessity of requiring aid from his disciples in order to repress his spirit; at 6:10 grace
  `invites both land and sea to the board of charity, only it shows a greater care for its
  own household` (PAT-009).
- **Aquinas**, *Super Epistolam ad Galatas lectura*, cap. 6 lect. 1-2 (Marietti para. 87781),
  Corpus Thomisticum. The lesson is an ordered instruction in how superiors treat inferiors
  (6:1), equals equals (6:2), inferiors superiors (6:6). `praeoccupatus` means overtaken by
  surprise and so more deserving of pardon; `in aliquo` excludes the habitual sinner, against
  whom severity is right; Paul wrote `instruite` and not `corrigite` because he speaks of men
  overtaken. Three modes of bearing another's burden, the third `pro poena sibi debita
  satisfaciendo, orationibus et bonis operibus`. Three reasons why charity is the law of
  Christ. At 6:7, the second of two expositions is Jerome's argument almost word for word
  (PAT-017).

**How they differ, and it must not be merged.** Jerome and Augustine make Gal. 6:1-2 a
discipline of the corrector's own conscience; Chrysostom makes it an economy of complementary
weaknesses inside one body, and reads 6:6 as a deliberate institution for the teacher's
humility rather than a rule about stipends (PAT-009). On 6:5, Jerome moves the unshareable
burden to the tribunal, Augustine identifies it with conscience, Chrysostom with self-regard,
Aquinas with the rendering of account. Aquinas's third mode — satisfaction for another's due
penalty — is a development beyond anything in Jerome, Augustine or Chrysostom on this verse
and must be attributed to him and not to the tradition (PAT-017). Aquinas's second exposition
of 6:7 is Jerome's and should be presented as a continuous tradition rather than as two
independent witnesses (PAT-017).

**A citation hazard specific to Augustine on this lesson.** Augustine reads a pre-Vulgate
Latin Galatians and his lemmata differ from the missal's at nearly every appointed verse:
`spiritu et sectemur` for `spiritu et ambulemus` (5:25), the two participles of 5:26 in
reverse order, `in spiritu mansuetudinis intendens teipsum` for `in spiritu lenitatis,
considerans teipsum` (6:1), `et tunc in seipso` for `et sic in semetipso` (6:4), `proprium
onus portabit` for `onus suum portabit` (6:5), `Deus non subsannatur` for `Deus non
irridetur` (6:7), `non infirmemur; proprio enim tempore metemus infatigabiles` for `non
deficiamus: tempore enim suo metemus, non deficientes` (6:9). His §54 argument depends
entirely on his verb `sectemur`. Anything quoted as his lemma must be given in his words and
never silently conformed to the missal's (PAT-008). Jerome's lemmata, by contrast, agree with
the missal at the points that matter — `Deus non irridetur` at 6:7, `considerans teipsum` at
6:1 — which is expected, the Vulgate Galatians being his own revision, so the guide may quote
Jerome's lemma and the missal's text as the same words (PAT-010).

**How the retained witnesses are used.** Jerome, Augustine, Chrysostom and Aquinas at C2 and
C6; Aquinas and Jerome at the resolution of the two burdens; Chrysostom at the Secret's
`diabolicos incursus` (THE-012, on his `arraigning the malice of the devil`).

**Material negatives and coverage.** No registered passage record exists at any verse of this
pericope, though three already-registered artifacts span it whole: Cornelius a Lapide's
Antwerp 1614 folio, PG 82 (Theodoret), and the Corpus Thomisticum Super Galatas. Chrysostom
on Galatians and Jerome on Galatians have **no work record in the library at all** — the
former holds Matthew, Colossians, Ephesians, First Corinthians, John, Romans and the Statues;
the latter Matthew, De viris illustribus, letters 106 and 108, and the Quaestiones Hebraicae
(COV-005). Not opened by any lane: Ambrosiaster, Theodoret's *Interpretatio in xiv epistulas*,
Marius Victorinus, Theophylact, Haymo of Halberstadt, Peter Lombard's *Collectanea*, the
*Glossa ordinaria*, Nicholas of Lyra, Denis the Carthusian, Cornelius a Lapide, William Estius
(PAT-021).

### 2.3 Ps. 91 (Vulg.) 2-3 — Gradual

**Used by:** Gradual. Within the 1962 registry the same antiphon and verse range stands at
`lent-2-saturday` as Gradual, and Ps. 91:2 at `septuagesima` as Offertory (LIT-009, PRE-004);
Schuster states the Lenten identity independently at printed p. 139 of vol. III of *The
Sacramentary* (LIT-009).

**Text.** The chant sings the psalm's exordium and stops. The rest, unsung, runs from the
sabbath inscription at v. 1 (`Psalmus cantici, in die sabbati`) through the wicked springing
up as grass at vv. 7-8 to `Justus ut palma florebit; sicut cedrus Libani multiplicabitur`
(v. 13), `Plantati in domo Domini ... florebunt` (v. 14) and `Adhuc multiplicabuntur in senecta
uberi` (v. 15) — the harvest imagery that answers the Epistle's sowing and that nobody hears
(SCR-007). The appointed text departs from the Clementine only in pointing, which
`propers/verified.md` tables at rows 13-16; nothing here is a word-level variant. The chant's
first word answers the lesson's last: the lesson ends `Bonum autem facientes, non deficiamus
... operemur bonum ad omnes` and the responsory that follows it immediately begins `Bonum est
confiteri Domino`, with the Gradual printed directly below the Epistle at printed p. 396,
right column (SCR-022). Two qualifications on the catchword: in the Greek the lesson uses
`kalon` at 6:9 and `agathon` at 6:10, so the triple repetition is partly the Latin's, and the
chant's `agathon` answers only the second; and the further pairing with the psalm's unsung
palm and cedar is drawn from verses the chant does not sing and is an inference (SCR-022).
`misericordiam tuam et veritatem tuam` as a fixed collocation returns exactly three verses in
an exhaustive sweep of the tracked Clementine — Ps. 39:11, Ps. 87:12 and Ps. 91:3 — and two of
the three stand in psalms this Mass sings from (SCR-023).

**Direct ancient exegesis checked.** Augustine, *Enarratio in Psalmum 91* §§1-4 (CCSL 39;
PL 37, 1171-1178), Latin at www.augustinus.it, file `esposizione_salmo_112_testo.htm`. The
sabbath is interior — `Intus est, in corde est sabbatum nostrum ... Cui autem bona est
conscientia, tranquillus est` — and against a merely bodily observance, `Melius est enim arare,
quam saltare`. Confessing is two-sided: `In utraque re, et in peccato tuo, quia tu fecisti, et
in bono facto, confitere Domino, quia ipse fecit`, with the excuses of Satan and of the stars
each refused. `Psallere nomini tuo` is distinguished from singing: `Opus nostrum, psalterium
nostrum est ... Canta ore, psalle operibus`, and the singing seeks `gloriam Dei, non tuam;
nomen ipsius, non tuum`. On the versicle, `Mane dicitur, quando nobis bene est: nox dicitur,
quando tristitia tribulationis est` (PAT-012). Hilary of Poitiers, *Tractatus super Psalmos*,
`Tractatus de titulo psalmi XCI` §§1-3, ed. Zingerle, CSEL 22, pp. 339ff.: the sabbath the
title names cannot be the seventh day of Genesis, because the psalm says nothing about that
rest — `nulla hic diei septimi requies, nullum humanorum operum otium decantatur` — so the
rest meant must be sought at Ps. 94:11, and the Lord himself loosed the seventh-day sabbath
when he said the priests in the temple profane it and are blameless (PAT-020).

**Medieval and later reception checked.** Cassiodorus on both sung verses: confession is
two-fold, of sins and of benefits, `nec meritorum nostrorum esse dicimus quod ipsius
miseratione praestatur`; `Psallere enim significat piis operibus Domini mandata peragere, ut
sicut psalterium de superioribus sonat, ita operatio nostra ad aures Divinitatis ascendat`;
on the versicle, `Mane significat gaudium, nox autem cognoscitur indicare tristitiam ... Per
noctem vero, id est per tribulationem ... veritatem ipsius similiter annuntiare debemus: quia
merito patimur quod delictis gravantibus sustinemus` (PAT-024). Not opened: Jerome, Theodoret,
Albert the Great, Bellarmine, Nicholas of Lyra (PAT-021).

**How the retained witnesses are used.** Augustine and Cassiodorus at C1, C2 and C6, and
Augustine's `nomen ipsius, non tuum` at the point where the Gradual states in praise what the
lesson forbids at Gal. 5:26 (THE-006). Hilary is used for the psalm's inscription only.

**A bound that governs Hilary.** What survives of Hilary on this psalm is a tractate on its
*title*, not a commentary on its verses. He has nothing on `Bonum est confiteri Domino` or on
`Ad annuntiandum mane misericordiam tuam` — the two clauses the Gradual actually sings — and
he must not be presented as expounding the Gradual's text (PAT-020).

**Textual note.** Augustine's lemma matches the Gradual's wording at both verses, including
`Altissime`, differing only in the comma the 1962 book drops after `misericordiam tuam`
(`propers/verified.md` row 15; THE-016). Cassiodorus's verse numbering runs one lower than the
Vulgate's in psalms carrying an inscription, so his `Vers. 1` at Ps. 91 is the Gradual's
Ps. 91:2 (PAT-024).

### 2.4 Ps. 94 (Vulg.) 3 — Alleluia

**Used by:** Alleluia. `Quoniam Deus magnus` occurs at `pentecost-15` and nowhere else in the
1962 temporal registry (LIT-009). The Fourteenth Sunday's Alleluia is `Venite, exsultemus`,
Ps. 94:1, so this chant continues the preceding Sunday's psalm — Schuster says `a continuation`
and not `the next verse`, and it is not: 94:1 to 94:3 skips v. 2 (LIT-011).

**Text and its literary frame.** Ps. 94 is a summons to worship in vv. 1-7 and an oracle of
warning in vv. 8-11, and the chant takes the verse at the hinge, carrying in the summons and
not the warning. The warning half is what Hebrews quotes at length, returning to `Hodie si
vocem eius audieritis` four times at Heb. 3:7-11, 3:15, 4:3, 4:5, 4:7 (SCR-008).

**The divergence, and the negative it produces.** See §1.1. Nothing further needs restating
except its extent: this is the only element of the ten for which no checked witness expounds
the appointed wording, and no dedicated search for one was made (PAT-021).

**Direct ancient exegesis checked, of the psalm and not of the chant.** Augustine,
*Enarratio in Psalmum 94* §§5-6 (CCSL 39; PL 37, 1217-1234), Latin at www.augustinus.it, file
`esposizione_salmo_115_testo.htm`, lemma `et rex magnus super omnes deos` quoted three times
in §§5-6 and again at the close of §6. First development, with 1 Cor. 8:5-6 and Ps. 95:5:
`Parum enim erat quia terribilis super omnia daemonia Deus: quid magnum, esse super omnia
daemonia?` Second: `Hic accipe homines deos ... Deos dixit participatione, non natura; gratia,
qua voluit facere deos ... Deus verus facit deos credentes in se` (PAT-013). Cassiodorus at
the same verse: `Deos frequenter legimus ab hominibus confictos, ut Iovem, Martem, Saturnum
... Legimus etiam deos, sanctos viros a Domino constitutos, ut est illud: Deus stetit in
synagoga deorum` (PAT-024).

**Not opened:** Theodoret, Jerome, Arnobius the Younger, Bellarmine, Euthymius Zigabenus on
this psalm (PAT-021). No Greek exposition, no chant-book commentary (THE-017).

**How the witnesses may be used.** Augustine's and Cassiodorus's exposition may be quoted as
exposition of Ps. 94:3 as the Latin Bible reads it, never as reception of the Alleluia as sung.
Augustine's §6 reading of the neighbouring `in manu eius fines terrae` christologically, of
Christ joining two peoples, is adjacent in sense to the chant's reading and is a verse the
chant does not sing; it is an adjacency, not a witness, and must not be presented as one
(THE-017).

**Liturgical-history negatives.** The Ottobonianus margins cue this Alleluia at a Paschal
Friday and at the Mass whose Introit is the 1962 Ninth Sunday's, not here (LIT-008). In the
AMS books the verse is recorded at eight places — Feria IV, V and VI infra octavam Paschae,
Dominica II and IV post Pascham, Dominica IV and VIII post Pentecosten, and the general
`De circulo anni` pool in Mont-Blandin, Compiegne and Corbie — and at no Sunday after
Pentecost later than the Eighth, so those books give this Mass no fixed Alleluia (LIT-017).
Honorius's Fifteenth records three Alleluias, none of them this one, and says so himself:
`Alleluia, Domine, exaudi (Psal. xvi) ... Alii cantant Alleluia, Confitemini Domino ... Alii
cantant Alleluia, Paratum cor meum`; this Alleluia stands at his Fourteenth (LIT-014).

### 2.5 Lk. 7:11-16 — Gospel

**Used by:** Gospel. The same pericope is appointed at `lent-4-thursday` in the 1962 registry,
where the Missal pairs it with 4 Kings (2 Kings) 4:25-38, Eliseus and the Sunamitess's son
(PRE-004); Schuster and Gueranger both name the Lenten Thursday independently, and
`propers/verified.md` records the Pustet 1862 cross-reference to it (LIT-009, LIT-020).
Gueranger names the day `the Sunday of the widow of Naim` (LIT-020).

**Text.** Seven mechanically checked results, each exhaustive over the corpus named.

1. The missal's incipit cuts the connective. Luke's `Et factum est: deinceps ibat` makes the
   raising the next thing after the centurion's servant — Nestle 1904 `en to: hexe:s`, `next
   in order` — and `In illo tempore: Ibat Iesus` replaces it while supplying the subject the
   sentence needs. Two consequences follow, both facts about where the excerpt sits: the pair
   Luke makes (a servant healed at a distance for a Gentile who asks, then a son raised at
   close quarters for a widow who does not) is severed, and `mortui resurgunt` at Lk. 7:22,
   six verses after the pericope ends, has no antecedent in the Third Gospel but this.
   `Naim` is a hapax in the tracked Clementine (SCR-010, SCR-026).
2. The narrative is told in the words of Elias at Sarepta, and the identity is exact in the
   Greek: the clause `kai edo:ken auton te: me:tri autou` stands at LXX 3 Kingdoms 17:23 and
   is letter for letter the clause Luke 7:15 ends with. It is where the two verses meet and
   not where both stop: Luke ends there, and the LXX verse runs on past the clause into
   Elias's own words, Swete giving 3 Kingdoms 17:23 whole as `kai kate:gagen auton apo tou
   hypero:ou eis ton oikon kai edo:ken auton te: me:tri autou; kai eipen E:leiou Blepe, ze: ho
   huios sou`. The tracked witness supports the shared clause and not a shared ending, so a
   sentence saying the two verses end alike overstates it and must not be written. **The
   Vulgate breaks the link**, reading `tradidit matri suae` at 3 Kings and `dedit illum matri
   suae` at Luke, so the reader of the Latin column cannot see the quotation and a guide that
   claims Luke is quoting must say it is quoting the Greek.
   Two weaker supports: the gate-and-widow agreement at 3 Kingdoms 17:10 against Luke 7:12
   uses cognate but different nouns; 4 Kings 4 shares the raising and the restoring but no
   verbal formula. The strongest text-internal support for the Elias figure is Luke's own, at
   4:25-26, where the Sarepta widow is the programmatic example of the Nazareth sermon
   (SCR-011).
3. `Deus visitavit plebem suam` at v. 16 repeats the verb and object of Zachary's `quia
   visitavit et fecit redemptionem plebis suae` at 1:68. An exhaustive sweep of all
   twenty-four chapters, run over both the Latin stem `visita-` and the Greek lemmata
   EPISKEPTOMAI / EPISKOPE:, returns exactly four verses and the two sweeps agree verse for
   verse: 1:68, 1:78, 7:16, and 19:44 `eo quod non cognoveris tempus visitationis tuae`. The
   phrase is in the mouth of the crowd and not of the Evangelist (SCR-012).
4. `filius unicus` at v. 12 renders MONOGENE:S. An exhaustive lemma sweep returns nine
   occurrences in the Greek New Testament — Lk. 7:12, 8:42, 9:38; Jn. 1:14, 1:18, 3:16, 3:18;
   Heb. 11:17; 1 Jn. 4:9 — and the Latin split is complete with no exception: `unicus`/`unica`
   in Luke, `unigenitus` everywhere else. The Latin column cannot show the connection
   (SCR-013).
5. `misericordia motus` renders SPLANCHNIZOMAI, which Luke uses exactly three times: here, of
   the Samaritan (10:33), and of the prodigal's father (15:20). The cognate noun occurs once
   in Luke, at 1:78 `per viscera misericordiae Dei nostri` — the same Benedictus that supplies
   the visitation language — and there the Latin does keep the anatomy. So the Naim pericope
   answers the Benedictus twice, at v. 13 and v. 16, and only the second is visible in the
   Latin. `Noli flere` / `nolite flere` returns in Luke exactly three times: 7:13, 8:52, 23:28
   (SCR-014).
6. Lk. 7:13 is the first place in the Third Gospel where the narrator's own voice calls Jesus
   `the Lord`, and the appointed text keeps it: every KURIOS token in Lk. 1:1-7:16 was listed
   and classified, and before 7:13 the word is God in narration or cited Scripture, or a
   character's vocative, or inside a character's speech (SCR-015).
7. `surge` at v. 14 and `surrexit` at v. 16 are the one Greek verb EGEIRO:, aorist passive
   imperative and aorist passive indicative. The Vulgate Deut. 18:15 reads `suscitabit`; its
   Greek was not collated (§3.1 opened Swete's Psalmi, Regnorum III and Regnorum IV only), so
   no identity of verb between Lk. 7:16 and Deut. 18:15 is asserted here, and a guide must not
   claim one on this record. `Propheta magnus` is not a stock biblical title: an exhaustive
   sweep returns only Ecclus. 48:25, of Isaias, and this verse (SCR-016).

**Direct ancient exegesis checked, six witnesses or witness-sets.**

- **Ambrose**, *Expositio Evangelii secundum Lucam* V.88-92, ed. Schenkl, CSEL 32 pars 4
  (Vienna, 1902), pp. 216-218; the running head of p. 217 gives the Migne concordance
  PL 15, 1376B-1377A. Three moves at once: the widow is holy Church, who `populum iuniorem a
  pompa funeris atque supremis sepulchri suarum reuocet ad uitam contemplatione lacrimarum,
  quae flere prohibetur eum cui resurrectio debeatur`, and ringed by the crowd of peoples
  seems `plus ... esse quam feminam` (§89); the bier is wood, and because it is wood the dead
  man already has hope of rising — `spem resurgendi habebat, quia ferebatur in ligno ... ut
  esset indicio salutem populo per crucis patibulum refundendam` (§90); the bearers who halt
  are the mortal fluxes of material nature, `cum uel ignis inmodicae cupiditatis exaestuat
  uel frigidus umor exundat uel pigra quadam corporis habitudine uigor habetatur animorum ...
  hi sunt nostri funeris portitores` (§90). At §§91-92, `quis iste est tumulus tuus nisi mali
  mores? tumulus tuus perfidia est, sepulchrum tuum guttur est` (Ps. 5:10), and `fleat pro te
  mater ecclesia, quae pro singulis tamquam pro unicis filiis uidua mater interuenit`
  (PAT-001). At VI.64, outside the pericope's own place, he explains why this raising is
  public where Jairus's daughter's is private, and reads the two as two peoples: `est etiam
  forma sapientiae in uiduae filio cito ecclesiam credituram, in archisynagogae filia
  credituros quidem Iudaeos, sed ex pluribus pauciores` — marked with `puto` and `est etiam
  forma`, offered as reading and not demonstration (PAT-002).
- **Augustine**, *Sermo 98*, `De verbis Evangelii Lc. 7, 11-15, et de tribus mortuis quos
  Dominus suscitavit`, PL 38, 591-595, Latin read at www.augustinus.it. The three raisings are
  three grades of sin: the girl inside the house is sin consented to in the heart; the young
  man carried out of the gate but not yet buried is sin gone out into the deed; Lazarus,
  buried under the stone, is sin hardened into habit, `Moles illa imposita sepulcro, ipsa est
  vis dura consuetudinis, qua premitur anima, nec surgere, nec respirare permittitur` (§5).
  The four-step analysis behind it is at §6: `Prima est enim quasi titillatio delectationis in
  corde; secunda, consensio; tertium, factum; quarta, consuetudo.` At §2 the widowed mother is
  mother Church rejoicing at each raising. At §6 the loosing of Lazarus is the office given to
  the disciples, the raising staying Christ's (PAT-003, THE-014).
- **Cyril of Alexandria**, *Commentary on the Gospel of Saint Luke*, Sermon XXXVI, in R. Payne
  Smith (trans.), Oxford: University Press, 1859, vol. 1, pp. 132-135. Christ comes uninvited
  (pp. 132-133); the Evangelist supplies two proofs of a real raising, that the dead man began
  to speak and that Christ gave him to his mother; the three raised are `a pledge of the hope
  prepared for us of a resurrection of the dead`, confirmed from Is. 26:19 and
  Ps. 103(104):29-30 (p. 133); and the touching of the bier teaches the life-giving flesh
  (p. 135) (PAT-004).
- **Bede**, *In Lucae evangelium expositio*, lib. II, on Lk. 7:11-17, PL 92, cols. 417-420, read
  on the OCR of the Google scan of PL 92 (Internet Archive item `patrologiaecurs54unkngoog`).
  Naim's topography at v. 11; at v. 12 the dead man carried out is sin no longer hidden in the
  heart's chamber but published abroad, `quasi per suae civitatis ostia propalantem`, and the
  widowed mother is mother Church, one perfect and immaculate virgin though gathered from many
  persons, `licet e multis collecta personis, una sit perfecta et immaculata virgo, mater
  Ecclesia`, each of the faithful being her only son, `singuli quique tamen fidelium
  universalis se Ecclesiae filios rectissime fatentur`, with Gal. 4:19 quoted for her
  motherhood; the gate is one of the bodily senses, closing with
  `Obsecro, Domine Jesu, cunctas meae civitatis portas justitiae facias` (Ps. 117:19); against
  Novatian, who `veramque matrem Ecclesiam ... negat consolari debere`; at v. 14 the bier is
  `male secura desperati peccatoris conscientia` and its bearers `vel immunda desideria ... vel
  lenocinia blandientium ... venenata sociorum`; at v. 15 `Redditur matri, cum ... per
  sacerdotalis decreta judicii communioni sociatur Ecclesiae`; **at v. 16**, `Quia Deus
  visitavit plebem suam. Non tantummodo verbum suum semel incorporando, sed etiam ... semper in
  corda mittendo` (PAT-005). Bede is the only witness located who comments on v. 16, and two of
  his moves are his own and not Ambrose's or Augustine's: the anti-Novatianist application and
  the reading of the restoration to the mother as sacramental reconciliation. The v. 12 figure
  and the word `suae` stand here as the registered Corpus Corporum transcription reads them at
  col. 417C (artifact `wikisource-in-lucam-116db0ca`, hash-verified at evaluation, CON-EVI-035a
  and CON-EVI-040); this entry's first reading, taken from the OCR layer, attached the virgin
  tag to the son and lacked `suae`.
- **Gregory of Nyssa**, *De hominis opificio* 25.6-11 (PG 44, 213-217), read in NPNF2/5 English
  at www.newadvent.org/fathers/2914.htm. The raising is fourth in a graded pedagogy by which
  Christ accustomed human weakness to believe in resurrection — the fever, the nobleman's son,
  the ruler's daughter, the young man on his way to the tomb, four-day Lazarus — and the widow's
  only son is `the stock of her race, the shoot of its succession, the staff of her old age`
  (PAT-006).
- **Thomas Aquinas**, *Catena aurea in Lucam*, cap. 7 lect. 2 (Marietti para. 85817), Corpus
  Thomisticum, assembling Cyril, Bede, Gregory of Nyssa, Chrysostom, Titus of Bostra, Ambrose
  and Theophylact. Three of four tested attributions hold at the locus; one does not (PAT-007;
  see §1.3 and §5).

**Where they disagree, and it must be preserved.** Augustine reads the raisings morally,
Cyril eschatologically, Ambrose ecclesially, Gregory of Nyssa as a pedagogy of increasing
difficulty. A guide that presents the three-raisings schema as one tradition's single teaching
will have manufactured a consensus (THE-014, PAT-006). On the widow the two Latin readings are
not identical either: Ambrose's Church weeps and intercedes for each sinner as for an only son,
and is forbidden to weep because resurrection is owed to her; Augustine's rejoices. Grief and
joy, not one doctrine (THE-013). Augustine's Sermo 98 stops at v. 15, one verse short of the
appointed pericope, and nothing in it comments on v. 16 (PAT-003).

**Bounded absence.** Gregory the Great has nothing on this pericope. On the registered Corpus
Thomisticum page, over Catena in Lc. cap. 7 lect. 1-6 (Marietti paragraphs 85816-85821), no
excerpt heading of Gregory the Great stands at lect. 2; all twelve of his headings — nine bare
`Gregorius` and three `Gregorius in Evang.`, a heading being the plain-text author name that
precedes one excerpt — stand at lect. 6, on the sinful woman of Lk. 7:36-50. The five
`Gregorius Nyssenus` headings, at lect. 2, 5 and 6, are another Gregory and are not counted
with his. The per-author frequency row PAT-019 reported for the whole chapter did not describe
the page and is withdrawn; the Gregory figures above are the count taken of the hash-verified
page at evaluation (CON-CIT-028a). Gregory's *Homiliae in Evangelia* treat Luke 7 only at
Homily 33, on Lk. 7:36-50. PL 76 and CCSL 141 were not opened (PAT-019).

**Coverage.** This is the least-covered element in the library: an exhaustive scan of all 5,127
registered passage records for any locus matching Luke 7 / Lucam 7 / Lk 7 returns **zero
matches**. Bede's registered passages are at Lk. 10:23-37 and Lk. 24:12; Cyril's Luke commentary
holds one passage, sermon 120, on a restricted remote artifact; Gregory's homilies hold homily
22 only; there is no Catena aurea work record of any kind. The nearest already-registered
holding is `artifact.bede.in-lucae-evangelium-expositio.latin-migne-corpus-corporum-web-2026-07-25.wikisource-in-lucam-116db0ca`,
973,336 bytes, all six books in one file with Migne column markers, which contains Bede on
Luke 7 and needs retrieval and a passage record and no new edition record (COV-001). Ambrose is
registered at edition level in the very edition that carries V.89-92 and the library holds only
book X of it, so the decisive Latin witness is a missing retrieval and not a missing edition
record (COV-002). Not opened by any lane: Theophylact (PG 123), Titus of Bostra's catena
fragments, Euthymius Zigabenus, Albert the Great, Bonaventure, Denis the Carthusian, Hugh of
Saint-Cher, Maldonado (PAT-021).

**A citation hazard on Anthony of Padua.** Anthony's sermon on this Gospel is his *Dominica XVI
post Pentecosten*, not his *Dominica XV*: the Centro Studi Antoniani PDF `sd45` opens
`Evangelium in decima sexta dominica post Pentecosten: Ibat Iesus in civitatem quae vocatur
Naim` and pairs the pericope with Eph. 3:13-21, while `sd44`, his Fifteenth, opens `Nemo potest
duobus dominis servire`. A binding made by matching the ordinal would cite the wrong sermon
silently. The whole Centro Studi family is storage- and rights-restricted in the library
(COV-003).

### 2.6 Ps. 39 (Vulg.) 2, 3 and 4 — Offertory

**Used by:** Offertory. Also `lent-4-tuesday` in the registry (LIT-009, PRE-004); Schuster
states the same at printed p. 140 (LIT-009). The Sixteenth Sunday's Offertory takes
Ps. 39:14-15 (LIT-012, PRE-021, PRE-005).

**Text.** The antiphon cuts the whole of v. 3 from `et eduxit me de lacu miseriae` to `direxit
gressus meos`, and cuts v. 4b (`propers/verified.md` rows 28-29; SCR-017, PRE-014). What lies
immediately past the cut is `Videbunt multi, et timebunt, et sperabunt in Domino` (v. 4b) and
`Beatus vir cuius est nomen Domini spes eius` (v. 5) (PRE-015). Two verses further on, the
psalm's verses 7-9 are what Hebrews puts in the mouth of Christ coming into the world to offer
himself — and the Latin Bible disagrees with itself across that seam, the psalm reading `aures
autem perfecisti mihi` where Heb. 10:5 quoting the psalm reads `corpus autem aptasti mihi`.
Swete's Greek Psalter at Ps. 39:7 reads `so:ma de kate:rtiso: moi`, with Hebrews and against
the Latin psalm. Why the Latin psalm reads `aures` was not investigated by any lane; the
antiphon stops at `Deo nostro` and the Hebrews verses are not sung, and no claim is made that
the antiphon stands at the offering of the gifts because of Hebrews (SCR-017).

**The three departures.** See §1.1 and C5. In summary: `respexit me` for `intendit mihi`,
`exaudivit deprecationem meam` for `exaudivit preces meas`, `hymnum` for `carmen`. Against
Swete, the second and third are the literal renderings and the Clementine's are the freer;
the first answers neither Swete's `prosesche:n moi` nor any variant the sweep opened
(SCR-018). Against the Latin commentators, all three stand in Cassiodorus's lemma, two in
Augustine's, and the incipit and `hymnum` in Ambrose's (PAT-024, PAT-014, PAT-023). So the
question SCR-018 left open — where `respexit me` comes from — has a witness even though it
has no derivation.

**Direct ancient exegesis checked.** Augustine, *Enarratio in Psalmum 39* §§2-4 (CCSL 38;
PL 36, 433-450), Latin at www.augustinus.it, file `esposizione_salmo_054_testo.htm`. The
dative is the point: `Ergo et cum malus esses, attendebat te, sed non attendebat tibi ...
Attendit mihi, inquit, id est, consolando attendit, ut mihi prodesset` (§2). The pit of misery
is `Profunditas iniquitatis, ex carnalibus concupiscentiis`, and the rock is Christ (§3). The
new song is the song of the new man: `novus homo, dicat canticum novum: innovatus amet nova
quibus renovatur ... Dicimus ergo hymnum Deo nostro, et ipse hymnus liberat nos` (§4). At §5
he answers the question of the psalm's speaker outright: `It is Christ ... sometimes ... in
the name of our Head; sometimes also He speaks of us who are His members` (PAT-014, THE-009).
Ambrose, *In Psalmum XXXIX enarratio* §§1-4, PL 14, cols. 1109-1110, read on the page images of
the Documenta Catholica Omnia PDF at PDF p. 74. The psalm announces the New Testament, and the
doubled verb is the point: `non est perfecti exspectare, sed exspectasse; nemo enim nisi qui
perseveraverit usque in finem, salvus erit` (§1). At §3, `immisisti in os meum canticum novum,
quod est Novum Testamentum. Gaudentes iam canimus hymnum Deo nostro, quia novarum virtutum
praecepta cognovimus; ut nostra omnia relinquamus, Christum sequentes, et nostros diligamus
inimicos ... Maledicentes ecce benedicimus` (PAT-023).

**Medieval and later reception checked.** Cassiodorus at Ps. 39: `Ecclesia catholica ...
patientiae virtutem praedicat ... Sed considerandus est hic sermo geminatus, quia superflua non
est tam decora repetitio ... Respexit: quia praevenit omne bonum nostrum ... Deprecatio quoque
significat frequentissimam precem`; on `hymnum Deo nostro`, `Hymnus autem Graecus sermo est, id
est, laus carminum lege composita. Et quoniam hymni erant quos idolis suis etiam gentilitas
personabat, addidit, Deo nostro` (PAT-024). Not opened: Theodoret, Jerome, Albert the Great,
Bellarmine, Peter Lombard, Hugh of Saint-Cher (PAT-021).

**How the retained witnesses are used.** Augustine and Cassiodorus at C4 and C5; Ambrose at
C6; all three at C5's grouping of the departures. Note that Ambrose's reason for the doubled
verb — Matt. 10:22 — is the same verse Jerome and Augustine both reach for on the lesson's
`non deficiamus` (PAT-023, PAT-008, PAT-010), a convergence of two texts and not an editorial
construction.

**A note carried from a neighbouring claude leaf.** `claude/52` establishes the Ps. 39 / Ps. 69
doublet with named witnesses: Bellarmine states it (`The remainder of the Psalm is almost word
for word with Ps. 39, which see`), Cassiodorus cites the twin verse with a division of speakers
— in Ps. 39 the words being Christ's in his own person and in Ps. 69 his members' — and
Augustine nowhere mentions the other psalm, a bounded negative verified there against the full
Latin of both expositions. Ps. 39 material stands at three Sundays inside a five-Sunday span
(pentecost-12 Introit Ps. 69:2-3, pentecost-15 Offertory Ps. 39:2-4, pentecost-16 Offertory
Ps. 39:14-15). No leaf in either provider connects this Sunday's Offertory to that work
(PRE-005). Two limits travel with it: the Cassiodorus and Bellarmine loci are that leaf's
readings and were not re-verified here, and Cassiodorus's speaker division is his theology and
not a fact about the psalm.

**Prologue caution.** Augustine's Enarratio on this psalm opens with a long anti-Donatist
prologue at §1 that has nothing to do with the appointed verses; a guide quoting §§2-4 should
not carry that framing over (PAT-014).

### 2.7 Io. 6:52 — Communion

**Used by:** Communion. Also `lent-1-thursday` in the registry (LIT-009, PRE-004); Schuster
states the same at printed p. 141 and adds that the Ninth Sunday's antiphon is drawn from the
same discourse, though his `vi, 52` is loose there, the Ninth Sunday's being `Qui manducat
meam carnem` at Jn. 6:57 (LIT-009).

**Text and literary frame.** The antiphon sings the exact clause at which the Bread of Life
discourse turns from bread to flesh, and only its second half: the half not sung is `Si quis
manducaverit ex hoc pane, vivet in aeternum`, and the words that are sung are the ones that
make the crowd start quarrelling in the next verse, `Litigabant ergo Iudaei ad invicem,
dicentes: Quomodo potest hic nobis carnem suam dare ad manducandum?` The element the excerpt
carries in and cannot state is `et ego resuscitabo eum in novissimo die`, which in the
Clementine/missal series this entry uses stands three times in the chapter, at Jn. 6:40,
6:44 and 6:55, with the same promise made in different words at 6:39, `sed resuscitem illud
in novissimo die`. SCR-019 gives four loci, `vv. 39, 40, 44 and 54`: its `54` is the modern
series' number for Clementine 6:55 and its `39` is that different wording, so in the series
this brief cites the clause itself is threefold, and the offset is the one the numbering
hazard below sets out (SCR-019). Both of the antiphon's departures are Latin-side: Nestle
1904 has a simple future `do:so:` and `kosmou`, so `dedero` for `dabo` and `saeculi` for
`mundi` answer nothing in the Greek. The finding is narrow — these readings are not
translations of this Greek, not that they are errors; `dedero` is a future perfect and both
`saeculum` and `mundus` are live Latin renderings of KOSMOS elsewhere in the Vulgate
(SCR-020).

**Direct ancient exegesis checked.** Augustine, *In Iohannis evangelium tractatus* 26 §§12-18
(CCSL 36; PL 35, 1607-1616), Latin at www.augustinus.it, whose printed heading is `TRACTATUS 26
— Panis quem ego dabo, caro mea est pro mundi vita (Io 6, 41-51)`. The eating that gives life
is inward: `qui manducat intus, non foris; qui manducat in corde, non qui premit dente` (§12).
Only the body of Christ lives by the Spirit of Christ: `De Spiritu Christi non vivit, nisi
corpus Christi ... O Sacramentum pietatis! o signum unitatis! o vinculum caritatis! Qui vult
vivere, habet ubi vivat, habet unde vivat. Accedat, credat ut incorporetur, ut vivificetur`
(§13, on 1 Cor. 10:17). The res of the sacrament is `societatem corporis et membrorum suorum,
quod est sancta Ecclesia`, and it is `omni homini ad vitam, nulli ad exitium` where the
sacrament is `quibusdam ad vitam, quibusdam ad exitium` (§15); the species is made of many
grains and many berries (§17) (PAT-015). Cyril of Alexandria, *Commentary on the Gospel
according to S. John*, book IV chapter 2, P. E. Pusey (trans.), Library of the Fathers vol. 43
(Oxford: James Parker, 1874), vol. 1, pp. 409-412 — the passage quoted at C3, with
Jn. 17:19 read alongside and the heave-offering of Num. 15:18-21 as its figure (PAT-016).
Chrysostom, *Homily 46 on the Gospel of John* (PG 59, 257-260), NPNF1/14 at
www.newadvent.org/fathers/240146.htm: `By bread He means here either His saving doctrines and
the faith which is in Him, or His own Body; for both nerve the soul`, and on the appointed
clause, `observe how little by little He led them up to Himself. Here He says that Himself
gives, not the Father`; and `when questioning concerning the how comes in, there comes in with
it unbelief` (PAT-016).

**Medieval reception checked.** Aquinas, *Super Evangelium S. Ioannis lectura*, cap. 6 lect. 6
(Marietti para. 87463): the flesh is `organum divinitatis suae`, and `cum instrumentum agat
virtute agentis ... unde Christus tactu suo sanabat infirmos`; the bread is the sacrament of
the Church's unity because made of many grains; on the verb, `dicit dabo, quia nondum
institutum erat hoc sacramentum`; `Non dicit autem carnem meam significat sed caro mea est`;
and on `pro mundi vita`, `vita quam confert ... quantum in se est, totius mundi` (PAT-022).
Bonaventure's exposition of this verse is located but not read: *Commentarius in Evangelium
Ioannis* section 81, in the tracked public-domain artifact
`artifact.bonaventure.opera-omnia-quaracchi.1893-volume-6.ia-djvu-ocr-e7f54e11`, at physical
lines 55664-55695, opening `Et panis, quem ego dabo` and expounding `caro mea est` and `Pro
mundi vita`, with section 82 `Litigabant ergo Iudaei` beginning at line 55696 (COV-006). Not
opened: Hilary *De Trinitate* VIII, Irenaeus *Adversus haereses* IV-V, Theophylact, Rupert of
Deutz, Albert the Great, Maldonado (PAT-021).

**A numbering hazard that is live inside the library's own records.** The missal cites the
antiphon as Io. 6, 52, and the tracked Clementine and Douay both number it 52; the Greek
Fathers and the translations made from them number this clause 6:51 and reserve 6:52 for
`How can this man give us his flesh to eat`. The registered Cyril passage id and locus label
read `book-4-jn-6.51-58` in the modern series while `propers/verified.md` cites Io. 6, 52, and
neither record says which series it is using. It is not an error — Pusey's chapter really is
modern 6:51-58 — but a citation carried into the guide from that id without a numbering note
would be wrong by one verse. The offset was checked, not assumed, against the repository's own
tracked bibles (COV-006, PAT-016).

**Textual cautions.** Augustine's lemma is the Clementine's, `et panis quem ego dabo, caro mea
est pro mundi vita`; Aquinas's likewise, and his gloss on the tense turns on the simple future
and cannot be transferred to `dedero`, nor his exposition of the universality of the effect to
`saeculi` (PAT-015, PAT-022). `propers/verified.md` already declares that the registered Douay
answers neither reading and that the guide must print the Douay verse and supply no rendering
of its own; no witness checked by the coverage lane — the tracked Douay, Keating 1806,
Cummiskey 1861, Lasance 1945, Gueranger 1900 — carries a future perfect for `dedero` or renders
`saeculi` distinctly from `mundi` (COV-009).

### 2.8 The three composed orations — Collect, Secret, Postcommunion

**Direct patristic commentary: none, and none of this kind exists.** No Father or Doctor is on
record commenting on `Ecclesiam tuam, Domine, miseratio continuata`, `Tua nos, Domine,
sacramenta custodiant` or `Mentes nostras et corpora possideat`. The repository's own
commentary apparatus cannot supply one because it is keyed to Scripture alone:
`src/sources/commentary/passage-commentary-index.yaml` (schema
`triptych-commentary-work-index/v1`, updated 2026-08-02) carries 562 passage keys, every one a
scriptural chapter reference, checked programmatically. A grep of the whole of `src/sources/`
for the three incipits returns only text witnesses and inventories, never commentary. The nine
works the patristic lane opened comment on Scripture and nowhere touch these prayers. Any
patristic sentence printed beside them must be labelled as illumination of their doctrine and
not as reception of their text. The bound is exact: this is a search of the repository's
tracked sources and of the nine works that lane opened, not of the patristic corpora at large,
and it is correctable by anyone who finds a Father quoting one of the three incipits (PAT-018).

**Documented liturgical reception, which is where their history is.** See §1.1 for the
Hadrianum and Old Gelasian attestations and the one word-order change. Four further witnesses
number the Mass and disagree by one: Wilson's apparatus at Gelasian III.XI records `This Missa
is assigned by R. S. Gerb. to the seventeenth Sunday after Pentecost: see the Missa for the
sixteenth Sunday in Pam., and that for the seventeenth Sunday in Men.`, with marginal citations
`R. S. / Gerb. 175. / Pam. 411. / Men. 179.` — Rheinau, S. Gallen, Gerbert and Menard at 1962
+ 2, Pamelius at 1962 + 1. The page image adds what the optical layers lose: the second Collect
of the Gelasian Mass, `Da, quaesumus, Domine, hanc mentem populo tuo`, carries only `R. S. /
Gerb. 175.` in its margin, so Pamelius and Menard do not have it, and the three prayers all four
numbering witnesses carry are exactly the three the 1962 formulary keeps. The one-Sunday split
reproduces at the adjoining section, so the offset is a numeration base and not a local accident
(LIT-004). Honorius Augustodunensis, *Gemma animae* IV cap. LXXVIII, `Dominica decima quinta,
<Inclina:> sub lege`, PL 172 cols. 719-720, has this Sunday's Collect where 1962 has it — `cui
Oratio, Ecclesiam tuam, Domine, concordat, quam ut Deus mundet, muniat, rogat` — and repeats it
at cap. LXXIX (LIT-013). His Sixteenth offers `Absolve, Domine, tuorum vincula populorum, vel in
Oratione, Tua nos, Domine`, so the Secret's incipit appears there as an oration alternative
(LIT-014).

**A bounded negative over the declared ancient-sacramentary corpus.** None of the three occurs
anywhere in the four non-Gregorian, non-Gelasian books of
`src/sources/corpora/ancient-sacramentaries-2026-08-01.toml` (snapshot
`sha256:af10423188c72d0515bfddb7a357a3e63206581be01f8bb197cd20b743a759dd`, eight optical layers
of six printed critical editions, all eight digests reproduced by refetching for the sweep):
the Veronense in two independent scans, Ferotin's *Liber mozarabicus sacramentorum*, Bannister's
*Missale Gothicum*, Lowe's *Bobbio Missal*. Fourteen distinctive strings were searched after
de-hyphenation across line ends, Unicode decomposition, accent stripping, lowercasing, reduction
to single-spaced alphabetic text and folding of v/u, j/i and ae/oe to e; counts were zero for
every one of the fourteen in all five negative layers, and twelve of the fourteen hit in Wilson
1915 or Wilson 1894 or both, which is the positive control. Vocabulary controls show the
negatives are not scan damage: `incursus` occurs 4, 4, 2 and 3 times in the two Feltoe layers,
the Mozarabic and the Gothicum; `possideat` 1, 1 and 16; `continuata` 10 and 11; `mentes
nostras` 5, 6 and 1; `ecclesiam tuam` 33, 36, 10 and 3 (LIT-021, COV-007). Four bounds travel
with it: it is a literal search over uncorrected optical text and cannot see a paraphrase or a
recentonised descendant; three of the four books are diplomatic editions printing manuscript
contractions; two positive-control strings return zero in the Hadrianum layer for a known reason
(Wilson prints the e-caudata, which the layer renders as a caret, so `celestis operatio` and
`doni celestis` cannot be found there even though the page image shows them, and the same
failure mode would hide the prayer in any of the four negatives); and the corpus's own
completeness note excludes every eighth-century Gelasian, every Ambrosian book, every Gregorian
supplement beyond Wilson, every medieval monastic or diocesan sacramentary and every later
Roman missal (LIT-021).

**Structure, and its defeater.** The objects narrow — `Ecclesiam tuam`, `nos`, `mentes nostras
et corpora` — while the verbs move from cleansing and fortifying to guarding and defending to
possessing and going before (THE-003). `propers/verified.md` reads the Postcommunion's grammar

its `effectus` and not `noster sensus` that is asked to `praevenire`, with no comma after
`nostras`, so `Mentes nostras et corpora` is a single object pair. The defeater is that each
oration's object is largely determined by its ritual slot, and the comparison across
neighbouring Sundays that would test it was not made (THE-003).

**Rubric.** The instrument that removed the seasonal second and third orations from this
formulary is *Variationes in Breviario et Missali Romano* cap. IV n. 18, `Orationes pro

declares. The citation hazard it names is real and visible: the same promulgation carries a

   alia Missa` in an unassigned series (LIT-003). Rheinau, S. Gallen, Gerbert and Menard assign

   apparatus and not this production's reading of those books). The Hadrianum heads the section
   carrying these orations `DOMINICA .XVI.` while its margin carries the chant set 1962 gives the
   Seventeenth (LIT-001, LIT-007). Honorius calls it the fifteenth (LIT-013). The Liber Comitis
   heads it `Dominica mensis vi`, its count from St Lawrence having stopped one Sunday earlier
   (LIT-018), where Schuster calls this one `Quinta post Sancti Laurentii`, the last of the
   Lawrence Sundays (LIT-010). The two reckonings differ by one, exactly as the neighbouring
   Sunday's record already noted.
2. **Ottobonianus 313's date.** Delisle assigns the manuscript to the second half of the ninth
   century and Ebner follows him, but Ebner's later judgment favoured an earlier date. Wilson
   reports both and adopts neither, and neither is adopted here (LIT-006).
3. **This Sunday's Communion.** AMS 187, the Ottobonianus margin and Honorius's Fifteenth all
   give `Qui manducat carnem meam`; 1962 gives `Panis, quem ego dedero`, which AMS and Honorius
   both place at the Fourteenth (LIT-008, LIT-014, LIT-015, LIT-017).
4. **This Sunday's Gradual.** The Ottobonianus block under this Introit and Honorius's Fifteenth
   read `Bonum est confidere`; 1962 and AMS 187 read `Bonum est confiteri` (LIT-008, LIT-014,
   LIT-015, LIT-017). See §5 for the unresolved question behind it.
5. **Transmitted variants of the Postcommunion.** Pamelius reads `iugiter aevi praeveniat`;
   S(2) and Gerbert read `iugiter eius proveniat`; the Vatican manuscript's `sunt` for `sed` is a
   scribal error corrected by Tommasi, on Wilson's own statement (LIT-004). The Old Gelasian reads
   `possideat, Domine, quaesumus` where the Hadrianum and 1962 read `possideat, quaesumus,
   Domine` (LIT-002, COV-007).
6. **How the raisings are to be read.** Augustine morally, as three grades of sin; Cyril
   eschatologically, as a pledge of the general resurrection; Ambrose ecclesially, at the bier of
   mother Church; Gregory of Nyssa as a graded pedagogy of increasing difficulty. Neither denies
   another, but they answer different questions (THE-014, PAT-003, PAT-004, PAT-001, PAT-006).
7. **How the widow is to be read.** Ambrose's Church weeps and intercedes for each sinner as for
   an only son; Augustine's rejoices at each raising (THE-013).
8. **How Gal. 6:1-2 disciplines the corrector.** Jerome and Augustine make it a discipline of his
   own conscience; Chrysostom makes it an economy of complementary weaknesses inside one body
   (PAT-009).
9. **What the burden of Gal. 6:5 is.** Jerome moves it to the tribunal; Augustine identifies it
   with conscience; Chrysostom with self-regard; Aquinas with the rendering of account (PAT-010,
   PAT-008, PAT-009, PAT-017).
10. **When Galatians was written.** The chronology corpus carries five labels spanning nine years
    inside one profile, four alternate and one preferred; see §9.
11. **When Luke and John were written.** Two `disputed` labels each; see §9.
12. **The Catena's attributions.** `Cyrillus in Ioannem` lemmatises a passage that is from Cyril's
    *Luke* commentary; the discrepancy is worth preserving rather than smoothing, and Payne
    Smith's own footnote at p. 135 records that Aquinas and Cramer's catena manuscript attribute
    the neighbouring fragment to Cyril too (PAT-004).
13. **The repository's own records disagree about the orations' English.** See §11.

  range in its own heading. Responsible institution: UK Parliament, official historic Hansard

  `https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1942/jun/23/national-scheme-of-family-allowances`

  Access date: 2026-09-05. Exact usable locus: column 1895, fixed from the page's per-column

  and quotes both verses in the Authorised Version.
- **Wording check.** `Bear ye one another's burdens` and `For every man shall bear his own burden`
  are the KJV forms, and the official report sets them as displayed quotations without quotation
  marks. Appointed Latin: `Alter alterius onera portate` and `Unusquisque enim onus suum portabit`.
- **Context and the turn.** Political, and the turn is that the appointed lesson is treated as a
  self-contradicting statute to be construed. Paul sets the two sentences four verses apart in one

- **Translation and rights.** UK official parliamentary report, published by Parliament in its own
  digitisation; no protected text.
- **Cultural payoff.** Two sentences read aloud four verses apart at this Mass, quoted against

- **Limiting qualification.** Retrieved and read in a digital surrogate — the official historic

## 8. Interpretive-proposal audit

Six proposals, each joining at least two precisely named appointed elements of this formulary.
Every one is selected from a conjunction the `precedent-search` lane actually reached, and each
carries that lane's classification unchanged. The search boundary for every classification is the
one at §3.5, and each is bounded and correctable: none is a claim that a connection is unknown
anywhere.

### IP-1 — Incapacity stated twice in one Mass, once of the Church and once of the person

- **Anchors.** Collect (`quia sine te non potest salva consistere`) and Epistle (Gal. 6:1
  `considerans teipsum, ne et tu tenteris`; Gal. 6:3 `si quis existimat se aliquid esse, cum
  nihil sit, ipse se seducit`).
- **Mechanism.** The same denial made at two scales in one formulary: the Church cannot stand
  safe of herself, and the man who takes his own measure deceives himself. `propers/verified.md`
  identifies the semicolon after `consistere` as the Collect's hinge, dividing the causal clause
  from the petition, so the prayer's own grammar states the incapacity before it asks anything.
  The doctrinal support is at §2.2 and C2: Jerome's `ne et tu tenteris` and not `ne tu cadas`
  (PL 26, 426), Aquinas's `Nihil autem ita frangit hominis severitatem in corrigendo, quam timor
  proprii casus`, and Augustine's requirement that the corrector first answer God that he acts
  from love.
- **Nearest located precedent or analogue.** No leaf in either provider joins this Collect to
  this Epistle. The nearest analogue is `claude/54` P2, `What man cannot add`, an incapacity in
  the Gospel and the Collect answered by a divine supply, with two of four anchors joining
  doctrinally rather than lexically and the leaf saying so; and `claude/52`'s `Sufficiency is
  confessed before it is argued`, an incapacity-then-grace movement built from Epistle and
  orations together. The adjacent version of this conjunction — the two consecutive Sundays'
  Collects sharing the `sine te` grammar — was reached by `claude/54`'s own precedent lane and
  recorded there as not located and not retained.
- **Classification.** **Not located in the checked corpus.**
- **Controlling limit.** `salva consistere` and `existimat se aliquid esse` share no Latin root,
  so this is a doctrinal join throughout and not a word-play, and the ecclesial-to-personal shift
  is the proposal rather than an observation. Whether any checked expositor reads Gal. 6:3
  ecclesially was not reached. (PRE-020.)

### IP-2 — The great King over all the earth, and the town he visits

- **Anchors.** Alleluia (`et Rex magnus super omnem terram`, Ps. 94:3 as the chant sings it) and
  Gospel (Lk. 7:16 `Quia propheta magnus surrexit in nobis: et quia Deus visitavit plebem suam`).
- **Mechanism.** The chant's substituted predicate is a claim of universal scope, and the Gospel
  that follows it puts God at one town's gate; the two `magnus` predications stand four elements
  apart, one of God as king over all the earth and one of a prophet risen among us. The
  substitution is not an invention: `rex magnus super omnem terram` is the Latin Bible's own
  phrase at Ps. 46:3, and an exhaustive sweep of the tracked Clementine returns that verse as the
  only place where the two collocate (SCR-009). The 1962 Seventh Sunday after Pentecost sings
  Ps. 46:3 as its Introit psalm verse, which is the corpus's unused control on the reading.
- **Nearest located precedent or analogue.** The join is not located. For the mechanism — an
  exploratory proposal carried by a chant variant against the Clementine — `claude/52`'s `The
  day's danger is a verb of thinking, and it survives only in the chant's psalter` and
  `claude/53`'s `The Gradual corrects the Introit's verb` are near analogues, twice in the same
  provider.
- **Classification.** **Not located in the checked corpus.**
- **Controlling limit.** Severe and threefold. No checked witness expounds the chant's predicate
  at all: Augustine and Cassiodorus both read `super omnes deos` and build their expositions on
  the word the chant removes, so the proposal has no reception behind its key phrase and belongs
  in the exploratory section and nowhere else (PAT-013, PAT-024, THE-017). Nothing shows the chant
  took the phrase from Ps. 46:3 rather than from a psalter this production has not opened, and
  `propers/verified.md` declines the derivation. And the reading is inherited chant wording
  standing in the Pustet 1862, the Venice 1570 and the Vatican typica 1604, so nothing about a
  1962 compiler may be claimed. (PRE-011.)

### IP-3 — The ear the Mass asks God to bend, and the visitation the crowd reports

- **Anchors.** Introit (`Inclina, Domine, aurem tuam ad me`, with the missal's own `ad me`) and
  Gospel (Lk. 7:16 `et quia Deus visitavit plebem suam`).
- **Mechanism.** The Mass opens by asking God to bend down toward one speaker and closes its
  Gospel with a crowd reporting that he has come. What makes it more than a theme is the added
  word: the tracked Clementine reads `Inclina, Domine, aurem tuam et exaudi me` and the antiphon
  supplies a direction the Bible verse does not, while the Gospel supplies the arrival. The
  Gospel side is unusually well controlled: `visitare` of God occurs in Luke exactly four times,
  established by exhaustive sweeps over both the Latin stem and the Greek lemma that agree verse
  for verse — 1:68, 1:78, 7:16, 19:44 — so the word enters Luke twice in the Benedictus, is
  spoken once by the crowd at Naim, and returns once over Jerusalem (SCR-012).
- **Nearest located precedent or analogue.** The join is not located; `visitavit plebem` returns
  no hit in either provider outside the two `55` leaves. Near analogue for the mechanism of
  joining a chant petition to a Gospel outcome: `claude/53`'s `The voices God is asked not to
  forget are the ten that cried and the one that came back`, which also warns against building on
  a supposed chant intention.
- **Classification.** **Not located in the checked corpus.**
- **Controlling limit.** The addition is inherited chant wording and not a 1962 act, so nothing
  about a compiler may be claimed; `inclina aurem` is common psalter idiom, appointed at
  `pentecost-7` from a different psalm, so its recurrence proves nothing on its own; and the
  appointed Gospel prints `Deus visitavit plebem suam` in the mouth of the crowd and not of the
  Evangelist, so a reading that makes it Luke's own verdict is an interpretation and not the text
  (SCR-012). Cassiodorus's lemma does carry `ad me`, so the addition is older than the Missal
  (PAT-024). (PRE-022.)

### IP-4 — Four editorial operations on the appointed extents

- **Anchors.** Introit, Offertory, Communion and Gospel.
- **Mechanism.** Four different operations on the extent of what is read or sung, in one
  formulary. The Introit is a three-segment centonisation, v. 1a to `exaudi me`, then v. 2b from
  `salvum fac`, then v. 3 entire, with `ad me` added and `mihi` for `mei`. The Offertory cuts the
  whole of Ps. 39:3 from `et eduxit me de lacu miseriae` to `direxit gressus meos`, and cuts

  hoc pane, vivet in aeternum`. The Gospel's incipit `In illo tempore: Ibat Iesus` replaces the
  Bible text's `Et factum est: deinceps ibat`. Each is checkable in `propers/verified.md`; the
  literary consequence of each is at §2.1, §2.6, §2.7 and §2.5.
- **Nearest located precedent or analogue.** Three, none for this formulary's instance:
  `claude/51`'s `The shape of the lesson preaches what the lesson does not say`, three independent
  editorial boundaries in one formulary; the postconciliar `pc-s42`'s `The liturgy disarms the
  psalms it gives the assembly`, three converging omissions; and `claude/54`'s P4, three appointed

- **Classification.** **Near analogue located.**
- **Controlling limit.** All three analogues carry the same defeater and it must travel with any
  reuse: antiphon and pericope extents are inherited chant and lectionary tradition, and nothing

### IP-5 — What lies immediately past the cuts

- **Anchors.** Offertory, Introit and Secret.
- **Mechanism.** Two cuts in one Mass stop one clause short of the same theme, and the Secret then
  prays the word one of them withheld. The Offertory ends at `hymnum Deo nostro`, so Ps. 39:4b
  `Videbunt multi, et timebunt, et sperabunt in Domino` is cut mid-verse and Ps. 39:5 `Beatus vir
  cuius est nomen Domini spes eius` is not reached — while the Introit's own antiphon prays
  `sperantem in te`, so the hope the Offertory stops before is the disposition the Introit already
  names. The Introit's centonisation passes over Ps. 85:1b `quoniam inops et pauper sum ego` and
  Ps. 85:2a `Custodi animam meam, quoniam sanctus sum`, and the Secret asks `Tua nos, Domine,
  sacramenta custodiant` — the verb the unsung half-verse used of the soul, now asked of the
  sacraments.
- **Nearest located precedent or analogue.** `claude/54`'s P4, `The hope-formula lies past the
  cut, and is handed on at another Mass`, is exactly this mechanism: state the cut, state what
  lies past it, and produce another Mass in the collated set that sings the withheld words.
- **Classification.** **Precedent located** (same provider, immediately preceding Sunday).
- **Controlling limit.** Three. `claude/54`'s P4 restricts itself to the exact formula `beatus
  ... qui sperat` and counts exactly two Clementine verses; Ps. 39:5 reads `spes eius` and not
  `sperat`, so it is a hope-beatitude in a different construction and citing it as a third
  instance of that leaf's formula would misstate its count. The unsung matter is not appointed
  text, and a proposal that leans on it presses verses the assembly does not hear. And a
  proposal here is a re-treatment of the neighbouring leaf's movement and must do something that
  one did not — the Introit-and-Secret leg, which `claude/54` did not have, is the candidate.

### IP-6 — Let us walk, and Jesus was going

- **Anchors.** Epistle (Gal. 5:25 `Si spiritu vivimus, spiritu et ambulemus`, the lesson's first

- **Mechanism.** The appointed lesson's first imperative and the appointed Gospel's first finite

- **Classification.** **Not located in the checked corpus.**
- **Controlling limit.** Obvious and severe: `In illo tempore` is the standard Roman Gospel
