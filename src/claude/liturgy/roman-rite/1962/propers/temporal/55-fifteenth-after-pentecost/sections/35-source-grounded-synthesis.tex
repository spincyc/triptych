\section*{Source-Grounded Synthesis Across the Propers}

\subsection*{C1. What the choir sings is not what the Bible on the facing page reads, at eight places in the sung texts, and the difference is older than 1962}

Eight word-level departures separate the appointed antiphons and the Alleluia's
versicle from the Clementine: \latin{ad me} and \latin{miserére mihi} at the
Introit; \latin{super omnem terram} at the Alleluia; \latin{respéxit me},
\latin{deprecatiónem meam} and \latin{hymnum} at the Offertory; \latin{dédero}
and \latin{sǽculi} at the Communion. A ninth stands outside that count, in the
Introit's psalm verse, where the missal sings \latin{quia ad te} for the
Clementine's \latin{quoniam ad te} at Ps.\ 85:4. \textbf{Every one of the eight
stands also in the uncorrected optical layers of the Pustet Ratisbon 1862, the
Venice 1570 and the Vatican \latin{typica} of 1604}, so none of them is a 1962
act. They fall into three groups, and each of the eight into one group only.

\textbf{Four of the eight are attested in Latin by the sixth century.}
Cassiodorus's lemma carries \latin{ad me} at Ps.\ 85:1, and \latin{respexit me},
\latin{deprecationem meam} and \latin{hymnum} at Ps.\ 39:2--4; Ambrose carries the
Offertory's incipit and \latin{hymnum}; Augustine carries \latin{deprecationem
meam} and \latin{hymnum}. Two of those same four are the literal renderings of the
Greek where the Clementine is the freer: Swete's \emph{deeseos} is a singular that
\latin{deprecationem meam} renders exactly where \latin{preces meas} does not, and
\emph{hymnon} is \latin{hymnum} and not \latin{carmen}. At those two places the
chant is the closer rendering and the Bible text the looser one. Beside the four,
and not itself one of the eight, stands \latin{Lætífica} at Ps.\ 85:4, where
Cassiodorus reads with the missal and Augustine reads \latin{Iucunda}.

\textbf{Two of the eight are unattested in the Bible text and in the Latin and
Greek lemmata collated here.} Augustine and Cassiodorus both read \latin{miserere
mei} at Ps.\ 85:3, and no Bible witness and no patristic lemma opened here reads
\latin{miserere mihi}; and at Ps.\ 94:3 every Bible witness and every lemma opened
here reads \latin{super omnes deos}. Nothing in Swete answers the Introit's
\latin{ad me} either, which Cassiodorus's Latin lemma nonetheless carries.

\textbf{The Communion's two stand outside both groups.} \latin{Dédero} and
\latin{sǽculi} answer nothing in the Greek, which has a simple future and
\emph{kosmou}, and no registered public-domain English renders either reading
distinctly. They are not variants a Greek witness can adjudicate but a future
perfect and a second noun standing where the Bible text has neither.

The sixth-century lemmata put four of these readings in Latin before the
Clementine, which is a date.

\subsection*{C2. The strongest joins in this formulary are in the Greek, and the Latin the priest reads hides them}

\textbf{The Gospel tells its story in the words of Elias at Sarepta, and the
identity is exact only in the Greek.} The clause \emph{kai edoken auton te metri
autou} stands at LXX 3~Kingdoms 17:23 and is letter for letter the clause Lk.\ 7:15
ends with, the Greek verse running on past it into Elias's own words. The
Vulgate breaks the link, reading \latin{tradidit matri suæ} at 3~Kings and
\latin{dedit illum matri suæ} at Luke, so a reader of the Latin column cannot
see the quotation and the claim that Luke is quoting is a claim about the Greek.
Luke's own Gospel supplies the control at 4:25--26, where the Sarepta widow is
the programmatic example of the Nazareth sermon.

\textbf{Two words about the widow's son are Greek connections the Latin
dissolves.} \emph{Monogenes} stands nine times in the Greek New Testament and the
Latin splits it without a single exception --- \latin{unicus} in Luke,
\latin{unigenitus} everywhere else --- so the appointed \latin{filius únicus} and
the \latin{unigenitus} of Jn.\ 1:14 are one word in the original and two in the
book the priest reads. \emph{Splanchnizomai}, which the missal renders
\latin{misericórdia motus}, Luke uses exactly three times, here and of the
Samaritan and of the prodigal's father, and its cognate noun once, at the
Benedictus' \latin{per viscera misericordiæ Dei nostri}, where the Latin does
keep the anatomy. So this pericope answers the Benedictus twice, at v.~13 and at
v.~16, and only the second answer survives into the Latin.

\textbf{Three verses past the Offertory's last word the Latin Bible disagrees
with itself, and the Greek sides against the psalm.} Ps.\ 39:7--9 are the verses
Hebrews puts in the mouth of Christ coming into the world; the psalm reads
\latin{aures autem perfecisti mihi} where Heb.\ 10:5 quoting it reads
\latin{corpus autem aptasti mihi}, and Swete's Greek Psalter reads \emph{soma}
with Hebrews and against the Latin psalm.

\textbf{And at the Communion the two languages do not number the clause the same
way.} The missal cites Io.\ 6, 52, and the tracked Clementine and Douay number it
52 with the missal; the Greek Fathers and the nineteenth-century English made from
them number this clause 51 and reserve 52 for \latin{Quomodo potest hic nobis
carnem suam dare ad manducandum?} A citation of Cyril's chapter by its modern
label is wrong by one verse against the missal's reference unless the series is
named.

\subsection*{C3. Almost nothing this Mass sings is sung here alone}

Against the tracked 1962 calendar registry --- a repository derivative and not the
printed book --- most of this formulary's chants, and its Gospel with them, are
appointed somewhere else in the year as well, and the pattern is not random.

\textbf{Four of the chant texts are Lenten in their other life.} The Gradual's
antiphon and verse range stand at the Saturday of the second week of Lent, and
Ps.~91:2 again as a Septuagesima Offertory; the Offertory stands at the Tuesday of
the fourth week of Lent; the Communion stands at the Thursday of the first week of
Lent; and Ps.~85:2 \latin{Salvum fac servum tuum} is the Gradual of the Ember
Friday in the first week of Lent.
Schuster states the Lenten identities of the Gradual, the Offertory and the
Communion independently at printed pp.~139--141 of volume~III of \work{The
Sacramentary}.

\textbf{The Gospel's other appointment reads it against its own Old Testament
figure.} It stands at the Thursday of the fourth week of Lent, where the Missal
pairs it with 4~Kings 4:25--38, Eliseus and the Sunamitess's son; Schuster and
Guéranger both name that Thursday, and the Pustet Ratisbon 1862 does not print
this Sunday's Gospel at all but cross-references it there.

\textbf{The neighbouring Sundays share this Mass's psalm stock.} Ps.~85:3 is the
Sixteenth Sunday's Introit incipit and Ps.~85:1 its psalm verse, so this Sunday
takes the head of the psalm and the next its continuation; Ps.~39 supplies two
consecutive Offertories, this one at Ps.\ 39:2--4 and the Sixteenth Sunday's at
Ps.\ 39:14--15, while the Twelfth Sunday's Introit sings Ps.\ 69:2--3, whose
doublet with Ps.~39 stands in the guide to that Sunday at loci not opened here;
and the Communion's own discourse supplies the Ninth Sunday's antiphon as well, at
Jn.\ 6:57.

\textbf{The Alleluia is the exception.} Within the 1962 temporal registry Ps.\
94:3 is sung at this Mass and at no other, while the Fourteenth Sunday's Alleluia
takes Ps.~94:1 from the same psalm and skips v.~2 between them. The one element
the rest of the year does not reuse is also the one whose sung predicate is not
the Bible's.

\subsection*{C4. The word for the cross stands in none of the ten elements, and Ambrose reads the cross out of the wood of the bier}

\textbf{Ten elements, and the word stands in none of them.} The extent the missal
appoints is cut on both sides of it: the lesson begins one verse after the
Clementine's \latin{Qui autem sunt Christi, carnem suam crucifixerunt} and ends
four verses before its \latin{Mihi autem absit gloriari, nisi in cruce Domini
nostri Jesu Christi}. The Gospel gives a death undone at
a town gate rather than a death died. The Communion sings \latin{caro mea est pro
sǽculi vita} and stops before the discourse says how the flesh is given.

\textbf{Ambrose finds the cross in the one piece of furniture the pericope
names.} The bier is wood, and because it is wood the dead man already had hope of
rising --- \latin{spem resurgendi habebat, quia ferebatur in ligno \dots\ ut esset
indicio salutem populo per crucis patibulum refundendam} (\work{Expositio
Evangelii secundum Lucam} V.90, CSEL~32 pars~4, pp.~216--218) --- so the
\latin{crucis patibulum} enters this Gospel through the \latin{lóculum} Christ
touches, and not through any word the appointed text prints. What the appointed
wording puts where that word would stand is a visitation and three petitions: a
crowd saying \latin{Deus visitávit plebem suam}, and prayers asking that the
Church be cleansed, that the congregation be guarded, and that mind and body be
possessed.
