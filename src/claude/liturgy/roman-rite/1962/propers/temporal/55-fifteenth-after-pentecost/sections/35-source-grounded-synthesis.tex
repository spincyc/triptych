\section*{Source-Grounded Synthesis Across the Propers}

\subsection*{C1. What the choir sings is not what the Bible on the facing page reads, at eight places in the sung texts, and the difference is older than 1962}

Eight word-level departures separate the appointed antiphons and the Alleluia's
versicle from the Clementine --- two at the Introit, one at the Alleluia, three
at the Offertory and two at the Communion --- and a ninth, in the Introit's psalm
verse, stands outside that count. The divergence table in the commentary prints
each of the eight beside the Bible's reading, and the three older Missals the
commentary names there carry every one of them in their optical layers, so none
of the eight is a 1962 act. Set against the witnesses collated for this Mass they
fall into three groups, and each of the eight into one group only.

\textbf{Four of the eight are attested in Latin by the sixth century.}
Cassiodorus's lemma carries \latin{ad me} at Ps.\ 85:1, and \latin{respexit me},
\latin{deprecationem meam} and \latin{hymnum} at Ps.\ 39:2--4; Ambrose carries the
Offertory's incipit and \latin{hymnum}; Augustine carries \latin{deprecationem
meam} and \latin{hymnum}. Two of those same four, \latin{deprecationem meam} and
\latin{hymnum}, are also the nearer renderings of Swete's Greek, the Clementine's
\latin{preces meas} and \latin{carmen} the freer, as the Offertory subsection of
the commentary shows word by word: at those two places the chant is the closer
rendering and the Bible text the looser one. Beside the four, and not itself one
of the eight, stands \latin{Lætífica} at Ps.\ 85:4, where Cassiodorus reads with
the missal and Augustine reads \latin{Iucunda}.

\textbf{Two of the eight stand in no Bible witness and in no Latin or Greek
lemma collated here}: \latin{miserére mihi} at Ps.\ 85:3, where both Latin
commentators read \latin{mei}, and \latin{super omnem terram} at Ps.\ 94:3, where
every witness reads \latin{super omnes deos}; the Introit and Alleluia
subsections carry the collation. Nothing in Swete answers the Introit's
\latin{ad me} either, which Cassiodorus's Latin lemma nonetheless carries.

\textbf{The Communion's two stand outside both groups.} \latin{Dédero} and
\latin{sǽculi} answer nothing in the Greek, which has a simple future and
\emph{kosmou}, and no registered public-domain English renders either reading
distinctly. They are not variants a Greek witness can adjudicate but a future
perfect and a second noun standing where the Bible text has neither.

The sixth-century lemmata put four of these readings in Latin before the
Clementine, which is a date.

\subsection*{C2. The lesson's burden and the bier's load are carried by one Latin verb, and Augustine and Bede put the same thing under it}

The appointed Latin prints \latin{portáte} at Gal.\ 6:2, \latin{portábit} at 6:5
and \latin{qui portábant} at Lk.\ 7:14, and the Epistle subsection of the
commentary shows one Greek verb behind the three where the Greek nouns for the
two burdens differ. What the two elements' expositors put under that verb is the
same thing. Augustine, at the burden no one can hand to another, names it
conscience, and adds that our praisers do not lighten it and may add to it
(\work{Exp.\ Gal.}~§59), the bearing of one another's burdens at 6:2 fulfilling,
on his reading, the law of charity (§58); Bede, at the bier, makes the thing
carried the falsely
secure conscience of a despairing sinner, and its bearers his unclean desires,
the enticements of flatterers and poisonous company (\work{In Luc.}~II, PL~92,
417--420); Ambrose's bearers are the body's own fluxes, \latin{nostri funeris
portitores} (\work{Exp.\ Luc.} V.90). \textbf{So the verb keeps its object across
the two elements and reverses its office.} In the lesson the conscience is a load
each man carries for himself and no admirer lightens, while charity carries the
loads that can be shared; at Naim the conscience is what is carried, by the very
things Augustine says add to its weight, and the carrying runs toward the tomb
until Christ's touch stops the bearers, \latin{stetérunt}.

Two bounds hold the join where the witnesses put it. Each expositor reads his
own passage, and none sets Gal.\ 6:5 beside Lk.\ 7:14: the identity is of the
object each names, not a comparison either draws. And Jerome, Chrysostom and
Aquinas name the unshareable burden otherwise --- the tribunal, self-regard, the
rendering of account --- so the conscience on the bier answers Augustine's
reading of the lesson and not a reading the four expositors share.

\subsection*{C3. Almost nothing this Mass sings is sung here alone}

Against the tracked 1962 calendar registry --- a repository derivative and not the
printed book --- most of this formulary's chants, and its Gospel with them, are
appointed somewhere else in the year as well, and the pattern is not random.

\textbf{Four of the chant texts are Lenten in their other life.} The Gradual,
the Offertory and the Communion each stand at a Lenten weekday --- a Saturday of
the second week, a Tuesday of the fourth, a Thursday of the first --- and
Schuster states all three identities independently of the registry; Ps.~85:2,
the Introit's own \latin{salvum fac}, is an Ember-Friday Gradual of Lent's first
week, and Ps.~91:2 returns as a Septuagesima Offertory. The loci stand at the
four element subsections.

\textbf{The Gospel's other appointment reads it against its own Old Testament
figure.} At the Thursday of the fourth week of Lent the Missal sets this
pericope beside Eliseus and the Sunamitess's son, 4~Kings~4; the Gospel
subsection of the commentary carries the witnesses to that day.

\textbf{The neighbouring Sundays share this Mass's psalm stock.} The Sixteenth
Sunday takes both the Introit's psalm and the Offertory's up where this Sunday
leaves them --- Ps.~85 at v.~3 with v.~1 as its verse, Ps.~39 at vv.~14--15 ---
so this Mass sings the head of both psalms and the next their continuation; the
Twelfth Sunday's Introit sings Ps.~69, whose doublet with Ps.~39 stands in the
guide to that Sunday at loci not opened here; and the Ninth Sunday's Communion,
Jn.\ 6:57, comes from the same discourse as this one's. Shared psalm stock
across neighbouring Sundays is what a chant repertory produces of itself.

\textbf{The Alleluia is the exception.} Within the 1962 temporal registry Ps.\
94:3 is sung at this Mass and at no other, while the Fourteenth Sunday's Alleluia
takes Ps.~94:1 from the same psalm and skips v.~2 between them. The one element
the rest of the year does not reuse is also the one whose sung predicate is not
the Bible's.

\subsection*{C4. The word for the cross stands in none of the ten elements, and the expositors of the bier supply the cross, the life-giving flesh and the sin}

\textbf{Ten elements, and the word stands in none of them.} The extent the missal
appoints is cut on both sides of it: the lesson opens one verse after the
letter's \latin{crucifixerunt} at Gal.\ 5:24 and closes four verses before its
\latin{in cruce} at 6:14, as the Epistle subsection of the commentary sets out.
The Gospel gives a death undone at a town gate rather than a death died. The
Communion sings \latin{caro mea est pro sǽculi vita} and stops before the
discourse says how the flesh is given.

\textbf{What the appointed wording withholds, the expositors of the one scene
supply, each from the bier.} Ambrose reads the wood: the dead man carried
\latin{in ligno} already had hope of rising, the wood being a sign of salvation
\latin{per crucis patibulum} (\work{Exp.\ Luc.} V.90), so the cross enters this
Mass through the \latin{lóculum} Christ touches and through no word the missal
prints. Cyril reads the touch as instruction in the life-giving flesh, the
doctrine the third unit of the thematic movement sets out from his sentence and
Aquinas's. Augustine and Bede read the
death: the young man carried out of the gate is sin gone out into the deed, the
middle of the three raisings (\work{Sermo}~98 §5), and the bier is the sinner's
falsely secure conscience, the restoration to the mother his return to the
Church's communion by priestly judgement (\work{In Luc.}~II, PL~92, 417--420).
So the cross reaches this Mass as the wood under the corpse; the flesh that
touches the bier is, on Cyril's and Aquinas's showing, the flesh the Communion
sings; and the death undone is read as sin. What the appointed wording itself
puts where the word would stand is a visitation and three petitions --- a crowd
saying \latin{Deus visitávit plebem suam}, and prayers asking that the Church be
cleansed, that the congregation be guarded, and that mind and body be possessed.
Ambrose alone names the cross; Augustine's sermon stops at v.~15 and reads no
crowd; and Cyril's Luke reaches this page in an English of a Syriac version.
