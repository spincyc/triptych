## 2. Literary context of each appointed passage

Each passage was read in the Clementine and the Douay over the stated span.

- **Apoc 5:12 (Introit)** in Apoc 4–5: the throne vision, the sealed book that
  no one in heaven or on earth is worthy to open, the Lion of Juda who has
  prevailed and is seen as a Lamb standing as slain (5:5–6), the new canticle
  of the elders for a Lamb who redeemed men from every nation and made them a
  kingdom and priests who shall reign on the earth (5:9–10), then the angels'
  sevenfold acclamation (5:11–12) and every creature's (5:13). The Introit
  takes v. 12 without its last two nouns.
- **Apoc 1:6 (Introit)** in Apoc 1:4–8: the greeting from Jesus Christ, the
  faithful witness, the first-begotten of the dead and the prince of the kings
  of the earth, who loved us and washed us in his blood (1:5); and made us a
  kingdom and priests (1:6a); to him glory and empire (1:6b), which alone the
  Introit takes. John places his visions on the island called Patmos (1:9).
- **Ps 71 (Introit verse; Gradual vv. 8, 11)** whole: a prayer for the king and
  the king's son, his justice to the poor, a peace as long as the moon, a
  dominion from sea to sea, the homage of the kings of Tharsis, Arabia and
  Saba, the deliverance of the needy, the blessing of all nations in him; the
  Vulgate title *in Salomonem* and the closing *Defecerunt laudes David, filii
  Jesse* (v. 20).
- **Col 1:9–23 (Epistle 1:12–20)**: Paul's prayer for the Colossians (vv.
  9–11); the thanksgiving for deliverance from the power of darkness and
  translation into the kingdom of the Son (12–14); the Son as image, firstborn
  of every creature, in whom, through whom and unto whom all things were
  made (15–17); head of the body, the Church, firstborn from the dead (18);
  the fulness, and the reconciliation of all things through the blood of his
  cross (19–20); its application to the Colossians, once enemies (21–23).
  Paul writes in bonds (4:3, 18) to the saints at Colossa (1:2).
- **Dan 7:9–27 (Alleluia 7:14b)**: dated to the first year of Baltasar
  (7:1); four beasts; the Ancient of days and the judgment; one like a son of
  man brought before him and given power, honour and a kingdom, an everlasting
  power and an indestructible kingdom (13–14); the interpretation, in which the
  saints of the Most High receive the kingdom for ever (17–18, 27). The
  Alleluia takes only the second half of v. 14.
- **Jn 18:28–19:16 (Gospel 18:33–37)**: the delivery to Pilate at the
  praetorium, the governor's hall, on the morning before the Pasch (18:28); the
  first interrogation inside it (33–37), *What is truth?* (38), Barabbas
  (39–40), the scourging and mock coronation (19:1–3), *Ecce homo*, *We have
  no king but Caesar*, the handing over (19:4–16). The pericope ends at 37,
  before Pilate's question.
- **Ps 2 (Offertory v. 8)** whole: the nations' rage against the Lord and his
  Christ, the derision of him who dwells in heaven, the king set on Sion, the
  decree *Filius meus es tu, ego hodie genui te* (v. 7), *Postula a me* (v. 8),
  the rod of iron (v. 9), and the warning to the kings, *Et nunc, reges,
  intelligite … Servite Domino in timore* (vv. 10–11). Acts 4:25 ascribes the
  psalm to David.
- **Ps 28 (Communion vv. 10–11)** whole: *Psalmus David, in consummatione
  tabernaculi*; the sevenfold voice of the Lord over the waters, in the cedars,
  the desert and the woods; *Dominus diluvium inhabitare facit, et sedebit
  Dominus rex in aeternum. Dominus virtutem populo suo dabit; Dominus benedicet
  populo suo in pace.* The Communion takes the second half of each verse.
- **Not said: Ps 88:20–38 (Tract 27–28, 30)**, the oath to David and his seed,
  firstborn and highest of the kings of the earth (title *Intellectus Ethan
  Ezrahitae*); **Apoc 19:11–16 (paschal Alleluia 19:16)**, the rider called
  Faithful and True with the name written on his garment and thigh.

### 3.1 Liturgical commentators

`commentary-work-index formulary --calendar roman-1962 --mass christ-the-king`
compared 40 loci on their elements and listed none: no liturgical commentator
in `src/sources/commentary/formulary-loci.yaml` has this formulary. That is
expected of the medieval expositors (Rupert, Honorius, Sicard, Durandus) and of
Guéranger, who predate a Mass composed in 1925.

**Second search: Schuster.** The library registers the English *Sacramentary*
(`edition.ildefonso-schuster.the-sacramentary.burns-oates-washbourne-english-1927`)
but holds only volume III, which has no treatment of this Mass. The Internet
Archive item behind that record (`LiberSacramentorum`) carries all five
volumes. Each volume's OCR was fetched whole and matched to the item's SHA-1
(volume III's SHA-256 also matches the registered artifact
`…ia-volume-3-ocr-4d8c8988`), and each was searched for *Christ the King*,
*Kingship of Christ*, *Quas primas*, *Christi Regis* and the Introit's English.
Volumes I–IV have no hit. **Volume V** (Parts VIII and IX, *The Saints in the
Mystery of the Redemption*; Burns Oates & Washbourne, London, *First published
1930*, imprimatur 26 June 1930; translated by Arthur Levelis-Marke, completed by
Mrs W. Fairfax-Cholmeley) carries the chapter *Sunday before the Feast of All
Saints: Feast of the Kingship of Our Lord Jesus Christ*, printed **pp. 200–204**,
read on the volume's page images (PDF pp. 220–224 of the volume PDF, SHA-256
`016b212d…9d00`, SHA-1 matched to the item listing), with the OCR (SHA-256
`65192aa1…9b11`) as text; re-read on the same OCR in iteration 2. It is a
liturgical commentary on this Mass as a Mass, **element by element**: after an
introduction, each element is quoted or named and commented on in its own
paragraph or paragraphs, in the Missal's order.

- *Introduction*: the feast was instituted against *laicisme*, which denies
  the supremacy of God and the Church over society and the State, as *a
  protest and an act of reparation*, with Ps 2:2 (*reges terrae et principes
  … adversus Dominum et adversus Christum eius*); the date was set on the
  Sunday before All Saints to join the feast to the heavenly Jerusalem and the
  court of the King (pp. 200–201);
- **Introit** (p. 201): the exaltation is, *according to St Paul*, *in
  proportion to the humiliation*, and John recalls the Passion (*immolated and
  slain*) before the Lamb's glory;
- **Introit verse** (pp. 201–202): Ps 71:1 is *distinctly Messianic*, *the
  Messianic office conferred upon Jesus*, who will govern *until … he shall
  have conquered the enemy*, then *lay at his Father's feet the sceptre and the
  crown*;
- **Collect** (p. 202): *inspired by the words of St Paul*; sin is a
  *disruptive force* that ruined the unity of the race, restored through
  Christ the new Adam;
- **Epistle** (p. 202): Christ's primacy over all creation, angels included,
  *founded on the hypostatic union*, and over the Church as head;
- **Gradual** (p. 202): the prophecy is being fulfilled as the Church extends
  her peaceful conquests; the kingdom is *complete and absolute de jure, but …
  opposed and incomplete de facto*, and will be entire at the judgment;
- **Alleluia** (pp. 202–203): its partial accomplishment is visible in the
  Church's two thousand years against the empires that have passed;
- **Gradual and Alleluia together** (p. 203), *the double chant after the
  Epistle*: a threefold catholicity of place (*a mari usque ad mare*), of
  subjects (*omnes reges … omnes gentes*, kings and *wretched pariahs* alike)
  and of ages (*potestas aeterna … non auferetur*);
- **Gospel**: *regnum meum non est hinc*, *but it comprehends the world*;
  *Jesus does not come to dethrone the sovereigns of the earth*, but gives the
  laws of truth and justice to rulers and subjects; it is *the obvious task* of
  those who govern to help the Church *always of course within the bounds of
  civil authority*; and the medieval *supremacy of the Catholic Church and the
  Pope over nations and their monarchs*, under which Popes deposed unworthy
  kings (p. 203);
- **Offertory** (p. 203): to *the nations and the kings who rise up against*
  his design (Ps 2:1–2), Christ shows Ps 2:8 as *the solemn deed of investiture
  which he has received from his Father*;
- **Secret** (p. 204): its text is given without comment;
- **Preface** (p. 204): its conception of Christ as Pontiff and King *who
  reconquers and restores to his Heavenly Father the realm of creation … that
  God might eternally be all in all* is *borrowed from St Paul*; *the
  compiler* gave it the lyrical character of the early eucharistic *Hymnus*;
- **Communion** (p. 204): the antiphon is *a prelude to the final effect of the
  eucharistic Sacrifice which ends with the divine blessing*; *At the moment of
  Holy Communion Jesus is enthroned in the faithful heart*; he is the Blessed
  through whom, by the promise to Abraham, all nations are blessed
  (Gen 22:18);
- **Postcommunion** (p. 204): *The Royal Standard of Jesus Christ is the Cross:
  Dicite in nationibus: regnavit a ligno Deus; for the kingdom of Christ is
  that of obedience, humility and sacrifice*.

**What Schuster joins himself, and what he does not.** The one connection
between two elements that Schuster makes in his own words is the Gradual with
the Alleluia, *the double chant after the Epistle* (p. 203). No sentence of his
relates any other two elements. His Offertory paragraph looks back to the
hostile kings of Ps 2:1–2, which his introduction quotes (p. 201), not to the
Introit; his Introit-verse, Preface and Communion paragraphs never refer to one
another, and his Communion paragraph ties the antiphon only to the Sacrifice's
final blessing and to the promise to Abraham; his Postcommunion note does not
mention the Gospel or its servants who do not fight. Every other grouping of
his comments in `research/interpretations.md` is editorial synthesis over
separate element comments, and is labelled so there.

Schuster is Blessed (§4): he may be one of a reading's two authors where the
other is a Father or canonized saint, and he is the one witness read to this
Mass as a Mass. His p. 203 paragraph on papal deposition of kings is his own
1930 historical claim; no reading here adopts it, and the studies must not
present it as the sense of the Gospel or the formulary (§6.9). The asterisk on
the chapter heading has no footnote on the pages read. His Italian original
was not sought; the English is the witness read.

The instituting act, *Quas primas* (§3.13), is an official act and not a
commentator.

### 3.2 Apoc 5:12 — Introit

- *Ancient direct exegesis.* **Victorinus of Pettau** [no standing row],
  *Commentary on the Apocalypse* on 5:2–5 (ANF VII text at New Advent, page
  0712, read whole): to open the book is to overcome death for man; none was
  found worthy save Christ, the Lamb as it were slain; because he overcame
  death as testator it was just that he be appointed heir and possess the
  substance of the dying man, the human members. He does not gloss 5:12 by
  itself. **Bede**, *Explanatio Apocalypsis* I on 5:5–14 (Marshall's English of
  1878, Internet Archive OCR; the Latin of PL 93, book I, in its Internet
  Archive OCR, whose column number at this page is illegible): the Lord is a
  Lamb in dying innocently and a Lion in conquering death; on 5:7, *Librum de
  dextera Dei, dispensationem ipsam Incarnationis a Patre et a seipso,
  secundum id quod Deus est, dispositam accepisse Filius hominis dicitur*: the
  Son of man is said to have received from God's right hand the very
  dispensation of the Incarnation, disposed by the Father and by himself
  according as he is God; a Lamb in his humanity and the Father's right hand in
  his deity (5:6–7); the redeemed are gathered out of the nations and reign on
  the earth (5:9–10). He reports Tyconius: *Agnum Ecclesiam … quae in Christo
  accepit omnem potestatem*.
- *Negative result.* Bede's lemmata pass from v. 11 to v. 13: neither the
  English nor the Latin text read carries a comment on v. 12 itself
  (*accipere … divinitatem*). No direct comment on v. 12 was found in the
  tracked NPNF and ANF containers by literal search for the verse's English
  (`Worthy is the Lamb`, `receive power, and divinity`, `Lamb that was slain`)
  or its citation forms (`Rev. v. 12`, `Apoc. v. 12`).
- *Liturgical reception.* **Schuster** (§3.1, p. 201) reads the Introit as an
  exaltation proportioned to the humiliation, with the Passion named first.
- *Reasoning that bears on v. 12's verb.* The receiving of power by the one who
  is God is expounded at Ps 2:8 by Hilary, Augustine, Cassiodorus, Theodoret,
  Aquinas and Bellarmine (§3.8), at Ps 71:1 by Cassiodorus and Theodoret
  (§3.3), and at Dan 7:14 by Jerome (§3.6); joining those to Apoc 5:12 is
  editorial synthesis (`research/interpretations.md`, reading A).
- *Holdings.* Bede's work is cataloged only; Andrew of Caesarea cataloged only;
  Primasius, Ambrose Autpert and the medieval expositors listed by `discover`
  are not held. Andrew's Greek, which a lead says treats the receiving as the
  humanity's, was not opened (§7).

### 3.3 Ps 71:1 (Missal numbering) — Introit verse

- **Augustine**, *Enarrationes in Psalmos* 71.1–2 (NPNF I.8, CCEL text,
  physical lines 33638–33700): the title *for Salomon* is figurative because
  the psalm says what could not apply to Solomon after the flesh; Solomon means
  peacemaker, and the true Peacemaker is the Mediator; *O God, thy judgment
  give to the King, and thy justice to the King's Son* is Christ, both King
  and King's Son, to whom the Father gave all judgment (Jn 5:22). Augustine
  numbers this clause ver. 1 with the title unnumbered, as the Missal does.
- **Robert Bellarmine**, *Explanatio in Psalmos*, Ps 71, the paragraph on
  *Give to the king thy judgment* (O'Sullivan's English of 1866, tracked
  eCatholic2000 text `…historical-text-357551e1`, hash matched, line 2533;
  wording checked in the registered 1866 facsimile, PDF p. 224 = printed
  p. 214, text layer): **the literal sense is David's prayer for his son
  Solomon, *just anointed king***, asking not riches but the grace of judging
  rightly, *for kings were created for the people, not the people for kings*;
  the application to Christ (Jn 5:22) is allowed as another reading; at *he
  shall continue with the sun* (line 2537) *he now begins to pass from Solomon
  to Christ, this verse being quite inapplicable to Solomon*. **This differs
  from Augustine**, for whom the title is figurative throughout (§6.7).
- **Cassiodorus** [ecclesiastical writer], *Expositio Psalmorum* 71, §§1, 3, 5
  (Vers. 1) (Migne's text as transcribed by Corpus Corporum/monumenta.ch,
  fetched whole 2026-10-19 at the addressing rule the library's edition record
  states, SHA-256 `374b3e17…1ae9`; no PL 70 column marker on the page):
  Solomon is *pacificus*, and the true peacemaker is Christ; the whole psalm
  signifies the Saviour's advent, *now his humanity, now his divinity*; *Deus*
  here is the Father, whom *da Filio* shows to be invoked; and the rule:
  *quotiescunque aut ipse Dominus Christus sibi petit dari, aut alter ut ei
  concedatur expostulat, naturam humanitatis evidenter ostendi. Caeterum
  Verbum omnipotens cum totum habeat Patris, nihil sibi indiget dari* (Jn
  16:15).
- **Theodoret of Cyrus** [ecclesiastical writer], *Interpretatio in Psalmos*
  71, on the title and v. 1 (PG 80 cols. 1429–1430, registered PDF
  `…wikimedia-pdf-72ee8714`, fetched whole and matched; read on the PDF's
  page image, PDF p. 753, Greek column): the psalm fits Solomon *in no way*,
  who neither ruled the ends of the earth nor received tribute from west and
  east, and ended his life without glory; the title fits the Saviour, for
  Solomon means peaceful (1 Par 22:9; Eph 2:14; Jn 14:27); *since he is God and
  man, the one always, the other assumed for our sake, the prophecy teaches
  both: it says that he has as God authority over all, and receives it also as
  man from his own Father*; he is King and King's Son, as God begotten of the
  God who is King of all, as man descended from King David.
- **Schuster** (§3.1, pp. 201–202): *distinctly Messianic*, the Messianic
  office conferred on Jesus.
- *Bounded negatives.* Hilary has no tractate on Ps 71 in CSEL 22 (its running
  heads pass from Ps 68 to Ps 91). Aquinas's *Super Psalmos* ends at Ps 54
  (edition record `…latin-corpusthomisticum-web-2026-09-05`), so has no
  Ps 71. Basil left no homily on Ps 71: the PG 29 *Ordo rerum* (tracked text
  layer `…full-text-layer-2898b217`, line 56898 ff.) lists homilies on Pss 1,
  7, 14, 28, 29, 32, 33, 44, 45, 48, 59, 61 and 114.

### 3.4 Ps 71:8 and 11 — Gradual

- **Augustine**, *En. in Ps.* 71.11–14 (NPNF I.8, lines 33936–34010): *from sea
  to sea* means from any end of the earth to any other, the Church spread
  through the whole world; *from the river* names the Jordan, where the Father's
  voice was heard at the baptism, the point from which the Gospel of the
  kingdom went out to all nations (71.11). On v. 10, the gifts the kings *shall
  lead* *seem to me to signify men, whom into the fellowship of the Church of
  Christ the authority of kings doth lead: although even persecuting kings
  have led gifts, knowing not what they did, in sacrificing the holy Martyrs*
  (71.13, lines 33989–34004), immediately before he quotes v. 11, *And there
  shall adore Him all kings of the earth, all nations shall serve Him*. The
  reason for that honour and service is that he delivered the needy from the
  mighty; this poor people is *the people of men believing in Him. In this
  people are also kings adoring Him*, who do not disdain to be needy and poor,
  humbly confessing their sins (71.14). **Augustine thus gives the kings both a
  part in the poor people and an office: their authority leads men into the
  Church.**
- **Augustine**, *Letter* 185 (*The Correction of the Donatists*), §§19–20
  (NPNF I.4, J. R. King's English, New Advent page 1102185, fetched whole,
  SHA-256 `0e38b092…4bea`, unregistered): in the apostles' time no emperor yet
  served Christ by laws, because Ps 2:2 was being fulfilled and *Be wise now,
  therefore, O you kings … Serve the Lord with fear* (Ps 2:10–11) not yet;
  *How then are kings to serve the Lord with fear, except by preventing and
  chastising with religious severity all those acts which are done in
  opposition to the commandments of the Lord? For a man serves God in one way
  in that he is man, in another way in that he is also king*: as man by living
  faithfully, as king by enforcing just laws; and *so soon as the fulfilment
  began of what is written in a later psalm, "All kings shall fall down before
  Him; all nations shall serve Him"*, no sober man could tell kings that
  religion is no concern of theirs (§20). This is Augustine's own reading of
  the Gradual's v. 11 and of the Offertory's context, made in defence of the
  imperial laws against the Donatists; that context is part of the locus and
  limits its use (§6.9).
- **Cassiodorus**, *Exp. Ps.* 71, §§12 (Vers. 8) and 15 (Vers. 11) (as §3.3):
  v. 8 *cannot pertain at all to Solomon*, who reigned only in Judaea; the
  Ocean encircles the earth; *a flumine* is the Jordan, *unde per totum orbem
  terrarum saluberrima regula sacri baptismatis emanavit*; on v. 11, *by all
  kings he wished us to understand all nations*, and he added *omnes gentes*
  lest it be thought said of kings only; the Church spread through the world
  refutes the Donatists' local Church; *kings adore, who are thought to be
  adored; nations serve, who are known to have earthly lords*.
- **Theodoret**, *In Ps.* 71:8–13 (PG 80 cols. 1433–1436; v. 11 read on the
  page image of PDF p. 756, Latin column; vv. 8 and 12–13 on the PDF's text
  layer): *from sea to sea* is the inhabited world bounded by the outer seas;
  *the river* is the Jordan, where the Father bore witness to the Son, and from
  that time the preaching *Do penance, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand*
  began; on v. 11, ***some willingly in the present life, but all after the
  resurrection***: *we see not yet all things subject to him* (Heb 2:8), but
  then every knee shall bend (Phil 2:10); *all nations shall serve him*
  answers Jacob's prophecy (Gen 49:10), and *we see the end of the prediction:
  there is no nation that has not heard the preaching of the Gospel*; on
  vv. 12–13, all adore him because freed from the devil's tyranny, and by his
  divine laws he restrains the powerful, terrifies them with the threat of
  gehenna and makes them deal indulgently with the poor.
- **Bellarmine**, *Explanatio*, Ps 71, paragraphs on *He shall rule from sea
  to sea* (line 2543), *All kings of the earth shall adore him* (line 2549,
  paragraph 10 in the 1866 printing, checked on the facsimile's page image,
  PDF p. 226 = printed p. 216) and *For he shall deliver the poor* (line 2551):
  the river is better the Jordan, where his kingdom had its rise, following
  Augustine; the kings' adoration is fulfilled *to a certain extent* in that *a
  great many princes and nations will be converted to the service and worship
  of Christ*, *to the letter* at the day of judgment when every knee shall
  bend, and *always* *if we refer it to the actual power that Christ has over
  all princes and all nations, so that, with or without their knowledge, with
  or without their consent, he may deal as he pleases with them* (Mt 28:18;
  Apoc 1:5; Apoc 19:16); kings and people serve him *because, through him they
  will be delivered from the power of the devil … and will be introduced to his
  own most peaceful kingdom*.
- **Schuster** (§3.1): on the Gradual, *de jure* complete, *de facto*
  incomplete until the judgment (p. 202); with the Alleluia as *the double
  chant*, a threefold catholicity of place, subjects and ages (p. 203).
- *Bounded negatives.* As §3.3 for Hilary, Aquinas and Basil.

### 3.5 Col 1:12–20 — Epistle

- **John Chrysostom**, *Homilies on Colossians* 2 and 3 (NPNF I.13, CCEL text,
  lines 23765–24515): on v. 13, God did not only deliver from darkness but
  *translated* us into a kingdom, and named it the kingdom of the Son of his
  love, giving us the same honour with the Son; on v. 15, *firstborn* is not
  *first created*; on v. 18, *firstborn from the dead* means the firstfruits of
  the resurrection; on v. 20, he reconciles through himself, through death,
  through the cross, and the things in heaven are reconciled because he
  brought man up to the angels, which Chrysostom joins to Eph 1:10, *to sum up
  all things in Christ* (Vulgate *instaurare omnia in Christo*).
- **Thomas Aquinas**, *Super Epistolam ad Colossenses lectura* 1 lect. 3–5
  (Corpus Thomisticum page `ccl`, fetched whole, SHA-256 `d055308f…dda`,
  unregistered; lect. 3 on v. 13 checked against the registered Dessain 1857
  OCR `…book-ocr-2b7b11dc`, printed p. 409, physical line 43530, same words):
  on v. 13, *transtulit … id est, ut essemus regnum Dei*, with *regnum meum non
  est de hoc mundo* (Jn 18:36; both texts misprint the chapter) and *fecisti
  nos Deo nostro regnum* (Apoc 5:10), *or, ad litteram, that we might attain
  eternal life*; and the redemption is his *inquantum homo, factus … pro nobis
  sacrificium*, the remission his *inquantum est Deus*; on vv. 15–17 (lect. 4),
  *primogenitus* is not first creature, as the Arians read it, but the Word
  begotten as principle of every creature; on v. 18 (lect. 5), Christ *secundum
  quod huiusmodi* is made head of the Church, first in grace and first in
  glory, *primogenitus ex mortuis* because the resurrection is a second
  generation; on vv. 19–20, the wills of men, God and angels, and of Jews and
  gentiles, concord in Christ, by the blood of the cross which destroyed sin
  and fulfilled the law (Lk 2:14; Eph 2:14).
- **Theodoret**, *Interpretatio Epistolae ad Colossenses* 1:12–20 (PG 82 cols.
  597–602, Wikimedia Commons PDF *Patrologia Graeca Vol. 082*, fetched whole,
  SHA-1 `957bae83…34` matching the Commons record, SHA-256 `7d6dd76e…3b25`,
  unregistered; read on the PDF's text layer, with col. 599 checked on the page
  image of PDF p. 293): v. 13, the *Son of his love* is the beloved, genuine
  Son, loved not as creature and servant; God made us worthy of the Son's
  kingdom, *as he says to Timothy: If we suffer, we shall also reign with him*
  (2 Tim 2:12); v. 14, the redemption given through baptism; v. 15, the image
  shows the consubstantial, and *firstborn* means born before all creation, as
  *firstborn from the dead* means the first to rise; v. 16, *per ipsum* is the
  first creation, ***in ipsum* the work done through his incarnation**, with
  2 Cor 5:17 and *Instaurare omnia in Christo* (Eph 1:10); v. 18, *from
  theology he passes to the economy*: he is our head according to the
  humanity; as God before all and with the Father, as man firstborn from the
  dead and head of the body; v. 20, he wrought our reconciliation by suffering,
  shedding his blood and offering the sacrifice for us, and joined the
  heavenly to the earthly, for the hosts of angels had turned from us (Lk
  2:14).
- **Augustine**, *Tract. in Io.* 115.2 (§3.7), cites Col 1:13 to explain
  Jn 18:36. **Hilary**, *Tract. in Ps.* 2.31 (§3.8), speaks of the baptized
  nations *in regnum aeternum diuinumque translatae* and of the *primogenitus
  ex mortuis*. **Hippolytus**, scholion on Dan 7:14 (§3.6), uses
  first-begotten of God, of a virgin and from the dead.
- **Schuster** (§3.1, p. 202): primacy over all creation founded on the
  hypostatic union, and headship of the Church.
- *Doctrinal illumination.* *Quas primas* n. 13 grounds the kingship in the
  hypostatic union and in acquired right by redemption (§3.13).
- *Bounded negatives.* Bellarmine wrote on the Psalms, not on Colossians;
  Basil has no commentary on Colossians in PG 29. **Cassiodorus did treat
  Colossians**, in the *Complexiones in epistulas et acta apostolorum et
  apocalypsin* (`src/sources/commentary/book-audit-ledger.yaml`, Colossians;
  title in `work-aliases.yaml`); the library holds no edition of it and
  `discover` over Col 1:12–20 lists none, so it was **not searched** (§7).
  The iteration 1 statement that he wrote no commentary on Colossians was
  wrong and is withdrawn. Athanasius, *Contra Arianos* II.62–64 on
  *primogenitus*, remains unopened (§7).
- *Held work whose holding does not reach Colossians.* `discover` over
  Col 1:12–20 lists **Cornelius a Lapide**, *Commentaria in omnes D. Pauli
  epistolas* (`work.cornelius-a-lapide.commentaria-in-omnes-divi-pauli-epistolas`,
  edition Antwerp 1614), as held, matched on the chapter. Its one text
  artifact, the tracked OCR `…antwerp-1614.ia-djvu-text-197365b2` (SHA-256
  matched), was searched whole in iteration 2: its last running heads are
  *Comment. in Epist. ad Galat.* chapter VI and its text ends on the stigmata
  of Gal 6:17; its Colossians strings (OCR *Coloff.*, *Colof.*, 10 lines) are
  cross-references inside the commentary on the earlier epistles, and no
  running head found names Ephesians or Colossians (OCR-bounded); the artifact's own record says
  the item stops at Galatians. The held facsimile PDF is of the same
  577 images. Lapide on Colossians is therefore **not held**, and remains an
  unopened lead (§7).

### 3.6 Dan 7:14 — Alleluia

- **Jerome**, *Commentarii in Danielem* II on 7:13–14 and 7:17–18 (Migne's text,
  PL 25 cols. 670–671, in the tracked Wikisource transcription): the son of man
  coming with the clouds is the stone of Nabuchodonosor's dream, signifying the
  Son of God's assumption of human flesh; *that he was offered to the Ancient of
  days and received power, honour and kingdom is to be understood according to
  the Apostle*, *Qui cum in forma Dei esset … factus obediens usque ad mortem*
  (Phil 2:6–8), which the Arians, he says, should have read piously; against
  Porphyry, no man can be the one whose power is eternal *quae nullo fine
  claudatur*; on 7:17–18, the saints will have not an earthly but a heavenly
  kingdom, *cesset ergo mille annorum fabula*.
- **Hippolytus** [no standing row], scholia on Daniel 7:13–14 (ANF V, CCEL
  text, lines 19007–19030): the Ancient of days is the Lord and God of all,
  even of Christ; *His dominion is an everlasting dominion*: the Father put all
  things in subjection to the Son and showed him first-begotten of God, of a
  virgin, and from the dead; he is King of all in heaven because born the Word
  before all, of all on earth because made man, of all under the earth because
  numbered among the dead. The fragments are catena scholia transmitted under
  his name; ANF's attribution is recorded as such.
- **Cyril of Jerusalem** [no standing row], *Catechesis* 15.27–32 (NPNF II.7,
  CCEL text, lines 16736–16825): against any who say that Christ's kingdom
  will end, *another head of the dragon, lately sprung up in* [Galatia; the
  word is missing from the CCEL text], he cites Gabriel (Lk 1:33) and Dan
  7:13–14; *until he hath put all enemies under his feet* sets no term, as
  *until* in Rom 5:14 and 2 Cor 3:15 does not; the Son's subjection to the
  Father is a Son's free accord. Cyril names no one; **the identification
  with Marcellus of Ancyra and Photinus is the NPNF editor's (note 1950)**.
- **Aquinas**, *Super Io.* 18 lect. 6 (§3.7), cites v. 14b for *regnum meum non
  est de hoc mundo* in Chrysostom's sense, and v. 14a for *non est hinc*.
- **Schuster** (§3.1, p. 203): the partial accomplishment is visible in the
  Church's two thousand years against the empires that have passed.
- *Holdings.* Theodoret on Daniel, Albert and the medieval postillators are not
  held; Hippolytus's Daniel commentary is not registered beyond ANF V.

### 3.7 Jn 18:33–37 — Gospel

- **Augustine**, *Tract. in Io.* 115.1–4 (NPNF I.7, CCEL text, lines
  34853–35057): the Lord's counter-question exposes the vain notion that
  Gentiles and Jews alike had of his kingdom; *hear, all ye kingdoms of the
  earth: I interfere not with your government in this world*; he did not say
  *not in this world* but *not of this world*, and not *not here* but *not from
  hence*, for his kingdom, those who believe in him, is here until the end of
  the world with tares among it, a stranger sojourning; *so did God rescue us
  from the power of darkness, and translate us into the kingdom of the Son of
  His love* (Col 1:13); Ps 2:6 is the king set on Sion; *Thou sayest* neither
  denies nor concedes a carnal kingship; *for this was I born* is the temporal
  nativity from the Virgin; *every one that is of the truth* commends grace.
- **John Chrysostom**, *Homilies on John* 83.4 (NPNF I.14, New Advent page
  240183): not that he does not rule here, but that his empire is from above,
  not human and far greater; earthly kingship shows its weakness by needing
  servants, the kingdom above is self-sufficient; *If then He was born a king,
  all His other attributes are by Generation, and He has nothing which He
  received in addition*, followed at once by his rule for texts of giving: *So
  that when you hear that, As the Father has life in Himself, so has He given
  to the Son also to have life* (Jn 5:26), *deem of nothing else but His
  generation, and so of the rest* (re-read in iteration 2, page SHA-256
  matched); and in 84.1 (page 240184), *I am indeed a King, yet not such an one
  as you suspect*.
- **Cyril of Alexandria** [no standing row], *In Ioannem* XII on 18:36–37
  (LFC 48, pp. 599–601, tertullian.org transcription, registered remote
  artifact matched): Christ did not deny that he was King but showed he was no
  enemy to Caesar, reigning as God over heaven and earth; *as God He is King,
  whether man so will, or no*; he came into the world *when I became Man* to
  bear witness to the truth, a sovereignty *not … added unto it from without,
  but … essentially and naturally inherent*.
- **Thomas Aquinas**, *Super Evangelium S. Ioannis lectura* 18 lect. 6
  (Corpus Thomisticum page cih18, Marietti text): *regnum* means either the
  people who are ruled (Augustine: the faithful, with Apoc 5:10 and Col 1:13)
  or the royal power itself (Chrysostom: its origin is not from worldly causes,
  with Dan 7:14b); *non est hinc … est tamen hic, quia ubique est*, with Wis
  8:1, Ps 2:8 and Dan 7:14a; *in hoc natus sum* is the carnal nativity on
  Augustine's reading and the eternal nativity, *sicut Deus ex Deo, ita rex ex
  rege*, on Chrysostom's, with Ps 2:6–7; the king rules those who are of the
  truth as the shepherd his sheep.
- **Schuster** (§3.1, p. 203): *not hinc, but it comprehends the world*; he
  does not come to dethrone the sovereigns of the earth.
- *Bounded search.* Augustine's *De consensu evangelistarum*, the *Catena
  aurea* on John, Bonaventure, Cornelius a Lapide and Theophylact are held and
  were not read.

### 3.8 Ps 2:8 — Offertory (with vv. 7 and 10–11, its immediate context)

- **Hilary of Poitiers**, *Tractatus super Psalmos* 2.29–33 and 2.42–43
  (CSEL 22, ed. Zingerle 1891, pp. 59–62 and 70, read on Internet Archive page
  images n88–n91 and n99 after the registered OCR located them): *Filius meus
  es tu, ego hodie genui te* (v. 7) refers by apostolic authority (Acts 13:33)
  to the firstborn from the dead; *data est mihi omnis potestas* (Mt 28:18)
  shows that he asked for what he received; *accepit ergo hereditatem gentium,
  quam poposcit*, asked in Jn 17:1–2; the inheritance is all nations baptized
  and taught, *ex iniusto … iure dominantium in regnum aeternum diuinumque
  translatae*, the inheritance of *primogeniti huius ex mortuis*; with Phil
  2:6–11, abiding in the form of God he took the form of a servant and after
  the death of the cross is exalted, *eique hoc petenti, id est, ut esset,
  quod fuerat ante, donatur*; and (2.42–43) the kingdom of God is where sin is
  overcome and death destroyed, and those who become co-heirs of the eternal
  King must become kings.
- **Augustine**, *En. in Ps.* 2.5–9 (NPNF I.8, lines 579–700): v. 6, Christ set
  King over his holy Church; **v. 7, *To-day have I begotten Thee*, although it
  may seem spoken of the birth in the flesh, is given *a divine
  interpretation* of the eternal generation, *today* signifying the presence
  of eternity (2.6)**; v. 8, *Ask of Me* has a temporal sense with reference to
  the Manhood he took, *who offered up Himself as a Sacrifice in the stead of
  all sacrifices, who also maketh intercession for us*; the nations are joined
  to his Name and redeemed from death (2.7); vv. 9–11, the rod of iron is
  inflexible justice breaking earthly lusts; *understand, ye kings* is first
  read of the spiritual who govern what is brutish in themselves, and then
  *thus*: *now that I am set as King, be ye not sad, kings of the earth, as if
  your excellency were taken from you, but rather understand and be
  instructed. For it is expedient for you, that ye should be under Him*, and
  serve the Lord of all with fear (2.8–9).
- **Augustine**, *Letter* 185.19–20 (§3.4): the kings' service of Ps 2:10–11 is
  service as kings, by just laws.
- **Cassiodorus**, *Exp. Ps.* 2, §§14–21 (Vers. 7–11) (as §3.3, SHA-256
  `30366668…fdda2`): v. 7, *hodie* shows the coeternity of his majesty, *Genui
  te* the generation *Lumen ex lumine*; v. 8, ***Hoc per habitum dicitur
  formamque servilem … Quidquid enim in tempore accepit Christus, secundum
  hominem ea quae non habebat acquirit***: he is bidden to ask according to the
  lower nature; *secundum potentiam Verbi … nec illi necesse est petere quae
  simul cognoscitur possidere*; *possessionem tuam*: *natura humanitatis
  accepit, quod semper divina possedit: maiestati enim ipsius dari non poterat
  quod habebat* (Mt 28:18; Phil 2:10); v. 9, *Reges eos* takes away tyrannical
  domination, for those are ruled who come to the gifts of salvation; vv.
  10–11, *reges* are *dominatores vitiorum*, *for kings need not always be
  understood as those clothed in purple* (1 Cor 4:8).
- **Theodoret**, *In Ps.* 2:7–8 (PG 80 col. 879, read on the page image of PDF
  p. 466, Latin column, with the Greek of col. 880): ***no one who believes
  the Spirit's teaching will apply v. 7 to the Godhead of the Lord Christ***,
  for of that generation God says *Ex utero ante luciferum genui te* (Ps
  109:3); *as man therefore he receives this voice, as man he also hears what
  follows*; v. 8, as God he is maker of all (Jn 1:3; Col 1:16), and Lord of what
  he made, *verumtamen cum Dominus natura sit ut Deus, etiam ut homo
  universorum dominium accipit*; the Lord's care passes from Israel to the
  nations.
- **Thomas Aquinas**, *Postilla super Psalmos* 2 nn. 5–9 (Corpus Thomisticum
  page `cps02`, the registered restricted artifact `…cps02-html-73a0a0c2`,
  fetched whole and matched; Parma vol. XIV text): v. 7–8 give Christ dominion
  over the nations ***duplici jure: scilicet hereditario, et meritorio***; the
  hereditary right is the eternal generation, *hodie genui te, idest
  aeternaliter* (n. 5); the meritorious right is the asking: God wills that we
  receive his gifts by asking, *et hoc exemplum voluit ostendere per Christum,
  quia voluit quod peteret, quod sibi jure hereditario competebat*; the asking
  for the nations is by prayer (Jn 17:20) and by the passion (Heb 9:15), and he
  holds the nations not as a minister but as lord (Heb 3:5–6) (n. 6); v. 9 is
  Christ's spiritual rule (n. 7); on vv. 10–11, he restrains the kings by
  warning them and drawing them to God's service (n. 8), and ***secundum
  Augustinum … rex servit Deo inquantum homo, in se juste vivendo, sed
  inquantum rex, leges ferendo contra ea quae sunt contra Dei justitiam***:
  the psalm prefigures the Church's state, *for at first the kings of the earth
  made laws against Christ and Christians, but afterwards they made laws for
  Christ* (n. 9).
- **Bellarmine**, *Explanatio*, Ps 2, paragraphs 7–8 and 10–12 (tracked text
  lines 113–125; wording checked in the 1866 facsimile, PDF p. 14 = printed
  p. 4, text layer): v. 7 is *the foundation of God's decree. For to Christ, as
  being the true and natural Son of God, is due all power in heaven and on
  earth*; three generations are meant, eternity, the flesh, and the
  resurrection; v. 8, *You my natural Son, the incarnation of my power raised
  from the dead, have just right to ask me for power over all nations*; the
  kingdom is *his spiritual kingdom, that is, his Church*, not confined like
  David's or Solomon's but over all nations, *infidels even included* (Mt
  28:18; Acts 10:42); v. 10, the prophet *exhorts the kings of this world … to
  freely subject themselves to him, the true and supreme king of all kings*,
  first correcting their judgment, then their affections; v. 11, *serve the
  Lord as a son would his father*; v. 12, that they do it with all their might.
- **Aquinas**, *Super Io.* 18 lect. 6 (§3.7), cites v. 8 to show that the
  kingdom not from hence is everywhere. **Schuster** (§3.1, p. 203): the deed
  of investiture received from the Father.
- *Bounded negative.* Basil left no homily on Ps 2 (§3.3).

### 3.9 Ps 28:10–11 — Communion

- **Augustine**, *En. in Ps.* 28 (NPNF I.8, lines 7348–7432): the title reads
  *of the consummation of the tabernacle*, the perfection of the Church in this
  world; *The Lord inhabiteth the deluge*: he first dwells in the flood of this
  world in his saints, kept in the Church as in the ark; *and the Lord shall
  sit a King for ever*: afterwards he will sit reigning in them for ever; *The
  Lord will give strength to His people* fighting the storms of this world,
  *for peace in this world He hath not promised them*; *The Lord will bless His
  people in peace*, giving them peace in himself (Jn 14:27).
- **Basil the Great**, *Homilia in Psalmum XXVIII* 8 (PG 29 cols. 304C–305A,
  read on the Internet Archive page images n562 and n563 of
  `patrologiae_cursus_completus_gr_vol_029`, SHA-256 `2ae57846…f150` and
  `2b782dbf…3d84`, Greek column, located through the registered PG 29 text
  layer `…full-text-layer-2898b217`): *κατακλυσμός* is a flood that effaces
  all beneath it and cleanses all that was defiled; ***the grace of baptism he
  calls a deluge***, so that the soul washed of its sins and purged of the old
  man is fit to be God's dwelling in the Spirit (with Ps 31:6 and Mich 7:19);
  ***Καὶ καθήσεται Κύριος βασιλεὺς εἰς τὸν αἰῶνα: settled in the soul that
  shines from the deluge, God makes it, as it were, a throne for himself***;
  on v. 11, the Lord takes strength from the sinful people and gives it to the
  one who does justice (Mt 25:29), and he who is strengthened for good works is
  worthy of God's blessing; peace, the *stability of the governing part* of
  the soul, seems the most perfect of blessings, the peace the Lord gave his
  disciples that passes all understanding (Jn 14:27; Phil 4:7).
- **Cassiodorus**, *Exp. Ps.* 28, §§16–17 (Vers. 9–10 in Migne's numbering)
  (as §3.3, SHA-256 `89d21e00…b734`): the threefold *Dominus* of the two verses
  shows *the Holy Trinity dwelling in the deluge, that is, the waters of
  baptism* (Mt 28:19), for the flood under Noe bore baptism's image; *et
  sedebit Dominus Rex in aeternum* is rightly understood of the Son; *populo
  suo* is the Christian people *quem de sacri baptismatis munere
  conquisivit*; *in pace*, the peace of Jn 14:27, one peace of the Father, the
  Son and the Holy Spirit.
- **Thomas Aquinas**, *Postilla super Psalmos* 28 n. 8 (Corpus Thomisticum page
  `cps21`, registered restricted artifact `…cps21-html-5e8fdbbe`, fetched whole
  and matched): the verses turn from benefits past to benefits hoped for; the
  Hebrew *Dominus diluvio sedit* has a plain sense, that from the beginning his
  judgments were manifest, as when he brought the deluge, *et sedebit dominus
  rex in aeternum, judicans scilicet populos in aequitate*; Jerome's *inhabitare
  facit*; three mystical readings: the saints dwell secure in the Church as in
  Noe's ark amid *the flood of tribulations*; the *diluvium*, the world and its
  carnal men, is made to dwell in his temple when they are converted, *et
  sedebit rex in aeternum*; or he dwells in the baptismal waters by the effect
  of grace; *virtutem … dabit* concerns progress (Isa 40:29), *benedicet … in
  pace* the end (Isa 32:18). On v. 5 (n. 5) he notes that *omnes reges per hoc
  conversi sunt ad Christum*.
- **Theodoret**, *In Ps.* 28:10–11 (PG 80 cols. 1069–1070, PDF p. 569, read on
  the PDF's text layer in both columns): *he will rebuild the world drowned by
  the torrent of lawlessness, renew it and make a new creation* (2 Cor 5:17),
  and having done so *will not allow impiety a place again, but will reign for
  ever*; v. 11 foretells the assaults of the impious, the victory of the
  martyrs and the peace given to the churches afterwards: strength in the time
  of war, blessing in peace once the long war is ended.
- **Bellarmine**, *Explanatio*, Ps 28, paragraphs on vv. 10–11 (tracked text
  lines 1009–1013; wording checked in the 1866 facsimile, PDF p. 82 = printed
  p. 72, text layer): on the first (natural) interpretation, God's rain
  nourishes all life and *the Lord shall sit king forever; for it is he that
  guides, governs, and directs all these matters*; on the second, ***when the
  Lord, on the day of judgment, shall have "discovered the thick woods" … then
  he will "make a flood to dwell," inundating the wicked with all manner of
  evils; and thus, all resistance being broken down, the whole power of
  demons, bad men, and all power in general being swept away, "the Lord shall
  sit King forever"***; *some will have the flood here spoken of to refer to
  the deluge, others to baptism; and those who so explain it being of great
  weight and high position, I will not contradict them*; v. 11 is strength
  against temptation in this pilgrimage and the blessing of everlasting life;
  some pious people refer the seven *voices* to the seven sacraments, *in
  magnificence* to the Eucharist (reported, not adopted).
- **Schuster** (§3.1, p. 204): at Holy Communion Jesus is enthroned in the
  faithful heart; the blessing of the nations (Gen 22:18).
- *Bounded negative.* No witness read joins v. 10's *sedebit* to the Preface's
  *traderet*; that join is editorial (reading C).
- *Held and not read.* `discover` over Ps 28:10–11 also lists **Athanasius**,
  *Expositiones in Psalmos* (`work.athanasius-of-alexandria.expositiones-in-psalmos`,
  edition Migne PG 27, Paris), as held. Its holdings are the remote whole-volume
  OCR `…migne-pg-27-paris.ia-djvu-ocr-969a381f` (not retained; its record
  states that the Greek column's recognition is corrupt and forbids quotation
  from it) and two tracked page images, `…ia-leaf-n191-b764aa2a` (cols.
  369–370, Ps 83) and `…ia-leaf-n213-d1db9915` (cols. 413–414, Ps 94), neither
  of which carries Ps 28. Reading it would need the PG 27 page image of the
  Ps 28 exposition, which is not held. It was not opened and is an unopened
  lead (§7); no reading rests on its absence.

### 3.10 Not said: Ps 88:27–28, 30 (Tract); Apoc 19:16 (paschal Alleluia)

- **Augustine**, *En. in Ps.* 88 sermo 2.26–28 (NPNF I.8, lines 44357–44385):
  Christ is now exalted among the kings of the earth, as the martyrs believed
  before they saw it; the testament is faithful in him, its mediator and heir;
  his seed, those who are Christ's and so Abraham's, endures, and his throne
  is as the days of heaven, not of the earth.
- **Bede** on Apoc 19:16 (Marshall, Internet Archive OCR): the name is written
  on the Church, on the tables of the heart; the thigh signifies the posterity
  of seed. **Bellarmine** cites Apoc 19:16 on Ps 71:11 for Christ's actual
  power over all princes (§3.4); *Quas primas* cites it in its note 17 (§3.13);
  **Schuster** opens his chapter with the title *Rex regum et Dominus
  dominantium* written on the royal mantle (p. 200).
- These two printed options are recorded because they are text of the printed
  formulary. They are not said on 25 October 2026 and are not developed. The
  Tract's rubric is *In Missis votivis post Septuagesimam* (no. 3943); the
  paschal Alleluia's is *Tempore autem paschali omittitur graduale* (no. 3944);
  the feast, on the last Sunday of October, falls in neither season, so both
  serve only a votive Mass of Christ the King said in those seasons
  (`propers/verified.md`).

### 3.11 Collect — composed text (1925)

- *Verbal echo, recorded as such.* `ómnia instauráre` reproduces the Vulgate of
  Eph 1:10, `instaurare omnia in Christo`; `in dilécto Fílio` echoes Col 1:13
  and Eph 1:6; `universórum Rege` names what Col 1:16–17 and the Preface state.
  Chrysostom joins Col 1:20 to Eph 1:10 (§3.5), and **Theodoret joins Col 1:16's
  *in ipsum* to Eph 1:10, *Instaurare omnia in Christo*, reading it of the work
  done through the Incarnation (§3.5)**: two witnesses give the Collect's
  source verse a tie to the Epistle; the Collect's own wording is not
  expounded by any patristic witness read.
- *Liturgical reception.* **Schuster** (p. 202) reads the Collect as inspired by
  St Paul: sin a disruptive force that ruined the unity of the race and of
  creation, restored through Christ the new Adam.
- *Documented reception.* *Quas primas* is the instituting act (§3.13); no
  passage of it read here comments on the Collect, and no compositional
  intention is asserted for it.

### 3.12 Secret, Preface and Postcommunion — composed texts (1925)

- **Secret.** `humánæ reconciliatiónis` echoes Col 1:20 and 2 Cor 5:18–19; `quem
  sacrifíciis præséntibus immolámus` names the Mass as the immolation of
  Christ; `unitátis et pacis dona` is a verbal echo of the Corpus Christi
  Secret's `unitatis et pacis propitius dona concede`, as the calendar finding
  aid `src/sources/calendars/roman-1962/propers.yaml` gives that Secret

  **Schuster** (p. 204) gives the Secret without comment.
- **Preface.** `óleo exsultatiónis unxísti` is Ps 44:8 as Heb 1:9 applies it to
  the Son; `Sacerdótem ætérnum et universórum Regem` joins priest and king;
  `seípsum in ara Crucis hóstiam … ófferens` is the priest's self-offering;
  `tráderet` the kingdom to the Father echoes 1 Cor 15:24; the threefold
  `regnum veritátis et vitæ …` lists the kingdom's goods. **Bede** on Apoc 1:6
  (PL 93 col. 135–136, Latin OCR; Marshall's English): *Quia Rex regum et
  Sacerdos coelestis se offerendo pro nobis nos suo corpori adunavit, nemo
  sanctorum est qui spiritualiter sacerdotii officio careat* — the King of kings
  as heavenly priest by self-offering, for the clause of Apoc 1:6 the Introit
  omits. **Augustine**, *De Trinitate* I.8.15–10.21 (Haddan's English, New
  Advent page 130101), and **Hilary**, *De Trinitate* XI.21–40 (NPNF II.9, CCEL
  text, lines 24580–24990), expound 1 Cor 15:24–28 (§5.3); **Cyril of

  office to the redeemer's and the priest's. **Schuster** (p. 204), the one
  witness to this Preface as a text: its conception of the Pontiff-King who
  restores the realm of creation to the Father *that God might eternally be
  all in all* is *borrowed from St Paul*, its lyrical form the compiler's.
- **Postcommunion.** `Immortalitátis alimóniam consecúti, quǽsumus, Dómine: ut`
  is also the opening of the Postcommunion of the Twenty-first Sunday after
  Pentecost as the calendar finding aid gives it (`… quod ore percepimus, pura
  mente sectemur`); seen in `tools/tpt mass-propers show`, not re-read on that

  militáre` uses the military figure of the standard; `in cælésti sede iúgiter
  regnáre` echoes 2 Tim 2:12 (which Chrysostom cites on Col 1:13, §3.5, and
  Theodoret on the same verse, §3.5) and Apoc 5:10. **Schuster** (p. 204): *The
  Royal Standard of Jesus Christ is the Cross … for the kingdom of Christ is
  that of obedience, humility and sacrifice*.
- *Bound.* Apart from Schuster, no witness was located commenting on any of
  these three composed texts as texts.

### 3.13 The instituting act (context, not a text of the Mass)

*Quas primas*, 11 December 1925, in the Holy See's English (response hashed by
the resolve-context stage, SHA-256 `f17b882f…`, re-read here from that stage's
copy). **Every number the readings rely on**, with what it says:

- n. 7: the metaphorical kingship in minds, wills and hearts;
- n. 11: Christ's own claims, including his reply to Pilate;
- n. 13: Cyril of Alexandria quoted for a dominion *his by essence and by
  nature*, founded on the hypostatic union, so that *to him as man angels and
  men are subject*; and Christ King also *by acquired, as well as by natural
  right, for he is our Redeemer*, with 1 Pet 1:18–19. The encyclical's note 20
  reads, in this copy, *In huc. x.* (apparently *In Lucam* x); that locus was
  not checked, and the quotation is reported as the encyclical's;
- n. 15: the kingdom is spiritual; before the Roman magistrate he declared it
  not of this world;
- n. 16: the kingly office joined to the redeemer's and the priest's;
- n. 17: *It would be a grave error … to say that Christ has no authority
  whatever in civil affairs*, all things being in his power; yet *during his
  life on earth he refrained from the exercise of such authority, and … he did
  not, nor does he today, interfere with those who possess* earthly goods
  (*Non eripit mortalia qui regna dat caelestia*);
- n. 18: his empire embraces all men, *whether collectively or individually*,
  the individual, the family and the State; rulers who wish to preserve their
  authority *will not neglect the public duty of reverence and obedience to the
  rule of Christ*;
- n. 19: his regal office *invests the human authority of princes and rulers
  with a religious significance*; rulers who know they rule *by the mandate and
  in the place of the Divine King* will rule piously and for the common good,
  and peace and harmony follow;
- n. 20: the hope of peace if all nations are received under the kingdom of
  the King of Peace, who came *to reconcile all things*, *not to be ministered
  unto but to minister*, whose yoke is sweet;
- n. 28: the feast on the last Sunday of October, with the annual renewal of
  the consecration to the Sacred Heart;
- n. 32: the feast will remind nations *that not only private individuals but
  also rulers and princes are bound to give public honor and obedience to
  Christ*, and of the last judgment.

## 5. Passage-level difficulty tests

### 5.1 *Accipere … divinitatem* (Apoc 5:12) and *Postula a me* (Ps 2:8)

- *The words.* The Introit says the slain Lamb is worthy *to receive* power and
  *divinity*; the Offertory has the Father say *Ask of me, and I will give
  thee* the nations, after *Filius meus es tu, ego hodie genui te* (v. 7). Read
  of the Son of God, both say he receives what he could not lack.
- *Hilary's inference.* v. 7 is, by Acts 13:33, the begetting of the firstborn
  from the dead; the Son asked for what he received (*data est*, Mt 28:18);
  what was given him on asking was *ut esset, quod fuerat ante*: the receiving
  is the glorifying of the assumed humanity, and its content is the nations
  translated into the eternal kingdom.
- *Augustine's inference, and its difference from Hilary's.* Augustine assigns
  **v. 7 to the eternal generation** (2.6) and **only v. 8 to the manhood**,
  which offered itself as sacrifice and intercedes (2.7). Hilary reads v. 7 of
  the resurrection. They agree on v. 8 and differ on v. 7 (§6.1).
- *Cassiodorus's inference* [supporting]: v. 7 eternal; v. 8 *secundum
  hominem*: what Christ received in time he acquired as man, *quod semper
  divina possedit*; the Word needs nothing given (also on Ps 71:1).
- *Theodoret's inference* [supporting]: v. 7 cannot be applied to the Godhead,
  which Ps 109:3 describes; *as man he receives this voice*; Lord by nature as
  God, he receives as man the lordship of all.
- *Aquinas's inference.* v. 7 eternal (*idest aeternaliter*), v. 8 the asking:
  a **double right**, hereditary by the eternal generation and meritorious by
  prayer and passion; *voluit quod peteret, quod sibi jure hereditario
  competebat*.
- *Bellarmine's inference.* v. 7 alludes to three generations, eternity, the
  flesh, the resurrection; the natural Son, incarnate and raised, has *just
  right to ask*.
- *Jerome's inference.* At Dan 7:14, the one presented to the Ancient of days
  *accepit potestatem, et honorem, et regnum* as Phil 2:6–8 teaches; read so,
  the text does not make the Son unequal.
- *What changes if accepted.* The kingship celebrated is received through the
  passion: the cross is the act by which the kingdom is acquired, so the
  Mass's own sacrifice (Secret, Preface) is where the received kingship is
  exercised. If refused, *accipere divinitatem* would say that the Lamb becomes
  divine, the reading Jerome names as Arian.
- *Counter-witness, and the answer the new witnesses give.* Chrysostom at
  Jn 18:37: *He has nothing which He received in addition*, all being by
  generation, and his next sentence gives the rule for such texts: *So that
  when you hear that, As the Father has life in Himself, so has He given to
  the Son also to have life* (Jn 5:26), *deem of nothing else but His
  generation, and so of the rest* (§3.7). Cyril of Alexandria: sovereignty not
  added from without. Both speak of the Word's kingship by nature.
  **Augustine's distribution (v. 7 eternal, v. 8 temporal), which Aquinas
  formulates as two rights and Cassiodorus and Theodoret state as *possessed
  as God, received as man*, grants Chrysostom's premise**: the kingship by
  generation is the premise of the decree (v. 7) and is not received. It does
  **not** meet his inference on his own ground. Chrysostom's rule refers a
  text of giving to the eternal generation; the answer refers the asking and
  receiving of v. 8 to the humanity, under the rule Cassiodorus states
  (*quotiescunque … sibi petit dari … naturam humanitatis evidenter ostendi*,
  §3.3) and Theodoret applies (*etiam ut homo universorum dominium accipit*,
  §3.8): what is received, asked and acquired is the same dominion held in the
  humanity and won by the passion. Chrysostom's locus does not share that
  rule. The outcome is a narrower disagreement, kept: on the premise (kingship
  by nature) the witnesses agree; on how a text of giving or asking addressed
  to the Son is to be read, Chrysostom and the two-rights witnesses answer
  differently (§6.1). Chrysostom does not comment on Ps 2:8 here, and his
  rule is not to be presented as a reading of it. Hilary's and Bellarmine's resurrection reading of v. 7 is
  compatible with that answer only so far as the resurrection is the manifesting
  of the Son in the humanity; read as the beginning of the Sonship it would
  fall under Chrysostom's objection, and neither of them reads it so (Hilary:
  *ut esset, quod fuerat ante*; Bellarmine: three generations, the first
  eternal). *Quas primas* n. 13 states both titles.
- *Limit.* No witness read comments on Apoc 5:12's *accipere divinitatem*
  itself (§3.2); applying these inferences to the Introit is editorial
  synthesis. Schuster's reading of the Introit (exaltation proportioned to the
  humiliation) is liturgical reception, not exegesis of the verb.

### 5.2 *Non est de hoc mundo … non est hinc* (Jn 18:36) against the subjection of kings and nations (Ps 71:8, 11; Ps 2:8, 10–11; Collect)

- *The words.* The Gospel denies the kingdom is of or from this world. The
  Gradual sings *adorabunt eum omnes reges terrae: omnes gentes servient ei*;
  the Offertory gives the nations and the ends of the earth, in a psalm that
  goes on *Et nunc, reges, intelligite … Servite Domino*; the Collect prays
  *ut cunctae familiae gentium … eius suavissimo subdantur imperio*. The
  instituting act draws from the feast that *rulers and princes are bound to
  give public honor and obedience to Christ* (n. 32; also nn. 18–19).
- *Augustine.* *Of* and *from*, not *in* and *here*: the kingdom is the
  believers, here until the end, gathered out of the world (Col 1:13); *I
  interfere not with your government in this world* (*Tract.* 115.2). The same
  author says that **the authority of kings leads men into the fellowship of
  the Church** (*En.* 71.13), that kings adore as members of the poor people
  who confess their sins (71.14), that kings should not be sad *as if your
  excellency were taken from you*, for it is expedient for them to be under
  him (*En.* 2.9), and that **a king serves God as king by just laws**, since
  the fulfilment of *All kings shall fall down before Him; all nations shall
  serve Him* began (*Letter* 185.19–20). Outcome: Augustine supports both the
  denial of a worldly origin and a positive service of kings, rendered in the
  exercise of their office. The *Letter* defends imperial laws against the
  Donatists; that is its occasion and must be stated wherever it is cited.
- *Chrysostom.* He rules here; the kingdom's empire is from above, not human,
  needing no servants to defend it; that is why he could be bound by consent.
- *Bellarmine.* The kings are exhorted *to freely subject themselves* to the
  supreme king of kings, correcting judgment and then affection, serving as
  sons (Ps 2:10–12); the adoration of Ps 71:11 is fulfilled partly in the
  conversion of princes and nations, to the letter at the judgment, and always
  in his actual power over princes *with or without their consent*.
- *Theodoret* [supporting]: *some willingly in the present life, but all after
  the resurrection* (Heb 2:8; Phil 2:10); by his laws he restrains the powerful
  and makes them merciful to the poor.
- *Cassiodorus* [supporting]: *omnes reges* means all nations; kings adore who
  are thought to be adored; and the kings of Ps 2:10 are the rulers of vices.
- *Aquinas.* Both senses of *regnum*; *non est hinc … est tamen hic, quia
  ubique est*, with Ps 2:8 and Dan 7:14 (*Super Io.*); and on Ps 2:10–11,
  reporting Augustine, the king serves as man by living justly and as king by
  laws; the psalm prefigures kings who first made laws against Christ and then
  for him (*Super Ps.* 2 n. 9).
- *Schuster* (liturgical reception, p. 203): the kingdom *comprehends the
  world*; Jesus does not dethrone the sovereigns of the earth, but gives rulers
  and subjects the laws of truth and justice; those who govern should help the
  Church *within the bounds of civil authority*.
- *What changes.* The subjection the formulary asks is not answered by the
  Gospel's denial alone. On the checked witnesses it has three moments: the
  free conversion of kings and nations, who come into the poor people that
  confesses its sins (Augustine 71.13–14; Bellarmine; Theodoret's *willingly*);
  the service of rulers *as rulers*, in the exercise of their own office
  (Augustine, *Letter* 185.19; Aquinas, *Super Ps.* 2 n. 9; *Quas primas* nn.
  18–19, 32); and the universal subjection at the judgment, when every knee
  bends (Theodoret, Bellarmine, Schuster). Beneath all three is a dominion that
  depends on no one's consent (Bellarmine; Cyril of Alexandria). None of these
  witnesses makes Christ a competitor for Caesar's throne: the origin and the
  means are not from hence (Augustine, Chrysostom), *he did not, nor does he
  today, interfere with those who possess* (n. 17), and *Jesus does not come to
  dethrone the sovereigns of the earth* (Schuster). On Augustine's sense of
  *regnum*, the Collect's *familiae gentium … subdantur* is the nations'
  gathering into the Church; on Chrysostom's, it is their subjection to a power
  whose origin and kind no earthly kingdom rivals.

### 5.3 *Traderet* the kingdom (Preface) against *non auferetur* (Dan 7:14) and *in aeternum* (Ps 28:10)

- *The words.* The Preface says the Son was anointed so that he might hand over
  an eternal and universal kingdom to the Father's majesty; the Alleluia says
  his power shall not be taken away, the Communion that he shall sit King for
  ever.
- *Cyril of Jerusalem.* Against any who say Christ's reign ends, Dan 7:13–14
  and Lk 1:33; *until* sets no term; the Son's subjection is free accord.
- *Augustine.* *When He shall have delivered up the kingdom* means *when He
  shall have brought believers to the contemplation of God*; the faithful he
  redeemed with his blood are called his kingdom; he does not take it from
  himself; as priest, in the form of a servant, he will be subject.
- *Hilary.* He delivers the kingdom not by resigning his power but by
  delivering us, made the kingdom by the glorifying of his body; then he is
  subjected *that God may be all in all*, translating his body through the
  subjection, not casting it off.
- *The Communion's session, on the new witnesses.* Augustine: he dwells first
  in the flood of this world in his saints and *afterward* sits reigning in
  them for ever. Basil: the baptismal flood makes the cleansed soul a throne
  where God is seated now. Cassiodorus [supporting]: the Trinity dwells in the
  baptismal waters, and *sedebit* is the Son's. Aquinas: the session is God's
  judging from the beginning, and mystically the converted world brought into
  his temple, the Church secure in the ark, or grace in the font; strength for
  progress, peace for the end. Theodoret [supporting]: the new creation after
  the torrent of lawlessness, where impiety has no more place. **Bellarmine:
  the session follows the judgment, when *all power in general* is swept
  away** — a judgment-centred reading different from Augustine's continuous
  one (§6.8). Schuster (liturgical reception): at Communion Jesus is enthroned
  in the faithful heart.
- *What changes.* The handing over is a consummation, not an abdication: the
  kingdom that is handed over is the redeemed themselves, so the eternal
  reign of the Communion and the handing over of the Preface name one end,
  the vision of God. The Communion's *sedebit* has, on these witnesses, a
  beginning now (in the baptized soul, Basil; in the communicant, Schuster;
  in the saints amid the flood, Augustine) and a completion after the judgment
  (Augustine's *afterward*; Bellarmine). Bellarmine's sweeping away of *all
  power* matches in thought the Preface's *suo subiectis imperio omnibus
  creaturis* and 1 Cor 15:24; Bellarmine does not cite that verse here, and the
  join is editorial.
- *Disagreement within it.* Hilary's XI.40 says he *must now become God only
  … that no trace of the nature of His earthly body may remain in Him*, while
  insisting the body is transfigured, not lost; Augustine I.8.15 reports,
  without adopting it, the view that the human nature will be converted into
  the Godhead, and I.10.20 keeps the form of the servant, the Priest, as what
  is subject. The difference is kept (§6.4).

### 5.4 *In hoc natus sum* (Jn 18:37)

Augustine: the temporal nativity from the Virgin; Chrysostom: the eternal
generation, *born a king*; Aquinas presents both and adds that *ad hoc veni in
mundum* is the temporal coming even on Chrysostom's reading; Cyril of
Alexandria reads *came into this world* as *when I became Man*. The difference
decides whether the Gospel grounds the kingship in the Incarnation or in the
eternal Sonship. (On Ps 2:7 Augustine takes the eternal side, §5.1; on Jn
18:37 the temporal: the two loci must not be merged into one Augustinian
position.)

### 5.5 *Primogenitus omnis creaturae* (Col 1:15)

Chrysostom: *firstborn*, not *first created*, means before all, and *firstborn
from the dead* means firstfruits of the resurrection. Aquinas: the Word
begotten as principle of every creature, against the Arians' first creature.
Theodoret [supporting]: born before all creation, not creation's brother, for
he is its maker. Hippolytus distinguishes three first-births (of God, of a
virgin, from the dead). All meet the Arian use of the verse; Athanasius on it
was not read (§7). Theodoret and Aquinas both turn at v. 18 to the humanity:
head *according to the humanity* (Theodoret), *secundum quod huiusmodi*
(Aquinas), which bears on reading A (§5.1).

## 6. Material disagreements preserved

1. **Received kingship or kingship by nature; and what Ps 2:7 is.** Hilary,
   Augustine (*En. in Ps.* 2.7), Cassiodorus, Theodoret, Aquinas, Bellarmine
   and Jerome read the receiving and the asking (Ps 2:8; Dan 7:14) of the
   humanity exalted through the passion; Chrysostom (*Hom. in Io.* 83) and
   Cyril of Alexandria insist that the kingship is by generation and nature and
   nothing was added, and Chrysostom adds the rule that a text of giving to the
   Son (Jn 5:26, *and so of the rest*) is to be referred to his generation.
   *Quas primas* n. 13 affirms both titles. Within the first
   group, **v. 7 divides them**: Augustine, Cassiodorus and Aquinas read
   *hodie genui te* of the eternal generation; Hilary of the resurrection (Acts
   13:33); Theodoret of the humanity (*as man he receives this voice*);
   Bellarmine of three generations. Augustine's and Aquinas's distribution
   (v. 7 natural, v. 8 acquired) is the one that keeps Chrysostom's premise;
   it meets his inference not on his ground but with a different rule for
   texts of asking and giving, which Cassiodorus states and Theodoret applies
   (the humanity is shown), and which Chrysostom's own rule (the generation is
   meant) reverses. That narrower disagreement stands (§5.1). Hilary's is not to be presented as the whole account, nor
   Augustine as supporting Hilary on v. 7.
2. **What *regnum* is in Jn 18:36.** The people ruled (Augustine) or the royal
   power (Chrysostom); Aquinas keeps both, and in *Super Col.* joins Col 1:13
   to Jn 18:36 in the first sense.
3. **What *natus sum* is in Jn 18:37.** Temporal (Augustine) or eternal
   (Chrysostom).
4. **The subjection of the Son (1 Cor 15:28).** Hilary's language of becoming
   *God only* against Augustine's keeping of the servant's form as what is
   subject.
5. **Kingship and civil rule.** Augustine (*Tract.* 115.2) and Chrysostom
   (83.4) make the denial to Pilate a reassurance to earthly rulers; Augustine
   also gives kings an office in the Church's spread (*En.* 71.13) and a
   service as kings (*Letter* 185.19–20); *Quas primas* n. 17 calls it a grave
   error to say Christ has no authority in civil affairs, while holding that he
   did not and does not interfere with those who possess earthly goods, and
   nn. 18–19 and 32 draw a public duty of rulers from the kingship. These are
   different questions answered compatibly, not a contradiction, and the
   studies do not dramatize them; but a reading that used Augustine's
   *Tractate* to deny any public consequence, or the encyclical or Augustine's
   *Letter* to make the Gospel a political claim, would misstate them.
6. **When the kings serve.** Augustine and Bellarmine see it begun in history
   (converted princes, laws for Christ); Theodoret divides it, *some willingly
   now, all after the resurrection*; Schuster: *de jure* complete, *de facto*
   incomplete until the judgment. Complementary emphases, kept as such.
7. **The literal sense of Ps 71:1.** Bellarmine: David's prayer for Solomon,
   *just anointed king*, the passage to Christ coming only at *he shall
   continue with the sun*; Augustine, Cassiodorus and Theodoret: the title is
   figurative and the psalm fits Solomon in no way. A real difference about the
   Introit verse's literal sense. `research/interpretations.md` states it in
   Reading A's literal sense and in Reading B's Introit contribution
   (Bellarmine's literal sense, *kings were created for the people*); Reading C
   does not turn on the verse's literal sense and does not state it.
8. **What *diluvium* is, and when the Lord sits King for ever (Ps 28:10).** The
   deluge of Noe and God's judgments from the beginning (Aquinas's Hebrew sense;
   *some*, says Bellarmine); the flood of this world or of lawlessness
   (Augustine; Aquinas's second mystical sense; Theodoret); the baptismal
   waters (Basil; Cassiodorus; Aquinas's third; *others*, says Bellarmine).
   The session for ever: in the saints now and afterward (Augustine), in the
   baptized soul as throne (Basil), in the communicant (Schuster), in the new
   creation (Theodoret), or after the judgment when all power is swept away
   (Bellarmine). Reading C keeps both the present and the judgment-centred
   senses and attributes each.
9. **How far the service of kings goes.** Augustine's *Letter* 185 extends the
   kings' service to religious laws and their enforcement, in the Donatist
   controversy; Aquinas reports that view; Schuster (p. 203) recalls the
   medieval papal deposition of unworthy kings. Bellarmine and Theodoret ask
   free subjection now and foresee universal subjection at the judgment. The
   readings rely on Augustine's distinction between serving as man and as king
   and on *Quas primas* nn. 18–19, 32; they do not adopt Augustine's coercive
   occasion or Schuster's deposition paragraph as the formulary's sense, and a
   study that cites either must state its context.
