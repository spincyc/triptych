\runninghead{Three Interpretations Compared}
\section{Where the Three Interpretations Agree and Differ}
\label{sec:comparison}

The three interpretations take up the three questions set out at the opening,
and each starts from different texts of the Mass.

\begin{comparisontable}{}{The Lamb receives the kingdom}{The kingdom from above}{The kingdom handed over}
Question &
How does the Lamb receive what the Son always had? &
Where is a kingdom not of this world, and how does it claim kings and nations? &
How can a kingdom handed over have no end?\\
Texts it starts from &
Introit, Offertory &
Gospel, Gradual, Collect &
Preface, Alleluia, Communion\\
Answer &
King by nature as God, he receives as man, through the cross, the same
dominion: one dominion by two rights &
Its origin and means are from above, so it is here in the faithful of every
nation; kings serve it freely, in their own office, and all at the judgment &
The kingdom handed over is the redeemed, brought to the vision of God; the
session begins in the cleansed soul and is completed after the judgment\\
Principal authors &
Hilary, Aquinas, Bede &
Augustine, Chrysostom, Bellarmine &
Augustine, Hilary\\
What it asks of the faithful &
To ask, and to share the King's self-offering &
To hear the truth and serve him in one's own office; to fight against sin
under the Cross &
Purity of heart for the vision\\
Strongest objection &
Chrysostom and Cyril of Alexandria: nothing received in addition; for
Chrysostom every text of giving refers to the generation &
That the Gospel's denial leaves the subjection no public consequence, or that
the subjection makes Christ a rival of Caesar &
That the reign ends when the kingdom is handed over: the teaching Cyril of
Jerusalem opposes, and Jerome answers from Dan 7:14\\
Where its authors differ &
On Ps 2:7: Hilary reads the resurrection, Augustine the eternal generation;
Theodoret applies the verse to the humanity &
On Jn 18:36, the people ruled (Augustine) or the royal power (Chrysostom); on
Jn 18:37, the birth from the Virgin (Augustine) or the eternal generation
(Chrysostom) &
Hilary and Augustine on the Son's subjection; Augustine and Bellarmine on
when he sits\\
\end{comparisontable}

All three identify the kingdom with the redeemed people, the Church. The cross
has a different place in each. In the first interpretation it is the means by
which the kingdom is received: the asking of the second psalm is made by his
prayer and his passion, and the Secret's victim is the same sacrifice offered
now. In the second it is
the standard of the Postcommunion, which Schuster's note on that prayer names
as the Cross; the second interpretation takes the faithful's fight under it to
be a fight against sin. In the third it is the priestly offering that the Preface
sets before the handing over.

They also attend to different times. The first looks to the passion
and the exaltation, when the kingship is received; the second to the present
Church, mixed with tares, and the service kings owe now; the third to the end,
the handing over and the eternal session.

The first and second interpretations differ most over the Gospel, which both
read of the same prisoner. For the first, the bound King is acquiring the
dominion of Ps 2:8 in the trial itself: he is captive ``by consenting, and
giving Himself up,'' in Chrysostom's words. For the second, the same prisoner
declares a kingdom whose power is from above and is ``sufficient for itself,
needing nothing,'' as Chrysostom says, a King as God ``whether man so will, or
no,'' as Cyril of Alexandria says. The first speaks of a dominion received, the
second of a dominion that needs nothing given. They part again at Jn 18:37.
Chrysostom's reading of ``For this was I born'' of the eternal generation is
the first interpretation's strongest objection: born a king, the Son ``has
nothing which He received in addition.'' The second holds with either birth.
Augustine reads Ps 2:7 of the eternal generation and Jn 18:37 of the birth from
the Virgin: the eternal birth grounds the decree of the psalm, and the temporal
birth the testimony before Pilate.

Each accounts for texts that the other two pass over lightly. Only the first
explains why the eternal Son is said to receive divinity in the Introit and is
bidden to ask in the Offertory, and it keeps that receiving from making the
kingship begin at Easter by Augustine's division of the two verses of the
psalm. The second is the one that says what the kingdom asks now of kings and
nations, in the time between the conversion of some and the homage of all, and
so gives the Collect's subjection and the Gradual's kings a present sense. The
third explains the Preface's handing over and the Communion's session for
ever, which the other two touch only in passing and could leave as ornaments
of a kingdom wholly present in history.

Each also draws on a different clause of the Preface's one sentence. The
first takes the anointing of the Son as King before the cross and his
offering of himself on it: the kingship he has by right of inheritance, and
the dominion he holds by right of merit, won by the passion. The second's subject, the subjection of kings and nations, falls under
the clause that comes next, all creatures subjected to his rule, and it takes
from the list of the kingdom's goods the first, truth. The third takes the last
clause, the eternal and universal kingdom handed over to the Father's
majesty, which it reads as the redeemed brought to the vision of God. The
clauses follow one another in the sentence, from the anointing to the handing
over, and so the three answers can stand together: each explains a different
stage of the one kingship.
