\runninghead{Themes and Movement}
\section{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\zlabel{triptych:concise:themes:start}

The chants of this Mass acclaim a King who rules everywhere and for ever: the
slain Lamb is worthy to receive power and divinity, he shall rule from sea to
sea and all kings shall adore him, the Son is promised the nations,
and the Lord shall sit King for ever. The Gospel seems to contradict them:
the King is a bound prisoner before Pilate and says that his kingdom is
not of this world and that his servants do not fight for him. Yet the Postcommunion asks that the faithful \latin{militare}, fight as
soldiers, under his standards. The texts raise two other questions: how
the eternal Son can receive divinity and be told to ask for the
nations, and how the kingdom he hands over to the Father in the Preface can be
one that shall not be destroyed.

\subsection{The throne and the nations}

The Introit begins where the Apocalypse has just shown a sealed book that no
one in heaven or on earth was worthy to open. The elders have sung to the Lamb,
``thou wast slain and hast redeemed us to God, in thy blood'' (Apoc 5:9), and
the angels take up the acclamation the antiphon keeps: \latin{Dignus est
Agnus, qui occisus est, accipere virtutem, et divinitatem}. Of the
acclamation's seven gifts the antiphon sings five and leaves out the last two,
``and glory and benediction.'' It adds, from John's greeting to the churches,
``to him be glory and empire for ever and ever'' (1:6), and passes over the
words before them in the same verse, ``And hath made us a kingdom, and priests
to God and his Father.'' Its verse asks God to give: it is the first line of
Psalm~71, ``Give to the king thy judgment, O God, and to the king's son thy
justice.''

The Collect prays for the peoples of the earth. God willed to restore all
things in his beloved Son, \latin{universorum Rege}, the King of all; its verb,
\latin{instaurare}, is the Vulgate's word for the summing up of all things in
Christ (Eph 1:10), and \latin{dilecto Filio} is the ``Son of his love'' of the
Epistle (Col 1:13). The prayer then names the condition of the peoples. They
are \latin{familiae gentium}, families of the nations, and they are
\latin{peccati vulnere disgregatae}: their scattering is a wound, and sin
inflicted it. Schuster reads the prayer as inspired by St Paul: sin is ``a
disruptive force'' that ruined the unity of the human race, which Christ the
new Adam restores (p.~202). What the prayer asks for the nations is
subjection, \latin{eius suavissimo subdantur imperio}, and it names the quality
of the rule first: it is most gentle.

\subsection{Translated into a kingdom that reaches every shore}

In the Epistle Paul, writing in bonds, gives thanks for those already brought
under that rule, to the Father ``who hath delivered us from the power of darkness and hath translated
us into the kingdom of the Son of his love'' (Col 1:13). Paul's hymn then says
who the King is: the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of every
creature, in whom thrones and dominations were created, the head of the body,
the Church, the firstborn from the dead, through whom the Father reconciles all
things, ``making peace through the blood of his cross'' (1:15--20).

The Gradual takes two verses of Psalm~71 and leaves out those between them,
in which the Ethiopians fall down before the king, his enemies lick the
ground, and the kings of Tharsis, the islands, Arabia and Saba bring gifts:
``he shall rule from sea to sea, and from the river unto the ends of the
earth''; ``all kings of the earth shall adore him: all nations shall serve
him'' (71:8, 11). Cassiodorus argues that the dominion from sea to sea cannot belong to Solomon,
who reigned in Judaea alone, and that from the river, the Jordan,
\latin{per totum orbem terrarum saluberrima regula sacri baptismatis
emanavit}, the saving rule of holy baptism flowed out through the whole world
(\work{Expositio Psalmorum} 71).
The Alleluia comes from Daniel's night vision, in which one like a son of man
comes with the clouds of heaven to the Ancient of days. It sings the second
half of the verse: ``his power is an everlasting power that shall not be taken
away: and his kingdom that shall not be destroyed'' (Dan 7:14). It keeps the
power as possessed and lasting and passes over the words that begin the verse,
``And he gave him power.'' Blessed Ildefonso Schuster reads the two chants
together as ``the double chant after the Epistle,'' a threefold catholicity:
of place, from sea to sea; of subjects, all kings and all nations; and of ages,
a power that shall not be taken away (\work{The Sacramentary} V, p.~203).

\subsection{The King before the governor}

Jesus has been brought early in the morning before the Pasch into the
praetorium, which his accusers will not enter, so that Pilate goes
in and out between them and the prisoner (Jn 18:28--29). ``Art thou the king of
the Jews?'' Jesus answers with a question of his own; Pilate replies that the
prisoner's own nation has delivered him up. Jesus answers: ``My kingdom is not
of this world. If my kingdom were of this world, my servants would certainly
strive that I should not be delivered to the Jews: but now my kingdom is not
from hence.'' The Latin makes the denial three times, \latin{de hoc mundo},
\latin{ex hoc mundo}, \latin{hinc}; the Douay renders the first two alike and
the third ``from hence.'' Pressed again, Jesus answers, ``Thou sayest that I am
a king. For this was I born, and for this came I into the world; that I should
give testimony to the truth. Every one that is of the truth heareth my voice''
(18:36--37). The pericope stops before ``What is truth?''

\subsection{The sacrifice and the end of the kingdom}

The Offertory is a verse of the second psalm. The psalm opens with the nations
raging and the kings of the earth standing up against the Lord and his Christ;
then the king set on Sion recites the decree, ``Thou art my son, this day have
I begotten thee,'' and the Lord goes on, ``Ask of me, and I will give thee the
Gentiles for thy inheritance, and the utmost parts of the earth for thy
possession'' (Ps 2:7--8). The antiphon sings the second verse only, but it is
spoken to the Son of the first, and the psalm continues by bidding the kings of
the earth understand and ``serve ye the Lord with fear'' (2:10--11).

The Secret names the offering by its effect. It is \latin{hostiam \dots\
humanae reconciliationis}, the victim of mankind's reconciliation, the
Son himself, \latin{quem sacrificiis praesentibus immolamus}.
It asks that he give all nations \latin{unitatis et pacis dona}, the
gifts of unity and peace, which the Collect's scattered families lack. The
proper Preface is a single sentence: the Father anointed his only-begotten Son
with the oil of gladness (Ps 44:8; Heb 1:9), eternal Priest and King of all
things, so that, offering himself on the altar of the cross as a spotless and
peace-making victim, he might accomplish the mysteries of man's redemption,
and, all creatures being subjected to his rule, deliver to the Father's
majesty ``an eternal and universal kingdom, a kingdom of truth and life; a
kingdom of holiness and grace, a kingdom of justice, love and peace,'' in the
English of the Lasance missal. The verb, \latin{traderet}, is Paul's
``when he shall have delivered up the kingdom to God and the Father'' (1~Cor
15:24).

The Communion sings two half-verses of Psalm~28: ``the Lord shall sit king for ever \dots\ the Lord will bless his
people with peace'' (28:10--11).
It leaves unsung the half-verses before and between them: the flood that the
Lord makes to dwell, and the strength he gives his people. The Postcommunion,
said after \latin{immortalitatis alimoniam}, the food of immortality, asks
that those who serve as the King's soldiers may reign with him for ever
\latin{in caelesti sede}.

Three interpretations of the whole Mass take up these questions. The first, the Lamb who receives the kingdom, starts from the
Introit's verb and the Offertory's command. St~Hilary of Poitiers answers that the risen Son asks back the glory he
had before; St~Thomas Aquinas, that the Son holds the nations by a right of
birth and by a right won in prayer and passion; St~Bede, that the Lamb who dies
and the Lion who conquers are one Lord. The second, the kingdom from above,
starts from the Gospel and the chants of universal
dominion. St~Augustine answers that it is not of this world but is in it;
St~John Chrysostom, that its empire is from above and needs no servants to
defend it; St~Robert Bellarmine, that the kings are bidden to subject
themselves freely and will all bend at the judgment. The third, the kingdom
handed over, starts from the Preface, the Alleluia and the
Communion. Augustine answers that
the kingdom handed over is the faithful, brought to the contemplation of God;
Hilary, that the Son delivers to God not his power but us, made his kingdom by
the glorifying of his body.
