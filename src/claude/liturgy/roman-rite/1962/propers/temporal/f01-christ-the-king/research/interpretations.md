# Proposed interpretations of the whole formulary

Research stage of `proper-study` v12 run `7397c12187517ff2`, 19 October 2026,
revised in iterations 1 and 2, for
`liturgy/roman-rite/1962/propers/temporal/f01-christ-the-king`. This record
proposes three readings of the Mass of Christ the King as the 1962 Missal
appoints it for Sunday 25 October 2026: eleven said elements (Introit, Collect,
Epistle, Gradual, Alleluia, Gospel, Offertory, Secret, the proper Preface,
Communion, Postcommunion). The votive Tract and the paschal Alleluia are
printed options not said on this date; they contribute to no reading and are
mentioned only where a witness's reasoning touches their verses.

Every locus cited here is read and recorded in `research/scope.md` (sections
in brackets). The Latin is in `propers/verified.md`. **The formulary is a
compilation**: every connection drawn between two elements is the editor's
synthesis unless a named witness is said to make it, and no Father is credited
with a whole-Mass reading. The witnesses who join appointed passages
themselves are marked *witness's own connection*:

- Augustine joins Jn 18:36 to Col 1:13 and Ps 2:6 (*Tract. in Io.* 115.2), and
  Ps 2:10–11 to Ps 71:11 (*Letter* 185.19–20);
- Aquinas joins Jn 18:36 to Dan 7:14 and Ps 2:8 (*Super Io.* 18 lect. 6), and
  Col 1:13 to Jn 18:36 and Apoc 5:10 (*Super Col.* 1 lect. 3);
- Hilary uses the vocabulary of Col 1:13 and 1:18 in expounding Ps 2:8
  (*Tract. in Ps.* 2.31);
- Chrysostom joins Col 1:20 to Eph 1:10, the Collect's source verse (*Hom. in
  Col.* 3), and Theodoret joins Col 1:16 to the same verse and Col 1:13 to
  2 Tim 2:12 (*In Col.* 1), and Ps 2:8 to Col 1:16 (*In Ps.* 2);
- Bellarmine joins Ps 71:11 to Apoc 1:5 and Apoc 19:16 (*Explanatio* 71).

None of them is commenting on this Mass, which did not exist before 1925. The
one witness read who comments on the Mass as a Mass is Blessed Ildefonso
Schuster (*The Sacramentary* V, 1930, pp. 200–204) [§3.1]. He treats the
elements one by one, in separate paragraphs, and each of his comments is cited
here under its own element and page. The one join between two elements that he
makes himself is the Gradual with the Alleluia as *the double chant after the
Epistle* (p. 203), marked *Schuster's own connection*. Every other grouping of
his comments in this record (for example, his Introit-verse, Preface and
Communion notes read together) is editorial synthesis over separate comments,
and the studies must not say that Schuster makes it.

Standing is as `src/sources/inventories/author-standing-v1.toml` records it.
Carrying authors are named by the inventory's own names; each reading has at
least two, of whom at least one is a Father or canonized saint. Witnesses with
no row there (Cyril of Alexandria, Cyril of Jerusalem, Hippolytus, Victorinus)
and the ecclesiastical writers Cassiodorus and Theodoret support claims and
carry no reading.

## 1. The questions the formulary forces

Three features of the appointed words are demanding enough that a reading
which passed over them would be thin.

1. **What is received.** The Introit opens with the slain Lamb *worthy to
   receive power and divinity*; the Offertory has the Father say *Ask of me,
   and I will give thee the Gentiles*, after *Thou art my Son, this day have I
   begotten thee*. Of the eternal Son both say he receives what he cannot lack.
   (*Introit, Offertory*; the Alleluia omits the half of Dan 7:14 that says
   *he gave him power*.)
2. **Where the kingdom is, and whom it subjects.** The Gospel has Jesus, bound,
   deny that his kingdom is *of this world* or *from hence*; the Gradual gives
   him dominion *from sea to sea* and sings that *all kings of the earth shall
   adore him, all nations shall serve him*; the Offertory gives him *the utmost
   parts of the earth* in a psalm that warns the kings to serve; the Collect
   prays that *all the families of the nations* be *subjected* to his rule. And
   the Postcommunion has us *militare* under his standards where the Gospel
   says his servants do not fight.
3. **How it ends.** The Preface says he was anointed priest and king so that,
   all creatures subjected, he might *hand over* the eternal and universal
   kingdom to the Father's majesty; the Alleluia says his power *shall not be
   taken away*, the Communion that he *shall sit King for ever*.

The three readings below each turn on one of these and let the other elements
contribute to it. They are complementary, not rival doctrines: they answer
*how he reigns as man*, *what kind of kingdom it is and how it claims the
nations*, and *where it is going*. Where their witnesses disagree, the
disagreement is stated.

## 2. Reading A — The slain Lamb receives the kingdom (`lamb-receives-the-kingdom`)

**Controlling claim.** The kingship this Mass acclaims belongs to the Son by
nature and is received by him in the humanity he assumed, asked for and won
through his death and given at his exaltation; its content is the nations he
redeemed, and the Mass's own sacrifice is where that received kingship is
exercised.

**Exegetical question.** How can the Lamb *receive* divinity and the Son *ask*
for the nations (difficulty 1)?

**Carrying authors.** Hilary of Poitiers; Thomas Aquinas; Bede.
**Supporting.** Augustine (*En. in Ps.* 2); Robert Bellarmine; Jerome;
Cassiodorus and Theodoret (ecclesiastical writers); Victorinus (no standing
row); Schuster (liturgical reception).

**The witnesses' reasoning.**

- *Hilary*, *Tract. in Ps.* 2.29–33, 42–43 [§3.8, §5.1]. The decree *Filius meus
  es tu, ego hodie genui te* is, on apostolic authority (Acts 13:33), the
  begetting of the firstborn from the dead; *data est mihi omnis potestas* (Mt
  28:18) shows that he asked for what he received, and *Posce a me* is that
  asking, made in Jn 17:1–2. What the one abiding in the form of God received,
  after the form of a servant and the death of the cross, was *ut esset, quod
  fuerat ante*. The inheritance is the nations baptized and taught, transferred
  from a perverse dominion *in regnum aeternum diuinumque*, the inheritance of
  *primogeniti huius ex mortuis*; and those made his co-heirs must become
  kings. Hilary gives the reading its fullest single statement on its own
  psalm, but he reads v. 7 of the resurrection, where Augustine and Aquinas
  read it of the eternal generation (below; §6.1).
- *Aquinas*, *Super Psalmos* 2 nn. 5–6 [§3.8, §5.1]. Christ holds the nations
  *duplici jure*: by **hereditary right**, from the eternal generation that
  v. 7 declares (*hodie genui te, idest aeternaliter*), and by **meritorious
  right**, which v. 8 declares: *voluit quod peteret, quod sibi jure hereditario
  competebat*, asking by his prayer (Jn 17:20) and by his passion (Heb 9:15),
  and holding the nations not as a minister but as lord. In *Super Col.* 1
  lect. 3–5 [§3.5] the redemption is his *inquantum homo, factus … pro nobis
  sacrificium*, and he is made head of the Church *secundum quod huiusmodi*,
  first in grace and first in glory.
- *Bede*, *Expl. Apoc.* on 1:5–6 and 5:5–10 [§3.2, §3.12]. The same Lord is a
  Lamb in dying innocently and a Lion in conquering death; the Son of man is
  said to have received from God's right hand the very dispensation of the
  Incarnation, disposed by the Father and by himself *secundum id quod Deus
  est*; a Lamb in his humanity and the Father's right hand in his deity; the
  first-begotten of the dead is the one crowned with glory for the suffering of
  death (Heb 2:9; Phil 2:9); and because *Rex regum et Sacerdos coelestis se
  offerendo pro nobis nos suo corpori adunavit*, every saint has a share in the
  priesthood. Bede gives the king-priest by self-offering for Apoc 1:6, whose
  first clause the Introit leaves out.
- *Augustine*, *En. in Ps.* 2.6–7 [§3.8]. *To-day have I begotten Thee* has *a
  divine interpretation*, the eternal generation; *Ask of me* has a temporal
  sense of the manhood, *who offered up Himself as a Sacrifice in the stead of
  all sacrifices*, so that the nations are joined to his Name and redeemed.
  This distribution — v. 7 eternal, v. 8 temporal — is the one Aquinas
  formulates as two rights.
- *Bellarmine*, *Explanatio* 2:7–8 [§3.8]. To the true and natural Son *is due
  all power in heaven and on earth*; v. 7 alludes to three generations,
  eternity, the flesh and the resurrection, and the incarnate Son raised from
  the dead has *just right to ask* for the nations; the kingdom is *his
  spiritual kingdom, that is, his Church*, over all nations, *infidels even
  included*.
- *Jerome*, *In Dan.* 7:13–14 [§3.6]. The receiving of power, honour and
  kingdom is to be understood by Phil 2:6–8; read so, it gives the Arians no
  ground.
- *Cassiodorus* [supporting], *Exp. Ps.* 2:8 and 71:1 [§3.8, §3.3]. *Quidquid
  in tempore accepit Christus, secundum hominem … acquirit*; *natura humanitatis
  accepit, quod semper divina possedit*; and whenever Christ asks or is asked
  to be given anything, *naturam humanitatis evidenter ostendi*.
- *Theodoret* [supporting], *In Ps.* 2:7–8 and 71:1 [§3.8, §3.3]. Lord by
  nature as God, *etiam ut homo universorum dominium accipit*; on the Introit
  verse, he has as God authority over all and receives it also as man from his
  own Father; and on Col 1:18 he is head of the body *according to the
  humanity* [§3.5].
- *Victorinus*, on Apoc 5:2–5 [§3.2]. Because he overcame death as testator, it
  was just that the Lamb be appointed heir and possess the human members.
- *Schuster* (liturgical reception), two separate comments [§3.1]. On the
  Introit (p. 201): *The exaltation of Jesus Christ is, according to St Paul,
  in proportion to the humiliation he endured*, and John recalls the Passion
  before the Lamb's glory. On the Offertory (p. 203): Christ shows the nations
  and kings who rise up against him (Ps 2:1–2) *the solemn deed of investiture
  which he has received from his Father*. Schuster does not relate the two
  paragraphs; reading them together under one theme of a received office is
  editorial synthesis.

**Contribution of every element** (editorial synthesis except where marked).

- *Introit.* The acclamation of the Lamb *qui occisus est* as worthy to receive
  is the reading's text; *Ipsi gloria et imperium* is what he receives. The
  verse *Deus, iudicium tuum Regi da* is a giving to the King and King's Son,
  which Augustine reads of the Father giving all judgment to the Son (Jn 5:22)
  and Theodoret of the authority he has as God and receives as man [§3.3].
- *Collect.* The Father *willed* to restore all things in his beloved Son; the
  petition is that the nations wounded by sin be subjected to his rule: the
  inheritance asked for, still being given.
- *Epistle.* Redemption *through his blood*, peace *through the blood of his
  cross*, *firstborn from the dead*: the means and the moment of the receiving,
  in the vocabulary Hilary uses of Ps 2:8; the head of the Church *according
  to the humanity* (Theodoret) and *secundum quod huiusmodi* (Aquinas) [§3.5].
- *Gradual.* Dominion from sea to sea and the kings' homage are the
  inheritance in possession.
- *Alleluia.* The Missal keeps only *potestas eius, potestas aeterna*: the power
  as possessed, not the giving that Jerome expounds by Phil 2; the reading
  notes the cut and does not lean on the omitted half.
- *Gospel.* The king stands bound before Pilate, the passion already begun;
  Chrysostom says he was made captive *by consenting, and giving Himself up*
  (*Hom. in Io.* 83.4) [§3.7]. The kingship is being acquired in the very act
  of the trial.
- *Offertory.* *Postula a me* is the reading's centre (Hilary, Aquinas,
  Augustine, Bellarmine), the decree of v. 7 its premise; for Schuster, on this
  antiphon alone, the deed of investiture received from the Father (p. 203).
- *Secret.* *Hostiam … humanae reconciliationis*, *quem sacrificiis praesentibus
  immolamus*: the sacrifice by which, Augustine and Aquinas say, the asking was
  made is offered now, and the gift asked of him is the nations' unity and
  peace.
- *Preface.* Anointed *Sacerdotem aeternum et universorum Regem*, he offers
  himself on the altar of the cross *ut … redemptionis humanae sacramenta
  perageret*, and only then, *suo subiectis imperio omnibus creaturis*, hands
  over the kingdom: the order of the Preface is the order of this reading.
- *Communion.* *Sedebit Dominus Rex in aeternum*: the session of the exalted
  King.
- *Postcommunion.* Those who fight under his standards ask to reign with him:
  Hilary's co-heirs who become kings, Bede's members who share the priesthood;
  the standard is the Cross, *for the kingdom of Christ is that of obedience,
  humility and sacrifice* (Schuster on the Postcommunion, p. 204; the join to
  Hilary and Bede is editorial).

**Four senses.**

- *Literal.* An apocalyptic hymn acclaims the slain Lamb as worthy of power; a
  royal psalm prays that God give judgment to the king and the king's son
  (David's prayer for Solomon on Bellarmine's literal reading, of Christ alone
  on Augustine's, Cassiodorus's and Theodoret's) and another has God promise
  the nations to his Son who asks; Paul thanks the Father for redemption
  through the blood of the Son's cross; Jesus, bound, admits before the
  governor that he is a king.
- *Allegorical.* Christ, God from eternity and King by that right, receives as
  man through his passion and resurrection the kingdom over the nations, which
  is the Church gathered from them; the Lamb and the Lion are one Lord (Bede).
- *Moral.* Kingship is received through obedience unto death, and God wills
  that even what is owed be asked (Aquinas): the faithful come into the kingdom
  by asking and by offering themselves with the Priest-King, as members who
  share his priesthood (Bede) and as co-heirs who rule sin in themselves
  (Hilary 2.42–43).
- *Anagogical.* The firstborn from the dead is the first of the exalted; his
  members will be raised and reign with him on the heavenly throne
  (Postcommunion).

**Strongest difficulty.** Chrysostom, *Hom. in Io.* 83.4: *If then He was born a
king, all His other attributes are by Generation, and He has nothing which He
received in addition*, and at once the rule he draws from it: *So that when
you hear that, As the Father has life in Himself, so has He given to the Son
also to have life* (Jn 5:26), *deem of nothing else but His generation, and so
of the rest* [§3.7]; Cyril of Alexandria, *In Io.* XII: his sovereignty is *not
… added unto it from without*. A reading that said Christ became king only at
Easter would contradict them and the faith they defend. The answer comes from
Augustine's distribution of Ps 2:7–8, which Aquinas states as two rights and
Cassiodorus and Theodoret as *possessed as God, received as man*: the kingship
by generation is the premise of the decree and is not received; what is asked
and received is the same dominion held in the humanity and won by the passion
[§5.1]. That answer grants Chrysostom's premise, but it does not meet him on his
own ground. His rule refers a text of giving to the eternal generation;
Cassiodorus's rule (*quotiescunque … sibi petit dari … naturam humanitatis
evidenter ostendi*, on Ps 71:1, applied by him to Ps 2:8 as *secundum
hominem*) and Theodoret's (*etiam ut homo universorum dominium accipit*) refer
the asking of v. 8 to the humanity. Chrysostom's locus does not share that rule,
and the disagreement over how to read texts of giving and asking stays
[§6.1]. Hilary's *ut esset, quod fuerat ante* says the same of the
humanity's glorifying, but his and Bellarmine's resurrection reading of v. 7
must not be presented as the beginning of the Sonship, and Augustine must not
be cited as supporting Hilary on v. 7 [§6.1]. *Quas primas* n. 13 states both
titles, by nature and by acquired right, and that *to him as man angels and
men are subject* [§3.13]. The reading keeps the kingship by nature as its
premise and does not let *accipere* mean that the Lamb becomes divine.

**Limit.** No witness read comments on Apoc 5:12's *accipere divinitatem*
itself [§3.2]; bringing the Ps 2 and Dan 7 reasoning to the Introit is
editorial synthesis (Schuster's reading of the Introit is reception, not
exegesis of the verb).

## 3. Reading B — Not from hence, yet claiming the nations: the kingdom of truth (`kingdom-not-from-hence`)

**Controlling claim.** Christ's kingdom takes neither its origin nor its means
from this world, and for that reason it reaches everywhere in it and claims
everyone in it: its subjects are those who hear the truth, gathered from every
nation, and the kings and nations whose subjection the Collect asks and whose
service the Gradual sings are brought under a rule whose instrument is truth
and grace. That rule takes no earthly throne for itself, yet rulers owe it, as
rulers, the service of their own office; it is begun by free conversion now
and completed at the judgment.

**Exegetical question.** How can a kingdom *not of this world* and *not from
hence* claim dominion *from sea to sea*, the nations as inheritance and the
service of all kings (difficulty 2)?

**Carrying authors.** Augustine of Hippo; John Chrysostom; Robert Bellarmine.
**Supporting.** Thomas Aquinas; Cyril of Alexandria (no standing row);
Theodoret and Cassiodorus (ecclesiastical writers); Schuster (liturgical
reception); *Quas primas* nn. 17–20, 32 (instituting act).

**The witnesses' reasoning.**

- *Augustine*, *Tract. in Io.* 115.1–4 [§3.7, §5.2]. The counter-question
  exposes the vain fear of Jews and Gentiles alike; *hear, all ye kingdoms of
  the earth: I interfere not with your government in this world*. He did not
  say *not in this world* but *not of this world*, not *not here* but *not from
  hence*: the kingdom is those who believe, here until the end with the tares,
  a stranger sojourning, gathered out of the world, as God *rescued us from
  the power of darkness and translated us into the kingdom of the Son of His
  love* (Col 1:13, *witness's own connection*); Ps 2:6 is the king on Sion
  (*witness's own connection*). *Thou sayest* neither denies nor concedes a
  carnal kingship. *Every one that is of the truth heareth my voice* commends
  the grace that calls.
- *Augustine*, *En. in Ps.* 71.11–14 [§3.4]. *From sea to sea* is the whole
  earth; *from the river* is the Jordan, from which the Gospel of the kingdom
  went out to all nations. The gifts the kings *lead* *signify men, whom into
  the fellowship of the Church of Christ the authority of kings doth lead*,
  though persecuting kings, unknowing, led the martyrs as gifts (71.13); the
  kings adore him because he delivered the poor, and they belong to that poor
  people, humbly confessing their sins (71.14).
- *Augustine*, *En. in Ps.* 2.8–9 and *Letter* 185.19–20 [§3.8, §3.4]. *Be ye not
  sad, kings of the earth, as if your excellency were taken from you … it is
  expedient for you, that ye should be under Him*; and *a man serves God in
  one way in that he is man, in another way in that he is also king*, the
  king by just laws, since the fulfilment of *All kings shall fall down before
  Him; all nations shall serve Him* began (Ps 2:10–11 with Ps 71:11, *witness's
  own connection*). The *Letter* defends imperial laws against the Donatists;
  the reading takes from it the distinction of man and king, not its coercive
  occasion [§6.9].
- *Chrysostom*, *Hom. in Io.* 83.4, 84.1 [§3.7]. He rules here; his empire is
  from above, not human, and far greater; earthly kingship shows its weakness
  in needing servants who fight, while the kingdom above needs nothing; *I am
  indeed a King, yet not such an one as you suspect*.
- *Bellarmine*, *Explanatio* 2:10–12 and 71:8–11 [§3.8, §3.4]. The prophet
  *exhorts the kings of this world … to freely subject themselves to him, the
  true and supreme king of all kings*, correcting first their judgment, then
  their affection, serving as sons; the adoration of all kings is fulfilled
  *to a certain extent* in the conversion of *a great many princes and
  nations*, *to the letter* at the judgment, and *always* in his actual power
  over princes *with or without their consent* (Mt 28:18; Apoc 1:5; Apoc 19:16,
  *witness's own connection*); they serve him because through him they are
  delivered from the devil's tyranny into *his own most peaceful kingdom*.
- *Aquinas*, *Super Io.* 18 lect. 6 [§3.7]; *Super Ps.* 2 n. 9 [§3.8]; *Super
  Col.* 1 lect. 3 [§3.5]. *Regnum* is the faithful (Augustine) or the royal
  power, whose origin is not from worldly causes (Chrysostom, with Dan 7:14b,
  *witness's own connection*); it is not from hence, *est tamen hic, quia
  ubique est* (with Ps 2:8 and Dan 7:14a, *witness's own connection*); on Ps
  2:10–11, reporting Augustine, the king serves as man by living justly and as
  king by laws; and Col 1:13's *transtulit* is *ut essemus regnum Dei*, with Jn
  18:36 and Apoc 5:10 (*witness's own connection*).
- *Cyril of Alexandria*, *In Io.* XII [§3.7]. Christ denied not his kingship but
  any enmity to Caesar's; he reigns as God whether men will or no.
- *Theodoret* [supporting], *In Ps.* 71:11–13 [§3.4]. *Some willingly in the
  present life, but all after the resurrection*: we see not yet all things
  subject to him (Heb 2:8); by his laws he restrains the powerful and makes
  them merciful to the poor.
- *Cassiodorus* [supporting], *Exp. Ps.* 71:11 [§3.4]. *All kings* means all
  nations; *kings adore, who are thought to be adored; nations serve, who are
  known to have earthly lords*.
- *Schuster* (liturgical reception) [§3.1]. On the Gradual (p. 202), the
  kingdom is *complete and absolute de jure, but … opposed and incomplete de
  facto*. *The double chant after the Epistle* sets out a threefold catholicity
  of place, subjects (*kings and princes or wretched pariahs*) and ages (p. 203;
  *Schuster's own connection* of Gradual and Alleluia). On the Gospel, in a
  separate paragraph (p. 203): *regnum meum non est hinc*, *but it comprehends
  the world*; *Jesus does not come to dethrone the sovereigns of the earth*,
  but gives rulers and subjects the laws of truth and justice.
- *Quas primas* (instituting act) [§3.13]. n. 17: a grave error to deny him
  authority in civil affairs, yet he did not and does not interfere with
  those who possess; n. 18: individuals, families and States are under his
  dominion, and rulers owe *the public duty of reverence and obedience*;
  n. 19: his office *invests the human authority of princes and rulers with a
  religious significance*; n. 20: the peace of the King who came *to reconcile
  all things* and *not to be ministered unto but to minister*; n. 32: *rulers
  and princes are bound to give public honor and obedience to Christ*.

**Contribution of every element.**

- *Gospel.* The reading's text: the bound prisoner, the *of* and *from*, the
  witness to truth, the hearers of his voice.
- *Gradual.* The earth-wide dominion is the Church through the world (Augustine,
  Cassiodorus, Theodoret: from the Jordan, the font of the Gospel and of
  baptism) [§3.4]; *adorabunt eum omnes reges terrae: omnes gentes servient
  ei* is the service of kings and nations, begun when rulers' authority leads
  men into the Church and kings serve as kings (Augustine 71.13; *Letter*
  185.20), fulfilled partly in converted princes and wholly at the judgment
  (Bellarmine; Theodoret).
- *Offertory.* The nations as inheritance, in a psalm whose next verses bid the
  kings *understand* and *serve the Lord with fear* (Ps 2:10–11): the free
  subjection Bellarmine reads there and the service as kings Augustine and
  Aquinas read there [§3.8].
- *Introit.* *Deus, iudicium tuum Regi da … Filio Regis*: the king's office is
  judgment and justice, Augustine's reading of the psalm [§3.3]; Bellarmine's
  literal sense adds that *kings were created for the people, not the people
  for kings* [§3.3]; *Dignus est Agnus*: the throne is the slain Lamb's, not a
  conqueror's.
- *Collect.* *Familiae gentium, peccati vulnere disgregatae*: what is scattered
  is scattered by sin, not by borders (Schuster: sin *a disruptive force*);
  they are to be subjected to his *suavissimo imperio*, a rule whose instrument
  is its sweetness, not force: the nations' gathering into the Church on
  Augustine's sense of *regnum*, their subjection to a power no earthly kingdom
  rivals on Chrysostom's [§5.2].
- *Epistle.* *Transtulit in regnum Filii dilectionis suae*: Augustine's own gloss
  of the Gospel, and Aquinas's *ut essemus regnum Dei* (*witness's own
  connections*); the kingdom is entered by being transferred out of the power
  of darkness.
- *Alleluia.* Dan 7:14b, cited by Aquinas for the origin and permanence of the
  power; for Schuster, in the double chant with the Gradual, the universality
  of the ages (p. 203).
- *Secret.* The gifts asked for *all nations* are *unity and peace*: the
  nations' gathering is a gift given at the altar, not a conquest.
- *Preface.* *Regnum veritatis et vitae … iustitiae, amoris et pacis*: the goods
  of a kingdom not from hence, and the first named is the truth to which the
  Gospel's king bears witness.
- *Communion.* *Benedicet populo suo in pace*: Augustine says the peace he
  promised is not peace in this world but peace in himself [§3.9]; Theodoret
  reads it of the peace given to the churches after the martyrs' war.
- *Postcommunion.* *Sub Christi Regis vexillis militare*: the one military
  figure in the Mass, set against the Gospel's servants who do not fight. This
  research resolves the tension by editorial synthesis from two witnesses who
  comment on neither text together: on Chrysostom's reasoning at Jn 18:36
  (*Hom. in Io.* 83.4), earthly kingship depends on servants who fight and
  Christ's does not, so the faithful's warfare is not his defence; on Hilary's
  (*Tract. in Ps.* 2.42, the kingdom is where sin is overcome), it is their own
  share in his victory over sin. Schuster, on the Postcommunion alone (p. 204),
  says only: *The Royal Standard of Jesus Christ is the Cross: Dicite in
  nationibus: regnavit a ligno Deus; for the kingdom of Christ is that of
  obedience, humility and sacrifice*. He does not mention the Gospel or its
  servants and resolves no tension; the studies may cite him for what the
  standard is, not for the resolution.

**Four senses.**

- *Literal.* Before the Roman governor Jesus denies that his kingdom has a
  worldly origin or worldly defenders and declares that he was born to bear
  witness to the truth; royal psalms foresee a king whose dominion reaches the
  ends of the earth, whom all kings adore and all nations serve, and they warn
  the kings of the earth to serve the Lord.
- *Allegorical.* The kingdom is the Church, here and not from here, gathered
  from all nations into one by the truth and spread from the Jordan through
  the world (Augustine); kings enter it as members of the poor people who
  confess their sins, and their authority leads others in (Augustine 71.13–14).
- *Moral.* To be his subject is to be *of the truth* and hear his voice. Every
  station owes him service in its own office: the man by living faithfully,
  the ruler also by ruling justly (Augustine, *Letter* 185.19; *Quas primas*
  nn. 18–19, 32). That service asks no rivalry with earthly power and no seizing
  of its throne; it is fought against sin rather than with swords, under the
  standard of the Cross.
- *Anagogical.* At the harvest the angels will gather out of his kingdom all
  that offends (Augustine, Mt 13:41); what kings and nations now give
  willingly, all will give after the resurrection, when every knee bends
  (Theodoret; Bellarmine); the mixed kingdom here becomes the manifest one, and
  the peace given in him becomes peace without storm.

**Strongest difficulty.** The formulary asks that the nations be *subjected*
and sings that all kings shall adore and all nations serve, and the
instituting act draws from the feast a public duty of rulers (*Quas primas* nn.
18–19, 32). Does *not of this world* empty that subjection of public
consequence, or does the subjection make Christ a rival of Caesar? Augustine's
*I interfere not with your government* (*Tract.* 115.2) could be pressed the
first way, and his *Letter* 185 the second. The checked witnesses refuse both.
Augustine himself holds the denial of a worldly origin together with an office
of kings in the Church's spread (*En.* 71.13) and a service of kings as kings
(*Letter* 185.19–20); Bellarmine reads the Gradual's verse of free conversion,
of the judgment, and of a power that needs no one's consent, and the
Offertory's psalm of kings exhorted to free subjection; Theodoret divides the
same verse into *willingly now* and *all after the resurrection*. The answer
the reading gives: the denial to Pilate is of a worldly origin and worldly
means, not of authority over the world; the subjection of kings and nations is
real, is rendered freely by conversion and in the exercise of each ruler's own
office, and is completed at the judgment. That is compatible with *Quas primas*
n. 17 (he did not and does not interfere with those who possess) and nn. 18–19,
32 (rulers owe him public honour and obedience). The studies must not make the
Gospel a political claim: they cite Augustine's *Letter* with its Donatist
occasion stated, and do not present Schuster's paragraph on medieval papal
deposition of kings (p. 203) as the formulary's sense [§6.9]. A second,
internal difference: Augustine's *regnum* is the people ruled, Chrysostom's
the power itself; Aquinas keeps both [§6.2].

## 4. Reading C — The kingdom handed over that has no end (`kingdom-handed-over`)

**Controlling claim.** The end of Christ's kingship is the Father: the eternal
kingdom the Preface says he hands over is his redeemed people, whom he brings
to the vision of God, so that handing over and reigning for ever are one
consummation, not an abdication; and the session for ever that the Communion
sings begins already in the souls he has cleansed and is completed after the
judgment.

**Exegetical question.** How can the Son *hand over* to the Father a kingdom
that *shall not be taken away* and in which he *shall sit King for ever*
(difficulty 3)?

**Carrying authors.** Augustine of Hippo; Hilary of Poitiers.
**Supporting.** Basil the Great; Robert Bellarmine; Thomas Aquinas (*Super Ps.*
28); Jerome; John Chrysostom (*Hom. in Col.* 3); Cyril of Jerusalem and
Hippolytus (no standing rows); Cassiodorus and Theodoret (ecclesiastical
writers); Schuster (liturgical reception).

**The witnesses' reasoning.**

- *Augustine*, *De Trinitate* I.8.15–10.21 [§3.12, §5.3]. *When He shall have
  delivered up the kingdom to God, even the Father* is *when He shall have
  brought believers to the contemplation of God*; *His faithful ones, whom He
  has redeemed with His blood, are called His kingdom*; he does not take it
  from himself; in so far as he is a Priest in the form of a servant he will
  be subject, in so far as he is God he subjects us with the Father. Then the
  Father is shown and it suffices; *Blessed are the pure in heart*.
- *Augustine*, *En. in Ps.* 28.10–11 [§3.9]. He dwells first in the deluge of
  this world in his saints, kept in the Church as in the ark, *and afterward He
  will sit reigning in them for ever*.
- *Hilary*, *De Trinitate* XI.39–40 [§3.12, §5.3]. He delivers the kingdom *not
  in the sense that He resigns His power*, but by delivering to God *us who
  have been made the Kingdom by the glorifying of His body*; then the just shall
  see God (*Blessed are the pure in heart*); his subjection is that God may be
  all in all, the body translated, not cast off.
- *Basil*, *Hom. in Ps.* 28.8 [§3.9]. The grace of baptism is the deluge that
  cleanses the soul and makes it God's dwelling in the Spirit; *settled in the
  soul that shines from the deluge, God makes it, as it were, a throne for
  himself*; peace, the stability of the soul, is the most perfect of blessings.
- *Bellarmine*, *Explanatio* 28:10–11 [§3.9]. On his second interpretation, after
  the judgment, *all resistance being broken down, the whole power of demons,
  bad men, and all power in general being swept away, "the Lord shall sit King
  forever"*; the blessing of v. 11 is everlasting life. On Ps 71:4 [§3.3],
  Christ's kingdom *will endure forever*, though the sun cease.
- *Aquinas*, *Super Ps.* 28 n. 8 [§3.9]. The verses turn to benefits hoped for:
  the converted world made to dwell in his temple, *et sedebit rex in
  aeternum*; strength for progress, the blessing of peace for the end (Isa
  32:18).
- *Cyril of Jerusalem*, *Catech.* 15.27–32 [§3.6]. Dan 7:13–14 and Lk 1:33 against
  any who say the reign ends; *until* sets no term; the Son's subjection is a
  Son's free accord.
- *Jerome*, *In Dan.* 7:14, 17–18 [§3.6]. A power *quae nullo fine claudatur*,
  which no man can hold; the saints receive not an earthly but a heavenly
  kingdom.
- *Hippolytus*, scholion on Dan 7:14 [§3.6]. King of heaven because born the
  Word before all, of earth because made man, of the underworld because numbered
  among the dead.
- *Chrysostom*, *Hom. in Col.* 3 [§3.5]. The things in heaven are reconciled
  because he brought man up to the angels; *to sum up all things in Christ*
  (Eph 1:10, *witness's own connection*).
- *Cassiodorus* and *Theodoret* [supporting], on Ps 28:10 [§3.9]. The Trinity
  dwells in the baptismal waters and *sedebit* is the Son's (Cassiodorus); the
  world drowned by lawlessness is made a new creation where impiety has no
  more place, *and he will reign for ever* (Theodoret).
- *Schuster* (liturgical reception), three separate comments [§3.1]. On the
  Introit verse (pp. 201–202), *the Messianic office conferred upon Jesus*:
  he will govern *until … he shall have conquered the enemy*, then lay *the
  sceptre and the crown* at the Father's feet. On the Preface (p. 204), its
  conception of the Pontiff-King who restores the realm of creation to the
  Father *that God might eternally be all in all* is *borrowed from St Paul*.
  On the Communion (p. 204), the antiphon is *a prelude to the final effect of
  the eucharistic Sacrifice which ends with the divine blessing*: *at the
  moment of Holy Communion Jesus is enthroned in the faithful heart*, and he is
  the Blessed through whom, by the promise to Abraham, all nations are blessed
  (Gen 22:18). The three paragraphs never refer to one another; reading them as
  one movement from office to handing over to enthronement is editorial
  synthesis.

**Contribution of every element.**

- *Preface.* The reading's text: anointed priest and king, he subjects all
  creatures and hands over *aeternum et universale regnum*; the kingdom is
  named by its goods, *veritatis et vitae, sanctitatis et gratiae, iustitiae,
  amoris et pacis*, which are the goods of persons, not of territory.
- *Alleluia.* *Potestas aeterna, quae non auferetur*: the text Cyril of
  Jerusalem sets against an end of the reign.
- *Communion.* *Sedebit … in aeternum*: begun in the soul cleansed by the
  baptismal flood and made a throne (Basil), in the communicant in whose heart
  he is enthroned (Schuster on the Communion, p. 204), in the saints amid the flood of this world
  (Augustine); completed *afterward* (Augustine), when after the judgment all
  power is swept away (Bellarmine); *benedicet populo suo in pace*: the peace he
  gives in himself (Augustine), the blessing of the end (Aquinas), everlasting
  life (Bellarmine).
- *Epistle.* *Primogenitus ex mortuis*, *reconciliare omnia … sive quae in
  terris, sive quae in caelis*: the head who goes first and brings the body
  after; Chrysostom's man brought up to heaven; Theodoret's heavenly joined to
  earthly through the sacrifice.
- *Collect.* *Omnia instaurare* is the Vulgate of Eph 1:10, the summing up of
  all things in Christ, which Chrysostom joins to Col 1:20 and Theodoret to
  Col 1:16, reading it of the work of the Incarnation; the Collect prays for its
  fulfilment among the nations.
- *Introit.* *Ipsi gloria et imperium in saecula saeculorum*: the doxology of the
  ages, with the heavenly liturgy of Apoc 5 around the Lamb; the verse's
  Messianic office, held until the enemy is conquered (Schuster on the verse,
  pp. 201–202; its tie to the Preface is editorial).
- *Gradual.* All kings adore and all nations serve: the universal homage the
  subjection of 1 Cor 15 completes, *all after the resurrection* (Theodoret).
- *Gospel.* *Regnum meum non est hinc*: its origin is the Father's, and so is its
  end; the king bears witness to the truth, whose vision Augustine and Hilary
  make the handing over.
- *Offertory.* The nations given as inheritance are, on Hilary's *Tract. in Ps.*
  2.31, the *aeterna … hereditas* of the firstborn from the dead [§3.8]: what is
  given to the Son is what the Son gives to the Father.
- *Secret.* The Priest still offers and still gives unity and peace now;
  Augustine says that when the kingdom is handed over he will no longer
  intercede for us as Priest (*De Trin.* I.10.20–21).
- *Postcommunion.* *In caelesti sede iugiter regnare*: the faithful's share in the
  reign that does not end, asked for after the food of immortality.

**Four senses.**

- *Literal.* Daniel sees a son of man given an everlasting kingdom; Paul says
  the Son, firstborn from the dead, reconciles all things; the Preface, in
  Paul's words of 1 Cor 15, has the anointed king hand over the kingdom to the
  Father; the psalm says the Lord makes the flood to dwell and sits king for
  ever, which the witnesses read of God's judgments from the beginning
  (Aquinas's Hebrew sense) or, mystically, of the world, of the Church in the
  ark, and of baptism.
- *Allegorical.* Christ the head brings his body, the Church, to the Father; the
  Church is the kingdom handed over (Augustine, Hilary); the flood is baptism,
  out of which the soul becomes his throne (Basil).
- *Moral.* The life now is the purification of heart that the vision requires
  (*Blessed are the pure in heart*, cited by both carrying authors), lived in the
  peace he gives in himself rather than in the world's; the soul cleansed in
  baptism and fed at the altar is made his throne (Basil; Schuster).
- *Anagogical.* God all in all; the vision face to face; an endless reign of the
  redeemed with Christ, who is not diminished by giving them to the Father,
  after the judgment has swept away every power that resisted him (Bellarmine).

**Strongest difficulty.** The view that took *until* and *delivered up* to mean
the reign ends: Cyril of Jerusalem opposes *another head of the dragon, lately
sprung up* without naming it, and the NPNF editor identifies it as Marcellus of
Ancyra and Photinus (note 1950) [§3.6]; the readings of Augustine and Hilary
answer it. Between them a real difference remains on the subjection of the
Son: Hilary (XI.40) speaks of his becoming *God only … that no trace of the
nature of His earthly body may remain in Him*, while insisting the body is
translated and not cast off; Augustine (I.8.15) reports, without adopting it,
the view that the human nature will be converted into the Godhead, and in
I.10.20 keeps the servant's form, the Priest, as what is subject [§5.3, §6.4].
The reading must not attribute either formulation to the other or to the
Preface. A second difference is on the Communion: Augustine's session runs
from the flood of this world into eternity, Bellarmine's follows the judgment;
the reading gives both, attributed [§6.8].

## 5. Comparison of the readings

| | Reading A | Reading B | Reading C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question | how he receives what he has | where a kingdom not from here is, and how it claims kings and nations | how a handed-over kingdom never ends |
| Text that turns it | Introit, Offertory | Gospel, Gradual, Collect | Preface, Alleluia, Communion |
| Answer | by nature as God, received as man through the cross (two rights) | of and from the Father; here in the faithful of every nation; served by kings freely, in their office, and wholly at the judgment | the redeemed brought to the vision of God; the session begun in the cleansed soul, completed after the judgment |
| Carrying authors | Hilary, Aquinas, Bede | Augustine, Chrysostom, Bellarmine | Augustine, Hilary |
| Moral consequence | ask, and share the king's self-offering | hear the truth; serve him in one's own office; warfare against sin, not swords | purity of heart for the vision |
| Witness against it, kept | Chrysostom, Cyril: nothing added, and for Chrysostom every giving referred to the generation (premise granted by Augustine's distribution of Ps 2:7–8; its rule for v. 8 is not Chrysostom's) | the Gospel's denial against the formulary's subjection, *Quas primas* nn. 17–19, 32 | Hilary and Augustine on the subjection; Augustine and Bellarmine on when he sits |

**What they share.** All three identify the kingdom with the redeemed people,
the Church, and all three place the cross at the centre: A as the means of the
receiving, B as the place where the king testifies before Pilate and as the
standard (Schuster), C as the priestly offering that precedes the handing over.
Augustine contributes to all three from different works and loci, Hilary to A
and C, Aquinas and Bellarmine to all three.

**Where their answers differ.** A answers from the Son's humanity and makes the
Mass's sacrifice the seat of a received kingship; it would mislead if it made
the kingship begin at Easter, which B's Chrysostom forbids. B answers from the
kingdom's origin and makes its universality a matter of truth and grace that
nonetheless claims rulers in their office; on its own it could leave the
Preface's handing over and the Communion's eternal session as mere ornaments.
C answers from the end and makes the eternal reign and the handing over one
consummation; on its own it could pass over the Gospel's present, mixed
kingdom and the service kings owe now. The difference between A and B is real
at Jn 18:37, where Augustine reads *natus sum* of the Virgin birth and
Chrysostom of the eternal generation [§5.4]; Aquinas presents both. On Ps 2:7,
Augustine takes the eternal side that Chrysostom's Jn 18:37 takes; the two
Augustinian loci are not one position.

**Editorial synthesis, labelled.** The three-part order of the readings (how,
where, to what end) and every cross-element connection not marked as a
witness's own or Schuster's are this research's. Schuster's one own join is the
double chant of Gradual and Alleluia; every other grouping of his element
comments is this research's. No Father read expounds this Mass, its Collect,
its Secret, its Postcommunion or its Preface as texts; Schuster comments on
them as a liturgist of 1930, and his comments are reception, not the
compilers' intention.
