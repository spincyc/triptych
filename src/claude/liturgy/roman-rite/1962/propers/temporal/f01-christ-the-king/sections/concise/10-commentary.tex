\runninghead{Detailed Commentary}
\section{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}
\zlabel{triptych:concise:commentary:start}

The three interpretations start from different questions, and the Fathers
and Doctors they draw on disagree on four verses, each tied to a text of this Mass: the
decree of the second psalm, spoken to the Son whom the Offertory bids ask; the
birth of Jn 18:37 in the Gospel; the Son's subjection to the Father after the
handing over that the Preface names; and the moment, in the Communion's psalm,
when the Lord sits King for ever.

\subsection{What the Lamb receives, and why the Son must ask}

The difficulty lies in two verbs. The Introit's slain Lamb is worthy
\latin{accipere \dots\ divinitatem}, to receive divinity; the Offertory's Son,
just declared begotten, is told \latin{Postula a me}, ask of me. If he is the
eternal Son, he can neither receive divinity nor lack the nations. St~Jerome,
expounding the verse of Daniel from which the Alleluia comes, writes that the
Son of man's receiving of power, honour and kingdom must be understood by the
Apostle's ``Who being in the form of God \dots\ emptied himself, taking the
form of a servant \dots\ becoming obedient unto death'' (Phil 2:6--8), which
the Arians, he says, should have read piously (\work{In Dan.}\ II, on
7:13--14). Read by that passage, any text of receiving speaks of what the
eternal Son receives in the nature he took. The Fathers work out this answer on the
second psalm and on Daniel, and in the first interpretation it holds for the
Introit's Lamb as well.

St~Hilary of Poitiers reads the decree of Ps 2:7 of the resurrection, on
apostolic authority: Paul at Antioch had said that God fulfilled the second
psalm's ``Thou art my Son: this day have I begotten thee'' by ``raising up
Jesus'' (Acts 13:33). The ``today'' of the decree is thus the begetting of the
firstborn from the dead (\work{Tractatus super Psalmos} 2.30). For Hilary the
risen Lord's ``All power is given to me'' (Mt 28:18) shows that he received
what he asked for, and the asking is the prayer of the Supper (2.31; Jn 17:1). What was asked he
answers from Philippians: the one who took the form of a servant is raised
into the name above every name, \latin{in deum namque, quia nullum ultra deum
nomen est, prouehitur}, raised into God, since there is no name beyond God,
and so given, at his asking, to be what he had been before (2.33). The inheritance
is the nations baptized and taught, brought
\latin{ex iniusto \dots\ iure
dominantium in regnum aeternum diuinumque}, out of the unjust right of those
who lorded it over them into an eternal and divine kingdom, the inheritance
\latin{primogeniti huius ex mortuis} (2.31). Hilary expounds the psalm in
the Epistle's words: the nations are those transferred ``into the kingdom of
the Son of his love,'' and the Son is ``the firstborn from the dead'' (Col
1:13, 18).

St~Augustine divides the same two verses differently. ``To-day have I begotten
Thee'' has ``a divine interpretation'': it is the eternal generation, and
``today'' signifies the presence of eternity (\work{Enarrationes in Psalmos}
2.6). Only ``Ask of Me'' is temporal, spoken of ``the Manhood which He took on
Himself'' (2.7). Hilary and Augustine therefore agree on the asking, which
belongs to the Son made man, and part on the decree, which Hilary reads of the
resurrection and Augustine of eternity. St~Thomas Aquinas turns Augustine's
division into a principle. Christ holds the nations \latin{duplici jure:
scilicet hereditario, et meritorio}: by inheritance, from the eternal
generation, \latin{hodie genui te, idest aeternaliter}; and by merit, because
God wills that his gifts be received by asking, and \latin{hoc exemplum voluit
ostendere per Christum, quia voluit quod peteret, quod sibi jure hereditario
competebat}, he willed to show this example in Christ, that he should ask for
what was his by right of inheritance. Christ asks by his prayer, ``for them
also who through their word shall believe in me'' (Jn 17:20), and by his
passion; and he holds the nations not as Peter or Paul held them, as
ministers, but as lord (\work{Postilla super Psalmos} 2, nn.~5--6).
Cassiodorus's rule (page~3) holds on both the Gradual's psalm and the Offertory's
(\work{Expositio Psalmorum}, on Pss 71 and 2). Theodoret of Cyrus says
the same of Ps 2:8, \latin{etiam ut homo universorum dominium accipit}, but
parts from Augustine on the decree, which he will not apply to the Godhead,
since of that generation the Father says ``from the womb before the day star
I begot thee'' (Ps 109:3).

St~Bede reads the Apocalypse in the same way. The one whom the
elder calls a Lion and John sees as a Lamb is one Lord, a Lamb in dying
innocently and a Lion in conquering death; and when the Lamb takes the book,
\latin{dispensationem ipsam Incarnationis a Patre et a seipso, secundum id quod
Deus est, dispositam accepisse Filius hominis dicitur}, the Son of man is said
to receive the very dispensation of the Incarnation, which the Father and he
himself, as God, had disposed (\work{Explanatio Apocalypsis} I, on 5:5--7).
In Bede's sentence the Son is both giver and receiver: as God he disposed the
Incarnation with the Father, and as Son of man he receives it. Put beside
Hilary's servant raised into the name of God, this is how the first
interpretation understands the Introit's \latin{accipere \dots\
divinitatem}: the slain Lamb receives, in the nature he took, the name and
glory of the God he always was.

The strongest objection to this interpretation comes from this Mass's Gospel. On
``For this was I born,'' the objection of St~John Chrysostom begins, ``If then
He was born a king, all His other attributes are by Generation,'' and he
draws from it a rule for every text of giving: when you hear that
the Father has ``given to the Son also to have life,'' ``deem of nothing else
but His generation, and so of the rest'' (\work{Homilies on John} 83.4; Jn
5:26). St~Cyril of Alexandria says of the same verse that the sovereignty is
``not \dots\ added unto it from without'' (\work{Commentary on John} XII).
Augustine's division grants their premise, since the kingship by generation is
the decree and is not received. It does not meet Chrysostom's rule, which refers
every text of giving to the generation, while Cassiodorus and Theodoret refer
the asking of Ps 2:8 to the humanity; Chrysostom does not expound that verse
here. All of
them hold that Christ is King by nature; they disagree on how to read the texts
of giving addressed to the Son. Hilary's resurrection reading is
compatible with that agreement only because he does not make the resurrection the
beginning of the Sonship: what is given is \latin{ut esset, quod fuerat
ante}. \work{Quas primas} names both titles, a dominion by essence
and nature and a second acquired by redemption, at the price of his precious
blood (1~Pet 1:18--19; n.~13).

In the first interpretation this answer extends to three more texts of the
Mass. The Alleluia sings the power as held for ever; the giving by which it
was received, which Jerome expounds by Philippians, is in the half of Dan 7:14
that the Alleluia leaves out. The Epistle names redemption ``through his
blood,'' and Aquinas reads
the headship of the Church as Christ's as man, \latin{secundum quod
huiusmodi} (\work{Super Col.}\ 1 lect.~5). If the sacrifice the Secret offers
is the Son's asking for the nations, offered now (page~4), what the Secret asks that he give the
nations, unity and peace, can be taken as the inheritance Hilary describes.

\subsection{A prisoner whose kingdom is everywhere}

In the first interpretation the Gospel's prisoner is acquiring his dominion
in the trial: he is captive, Chrysostom says, ``by consenting, and
giving Himself up'' (\work{Homilies on John} 83.4), and the passion has begun.
In the second, the prisoner's words say where his kingdom comes from, and so
how it can claim the kings and nations of the chants.

St~Augustine begins from Jesus' counter-question, ``Sayest thou this thing of
thyself?'', put so that the vain notion of his kingdom that Gentiles and Jews
alike had formed might be shown up (\work{Tractates on the Gospel of John}
115.1). Then, in the Lord's voice, he addresses Jews, Gentiles and every kingdom:
``hear, all ye kingdoms
of the earth: I interfere not with your government in this world'' (115.2).
The reassurance rests on his two distinctions, between
\emph{in} and \emph{of} this world and between \emph{here} and \emph{from
hence}. The believers who are the kingdom belonged to the world while they
belonged to its prince, and Augustine gives the Epistle's
verse for the change: ``For so did God rescue us from the power of darkness,
and translate us into the kingdom of the Son of His love'' (Col 1:13). ``Thou
sayest that I am a king'' neither denies a kingship nor confesses the one
Pilate imagines, ``Carnal thyself, thou sayest it carnally'' (115.3); and
``Every one that is of the truth heareth my voice'' commends ``the grace
whereby He calleth according to His own purpose'' (115.4).

Chrysostom reads the same sentence of the kingdom's power rather than its
people: Christ has his empire from above (page~3). He says the kingdom is
not of this world ``not because He does not rule here,'' and that it ``is not human, but far greater
than this and more splendid'' (83.4). St~Thomas Aquinas takes the people
ruled with Augustine and the royal power with Chrysostom, and cites the Alleluia's verse for that power (\work{Super Io.}\
18 lect.~6). His ``here, because it is everywhere'' (page~3) can be put in
terms of origin: a kingdom that
takes its origin from one place in the world is bounded by that place, and one
whose origin is above is bounded by none, so that it can claim the whole earth
without competing with any power in it. Both of his senses of \latin{regnum}
appear in this Mass: the Collect prays for a people gathered out of the
scattered nations, and the Alleluia sings a power that lasts.
Beside his gloss on the Epistle's \latin{transtulit}, Aquinas sets the
Gospel's \latin{regnum meum non est de hoc mundo} (\work{Super Col.}\ 1
lect.~3).

So the first and second interpretations differ at the Gospel without
contradicting each other. In the first, the prisoner is receiving a dominion as
man in the passion that has begun; in the second, his power has its origin and
means outside the world and needs no servants to defend it, and the Offertory's
nations are still given to the Son as an inheritance. Both come to Jn 18:37, where Augustine and Chrysostom, two
principal sources of the second, divide. Augustine
reads ``For this was I born'' of the birth from the Virgin (115.4), Chrysostom
of the eternal generation, and Aquinas gives both, the second \latin{sicut
Deus ex Deo, ita rex ex rege}, adding that ``for this came I into the world''
is the temporal coming even on Chrysostom's reading. The second interpretation
needs only the origin of the kingdom in verse~36 and the hearers in verse~37,
and either birth fits it. Augustine's own two loci speak of two births: he reads
Ps 2:7 of the eternal generation and Jn 18:37 of the birth from the Virgin,
so that the eternal birth grounds the decree of the psalm and the temporal
birth the testimony before Pilate.

\subsection{What the kings and nations owe}

The Gradual sings that all kings shall adore him, the Offertory's psalm bids
the kings ``serve ye the Lord with fear,'' and the Collect asks that the
nations be subjected, \latin{subdantur}. Beside the Gospel these texts raise a
question about the kings. If the kingdom is not of this world, it could seem
to ask nothing of rulers; \work{Quas primas} calls it a grave error to say
that Christ has no authority in civil affairs, and draws from the feast a
public duty of rulers (nn.~17--19, 32). If it claims their adoration, it could
seem to make Christ a rival of earthly kings, the suspicion Pilate's first
question already raises. The Fathers
and later commentators answer by giving the kings three distinct places.

The first is conversion. Augustine reads the Gradual's psalm of the Church
spread through the whole world, its river the Jordan from which the Gospel of
the kingdom went out (\work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 71.11). When the kings of
Tharsis and Saba ``lead'' gifts, a verb for things that walk, the gifts ``seem
to me to signify men, whom into the fellowship of the Church of Christ the
authority of kings doth lead: although even persecuting kings have led gifts,
knowing not what they did, in sacrificing the holy Martyrs'' (71.13). And the
kings who adore belong to ``the people of men believing in Him,'' who do not
disdain to be poor and humbly confess their sins (71.14). St~Robert Bellarmine
reads the Offertory's psalm as an exhortation to ``the kings of this world
\dots\ to freely subject themselves to him, the true and supreme king of all
kings,'' serving as a son serves his father (\work{Explanatio}, Ps~2).
Cassiodorus states the paradox of the Gradual's verse: \latin{reges
adorabunt, qui putantur adorandi; gentes autem servire, quae terrenos dominos
habere noscuntur} (\work{Expositio Psalmorum} 71).

The second is office. In \work{Letter}~185, on the laws against the
Donatists, Augustine explains why in the apostles' time no emperor served
Christ by laws: the raging of the kings in Ps 2:1--2 was being fulfilled, not
yet the command of verses 10--11. Once ``All kings shall
fall down before Him; all nations shall serve Him'' began to be fulfilled, no
sober man could tell kings that the Church was no concern of theirs
(185.19--20). Aquinas reports his distinction of man and king on Ps
2:10--11, \latin{secundum Augustinum \dots\ rex servit Deo inquantum homo, in se juste vivendo, sed
inquantum rex, leges ferendo contra ea quae sunt contra Dei justitiam}, and
sees in the psalm the Church's history, the kings' laws first against Christ
and afterwards for him (\work{Postilla super Psalmos} 2, n.~9). On the Introit's
verse, Bellarmine gives the literal sense as
David's prayer for Solomon, who asks the grace of judging rightly, ``for kings
were created for the people, not the people for kings'' (\work{Explanatio},
Ps~71), while Augustine, Cassiodorus and Theodoret hold that the psalm fits
Solomon in no way and speaks of Christ alone, the King to whom the Father has
given all judgment (Jn 5:22).

The third is the judgment, when every knee bends, the fulfilment Bellarmine
calls ``to the letter.'' Of Bellarmine's three
fulfilments of the Gradual's adoration, the one that holds
``always'' is ``the actual power that Christ has over all princes and all
nations, so that, with or without their knowledge, with or without their
consent, he may deal as he pleases with them'' (\work{Explanatio}, Ps~71).
Theodoret divides the verse in time, some kings willingly in this life
and all after the resurrection, since ``now we see not as yet all things
subject to him'' (Heb 2:8). Schuster gives the state of things between the
two: the kingdom is ``complete and absolute de jure, but \dots\ opposed and
incomplete de facto'' (p.~202), and on the Gospel, ``Jesus does not come to
dethrone the sovereigns of the earth'' (p.~203).

In the second interpretation the denial before Pilate is a denial of a worldly
origin and worldly means, not of authority over the world. The subjection is real,
rendered freely in conversion, rendered by rulers in their own office, and
completed at the judgment, over a dominion that depends on no one's consent.
\work{Quas primas} says both: Christ during his
earthly life refrained from exercising such authority and does not now
interfere with those who possess earthly goods, and yet individuals, families
and states are under his rule, and rulers owe him public honour and obedience
(nn.~17--19, 32).

The Postcommunion is the one text of the Mass that makes the faithful
soldiers, \latin{sub Christi Regis vexillis
militare}, a few minutes after the Gospel in which the King has said that if
his kingdom were of this world his servants would strive. Neither Chrysostom
nor Hilary speaks of this prayer, but their arguments bear on it. Chrysostom's
reasoning on Jn 18:36 excludes one sense of the figure: earthly kingship
depends on servants who fight and Christ's does not, so their warfare is not
his defence. Hilary, on the second psalm, supplies another in the kingdom of
God where sin is overcome (\work{Tractatus super Psalmos} 2.42--43), and
Schuster's note on that prayer names the standard (p.~204). In the first interpretation those who say
the prayer are the members whom Bede makes sharers in the priesthood: \latin{Quia Rex regum et
Sacerdos coelestis se offerendo pro nobis nos suo corpori adunavit, nemo
sanctorum est qui spiritualiter sacerdotii officio careat} (\work{Explanatio
Apocalypsis} I, on 1:6), on the clause of John's greeting that the Introit
leaves out.

\subsection{A kingdom handed over that has no end}

The Preface follows Paul's order in 1~Cor 15: the Son anointed Priest and
King offers himself on the cross, all creatures are subjected to his rule,
and he hands over \latin{aeternum et universale regnum} to the Father. Paul's
text reads, ``when he shall have delivered up the kingdom
to God and the Father \dots\ For he must reign, until he hath put all his
enemies under his feet,'' and ``then the Son also himself shall be subject
unto him that put all things under him, that God may be all in all''
(15:24--25, 28). Paul's ``until'' and the Son's subjection seem to set an
end to the reign, while the Alleluia sings a power that ``shall not be
taken away,'' the Communion a King who ``shall sit \dots\ for ever,'' and
Gabriel had told Mary, ``of his kingdom there shall be no end'' (Lk 1:33).

Some in St~Cyril of Jerusalem's day took the ``until'' to mean that the reign
stops. He warns his catechumens, ``shouldest thou ever hear any say that the
kingdom of Christ shall have an end, abhor the heresy; it is another head of
the dragon, lately sprung up,'' and sets against it Gabriel and Daniel's
everlasting power. Scripture's ``until,'' he argues, sets no term: death
reigned from Adam ``until'' Moses (Rom 5:14) and did not cease there. The Son's
subjection is no new obedience but ``a self-chosen accordance; for He is not a
servant, that He should be subjected by force, but a Son''
(\work{Catechetical Lectures} 15.27--32). Jerome had said the same from
Daniel: no man could hold a power \latin{quae nullo fine claudatur}, which no
end encloses (\work{In Dan.}\ II, on 7:14).

St~Augustine makes the handing over the bringing of believers to the
contemplation of the Father (\work{De Trinitate} I.8.16):
``For the Father will be manifested to us, and that will suffice for us,'' and
the pure in heart are made ready for that sight now (I.8.17). For
Augustine the handing over takes nothing from the Son, because the kingdom is
his people, whom he will make to abide in himself: ``His faithful ones, whom He has redeemed with
His blood, are called His kingdom, for whom He now intercedes; but then,
making them to abide in Himself there, where He is equal to the Father, He
will no longer pray the Father for them'' (I.10.21). St~Hilary says the
Son delivers the kingdom ``not in
the sense that He resigns His power by the delivering, but that we, being
conformed to the glory of His body, shall form the Kingdom of God''
(\work{De Trinitate} XI.39). Augustine and Hilary both make the handing over
the vision of God.

On the subjection that follows they speak differently. Augustine keeps the
form of the servant as what is subject: ``in so far as He is a Priest that has
taken the form of a servant for us,'' he ``shall be put under Him who has put
all things under Him'' (I.10.20). Hilary writes that the end of the subjection
is ``that God may be all in all, that no trace of the nature of His earthly
body may remain in Him. Although before this time the two were combined within
Him, He must now become God only; not, however, by casting off the body, but
by translating it through subjection'' (XI.40). He insists that the body is
translated and not cast off, while his ``God only'' goes further than
Augustine's servant who remains subject. The Preface uses neither
formulation; it says only that the kingdom is handed over and that it is
eternal.

In the third interpretation the handing over bears on other texts of the Mass
as well. Hilary says, on the Offertory's psalm, that the nations given to the Son are the eternal
inheritance of the firstborn from the dead (\work{Tractatus super Psalmos}
2.31), and, in the \work{De Trinitate}, that what the Son delivers is not his Kingdom but the Kingdom, us who have
been made the Kingdom (XI.39). If the two passages are put
together, the nations given to the Son in Ps 2:8 are the kingdom he delivers in
1~Cor 15:24. The Epistle's firstborn from the dead reconciles things ``on earth''
and ``in heaven,'' which Chrysostom explains by Christ's bringing man up to
the angels and connects with the summing up of all things in Eph 1:10, the
Collect's \latin{instaurare} (\work{Homilies on Colossians} 3). The Secret asks
that the Son, immolated in the present sacrifice, give unity and peace now, and
Augustine says that when the kingdom is delivered up he will no longer
intercede for us as Priest (I.10.20); the Secret's asking belongs to the time
before the handing over.

\subsection{When the King sits}

The Communion sings only part of Ps 28:10. The half of the verse it leaves out speaks of the flood that
the Lord ``maketh \dots\ to dwell,'' and those who explain the flood place
the session for ever at different moments. St~Thomas gives first the
plain sense, God's judgments manifest from the beginning, as in the deluge,
and then three mystical ones: the saints secure in the Church as in Noe's ark,
the world and its carnal men brought into his temple by conversion, \latin{et
sedebit rex in aeternum}, and the baptismal waters (\work{Postilla super
Psalmos} 28). St~Augustine reads the flood as this world, where the Lord
dwells first in his saints and afterward reigns in them for ever
(\work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 28.10). The blessing in peace is ``affording
them peace in Himself,'' ``for
peace in this world He hath not promised them'' (28.11). St~Basil the Great
reads the flood as the grace of baptism, after which God, settled in the soul
that shines from that deluge, makes it, as it were, a throne for himself
(\work{Homilia in Psalmum} 28.8). Blessed Ildefonso Schuster reads the
antiphon of the communicant: ``Jesus is enthroned in the faithful heart''
(p.~204). St~Robert Bellarmine, on his second reading, places the session
after the judgment: ``all resistance being broken down, the whole power of
demons, bad men, and all power in general being swept away, `the Lord shall
sit King forever' '' (\work{Explanatio}, Ps~28).

Between them, these writers give the session a beginning now, in the soul cleansed by
baptism (Basil), in the communicant (Schuster), in the saints amid the flood of
this world (Augustine), and a completion after the judgment. Augustine's
session runs from the one into the other; Bellarmine's begins when the
judgment is done. The Postcommunion is said by those who have just received
\latin{immortalitatis alimoniam}, and it asks to reign with him \latin{in
caelesti sede}. In the first interpretation the Communion's session is that of
the exalted King who received the kingdom; in the second its peace is the peace in himself that Augustine distinguishes
from the world's; in the third its ``for ever'' is the reign that the
handing over does not end.

\subsection{Three answers and their senses}

All three interpretations identify the kingdom with the redeemed people, the
Church. Each places the cross differently. In the first it is the means
by which the kingdom is received, the asking of the second psalm, made by his
prayer and his passion and offered again in the Secret's victim. In the second
it is the standard of the Postcommunion, which Schuster's note names as the
Cross; the second interpretation takes the faithful's fight under it to be a
fight against sin. In the
third it is the priestly offering that the Preface sets before the handing
over.

In the literal sense, the first interpretation has royal psalms that promise
the nations to a Son who asks, and the angels' acclamation of the slain Lamb,
with Bellarmine's literal Solomon beside the Christ alone of Augustine,
Cassiodorus and Theodoret. The second has a prisoner who denies his kingdom a
worldly origin and worldly defenders, and psalms that warn the kings to serve.
The third has Daniel's everlasting kingdom, Paul's handing over, and the
psalm's flood and session.
Allegorically, the Church is, in the first, the inheritance the Son receives
as man; in the second, here and not from here, spread from the Jordan; in the
third, the kingdom the Son hands over. Morally, the faithful are in the first
to ask and to share the King's self-offering; in the second, to hear the truth
and serve him in their own office, against sin and not with swords; in the
third, to have the purity of heart that the vision requires. And
anagogically, in the first the firstborn from the dead goes before his members
into the reign; in the second the mixed kingdom of the tares is made manifest
at the harvest, when every knee bends; in the third come the vision face to
face, God all in all, and a King not diminished by giving his people to the
Father.

The Preface names the subject of each interpretation, in order. The Father
anoints the Son eternal Priest and King, and the Son offers himself on
the cross: in the first interpretation that offering is the asking by which he
receives, as man, the nations that were his by right. The first good it names in
the kingdom he hands over is truth, the truth to which he bore witness before
Pilate: in the second it is the people who are of the truth and hear his
voice, in the world and not from it. And he hands it over to the Father: in
the third the handing over brings the redeemed to the vision of God and takes
nothing from a reign that has no end.
