``Art thou a king then?''

It is early morning, before the Pasch. The accusers will not enter the
governor's hall, so Pilate goes in to the prisoner, bound since his arrest in
the garden, and asks him whether he is a king.

This Mass began at the throne of heaven. The Introit gave us the angels' cry,
``The Lamb that was slain is worthy to receive power and divinity and wisdom
and strength and honour.'' The Gradual sang of a King who ``shall rule from
sea to sea,'' whom ``all kings of the earth shall adore,'' whom ``all nations
shall serve.'' And then the Gospel set the same King before us bound, in a
Roman governor's hall, saying, ``My kingdom is not of this world.''

So which is it: a King who rules from sea to sea, or a kingdom not of this
world?

\medskip

It is easy to hear ``My kingdom is not of this world,'' and take it to mean
that Christ is King only in heaven, or only in the soul. The rest of life
would then belong to some other king, or to none.

Three times in one verse the Lord says that his kingdom does not belong to
this world, and St~Augustine weighs the words he chooses. The Lord did not
say, my kingdom is not \emph{in} this world. He said it is not \emph{of}
this world. And at the end of the verse he did not say, my kingdom is not
\emph{here}. He said, ``my kingdom is not \emph{from hence}.'' ``For His
kingdom is here until the end of the world,'' Augustine says, ``having tares
intermingled therewith until the harvest.'' And again: ``It only sojourns as a
stranger in the world.''

A kingdom that comes from one place in the world is bounded by that place;
its borders are where its armies stop. Christ's kingdom does not take its
origin anywhere in the world, so no place bounds it. That is how the prisoner
before Pilate can claim the whole earth without taking it from any power in
it. St~Thomas Aquinas says that it is not from here, and yet it is here,
because it is everywhere.

\medskip

The Lord goes on: ``If my kingdom were of this world, my servants would
certainly strive that I should not be delivered to the Jews.'' St~John
Chrysostom comments, ``Here He shows the weakness of kingship among us, that
its strength lies in servants; but that which is above is sufficient for
itself, needing nothing.'' In place of servants who fight, the Lord names
those who hear the truth he was born to bear witness to: ``Every one that is
of the truth heareth my voice.''

Augustine says, too, who belongs to this kingdom: those who believe in
Christ. They were of the world once, while they belonged to its prince.
They were made a kingdom no longer of the world when they were reborn
in Christ. And for that change Augustine turns to the verse St~Paul gave us in
the Epistle, where he thanks the Father who ``hath translated us into the
kingdom of the Son of his love.''

To translate is to carry something over from one place to another. Paul
means that the Father has rescued us from the power of darkness and carried
us into his Son's kingdom. That is how each of you entered it. The Father
carried you in when you were reborn in Christ.

The Collect asked that the nations be subjected to Christ, and it named what
divides them: they are ``separated by the wound of sin.'' The rule it asks
for them is ``His most loving dominion.''

\medskip

Why, then, is the King bound? If his power is greater than Pilate's, how was
he made a captive? Chrysostom answers, ``By consenting, and giving Himself
up.''

Christ was born a King, as Chrysostom insists. Yet in this Mass he receives.
In the Offertory the Father says to his Son, ``Ask of me, and I will give thee
the Gentiles for thy inheritance.'' Why should the eternal Son ask? St~Thomas
answers that God wills even what is owed to be received by asking. Christ
asked, he says, by his prayer and by his passion. St~Augustine says that this
asking belongs to the Son made man, ``who offered up Himself as a Sacrifice in
the stead of all sacrifices.'' As man, then, Christ wins the nations in that
hall, by the passion that has begun. It is as the Lamb that was slain that
the angels of the Introit call him worthy to receive power.

The same sacrifice will be offered on this altar. In the Secret, the prayer
said quietly over the gifts, the priest will offer ``this host for the
reconciliation of humanity,'' and ask that Christ bestow on all the nations
``the gifts of unity and peace.'' That unity heals the wound the
Collect named. Then the Preface will name the kingdom by what it holds: ``a
kingdom of truth and life; a kingdom of holiness and grace, a kingdom of
justice, love and peace.''

\medskip

What, then, does this King ask of those who adore him?

The Gradual's psalm goes on to give the reason for the kings' homage: ``For
he shall deliver the poor from the mighty.'' That poor man, says
St~Augustine, ``is the people of men believing in Him. In this people are
also kings adoring Him.'' The king who adores Christ, then, kneels among the
poor and confesses his sins with them. And his authority has work to do: it
leads men, says Augustine, into the fellowship of the Church. St~Robert Bellarmine, on the
Introit's verse, says what that authority is for: ``kings were created for
the people, not the people for kings.''

You wear no crown, but many of you hold some authority over other people.
Christ's kingship leaves that authority in your hands and claims it as his.
Like those kings, you stand among the poor who confess their sins, and your
authority is given you for the people under it.
So before you use it, pray the Introit's verse: ``Give to the king thy
judgment, O God, and to the king's son thy justice.''

\medskip

After Communion we shall pray as those ``who glory to fight under the standard
of Christ the King'': soldiers, a few minutes after a Gospel in which the King
says that his servants do not fight for him. His kingdom, as Chrysostom said,
needs nothing, so this fight is not his defence. St~Hilary says that the
kingdom of God is where sin is overcome and death destroyed. Our fight is our
share in that victory over sin. Its weapons are the ones the King used in
Pilate's hall: the truth, and a life given up. Blessed Ildefonso
Schuster, on this prayer, names the standard: ``The Royal Standard of Jesus
Christ is the Cross.''

\medskip

We shall not see this kingdom complete. Some give him their homage willingly
now; all will give it at the judgment, when every knee bends. The Alleluia
sang that ``his power is an everlasting power that shall not be taken away,''
and the Communion will sing, ``the Lord shall sit king for ever \dots\ the Lord
will bless his people with peace'': not peace in this world, Augustine says,
but peace in himself. Schuster says of that chant that ``at the moment of Holy
Communion Jesus is enthroned in the faithful heart.''

\medskip

The prisoner's first words to Pilate were a question: ``Sayest thou this thing
of thyself, or have others told it thee of me?'' He asks us the same. Do we
call him King of ourselves, or only because others have told us? Soon he will
be on this altar, the King who gave himself up.

So the King who rules from sea to sea and the prisoner whose kingdom is not
of this world are one King. His
kingdom comes from above, and so it is here, until the harvest, in every
place. He rules it as he did in Pilate's hall: by the truth, and by the life
he gave up. He is
King, then, not only in heaven or in the soul. He is King of the whole of
your life, and of whatever authority you hold in it.

With the angels of the Introit, then, we say it of the prisoner of the Gospel:
``The Lamb that was slain is worthy to receive power and divinity and wisdom
and strength and honour.'' ``To him be glory and empire for ever and ever.''
