\runninghead{The Lamb Receives the Kingdom}
\properlane{lamb-receives-the-kingdom}{The Slain Lamb Receives the Kingdom That Was Always His}

The kingship this Mass acclaims belongs to the Son by nature, and he also
receives it: in the humanity he assumed, asking for it and winning it by his
death, and being given it at his exaltation. What he receives is the nations he
has redeemed, and that received kingship is exercised in the sacrifice the Mass
offers. This interpretation starts from the Introit's verb and the Offertory's
command, and follows the order of the Preface, in which the anointed Son first
offers himself on the cross and only then hands over a kingdom.

\subsection{What the Lamb receives}
\label{sec:lamb-question}

The angels of the Introit stand round the throne at the moment the Lamb has
taken the sealed book that no one else was worthy to open, and the reason the
elders have just given is a history: ``thou wast slain and hast redeemed us to
God, in thy blood'' (Apoc 5:9). Their acclamation names seven things the Lamb
is worthy to receive, and the first two that the Introit sings are
\latin{virtutem, et divinitatem}: power, and divinity. The Offertory raises the
same difficulty from another book. The king set on Sion recites the decree,
``Thou art my son, this day have I begotten thee,'' and the Lord goes on,
\latin{Postula a me, et dabo tibi gentes hereditatem tuam}: ``Ask of me, and I
will give thee the Gentiles for thy inheritance'' (Ps 2:7--8). If the Lamb is
the eternal Son, how can he receive divinity? If the one addressed is the Son
begotten, why must he ask for anything? The Alleluia sings only the possessing
half of Dan 7:14, ``his power is an everlasting power,'' but the verse it comes
from begins ``And he gave him power.''

St Jerome, expounding that verse of Daniel, writes that the Son of man's
receiving power, honour and kingdom must be understood by the Apostle's ``Who
being in the form of God \dots\ emptied himself, taking the form of a servant
\dots\ becoming obedient unto death'' (Phil 2:6--8), which the Arians, he
says, should have read piously (\work{In Dan.}\ II, on 7:13--14). The hymn
matters because it says who receives. Without it, a text in which the Son is
given power could be taken to make him a creature raised up for what he did.
With it, the text speaks of what the eternal Son receives in the form of a
servant that he took. The Fathers below work the
question out where the second psalm and Daniel raise it. Their answers apply
to the Lamb of the Introit as well, who is also said to receive, and is worthy
to receive because he was slain.

\subsection{St Hilary: the risen Son asks back the glory he had}
\label{sec:lamb-hilary}

St Hilary of Poitiers reads the decree of Psalm~2 on apostolic authority. St
Paul at Antioch had said that God ``hath fulfilled \dots\ raising up Jesus, as
in the second psalm also is written: Thou art my Son: this day have I begotten
thee'' (Acts 13:33). So Hilary takes the ``today'' of the decree as the
begetting of the firstborn from the dead (\work{Tractatus super Psalmos}
2.30). The risen Lord's own words, ``All power is given to me in heaven and in
earth'' (Mt 28:18), show that he asked for what he received; and when
the psalm goes on, \latin{Posce a me}, Hilary concludes, \latin{accepit ergo
hereditatem gentium, quam poposcit}, he received the inheritance of the
nations, which he asked for (2.31). The asking itself Hilary finds in the prayer of the Supper, ``Father, the hour is come. Glorify
thy Son \dots\ As thou hast given him power over all flesh'' (Jn 17:1--2).

Hilary explains what was asked and what was given from the hymn of
Philippians. He who abode in the form of God took the form of a servant, and
after the death of the cross he is exalted into the name that is above every
name: \latin{in deum namque, quia nullum ultra deum nomen est, prouehitur,
eique hoc petenti, id est, ut esset, quod fuerat ante, donatur}, for he is
raised into God, since there is no name beyond God, and to him who asks this,
namely that he might be what he had been before, it is given (2.33). The
receiving, then, is the glorifying of the humanity the Son assumed; as God he
gains nothing and loses nothing. The inheritance is the nations baptized and
taught, carried over \latin{ex iniusto \dots\ iure dominantium in regnum
aeternum diuinumque}, out of the unjust right of those who lorded it over them
into an eternal and divine kingdom, the inheritance \latin{primogeniti huius ex
mortuis}, of this firstborn from the dead (2.31). Hilary is expounding the
psalm, not the Epistle, but his words for the inheritance are the Epistle's:
those transferred ``into the kingdom of the Son of his love,'' and the Son
``the firstborn from the dead'' (Col 1:13, 18). Later in the same tractate he
draws the consequence for the inheritance itself: the kingdom of God is where
sin is overcome and death destroyed, and those who become co-heirs of the
eternal King must themselves become kings (2.42--43).

\subsection{St Augustine and St Thomas: one dominion by two rights}
\label{sec:lamb-two-rights}

St Augustine divides the two verses differently. ``To-day have I begotten
Thee,'' though it may seem spoken of the birth in the flesh, he gives ``a
divine interpretation'': it is the eternal generation, and ``today'' signifies
the presence of eternity (\work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 2.6). Only verse~8 is
temporal: ``Ask of Me'' ``has at once a temporal sense with reference to the
Manhood which He took on Himself, who offered up Himself as a Sacrifice in the
stead of all sacrifices, who also maketh intercession for us'' (2.7). So
Augustine and Hilary agree on verse~8, the asking and receiving of the nations
by the Son made man, and differ on verse~7: Hilary reads the decree of the
resurrection, Augustine of the eternal generation.

St Thomas Aquinas states Augustine's division as a principle. The two verses
give Christ dominion over the nations \latin{duplici jure: scilicet
hereditario, et meritorio}, by a double right, of inheritance and of merit.
The right of inheritance is the eternal generation, \latin{hodie genui te,
idest aeternaliter}. The right of merit is the asking: God wills that we
receive his gifts by asking, \latin{et hoc exemplum voluit ostendere per
Christum, quia voluit quod peteret, quod sibi jure hereditario competebat}, and
he willed to show this example in Christ, willing that he should ask for what
belonged to him by right of inheritance. Christ asks by his prayer, ``for them
also who through their word shall believe in me'' (Jn 17:20), and by his
passion, the death that redeems those called to the eternal inheritance (Heb
9:15); and he holds the nations not as ministers hold them, as Peter or Paul
did, but as lord, the Son in his own house (Heb 3:5--6; \work{Postilla super
Psalmos} 2, nn.~5--6). This is Aquinas's answer to the Offertory's
difficulty. The Son does not ask because he lacks anything, for what he asks
for is his by inheritance. He asks because the Father wills that even what is
owed be received as a gift, and his asking is his prayer and his passion.

Cassiodorus gives the rule its widest form. Whenever the Lord Christ asks that
something be given him, or another asks it for him, \latin{naturam humanitatis
evidenter ostendi}, the nature of his humanity is plainly shown; for the
almighty Word, having all that is the Father's, needs nothing to be given
(\work{Expositio Psalmorum} 71, on verse~1, with Jn 16:15). On Ps 2:8 he applies
it: \latin{Quidquid enim in tempore accepit Christus, secundum hominem ea quae
non habebat acquirit}; the possession is given because \latin{natura
humanitatis accepit, quod semper divina possedit}, the human nature received
what the divine always possessed. Theodoret of Cyrus, on the same verse, says
that he who is Lord by nature as God \latin{etiam ut homo universorum dominium
accipit}, receives also as man the lordship of all; on the Introit's verse he
adds that the psalm teaches both, that as God he has authority over all and as
man receives it from his own Father (\work{Interpretatio in Psalmos} 2; 71).
Theodoret parts from Augustine on verse~7, which he will not apply to the
Godhead at all, since of that generation the Father says ``from the womb before
the day star I begot thee'' (Ps 109:3): as man Christ receives that voice too.

St Robert Bellarmine hears three generations in verse~7, the eternal, the
birth in the flesh and the resurrection, and makes the first the ground of the
decree: ``to Christ, as being the true and natural Son of God, is due all
power in heaven and on earth.'' On verse~8 the Father says to him, ``You my
natural Son, the incarnation of my power raised from the dead, have just right
to ask me for power over all nations''; the kingdom so given is ``his spiritual
kingdom, that is, his Church,'' not bounded like David's or Solomon's but over
all nations, ``infidels even included'' (\work{Explanatio}, Ps~2).

\subsection{St Bede: the Lamb and the Lion, King and Priest}
\label{sec:lamb-bede}

St Bede reads the Apocalypse in the same way, of what the Son receives in his
humanity. The one whom the ancient calls a Lion and John sees as a Lamb is one
Lord: a Lamb in dying innocently, a Lion in conquering death. When the Lamb
takes the book from the right hand of him who sits on the throne (Apoc 5:7),
\latin{dispensationem ipsam Incarnationis a Patre et a seipso, secundum id quod
Deus est, dispositam accepisse Filius hominis dicitur}: the Son of man is said
to receive the very dispensation of the Incarnation, which the Father and he
himself, according as he is God, had disposed. He is the Lamb in his humanity
and the Father's right hand in his deity (\work{Explanatio Apocalypsis} I, on
5:5--7): the one who gives and the one who receives are the same Son, in his
two natures. On the greeting of chapter~1, Bede reads ``the first begotten of the
dead'' as the one who for the suffering of death was crowned with glory and
honour (Heb 2:9; Phil 2:9), and on the clause of Apoc 1:6 that the Introit
leaves out, ``hath made us a kingdom, and priests,'' he gives the consequence
for the redeemed: because the King of kings and heavenly Priest united us to
his body by offering himself for us, no saint lacks a share in the priesthood
(see the Preface, above).

Victorinus of Pettau had already read the sealed book this way. To open it is
to overcome death for man; because the Lamb overcame death as testator, it was
just that he be appointed heir and possess the human members (on Apoc
5:2--5).

\subsection{The objection: he received nothing in addition}
\label{sec:lamb-objection}

The strongest objection is St John Chrysostom's, on this Mass's own Gospel. On
``For this was I born,'' Chrysostom writes: ``If then He
was born a king, all His other attributes are by Generation, and He has
nothing which He received in addition.'' He draws at once a rule for every text
that speaks of giving to the Son: ``So that when you hear that, As the Father
has life in Himself, so has He given to the Son also to have life, deem of
nothing else but His generation, and so of the rest'' (\work{Homilies on John}
83.4; Jn 5:26). St Cyril of Alexandria says of the same verse that Christ's
sovereignty is ``not \dots\ added unto it from without, but \dots\ essentially and
naturally inherent'' (\work{Commentary on John} XII). To make Christ king only
from Easter, or to take the Introit's \latin{accipere divinitatem} to mean that
the Lamb becomes divine, would contradict them and the faith they defend.

Augustine's division grants Chrysostom's premise. The kingship by generation
is the decree of verse~7, and it is not received; the dominion asked for in
verse~8 is the same dominion, held now in the humanity and won by the passion.
But Augustine's division does not meet Chrysostom on his own ground. Chrysostom's rule refers a text
of giving to the eternal generation; the rule Cassiodorus states, and
Theodoret applies, refers the asking and receiving to the humanity. Chrysostom
does not expound Ps 2:8 at this place, and his rule is not a reading of it.
All of them hold that Christ is King by nature; they disagree on the narrower
question of how to read the texts in which the Son is given something or
bidden to ask. Hilary's reading of verse~7 of the resurrection fits Augustine's
answer only because he does not make the resurrection the beginning of the
Sonship: what is given is \latin{ut esset, quod fuerat ante}. Bellarmine, too,
grounds the decree in the eternal generation.

\work{Quas primas}, which instituted the feast, names both titles. It quotes
St Cyril for a dominion that is Christ's by essence and by nature, grounded in
the hypostatic union, so that to him as man angels and men are subject, and
then gives a second title, acquired by redemption, citing 1~Pet 1:18--19 on the price of his precious
blood (n.~13).

\subsection{The kingship received, from the Introit to the Postcommunion}
\label{sec:lamb-elements}

The Introit's Lamb \latin{qui occisus est} is worthy to receive because he was
slain, and its second sentence, \latin{Ipsi gloria et imperium}, gives him
glory and empire. Read with Hilary's servant raised into the name of God and
Bede's Son of man who receives what he disposed as God, its \latin{accipere
\dots\ divinitatem} means that the slain Lamb receives, in the nature he took,
the name and glory of the God he always was. Schuster's note on the antiphon
keeps the same order of death and glory: John names the Passion before the
glory, because the exaltation is in proportion to the humiliation (p.~201).
The Introit's verse, \latin{Deus, iudicium tuum Regi da}, is a second text in
which something is given to the King and the King's Son. Augustine reads it of
the Father giving all judgment to the Son (Jn 5:22), and Theodoret of the
authority he has as God and receives as man; and it is on this verse that
Cassiodorus states his rule that whenever something is asked for Christ, his
humanity is shown.

The Collect confesses that the Father willed, \latin{voluisti}, to restore all
things in his Son, and asks that the nations be subjected to him. If those
nations are the inheritance of Ps 2:8, the Collect asks for the inheritance
while it is still being given, and the Gradual, which sings a dominion from sea
to sea and the homage of the kings, sings it possessed. The Epistle speaks of
redemption ``through his blood,'' of peace ``through the blood of his cross,''
and of the ``firstborn from the dead'' who is head of the Church, according to
the humanity in Theodoret's reading and \latin{secundum quod huiusmodi} in
Aquinas's; it is under that title, firstborn from the dead, that Hilary has the
Son receive the nations. The Alleluia keeps only the power as possessed,
\latin{potestas aeterna}; the giving that Jerome expounds by Philippians stands
in the half of the verse that is not sung.

In the Gospel the King stands bound before the governor, the passion already
begun. Chrysostom says he was made captive ``by consenting, and giving Himself
up'' (\work{Homilies on John} 83.4). Since Aquinas has Christ ask for the
nations by his passion as well as by his prayer, the prisoner who could have
had servants fight for him, and gives himself up instead, is asking for the
kingship of Ps 2:8 in the trial itself. The Offertory then sings the command,
\latin{Postula a me}, as the gifts are brought to the altar, so that the
command to ask is heard while the sacrifice is made ready. Schuster, on this
antiphon, calls the verse the deed of investiture received from the Father
(p.~203).

The Secret says \latin{Hostiam \dots\ humanae reconciliationis offerimus} of
the Son \latin{quem sacrificiis praesentibus immolamus}, and asks of him for
all nations unity and peace. Augustine refers the asking of verse~8 to the
Manhood ``who offered up Himself as a Sacrifice in the stead of all
sacrifices,'' and Aquinas names the passion as one way in which Christ asks.
The Secret offers that same sacrifice now; read with Aquinas, it is still an
asking, and the unity and peace it asks for the nations can be taken as the
inheritance Hilary describes, the nations carried over into the kingdom. The
Preface puts the events in this order: the Son is anointed \latin{Sacerdotem
aeternum et universorum Regem}; he offers himself \latin{in ara Crucis} to
accomplish the mysteries of man's redemption; and only then, \latin{suo
subiectis imperio omnibus creaturis}, does he hand over the eternal and
universal kingdom. He is King from his anointing, before the cross, and the
subjection of all creatures comes after it. That order can be read against
the division Augustine makes between verses 7 and 8 and Aquinas states as two
rights: the kingship he has by inheritance, and the dominion he holds by
merit, asked for by his prayer and won by his passion.

The Communion sings the King seated for ever, \latin{Sedebit Dominus Rex in
aeternum}: the servant who in Hilary is raised into the name above every
name, and in Bede is crowned with glory and honour for the suffering of death,
now sits as King. The Postcommunion asks for those who fight under his
standards a share in his reign. This interpretation finds the people it asks
for in Hilary's co-heirs, who must themselves become kings, and in Bede's
saints, who share the priesthood of the King who offered himself; the standard
they fight under is, in Schuster's note on that prayer, the Cross (p.~204).

\begin{fourSenses}
\item[Literal.] An apocalyptic hymn acclaims the slain Lamb as worthy of power;
a royal psalm prays that God give judgment to the king and the king's son,
which Bellarmine reads literally of David's prayer for Solomon and Augustine,
Cassiodorus and Theodoret of Christ alone; another psalm has God promise the
nations to his Son, who is to ask for them; Paul thanks the Father for
redemption through the blood of the Son's cross; Jesus, bound, acknowledges
before the governor that he is a king.
\item[Allegorical.] Christ, God from eternity and King by that right, receives
as man, through his passion and resurrection, dominion over the nations, and
the nations so received are the Church gathered from them. The Lamb who dies
and the Lion who conquers are one Lord, who as God disposes the economy he
receives as man (Bede).
\item[Moral.] As man, Christ received the kingship through obedience unto death, and
God wills that even what is owed be asked (Aquinas). The faithful come into the
kingdom in the same way: by asking, and by offering themselves with the
Priest-King as members who share his priesthood (Bede); and they reign as
co-heirs by overcoming sin in themselves (Hilary).
\item[Anagogical.] The firstborn from the dead is the first of the exalted.
His members will be raised after him and will reign with him for ever in the
heavenly seat for which the Postcommunion asks.
\end{fourSenses}
