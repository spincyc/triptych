\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}

\movementthesis{This formulary reads two catastrophes --- a wilderness
generation that fell, and a city that did not recognize its visitation ---
onto the people standing in the church now, and then hands those people the
one thing that makes such a reading survivable: a memorial in which the work
of redemption is carried on, and an eating that makes an abiding. The warning
and the remedy are not two Sundays. They are one act.}

The order in which the book prints the ten texts is itself the argument. Read
as four stages:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=0.1em]
  \item Help confessed, then desire re-formed. \cue{Int.}, \cue{Coll.}
  \item A past disaster refused permission to stay past. \cue{Ep.}, \cue{Grad.}
  \item A visitation not recognized, and a house cleared for prayer. \cue{All.}, \cue{Gosp.}
  \item Judgment sung as sweetness, and the memorial that carries the redemption into a body. \cue{Off.}, \cue{Sec.}, \cue{Comm.}, \cue{Postcomm.}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{1. The formulary opens with help already given}

The Introit does something the psalm it quotes does not do. Psalm 53 opens
with a cry --- \latin{Deus, in nómine tuo salvum me fac} --- and reaches
\latin{Ecce Deus ádiuvat me} only at v.~6. The missal inverts that: the
antiphon takes vv.~6--7 and the psalm verse takes v.~3, so the assembly sings
the confession of present help first and the plea for rescue second. What the
psalm arrives at, the liturgy assumes.

The Collect then does something equally deliberate with the grammar of
petition. It asks that God's ears lie open to those who beg; but before it
asks that the things desired be granted, it asks God to \textit{make} the
petitioners ask for what pleases him --- \latin{fac eos, quæ tibi sunt
plácita, postuláre}. The printed semicolon after \latin{concédas} carries
that subordination. The Church here prays about her own praying. Nothing in
the day has yet said why that should be urgent. The Epistle says why.

\subsection*{2. A disaster that will not stay in the past}

The Epistle is a list of ruins: craving; an idol feast quoted verbatim from
Exodus 32:6; fornication with twenty-three thousand dead in one day; testing
answered by serpents; murmuring answered by the destroyer. Then comes the
hinge on which the whole Sunday turns. Paul will not let the list remain
history: \latin{Hæc autem ómnia in figúra contingébant illis: scripta sunt
autem ad correptiónem nostram, in quos fines sæculórum devenérunt.} These
things were written for \textit{our} correction --- and therefore
\latin{Itaque qui se exístimat stare, vídeat ne cadat.}

This is the interpretive key the formulary supplies for itself. Whatever else
the day's Gospel does, the Church has already declared, in the lesson read
immediately before it, that the catastrophes she reads out are aimed at the
people hearing them. The lesson then closes on the promise that God is
faithful and will not allow a testing beyond capacity but will make with the
testing an \latin{provéntum} --- an outcome, a way through --- so that they
can bear it.

The Gradual answers the sentence about self-estimate not with a rebuke but
with a name: \latin{Dómine Dóminus noster, quam admirábile est nomen tuum in
univérsa terra!} Its verse completes the same biblical verse ---
\latin{Quóniam eleváta est magnificéntia tua super cælos} --- so that the
whole chant is one verse divided at its own caesura, and that caesura is a
\latin{quóniam}: a reason. The man just told not to trust his own standing is
handed something else to stand on.

\subsection*{3. A city that did not know the time}

The Alleluia is the prayer of a man whose house is being watched by people
sent to kill him. Placed here it goes into the mouth of the assembly
immediately before a Gospel in which the one approaching the city is himself
days away from arrest.

That Gospel has four movements, and the pericope stops before a fifth. Christ
draws near, sees the city, and weeps. He names what is hidden: \latin{quæ ad
pacem tibi} --- a clause with no verb, broken off exactly where the Vulgate
breaks it. He foretells the rampart, the encirclement, the levelling, and
gives the cause: \latin{eo quod non cognóveris tempus visitatiónis tuæ}. He
enters the Temple, drives out the sellers with two prophetic texts fused into
one sentence, and then --- this is where the reading ends --- \latin{et erat
docens cotídie in templo}. The missal cuts before Luke 19:47b, where the chief
priests and scribes begin seeking to destroy him. The last thing the assembly
hears is not a plot against him. It is a teacher who has not stopped
teaching.

Two limits belong here rather than in the terminal appendix, because they
change what this section claims. First, Luke's form of the Temple saying is
the short one, \latin{domus mea domus oratiónis est}, without the
\latin{omnibus gentibus} that Mark 11:17 keeps from Isaiah 56:7; the guide
does not supply the missing words to the appointed text. Second, the Second
Vatican Council cited this very verse. \work{Nostra aetate}~4 writes that
``Jerusalem did not recognize the time of her visitation'' and attaches note~9,
\latin{Cf. Lk. 19:44}; the same article states that ``the Jews should not be
presented as rejected or accursed by God, as if this followed from the Holy
Scriptures.'' The Council read the verse and refused the inference. So does
this guide.

\subsection*{4. Judgment sung as sweetness, and carried into a body}

What the Church sings at the Offertory, directly after that Gospel, is a
centonisation about \latin{iudícia}: the Lord's judgments are upright, they
gladden hearts, they are sweeter than honey and the honeycomb, and the
servant keeps them. It is the least obvious available response to a prophecy
of siege. It is also the response the book prints.

The Secret then states, in a causal clause, what is happening on the altar:
\latin{quia, quóties huius hóstiæ commemorátio celebrátur, opus nostræ
redemptiónis exercétur}. That clause is not this formulary's private opinion.
The Second Vatican Council quoted it in the second article of
\work{Sacrosanctum Concilium} and identified its source in note~1 as
\latin{Missale romanum, oratio super oblata dominicae IX post Pentecosten};
\work{Presbyterorum ordinis}~13 reuses it with \latin{continuo} inserted and
names the same prayer in its own note. Of the ten texts on these two pages,
this is the one the Church has since quoted back.

The Communion says how that work reaches one person: by eating and drinking,
in a clause whose halves run in opposite directions --- \latin{in me manet, et
ego in eo}. The Postcommunion asks that a single act bear two fruits, one
personal and one corporate: \latin{et purificatiónem cónferat, et tríbuat
unitátem}. The Sunday that began by asking God to form our desires ends by
asking that what has been received make us clean and make us one. Neither is
described as finished.
