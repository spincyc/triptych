### 2.3 The chant forms and the lessons' departures: search for an origin

The chants depart from the Clementine at the Introit (a cento of Jer
29:11-14), the Gradual (*Liberásti … ex … et eos, qui nos odérunt … in
sǽcula*), the Alleluia and Offertory (*oratiónem meam*) and the Communion
(*Amen dico vobis, quidquid … et fiet vobis*); the Epistle opens *Fratres* and
reads *ex mórtuis*; the Gospel opens with the liturgical *In illo témpore: Dixit
Iesus turbis parábolam hanc*.

- The Pustet 1862 page images carry every one of these forms word for word.
  They are not 1962 novelties.
- The Communion differs from the Clementine Mk 11:24 (*Propterea dico vobis,
  omnia quaecumque … et evenient vobis*) at both edges: it opens *Amen dico
  vobis* and ends *et fiet vobis*. In Mark the appointed verse follows directly
  on the saying about faith that moves a mountain (11:23), which also opens
  *Amen dico vobis* and ends *fiet ei*. This is a textual observation about
  Mark's adjacent verse, not a derivation; how the antiphon's form arose is
  unresolved (next bullet).
- One datum read in this stage: Hilary, *Tract. in Ps.* 129 (PL 9 OCR, lines
  57618-57624), quotes the verse as *Domine exaudi vocem meam*, the Clementine
  form, not the chant's *orationem meam*. No other psalter or antiphonary
  witness was searched for any chant form, and no Old Latin (Vetus Latina)
  witness for the lessons. The forms' origin is **unresolved**; no study may
  assert one.
- The evangelist's own citation at Mt 13:35 (*eructabo abscondita a
  constitutione mundi*) is not the Vulgate psalm's wording (Ps 77:2 *loquar
  propositiones ab initio*). Jerome (section 7.4) discusses the citation's
  attribution, not this wording difference. This is a difference inside
  Scripture, recorded because readings 3 and 1 touch the verse.

Per the three-document profile, compilation history is recorded only where a
fact changes a claim. The facts that do: the 1862 and 1962 rubrics assign the
*Dicit Dominus* chants to the resumed Sundays (propers/verified.md), so a
commentator who expounds them under *Dominica XXIII* is commenting on this
formulary's chants (section 6); and Durandus's books gave his Sixth Sunday
after the Epiphany no proper Epistle or Gospel (section 6), so no medieval
commentator here expounds this formulary's lessons as a Sunday's.

## 7. The consequential textual difficulties

The test of the three-document profile: the appointed words that raise the
question, what each principal witness infers, and what changes if the answer
is accepted.

### 7.1 Gospel: *quod mínimum quidem est ómnibus semínibus … et fit arbor*

**The words.** The kingdom is like a seed that is *the least of all seeds* and
becomes *greater than all herbs* and *a tree*. Mustard is an herb; the text
says so (*oléribus*) and then calls it a tree. The question is what the small
thing is and what makes it great.

**The witnesses.**
- **Hilary** (13.4) identifies the seed with the Lord himself: the most
  pungent and smallest of seeds, *"cujus virtus ac potestas tribulationibus et
  pressuris accenditur"*. It is sown when he is seized, handed over to death
  and buried; it then outgrows the prophets, who were herbs for sick Israel;
  the branches are the apostles; the nations fly to them. The inference runs
  from the seed's pungency, released when it is crushed, to the Passion.
- **Ambrose** (VII.176-183) begins from the other saying of the mustard seed,
  faith that moves mountains, and concludes *"fides utique regnum coelorum
  est"*; then from the seed's nature: worthless and simple until crushed,
  *"si teri coeperit, vim suam fundit"*. So faith, *"si teratur adversis"*,
  pours out its power; so the martyrs, crushed by the sword, spread the grace
  of martyrdom through the world; so the Lord, crushed and buried in a garden.
  He holds the seed to be faith and Christ at once and gives each its proof.
- **Jerome** reads the seed as the preaching of the gospel: least of all
  disciplines because it preaches *"hominem Deum, Deum mortuum, et scandalum
  crucis"*; the philosophers' doctrines grow fast into herbs and wither. His
  inference is from the *content* of the preaching, not from crushing.
- **Chrysostom** (46.2) reads the parable as the Lord's answer to the
  disciples' fear of their fewness: *"His disciples were weakest of all … but
  nevertheless, because of the great power that was in them"*, the gospel was
  unfolded everywhere; and he turns it on the hearer (46.3): if twelve leavened
  the world, our failure to amend those near us is baseness.
- **Aquinas** gathers these: the doctrine seemed least because it preached God
  crucified, and grew greater in solidity, extent and use; he reports
  Chrysostom (the apostles, *modici et abiecti*) and Hilary (Christ).
- **Augustine** (I.11) reads the seed by its heat: *ob fervorem fidei*.
- **Bede** on Luke (*In Lucam* IV on 13:19) gives Jerome's and Augustine's
  readings in their words, then as a further sense the humility of the
  incarnation: the body Joseph buried in the garden rose and ascended, a tree
  whose branches are the preachers. The preaching grows in each believer by
  degrees, *"quia nemo repente fit perfectus"*.

**What changes.** If Hilary's and Ambrose's answer is accepted, the parable's
smallness is the smallness of the Passion and of faith under pressure, and its
greatness comes *through* the crushing, not after it; the Epistle's
Thessalonians, who received the word *in tribulatióne multa, cum gáudio
Spíritus Sancti* and whose faith then *sounded out* everywhere, are an instance
of that pattern. If Jerome's alone is accepted, the smallness is the
foolishness of the preached cross, and the Epistle's *non in sermóne tantum,
sed et in virtúte* (1:5; Aquinas: *non … in sublimitate sermonis*) is its
companion. Reading 1 takes the first; it keeps Jerome's as a compatible
emphasis, since both locate the smallness in the cross.

### 7.2 Gospel: *ferménto, quod accéptum múlier abscóndit in farínæ satis tribus, donec fermentátum est totum*

**The words.** Leaven elsewhere in Scripture is a figure of corruption (Mt
16:6; 1 Cor 5:7). Here it is the kingdom. A woman *hides* it in *three*
measures until *the whole* is leavened.

**The witnesses.** Each identifies the leaven, the woman and the three
measures differently. Ambrose says so himself: the comparison *"tantum
ambiguitatis offundit, ut diversas habeat sententias plurimorum"* (VII.187).
- **Augustine** (I.12): the woman is wisdom, the leaven *dilectio*, *"quod
  fervefacit et excitat"*; the three measures are heart, soul and mind (Mt
  22:37), or the three yields, or Noah, Daniel and Job.
- **Jerome**: the woman is apostolic preaching or the Church; the leaven the
  knowledge of Scripture; the three measures spirit, soul and body brought into
  one, so that they do not disagree and, agreeing, *"impetrent a Patre
  quodcumque postulaverint"* (Mt 18:19); or reason, anger and desire turned
  into prudence, hatred of vice and desire of virtue. He reports a Trinitarian
  reading and declines to let it ground doctrine.
- **Ambrose** reports and weighs readings before he states his own. He reports
  that many take the leaven for Christ, and on that reading has the Church hide
  the Lord Jesus *"in interioribus nostrae mentis"* (187); he finds it fitting
  (*"congruere visum est"*), from Matthew's three measures, to believe the Son
  hidden in the Law, covered in the Prophets, fulfilled in the Gospel (188);
  he reports some who take it of the world (189). His stated preference
  (*"Me tamen sequi iuvat"*, 190-191): the leaven is the Church's spiritual
  doctrine, as the Lord's warning about the Pharisees' leaven shows (Mt 16:6;
  1 Cor 5:8); the three measures are flesh, soul and spirit, the reborn man
  whom the Church sanctifies; the woman, the Church, mixes in doctrine
  *"donec fermentetur totus ille interior homo cordis absconditus"*. He then
  joins the three made one to the resurrection and to prayer that obtains
  (192-194; section 7.7).
- **Hilary** (13.5-6): the leaven is the Lord, hidden by the synagogue *"per
  judicium mortis"*; the measures are law, prophets and gospels; he rejects the
  Trinitarian and the three-nations readings.
- **Chrysostom** (46.2): the leaven is the disciples, hidden among the
  multitude; *hid* means mixed, not merely put; *three* means many.
- **Bede** on Luke (*In Lucam* IV on 13:21, PL 92 col. 507C-D): the leaven
  is love that heats and rouses the mind (Augustine's words); the woman is the
  Church, whose meal we are, ground between fear and hope into one bread; she
  hides love in the three measures *"quia praecipit Ecclesia ut diligamus
  Dominum ex toto corde, tota anima, tota virtute"*; or the measures are the
  three yields, the married, the continent and virgins. On *donec … totum*:
  *"charitas in nostra mente recondita eousque crescere debet, donec totam
  mentem in sui perfectionem commutet"*, so that the soul loves, does and
  remembers nothing but its maker's love; begun here, perfected where God is
  all in all. (Rabanus repeats this on Mt 13:33 without naming Bede; section
  5.)
- **Aquinas** names the difficulty: the same thing may be read *in malum* and
  *in bonum*; leaven by its corruption is evil, by its heat and power of
  spreading good.

**What changes.** There are two questions, what the leaven is and what the
measures are, and the witnesses divide differently on each. On the leaven:
love (Augustine; Bede, in Augustine's words), teaching (the knowledge of Scripture for Jerome; the
Church's spiritual doctrine for Ambrose, 190; *"caelestis doctrinae …
sacramentum"* in Chrysologus's allegory, *Sermo* 99), Christ (Hilary; the many
whom Ambrose reports at 187), or the disciples (Chrysostom). On the measures: the
whole person (Augustine's heart, soul and mind; Bede's heart, soul and
strength, which the Church commands us to love with; Jerome's spirit, soul and
body or the soul's three powers; Ambrose's flesh, soul and spirit, his stated
preference), or Law,
Prophets and Gospel (Hilary; Chrysologus; a reading Ambrose also finds
congruent at 188). If the measures are the whole person, whichever the leaven
is, the parable is about the transformation of one person until nothing in him
is unleavened, and *donec … totum* names its end, which Bede argues is love
changing the whole mind; Augustine, Bede, Jerome and Ambrose's stated
preference agree on this, though Augustine's and Bede's leaven is love and
Jerome's and Ambrose's is teaching. If the leaven is the disciples among
the nations (Chrysostom), the parable is about mission, and belongs with the
mustard seed (reading 1). If the measures are Law, Prophets and Gospel
(Hilary, with Christ as the leaven; Chrysologus, with heavenly doctrine;
Ambrose 188 as a congruent reading, with the Son hidden in them), the parable
is about the hidden unity of the two Testaments (reading 3 touches it through
13:35).
Reading 2 takes the first and keeps the others named; it must not give
Ambrose's 187 or 188 as his own preference.

### 7.3 Epistle: *óperis fidei vestræ, et labóris, et caritátis* (1:3)

**The words.** The Vulgate and the Missal coordinate three genitives,
*labóris* and *caritátis* standing apart. The Greek that Chrysostom,
Theodoret and Theophylact expound reads *τοῦ κόπου τῆς ἀγάπης*, *the labour of
love*.

**The witnesses.** Chrysostom: *"what labor is it to love? Merely to love is no
labor at all. But to love genuinely is great labor"*, and Jason's danger at
Thessalonica shows it. Theodoret: love has *"surpassing labour"*, for one must
bear a brother's faults, envy, anger, pride and ingratitude. Theophylact
repeats Chrysostom in Latin (*laborem charitatis*). Aquinas, on the Latin,
joins *labóris* to faith: *"memores fidei vestrae operantis et laborantis.
Item, charitatis"*, faith that works and toils, then charity.

**What changes.** On the Greek reading the labour belongs to love itself; on
Aquinas's Latin reading labour is the mark of living faith, and charity is
named beside it. Both readings make love costly and active, not a feeling.
Reading 2 uses both: the Greek Fathers for the cost of love, Aquinas for faith
that works. A study must not attribute *labor of love* to the Missal's Latin or
*fidei operantis et laborantis* to the Greek Fathers.

### 7.4 Gospel: *ut implerétur quod dictum erat per Prophétam … eructábo abscóndita a constitutióne mundi* (13:35)

**The words.** Matthew calls the psalmist a prophet and quotes Ps 77:2 in a
form not the Vulgate psalm's (section 2.3). Some codices Jerome knew named
Isaiah.

**The witnesses.** **Jerome**: the citation is from Ps 77, whose title is
*Asaph*; the original probably read *per Asaph prophetam*, and a scribe who
did not know the name corrected it to the better-known Isaiah; other codices
then dropped the name *"quia minime inveniebatur in Isaia"*; the psalmists
are rightly called prophets; the psalm, spoken in the Lord's person, narrates
the exodus. **Augustine** (on Ps 77): *parables and propositions* call for an
inward sense; the mystery of the kingdom was veiled in the Old Testament; we
must pray that he who opened his mouth in parables open our understanding.
**Aquinas**: the Lord spoke in parables in the prophets and in himself;
*eructabo* is speech from the depth of wisdom; hidden from the world's
foundation (Eph 3:5). **Chrysostom** (47.1): the prophet shows the Lord was
*"not making a new thing"*; parables lead to inquiry. Jerome's sentence ends
in col. 93: all that is written is to be understood *parabolice*, not only the
letter but *abscondita sacramenta*, as the Saviour promised in the psalm's
words (Rabanus repeats it).

**What changes.** If the psalm is the Lord's own voice (Jerome, Aquinas), the
parables of the mustard seed and the leaven are themselves a revelation of
things hidden from the beginning, and their hiddenness is purposeful (the
leaven is *hidden*; the seed is *buried*). Reading 3 builds on that. The
Asaph/Isaiah question is a textual note; no reading turns on it, and the
studies should not dramatize it.

### 7.5 The tenses: *avertísti*, *Liberásti*, *erípuit nos ab ira ventúra*

**The words.** The Introit verse (*avertísti captivitátem*) and the Gradual
(*Liberásti nos*) speak of deliverance as done; the Epistle ends *qui erípuit
nos ab ira ventúra*, a past rescue from a wrath still to come, and between them
*exspectáre Fílium eius de cælis*. The Introit antiphon itself speaks in
futures (*invocábitis … exáudiam … redúcam*), and the Communion promises
*accipiétis*. (The Greek of 1 Thess 1:10 has a present participle; Chrysostom's
translator renders *which delivereth us*; this is not a reading of the Missal's
Latin.)

**The witnesses.** **Augustine** on Ps 43:8: *"spoken of the future under the
figure of the past … because to God things to come also are as certain as if
they were past"*; on Ps 84:2: the captivity turned away is from sin, and the
psalmist prays *"because they are not yet done"*. **Aquinas** on 1 Thess 1:10:
we await deliverance from the punishment to come; from its cause, sin, we are
delivered by Christ, *"Unde dicit qui eripuit nos"*. On Ps 43:8: *vel in
praesenti … vel in futuro*. **Chrysostom** on Ps 43:8: we have present pledges
of providence and need not speak of the old things; on 1 Thess 1:10: *"The
terrible things are in hand, but the good things are in the future … See how
much hope is required."* **Jerome** on Jer 29:11-12: future things are known to
God alone; God could fulfil his promise without the captives' prayer, *"sed
hortatur eos ad preces, ut quod promissum est, mereantur accipere"*.

**What changes.** The witnesses agree that the past tenses state something
already done (deliverance from sin, present pledges) and something certain but
not yet seen; they differ in where they put the weight (Augustine: certainty of
the future; Chrysostom: present pledges; Aquinas: the cause removed now, the
penalty escaped later). Reading 3 is built on this agreement and keeps the
differences.

### 7.6 The orations

No commentary on the three orations was located (sections 4, 6). They are read
as composed texts:

- **Collect.** *Semper rationabília meditántes, quæ tibi sunt plácita, et dictis
  exsequámur et factis*: a petition that constant thought on what is
  reasonable be carried out in speech and act. Its pairing of word and deed
  stands beside the Epistle's *non … in sermóne tantum, sed et in virtúte*
  (1:5) and *óperis fidei* (1:3); this is a verbal and thematic relation
  observed by the editor, not a witness's. *Rationabília* has no checked
  witness tying it to Jerome's *rationabile* (the rational part of the soul in
  his leaven exposition); a study may set the words side by side as editorial
  synthesis and must not credit Jerome with commenting on the Collect. The
  same holds for Ambrose's *"diuturna meditatione collatum"*, said of the
  Church's leavening work in the whole person (VII.191), beside the Collect's
  *semper … meditántes*: two texts the editor sets side by side, no witness's
  comment on the Collect.
- **Secret.** *Mundet … et rénovet, gubérnet et prótegat*: four verbs of one
  petition, cleansing and renewal before governance and protection. The
  relation to Aquinas's *in malum* leaven (1 Cor 5:7, *expurgate vetus
  fermentum*) and to Chrysologus's leaven of death replaced by the leaven of
  resurrection is editorial.
- **Postcommunion.** *Ut semper éadem, per quæ veráciter vívimus, appetámus*:
  fed, we ask to go on desiring the same things by which we truly live. Its
  *semper … appetámus* is a petition for continuing desire after receiving;
  Chrysostom's remark that the prayer from the depths already changes the one
  who prays before he receives (Ps 129.1) and Bede's charity begun here and
  perfected hereafter (on Lk 13:21) bear on it only as editorial synthesis.

### 7.7 Communion: *quidquid orántes pétitis, crédite quia accipiétis, et fiet vobis*

**The words.** The antiphon promises, without stated limit, that whatever is
asked in prayer will be done, on the one condition of believing that it is
received. Read literally, it promises any object to any petitioner.

**The witnesses.**
- **Bede**, *In Marc.* III (tracked Latin, line 339, under the lemma of Mk
  11:25): *"Notanda distinctio deprecantium."* The perfect, whose faith
  *"per dilectionem operatur"*, can by prayer or command move spiritual
  mountains, as Paul did with Elymas and with the Pythoness at Philippi;
  persecution followed at once on the second. Those not yet perfect should ask that their sins
  be forgiven, *"et absque dubio impetrabunt quod petunt, si tamen in se
  peccantibus aliis primo ipsi dimittunt"*; if they refuse, they cannot obtain
  even pardon. On v. 23 (line 338) he reads the mountain as the devil, cast
  out of hearts by the preaching of holy teachers, while allowing that a
  literal mountain could be moved by prayer if need required; the answer to
  the pagans and the devil as the mountain follow Jerome on Mt 21:21, and the
  distinction of those who pray (on v. 25) is not in Jerome. His answer
  qualifies the promise by who prays and by the condition of forgiving.
- **Chrysostom**, *Hom. in Mt.* 67.2 (NPNF I.10, lines 37980-37998), on the
  Matthean parallel, Mt 21:21-22 (*omnia quaecumque petieritis in oratione
  credentes, accipietis*), read in iteration 2. The withered fig and the
  promise were given *"for His disciples' sake"*, *"that He might train them to
  feel confidence"*; his paraphrase: *"Ye also shall do greater things, if ye
  are willing to believe and to be confident in prayer"*, said *"so that they
  might not be afraid and tremble at plots against them"*, and repeated *"to
  make them cleave to prayer and faith"*. His answer places the promise in the
  disciples' coming trials: it is given to make them confident in faith and
  prayer under persecution, not to guarantee any object. This is his comment on
  Matthew's verse, credited to Mt 21:21-22 and not to Mk 11:24.
- **Jerome**, *In Mt.* III on Mt 21:21-22 (PL 26 col. 154, page image), and
  **Hilary**, *In Mt.* 21.7 (PL 9 OCR, cols. 1037-1038), read in iteration 2:
  both read the mountain as the devil, cast down by the apostles' faith, and
  Hilary praises the reward of faith; neither comments on v. 22's prayer. A
  negative result for the promise itself.
- **Hilary**, *Tract. in Ps.* 129.2-3 (PL 9 tracked OCR, lines 57595-57604,
  within the bound range 57590-57625; col. 718): God is everywhere and hears,
  sees and acts, *"sed orandus a nobis est, ut secundum precem nostram adsit, audiat,
  videat, efficiat. Naturae suae est ut audiat; sed fidei nostrae est ut
  precemur audiri"*; he hears even those who revile him, *"sed fides
  officium suum exsequitur, ut Dei auditionem roget"*. He comments on the
  psalm, not on Mark; his answer bears on the Communion only as the editor
  applies it.
- **Jerome**, *In Mt.* II (PL 26 col. 91, page image), and **Ambrose**,
  *Exp. in Lucam* VII.192-193: on the leaven, not on Mark, both join prayer
  that obtains to the unity of the whole person. Jerome: spirit, soul and body
  brought into one, *"impetrent a Patre quodcumque postulaverint"* (Mt 18:19).
  Ambrose puts the full obtaining at the resurrection, *"et tunc omnia quae
  petimus, impetremus"*, reports that many take Mt 18:19 to mean this, and
  reports readings in which soul and spirit, agreeing in the body, can bring
  all that is prayed for to effect (193).

**What changes.** If Bede's answer is accepted, the promise is not a
guarantee of any object to anyone: it is made to faith that works through
love, and for those not yet perfect its sure object is forgiveness, on the
condition that they forgive. If Hilary's is accepted, prayer does not inform
or move a God who already hears; it is the part faith plays, and the
antiphon's *crédite* names it. If Jerome's and Ambrose's is accepted, the
condition is the concord of the person who prays, and Ambrose places the full
answer at the resurrection. If Chrysostom's (on the parallel) is accepted, the
promise is the Lord's encouragement of disciples who will face plots, so that
they hold to faith and prayer. The answers are compatible: each locates the
promise's condition in the one who prays, not in what is asked. Reading 1 uses
Bede's perfect faith, with Chrysostom on the parallel; reading 2 uses Jerome
and Ambrose, with Bede's forgiveness; reading 3 uses Hilary, Jerome's *Petite,
et accipietis* (*In Hier.* V) and Chrysostom on the parallel. A study must not
present the promise as unconditioned, and must not credit Hilary, Jerome,
Ambrose or Chrysostom with commenting on Mk 11:24; every join of their words
to the Communion is the editor's.

## 8. Material disagreements and differences

### 8.1 What the mustard seed is

Christ himself (Hilary 13.4; Ambrose 179-183; Chrysologus 98; Bede, *In
Lucam* IV on 13:19, as a further sense, repeated by Rabanus); faith (Ambrose
176-178; Augustine's *fervor fidei*, which Bede repeats); the preaching or
doctrine of the gospel (Jerome; Aquinas after him; Bede on Mark, and on Luke
in Jerome's words); the
apostles (Chrysostom as Aquinas reports him; Chrysostom 46.2 speaks of the
gospel spread by the weak disciples). These are compatible identifications in
Ambrose's own exposition, which holds faith and Christ together; reading 1
does not harmonize them into one and names each.

### 8.2 What the leaven and the three measures are

Section 7.2. What the leaven is divides the witnesses: love (Augustine; Bede
on Lk 13:21, in Augustine's words), teaching (Jerome: the knowledge of Scripture; Ambrose's stated
preference, VII.190: the Church's spiritual doctrine; Chrysologus's allegory:
the sacrament of heavenly doctrine), Christ (Hilary; reported
by Ambrose at 187 as the view of many), the disciples (Chrysostom). On the
three measures, Augustine, Bede (heart, soul and strength), Jerome and Ambrose
(190-191: flesh, soul and spirit) agree that they are the whole person; Hilary and Chrysologus take them as Law,
Prophets and Gospel, which Ambrose also finds congruent (188) without making
it his preference. Another live disagreement is the Trinitarian reading of the
three measures: Jerome calls it *pius* but refuses it doctrinal weight; Hilary
doubts that reason permits it and argues against it; Chrysologus uses it
(Sara's three measures, *in aequalitate Patris, et Filii, et Spiritus
sancti*); Aquinas reports it without judgment. No study may present the
Trinitarian reading as the Fathers' consensus or as an argument for the
doctrine.

### 8.3 Who hid the leaven

Hilary: the synagogue, through the judgment of death; Ambrose and
Chrysologus: the Church (Chrysologus also Eve and Mary); Bede: the Church,
the daughter of Abraham raised up, hiding love by commanding it; Augustine: wisdom;
Jerome: apostolic preaching or the Church; Aquinas reporting Chrysostom:
divine wisdom, hiding the apostles in tribulations. Hilary's reading makes the
hiding an act of hostility God turns to good; the others make it an act of
grace. Both stand; reading 2 follows the second and names the first.

### 8.4 Labour of love (Greek) and faith that labours (Aquinas's Latin)

Section 7.3. A difference of text and construction, not of doctrine.

### 8.5 The tenses of deliverance

Section 7.5. A difference of emphasis among witnesses who agree.
