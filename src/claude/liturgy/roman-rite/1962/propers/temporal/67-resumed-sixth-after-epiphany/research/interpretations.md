## The question each reading answers

The Gospel sets two parables side by side and closes with the evangelist's
remark on parables. Each reading begins from a different hard word in it, and
each finds its answer worked out in the Epistle and the chants:

1. `crushed-seed` asks **how the least of all seeds becomes a tree**
   (*mínimum … ómnibus semínibus … fit arbor*, Mt 13:32). Its answer, from
   Hilary and Ambrose, is that the seed gives its power when it is crushed:
   Christ in the Passion, faith under adversity; and the Thessalonians, who
   received the word *in tribulatióne multa, cum gáudio Spíritus Sancti* and
   whose faith then *sounded out* everywhere, show the pattern happening. Its
   consequence is that the Church's smallness and suffering are the means of
   its growth.
2. `hidden-leaven` asks **what the leaven is, what the three measures are, and
   what *until the whole is leavened* requires** (Mt 13:33). Its answer, from
   Augustine, Bede, Jerome and Ambrose, is that the leaven, charity
   (Augustine; Bede, who argues that it must grow until it has changed the
   whole mind) or the teaching of Scripture and of the Church (Jerome;
   Ambrose's stated preference), is hidden in the whole person (heart, soul
   and mind; heart, soul and strength; spirit, soul and body; flesh, soul and
   spirit) until nothing in him is unleavened; the Epistle's *work of faith, labour and
   charity* and the Collect's *in word and in deed* are where that
   transformation shows. Its consequence is moral and interior: the kingdom
   works by permeation, slowly, from within.
3. `already-and-awaited` asks **why the Mass speaks of deliverance as done
   while it waits for it** (*avertísti*, *Liberásti*, *erípuit nos ab ira
   ventúra*; *exspectáre Fílium*; *abscóndita a constitutióne mundi*). Its
   answer, from Augustine and Jerome with Chrysostom and Aquinas, is that God's
   promise is certain as if already done, that its cause (sin) is already
   removed, and that the Church therefore prays and waits, as the exiles of
   Jeremiah's letter were commanded to pray for what was promised. Its
   consequence is anagogical: hope that asks, believes and waits.

They are complementary, not rival doctrines. They differ in which word of the
Gospel they take as the key, in what the Epistle shows (growth through
suffering; charity's labour; waiting for the Son), and in which of the four
senses carries the weight. Section *Comparison* states where their answers
meet and part.

## 1. `crushed-seed` — The least of all seeds, crushed, fills the world

### Controlling claim

The kingdom's smallness is the smallness of the crushed seed: Christ seized,
killed and buried, and faith pressed by adversity. Its power is released by
that crushing, and its growth fills the world. The Thessalonians are the
proof the Epistle gives: they received the word in much tribulation with joy,
and from them the word sounded out in every place.

### Carrying authors and their reasoning

- **Hilary of Poitiers** (father), *In Mt.* 13.4 (PL 9 col. 994, page image).
  He identifies the seed with the Lord: smallest and most pungent of seeds,
  *"cujus virtus ac potestas tribulationibus et pressuris accenditur"*; sown
  when seized, handed to death and buried, it outgrows the prophets, the herbs
  given to sick Israel; the apostles are its branches; the nations, buffeted
  by the devil, fly to them *"in spem vitae"*. His inference runs from the
  seed's pungency, kindled by pressure, to the Passion and to the mission of
  the apostles.
- **Ambrose** (father), *Exp. in Lucam* VII.176-183 (on the Lukan parallel;
  he cites Mt 13:32 at 183). Faith and the kingdom are both compared to the
  mustard seed, so *"fides … regnum coelorum est"*; the seed is worthless and
  simple until crushed, *"si teri coeperit, vim suam fundit"*; so faith, *"si
  teratur adversis"*, pours out its power; so the martyrs Felix, Nabor and
  Victor, *"contriti gladio"*, spread the grace of martyrdom through the world
  (Ps 18:5); so the Lord, crushed, pressed, sown and buried in a garden, rose
  a tree; *"semina in horto tuo Christum"*; *"Premitur fides, cum crucifixum
  credimus Christum"*. He holds faith and Christ together as the seed.
- **John Chrysostom** (father), *Hom. in Mt.* 46.2-3: the parable answers the
  disciples' fear of their fewness; they were *"weakest of all, and least of
  all"*, but by the power in them the gospel was unfolded everywhere; *"if
  twelve men leavened the whole world, imagine how great our baseness"*, and
  it was their life, not their signs, that made them great. And *Hom. in 1
  Thess.* 1-2: *"If thou believest, suffer all things"*; God allowed
  persecution at once *"that no one might say, that the Preaching was
  established lightly"*; *"The affliction is in things bodily, and the joy in
  things spiritual"*; the Thessalonians' report *"sounded forth"* like a
  trumpet.

Supporting:
- **Jerome** (father), *In Mt.* II (PL 26 col. 90): the preaching is *"minima
  … omnibus disciplinis"* because it preaches *"hominem Deum, Deum mortuum, et
  scandalum crucis"*; the philosophers' doctrines grow into herbs that wither;
  mustard-seed faith is great faith (1 Cor 13:2). Compatible: he locates the
  smallness in the cross preached rather than in crushing.
- **Thomas Aquinas** (doctor), *Super Mt.* c. 13 (pp. 186-187): the doctrine
  seemed least because it preached God crucified, and grew greater *"in
  soliditate, in generalitate, et in utilitate"*; the apostles, *modici et
  abiecti*, did more than Alexander and Rome. *Super I Thess.* c. 1 l. 1: *in
  virtute* means not in sublimity of speech (1 Cor 2:4); the joy of the Spirit
  *"facit gaudium patientibus propter Christum, quia amant eum"*.
- **Peter Chrysologus** (father), *Sermo* 98 (on the Lukan parallel): Christ
  the seed, *"in hortum virginei corporis missus, orbe toto crucis in arborem
  crevit"*, giving his flavour *"cum teritur passione"*.
- **Bede** (father), *In Marc.* on 4:30-32: the tree's height, breadth and
  duration; its breadth because *"per praedicatores … totius mundi terminos
  occupavit"*. And *In Lucam* IV on Lk 13:18-19 (PL 92 cols. 506D-507B; the
  Lukan parallel). His first reading is largely Jerome's and Augustine's
  words (the kingdom the preaching of the gospel and knowledge of the
  Scriptures; the seed *ob fervorem … fidei, vel quod dicatur venena
  expellere*). He then offers, as a further possible sense (*"Potest in grano
  sinapis ipsa dominicae incarnationis humilitas intelligi"*), the humility of
  the incarnation: Joseph buried the crucified Saviour's body in the garden;
  he rose and ascended, a tree; his branches are the preachers spread through
  the world, in whose words the faithful rest from this life's weariness. The
  thought of the Lord sown and risen in the garden is Ambrose's before him
  (VII.179-180), not in these words. Bede also says the preaching grows *"in
  mente cujusque credentis, quia nemo repente fit perfectus"* (Ps 83:6-8).

The carrying authors agree on the controlling claim: the seed's smallness is
real, its power comes through suffering (Hilary's *tribulationibus et
pressuris*, Ambrose's *si teratur*, Chrysostom's weak disciples and persecuted
Thessalonians), and it fills the world. They differ on what the seed is
(section *Difficulty*).

### What each element contributes

- **Gospel** (Mt 13:31-32; 13:33 as Chrysostom reads it, the disciples hidden
  in the multitude): the parable itself; the leaven read with Chrysostom as
  the same mission (46.2), not as reading 2's interior charity.
- **Epistle** (1 Thess 1:2-10): the instance. *Excipiéntes verbum in
  tribulatióne multa, cum gáudio Spíritus Sancti* (1:6) and *A vobis enim
  diffamátus est sermo Dómini … in omni loco* (1:8) are what Chrysostom (Hom.
  1-2) and Aquinas read as faith shown in suffering and fame spreading. The
  join of the parable to this lesson is *(editorial)*: no witness sets them
  side by side. Ambrose's *In omnem terram exivit sonus eorum* for the martyrs
  and the Epistle's *in omni loco* are two texts about the word's spread; their
  juxtaposition is *(editorial)*.
- **Introit** (Jer 29:11-14 cento; Ps 84:2): the exiles in Babylon, a small
  and captive people, are told that God's thoughts toward them are of peace
  and that he will bring them back *de cunctis locis*. It sets the Mass's
  first word inside a captivity *(editorial as to the link)*. Jerome's *finem
  captivitatis, et patientiam laborum praesentium* (*In Hier.* V) glosses *ut
  dem vobis finem et patientiam*, a clause of Jer 29:11 the antiphon omits; it
  is comment on the omitted context, not on the chant's words, and may be used
  only as such.
- **Gradual** (Ps 43:8-9): *Liberásti nos … ex affligéntibus nos … In Deo
  laudábimur tota die*. Augustine: *"the voice of the Martyrs"*; Chrysostom:
  *all the day* is the whole life, and we glory *"not only when you help us,
  but also when you forsake us"*, as Paul glories in the cross (Gal 6:14).
- **Alleluia and Offertory** (Ps 129:1-2): Hilary on the cry *"de profundo
  humanarum calamitatum aestu"*; Chrysostom: *"such are the souls of the
  afflicted"*, whose prayer, rooted deep like a strong tree, stands every gust.
  The image of the deep-rooted tree is Chrysostom's on the psalm; its
  likeness to the mustard tree is *(editorial)*.
- **Communion** (Mk 11:24): in Mark the appointed verse follows directly on
  the saying about faith that moves a mountain (11:23), which also opens *Amen
  dico vobis*; how the antiphon's form arose is unresolved (scope section 2.3).
  Jerome (col. 90-91) and Ambrose (176) read the mustard seed through the
  mountain-moving faith of Mt 17:19 / Lk 17:6. Bede on Mk 11:22-25: perfect
  faith *"quae per dilectionem operatur"* moves spiritual mountains, as Paul did
  at Philippi, whereupon persecution followed (scope section 7.7). On the
  Matthean parallel of the antiphon's verse (Mt 21:21-22), Chrysostom (*Hom. in
  Mt.* 67.2) says the sign and the promise were given to train the disciples to
  confidence, *"so that they might not be afraid and tremble at plots against
  them"*, and to make them cleave to prayer and faith; Jerome and Hilary take
  the mountain for the devil, cast down by the faith of the apostles. The link
  between the antiphon and the parable is *(editorial)*, made through Mark's
  sequence of verses and the Fathers' own joining of mustard seed and
  mountain-moving faith.
- **Collect** (*dictis exsequámur et factis*): beside the Epistle's *non … in
  sermóne tantum, sed et in virtúte* (1:5); Chrysostom: it was the apostles'
  life, not their signs, that made them great *(editorial as to the Collect)*.
- **Secret** and **Postcommunion**: touched lightly. The Secret's *mundet et
  rénovet, gubérnet et prótegat* asks protection for those whom the Gradual
  calls afflicted; the Postcommunion asks that those fed may always desire
  what gives true life *(editorial)*.

### The difficulty and the disagreement

*Mínimum … fit arbor* (section 7.1 of the scope). Hilary and Chrysologus: the
seed is Christ (Bede on Luke offers it as a further sense, the humility of the
incarnation); Ambrose: faith and Christ; Jerome, Aquinas and Bede: the
preaching of the gospel; Chrysostom: the gospel carried by the weak apostles
(Aquinas reports him as the apostles); Augustine: *fervor fidei*. The reading
does not reduce these to one: Hilary's identification carries the Passion,
Ambrose's carries the believer's share in it, Chrysostom's carries the
mission. They agree that the smallness is real and that the power is God's.
The botanical difficulty (an herb that becomes a tree) is answered by Aquinas
and Bede as a figure of the doctrine's surpassing the others, and by Hilary as
the Lord's surpassing the prophets; no witness treats it as a factual claim
about mustard to be defended.

The strongest alternative within the reading: Jerome's reading puts the
smallness in the *content* of the preaching (the cross is foolishness), not in
suffering. It is compatible and is kept as a supporting emphasis; a study must
not make Jerome say that the seed's power is released by persecution.

### Four senses

- **Literal.** A mustard seed, smallest of the seeds a Galilean farmer sowed,
  grows past the garden herbs to a plant birds lodge in; Jesus tells this to
  the crowds by the sea (Mt 13:1-2, 31-32). Paul writes to a young church,
  founded in a few weeks and at once persecuted (Acts 17:1-10), that its faith
  is spoken of everywhere (1 Thess 1:6-8).
- **Allegorical.** The seed is Christ, seized, killed and buried, who rose and
  filled the world through the apostles (Hilary 13.4; Ambrose 179-181;
  Chrysologus 98; Bede, *In Lucam* IV on 13:19, the humility of the
  incarnation buried in the garden and risen); the birds are the nations who
  rest in the Church (Hilary).
- **Moral.** Faith gives its power when it is pressed by adversity (Ambrose
  178); the believer sows Christ in his own garden (Ambrose 180) and suffers
  all things because he believes (Chrysostom, Hom. 1 in 1 Thess.); a few who
  live the gospel leaven many, and failure to do so is our fault, not the
  gospel's (Chrysostom 46.3).
- **Anagogical.** The tree under whose branches the nations rest, *"tuta iam
  sede"* (Ambrose 185-186), and the hope of life to which the nations fly
  (Hilary 13.4); its duration, *"quia veritas quam praedicat, nullo unquam fine
  delebitur"* (Bede on Mk 4).

### Limits

- Hilary, Ambrose and Chrysologus expound the seed; none of them comments on
  this Epistle, these chants or this Mass. Every link from the parable to the
  Thessalonians, the Gradual or the Communion is the editor's.
- Ambrose, Chrysologus and Bede (*In Lucam*) are cited for Luke's parallel;
  Ambrose may be cited for Matthew's wording where he quotes it (183, 188,
  191). Bede's first reading of the seed is largely Jerome's and Augustine's words;
  only his further sense, the incarnation's humility, is cited as his.
- Chrysostom, Jerome and Hilary on Mt 21:21-22 comment on Matthew's parallel
  of the Communion's verse, not on Mk 11:24.
- Chrysostom's 46.3 rebuke is moral exhortation to his hearers; it is not a
  reading of any other element.
- Augustine's *Quaest. Ev.* I.11 is two lines; it supports *fervor fidei* and
  no more.

## 2. `hidden-leaven` — Charity hidden in the whole person until all is leavened

### Controlling claim

The leaven is the love of God (Augustine; Bede), or the teaching of Scripture
and of the Church that kindles it (Jerome; Ambrose), hidden in the whole person
until it has changed everything in him (Bede: until it has changed the whole
mind into its own perfection); its slow and hidden work shows in faith that
labours and in word and deed together. The Epistle
names the signs of that work in a church (*óperis fidei … labóris …
caritátis*), and the Collect asks for it (*dictis exsequámur et factis*).

### Carrying authors and their reasoning

- **Augustine** (father), *Quaest. Ev.* I.12: *"mulierem, sapientiam dicit:
  fermentum, dilectionem; quod fervefacit et excitat"*; the three measures are
  *"Ex toto corde, et ex tota anima, et ex tota mente"* (Mt 22:37), or the
  three yields of the sower, or Noah, Daniel and Job. His inference runs from
  leaven's heat and rising to love, and from three to the whole of the person
  the great commandment names.
- **Bede** (father), *In Lucam* IV on Lk 13:20-21 (PL 92 col. 507C-D; the
  Lukan parallel). Part of this passage is other Fathers' words: the measure
  *satum* (Jerome's note), the leaven as love, *"Fermentum ergo dilectionem dicit, quae fervefacit, et
  excitat mentem"* (Augustine's gloss, with *mentem* added), and the Church
  *"cujus farina nos sumus"* (a phrase Ambrose uses at VII.187). The argument
  he builds on them is not in Augustine, Jerome, Ambrose or Chrysologus as read
  for this study. The woman is the Church, the daughter of Abraham whom the
  Lord has just raised up (Lk 13:10-17); we are her meal, ground between fear
  and hope as between the upper and the lower millstone, so that we may be one
  bread and one body (1 Cor 10:17). *"Abscondit ergo mulier fermentum
  dilectionis in farinae sata tria, quia praecipit Ecclesia ut diligamus
  Dominum ex toto corde, tota anima, tota virtute"*: the hiding is the
  Church's commandment of love. The three measures may also be the three
  yields (Augustine's alternative), which he names the married, the continent
  and virgins. Then *donec
  … totum*: *"charitas in nostra mente recondita eousque crescere debet, donec
  totam mentem in sui perfectionem commutet, ut nihil videlicet praeter ipsam
  conditoris sui dilectionem diligere, agere, recolere anima possit"*. This is
  begun here and perfected where God is all in all (1 Cor 15:28), who warms
  all *"uno eodemque suae dilectionis igne"*. Beyond Augustine's two-line
  gloss he gives three things: why the leaven is hidden in three measures (the
  Church commands love of the whole person), what the meal is (ourselves,
  ground and made one), and an argument from *donec* that love must change the
  whole mind, its loves, deeds and memory, with an end beyond this life.
  Rabanus repeats the yields and the *donec* sentence on Mt 13:33 (PL 107
  col. 950) under his own siglum; the words are Bede's.
- **Jerome** (father), *In Mt.* II (PL 26 cols. 91-92, page images): the woman
  *"mihi videtur"* is apostolic preaching or the Church; the leaven the
  knowledge of the Scriptures, hidden *"ut spiritus, anima, et corpus in unum
  redacta, non discrepent inter se; sed cum duobus et tribus convenerint,
  impetrent a Patre quodcumque postulaverint"* (Mt 18:19). He then gives
  another way the place is expounded (*"Disseritur locus iste, et aliter"*),
  from Plato's three parts of the soul, and takes it up in the first person
  plural: the gospel's leaven brings reason, anger and desire into one, *"ut
  in ratione possideamus prudentiam; in ira, odium contra vitia; in
  desiderio, cupiditatem virtutum"*. The Trinitarian reading he reports as
  some others' (*"tertiam quorumdam intelligentiam"*) and limits.
- **Ambrose** (father), *Exp. in Lucam* VII.187-194 (on the Lukan parallel;
  he quotes Mt 13:33 at 191). He first reports and weighs: many take the
  leaven for Christ, and on that reading the Church hides the Lord Jesus *"in
  interioribus nostrae mentis"* (187); it seemed fitting, from Matthew's three
  measures, to believe the Son hidden in Law, Prophets and Gospel (188); some
  take it of the world (189). Then his own preference, *"Me tamen sequi iuvat
  quod ipse Dominus docuit fermentum Ecclesiae spiritalem esse doctrinam"*
  (190): agreeing, he says, with good authors (*"bonis auctoribus
  acquiescimus"*), the Church *"renatum hominem qui est in corpore et anima et
  spiritu, fermento sanctificat spirituali"*; *"tres enim mensurae sunt, ut dixi,
  carnis, animae, et spiritus"*; the woman, the Church, mixes in spiritual
  doctrine *"donec fermentetur totus ille interior homo cordis absconditus"*
  (191). The work is *"non tumultuarium, nec fortuitum, sed diuturna
  meditatione collatum"*, that the three be one (191); when flesh does not
  lust against spirit they are one leaven (192), and if they remain so in this
  life, *"erit in futurum diligentibus Christum incorrupta communio"* (194).
  His inference runs from the Lord's own use of leaven for teaching (Mt 16:6)
  to the Church's doctrine, and from the three measures to the reborn person,
  body, soul and spirit.

Supporting:
- **Thomas Aquinas** (doctor), *Super Mt.* c. 13 (p. 188): leaven is read *in
  malum* by its corruption (1 Cor 5:7), *in bonum* by its heat and spreading;
  reporting Augustine (*"secundum Augustinum"*), *"per fermentum fervor
  caritatis significatur: quia sicut fermentum dilatat, ita caritas"* (Ps
  118:32), hidden *"in toto corde, in tota anima, in totis viribus"*. The words
  are Aquinas's report: Augustine's *Quaest. Ev.* I.12 has neither the
  spreading nor Ps 118:32, and has *mente* where Aquinas has *viribus*. *Super I Thess.* c. 1 l. 1: faith is not enough
  *"nisi habeat operationem et laborem"*; the joy of the Spirit is the love of
  God.
- **John Chrysostom** (father), *Hom. in 1 Thess.* 1: *"Merely to love is no
  labor at all. But to love genuinely is great labor."* **Theodoret**
  (ecclesiastical writer): love has surpassing labour, bearing a brother's
  envy, anger, pride and ingratitude. **Theophylact** (ecclesiastical writer)
  repeats Chrysostom.

The carrying authors agree on the controlling claim: the leaven works
inwardly and on the whole person (heart, soul and mind for Augustine; heart,
soul and strength, loved as the Church commands, for Bede; spirit, soul and
body, or the soul's three powers, for Jerome; flesh, soul and spirit, *"totus
ille interior homo"*, for Ambrose), and it works until the whole is changed,
which Bede states as love changing the whole mind. They differ on what the
leaven is: love for Augustine and Bede; teaching for Jerome (the knowledge of
the Scriptures) and for Ambrose (the Church's spiritual doctrine, his stated
preference; that it is Christ is a view he reports of many, 187). Jerome and
Ambrose therefore agree with each other, and differ from Augustine and Bede,
on the leaven, while all four agree that the measures are the whole person.
The reading holds love and teaching together as effect and cause (the teaching
received kindles love); that joining is *(editorial)*.

### What each element contributes

- **Gospel** (Mt 13:33): the leaven parable, read for *abscóndit* (hidden, not
  displayed) and *donec … totum* (until the whole). Chrysostom's remark that
  *hid* means mixed, *"not simply close, but so as to be actually mixed"*, may
  be used for the word *abscóndit* though he applies it to mission.
- **Epistle** (1 Thess 1:3): *mémores óperis fidei vestræ, et labóris, et
  caritátis, et sustinéntiæ spei*. The Greek Fathers read *the labour of love*
  (Chrysostom, Theodoret, Theophylact); Aquinas reads the Missal's Latin as
  faith that works and labours, then charity. Both make love active and
  costly. The Thessalonians' turning *a simulácris, servíre Deo vivo et vero*
  (1:9) is the whole life re-ordered; Aquinas on *vivo* and *vero*. Joining
  this to the leaven is *(editorial)*.
- **Collect**: *semper rationabília meditántes … et dictis exsequámur et
  factis*: constant thought carried into word and deed, a petition for the
  whole person *(editorial)*. Its *rationabília* and Jerome's *rationabile* are
  two different texts; a study may set them side by side as the editor's
  observation, never as Jerome on the Collect. Likewise Ambrose's *"diuturna
  meditatione"* (VII.191) beside *semper … meditántes*: the editor's
  juxtaposition, not Ambrose on the Collect.
- **Secret**: *mundet … et rénovet, gubérnet et prótegat*: cleansing and
  renewal before governance. Aquinas's note that leaven read *in malum* is the
  old leaven to be purged (1 Cor 5:7) stands beside it *(editorial)*.
- **Postcommunion**: *ut semper éadem … appetámus*: the continuing desire that
  Bede (on Lk 13:21) describes as charity growing until it changes the whole
  mind *(editorial)*.
- **Introit**: *cogitatiónes pacis, et non afflictiónis*: God's intention
  toward his people precedes their change; lightly used.
- **Gradual**: *in nómine tuo confitébimur in sǽcula*: Aquinas reads *tota
  die* as eternity, the praise that does not end; lightly used.
- **Alleluia and Offertory**: Chrysostom on Ps 129.1: prayer from the depths,
  before it receives anything, *"calms the passions, softens anger, drives out
  envy"*. This is the nearest a checked witness on a chant comes to the
  leaven's inward work; joining them is *(editorial)*.
- **Communion** (*quidquid orántes pétitis … et fiet vobis*): two carrying
  Fathers join the leavened whole person to prayer that obtains, at the leaven
  and not at Mark. Jerome: the three brought into one, *"non discrepent inter
  se; sed cum duobus et tribus convenerint, impetrent a Patre quodcumque
  postulaverint"* (Mt 18:19; PL 26 col. 91). Ambrose: from the incorrupt
  communion of body, soul and spirit at the resurrection, *"et tunc omnia quae
  petimus, impetremus"*, which many take Mt 18:19 to signify; and readings in
  which soul and spirit, agreeing in the body, bring all that is prayed for to
  effect (VII.192-193). Bede on Mk 11:25 adds the condition for those not yet
  perfect: they obtain *"si tamen in se peccantibus aliis primo ipsi
  dimittunt"*. Applying Jerome's and Ambrose's words to this antiphon is
  *(editorial)*: neither comments on Mk 11:24, and Mt 18:19 is a different
  saying. So is joining Bede's forgiveness to Theodoret's labour of love (scope
  section 7.7).

### The difficulty and the disagreement

*Ferméntum* (scope section 7.2). Leaven is elsewhere corruption (Mt 16:6; 1 Cor
5:6-8); Aquinas answers that one thing may be read in either sense, by its
corruption or by its heat and spreading. Ambrose answers from the same texts:
the Lord's warning against the Pharisees' leaven (Mt 16:6) and Paul's leaven of
malice (1 Cor 5:8) show that leaven is doctrine, *"sed aliud est fermentum
zizaniae, aliud fermentum tritici"* (VII.190). What the leaven is divides the
witnesses: love (Augustine; Bede, in Augustine's words), teaching (Jerome; Ambrose's stated
preference; Chrysologus's *caelestis doctrinae sacramentum*), Christ (Hilary;
the many Ambrose reports at 187), the disciples (Chrysostom). The three measures divide them too: heart, soul and mind
(Augustine); heart, soul and strength (Bede); spirit, soul and body, or
reason, anger and desire (Jerome); flesh, soul and spirit (Ambrose,
VII.190-191, his stated preference, with good authors); the yields of the
sower (Augustine; Bede, who names them married, continent and virgins); Noah, Daniel and Job (Augustine); Law, Prophets
and Gospel (Hilary, Chrysologus; Ambrose finds it congruent at 188 without
making it his preference, and reports it applied to the world at 189); the
Trinity (reported by Jerome and Hilary, used by Chrysologus). On the Trinity
reading they truly disagree: Jerome calls it
*pius* but refuses it doctrinal weight (*"numquam parabolae et dubia
aenigmatum intelligentia potest ad auctoritatem dogmatum proficere"*), Hilary
doubts that reason permits it, Chrysologus uses it. The reading takes the
measures of the whole person, the option Augustine, Bede, Jerome and Ambrose share,
and names the others.

The strongest alternative: Chrysostom and Aquinas's report of him take the
leaven as the apostles hidden among the nations, which makes the parable about
mission and joins it to reading 1. That reading is real and is left to reading
1; this reading does not deny it.

### Four senses

- **Literal.** A woman works a little leaven into a large batch of meal, three
  *sata*, about one and a half *modii* each in Jerome's note (*"Satum autem
  genus est mensurae, juxta morem provinciae Palaestinae, unum et dimidium
  modium capiens"*, PL 26 col. 92), and leaves it until the whole has risen. Paul thanks God for a
  church's faith at work, its labour and charity, its enduring hope (1 Thess
  1:3).
- **Allegorical.** The woman is the Church or wisdom (Jerome; Augustine;
  Ambrose; Bede). For Bede she is the daughter of Abraham whom the Lord has
  just raised up, and we are her meal, ground between fear and hope into one
  bread and one body (1 Cor 10:17). In Ambrose's own reading the Church mixes spiritual doctrine into
  the reborn man until *"totus ille interior homo cordis absconditus"* is
  leavened and rises *"in gratiam panis coelestis"*, for the leaven is Christ's
  doctrine and Christ is bread (VII.191, 1 Cor 10:17); on the reading he
  reports of many, the Church hides the Lord Jesus in the inner rooms of the
  mind (187). Chrysologus makes Christ the leaven of the human mass: the
  leaven of death received from Eve is replaced by the leaven of resurrection
  through Mary (*Sermo* 99).
- **Moral.** Charity hidden in the mind must grow until it changes the whole
  mind, so that the soul loves, does and remembers nothing but its maker's love
  (Bede on Lk 13:21); reason, anger and desire become prudence, hatred of vice and
  desire of virtue (Jerome); flesh, soul and spirit are brought to agree, the
  flesh no longer lusting against the spirit, by the Church's slow work, *"non
  tumultuarium, nec fortuitum"* (Ambrose VII.191-192); love is labour,
  bearing another's faults (Chrysostom; Theodoret); faith that does not work and labour is not enough
  (Aquinas).
- **Anagogical.** *Donec fermentátum est totum*: what is begun here is
  perfected where God is all in all, who warms all with the one fire of his
  love (Bede on Lk 13:21, 1 Cor 15:28); Chrysologus: *"fermentatur
  totum. Quando? Adveniente Christo"* (1 Cor 13:9-10); Ambrose: if the three
  measures remain one leaven in this life, *"erit in futurum diligentibus
  Christum incorrupta communio"*, and we shall be one, like him (VII.194,
  1 Jn 3:2).

### Limits

- Augustine's *Quaest. Ev.* I.12 is a short gloss; it gives the
  identifications, not an argument; the argument for the whole person is
  Bede's (on Lk 13:21), Jerome's and Ambrose's (VII.190-194).
- Bede's leaven as love *"quae fervefacit, et excitat"* is Augustine's
  phrase, his three yields Augustine's alternative, his *satum* Jerome's note
  and his *"cujus farina nos sumus"* Ambrose's phrase (VII.187); a study cites
  those as theirs. What is credited to Bede as his own here (the Church's
  commandment, the millstones, and the *donec* argument to the whole mind) is
  so only against the Fathers read for this study; no critical apparatus of his
  sources was consulted (scope section 12). Rabanus repeats Bede and is not a
  second witness.
- Ambrose, Bede and Chrysologus expound Luke 13:20-21; cite them for Luke
  unless they quote Matthew (Ambrose quotes Mt 13:33 at 191). Ambrose's 187 (the leaven as
  Christ) and 188 (Law, Prophets and Gospel) are readings he reports or finds
  congruent; only 190-191 is his stated preference, and a study must not
  invert that. His 1 Thess 5:23 (191) is not this Mass's Epistle.
- No witness comments on the Collect, Secret or Postcommunion; every link to
  them is the editor's, and the Collect's *rationabília* must not be credited
  to Jerome.
- The Greek *labour of love* is not the Missal's Latin construction; a study
  that quotes the Latin must give Aquinas's reading or none.

## 3. `already-and-awaited` — Delivered already, waiting still

### Controlling claim

The Mass speaks of deliverance in the past tense (*avertísti*, *Liberásti*,
*erípuit*) while it waits (*exspectáre Fílium eius de cælis*; *ab ira
ventúra*; *redúcam*; *accipiétis*), and its Gospel speaks of a kingdom still
hidden. The witnesses explain why: God's promise is as certain as if it were
done; its cause, sin, is already taken away; and the Church, like Jeremiah's
exiles, is commanded to pray for what is promised and to wait for the Son. The
hiddenness of the kingdom in the parables is the hiddenness of a thing already
present and not yet shown.

### Carrying authors and their reasoning

- **Augustine** (father), *En. in Ps.* 43.6-7 (NPNF I.8): *Thou hast saved us*
  is *"spoken of the future under the figure of the past … as certain as if it
  were past"*, as the Passion was foretold in past tenses; and the psalmist
  *"intermingles words expressive of a future time"*. *En. in Ps.* 84.1-4: the
  captivity turned away is from sin, *"setting us free from bad works"*, and
  the psalmist prays *"because they are not yet done"*. *En. in Ps.* 77.1-4:
  the mystery of the kingdom *"was veiled in the Old"*; *"O that He that hath
  said, 'I will open in parables My mouth,' would also vouchsafe to open our
  understanding"*.
- **Jerome** (father), *In Hier.* V on 29:10-14 (PL 24 col. 893, page image):
  *"Futurorum igitur scientia soli Deo competit"*; God could have fulfilled his
  promise without the captives' prayer, *"sed hortatur eos ad preces, ut quod
  promissum est, mereantur accipere"*, with Mt 7:7 *Petite, et accipietis*;
  anagogically, *"tamdiu in saeculi hujus confusione versamur, quamdiu
  requiem septenarii numeri mereamur accipere, et accepta poenitentia, Deus
  impleat quod promisit, et reducamur ad locum nostrum Ecclesiam"*: we remain
  in the confusion of this world until we merit the rest of the seventh number;
  then, when repentance has been received, God fulfils his promise and we are
  brought back to our place, the Church. The same anagogy continues at col. 894
  (page image): *"Idcirco enim Dominus percutere visus est, ut sanaret:
  dabitque nobis finem laboris nostri atque patientiae: et invocabimus eum, et
  revertemur in Ecclesiam, et orabimus, et exaudiemur"*; we shall seek and find
  him when we seek with the whole heart, *"et tunc apparebit nobis"*. God
  seemed to strike in order to heal; he will give the end of our labour and
  patience (Jerome's anagogy of *ut dem vobis finem et patientiam*, the clause
  the antiphon omits); and we shall call on him, return to the Church, pray and
  be heard, which is his anagogy of the antiphon's own *invocábitis me, et ego
  exáudiam vos*. He then reports a reading of others (*"Quidam"*): the seventy
  years as the span of a life (Ps 89:10), after which we return to the Lord,
  *"nunc enim in umbra nos omnia et in imagine possidere"*. *In Mt.* II on 13:35
  (PL 26 cols. 92-93): the psalm is spoken *"ex persona Domini"* and narrates
  the signs of the exodus, *"Ex quo intelligimus universa illa quae scripta
  sunt, parabolice sentienda: nec manifestam tantum sonare litteram, sed et
  abscondita sacramenta"* (col. 93).

Supporting:
- **John Chrysostom** (father), *Hom. in 1 Thess.* 2 on 1:10: *"Him that was
  crucified, Him that was buried; to wait for Him from heaven"*; *"The terrible
  things are in hand, but the good things are in the future … See how much
  hope is required."* *Expositio in Ps.* 43 on v. 8: *"Why speak of the old
  things?"* we have present pledges of providence. *Hom. in Mt.* 47.1:
  parables were spoken *"not that they might be ignorant, but that He might
  lead them to inquiry"*.
- **Thomas Aquinas** (doctor), *Super I Thess.* c. 1 l. 1: we await the
  resurrection and deliverance from the punishment to come; from its cause,
  sin, we are delivered by Christ, *"Unde dicit qui eripuit nos"*. *Super Ps.*
  43 n. 3: *Salvasti* *"vel in praesenti … vel in futuro"*. *Super Mt.* c. 13
  on v. 35: the Lord spoke parabolically in the prophets and in himself; the
  hidden things are those *"quae fuerunt occulta a mundi constitutione"* (Eph
  3:5).
- **Peter Chrysologus** (father), *Sermo* 99: *donec fermentatum est totum*,
  *"Quando? Adveniente Christo"*.
- **Rupert** and **Durandus** (ecclesiastical writers), on the chants as one
  sequence: the Introit's promise, the Gradual's thanks for deliverance from
  the captivity of souls, the Offertory's plea for pardon, the Communion as the
  answer (Rupert XII.23; Durandus VI.140); **Sicard** (ecclesiastical writer,
  *Mitrale* VIII.23) reads the same four chants as the remnant of Israel
  answering the promise and receiving assurance of salvation, without Joseph.
  Durandus's Joseph frame is Rupert's before him; the words quoted from
  Durandus are in neither Rupert nor Sicard as held (scope section 6). Their
  Masses had other lessons, so they support only the chants' sequence and
  never a link to this Epistle or Gospel.

The carrying authors agree on the controlling claim: deliverance is spoken of
as done because it is certain and its cause is removed, and the people are
commanded to pray and wait for its fulfilment (Augustine on Pss 43 and 84;
Jerome on Jer 29). They differ in emphasis (scope section 7.5): Augustine on
certainty, Chrysostom on present pledges, Aquinas on cause and penalty.

### What each element contributes

- **Introit** (Jer 29:11-14 cento; Ps 84:2): the antiphon is a promise in
  futures (*invocábitis … exáudiam … redúcam*); its verse a past tense
  (*avertísti*). Jerome: God's thoughts are known to him alone; prayer is
  commanded though God could fulfil without it; anagogically, God seemed to
  strike in order to heal, and *"invocabimus eum … et orabimus, et
  exaudiemur"* (col. 894), his reading of the antiphon's own *invocábitis me,
  et ego exáudiam vos*. Augustine: the past tense
  prophesies, and the psalmist prays because it is not yet done.
- **Gradual** (Ps 43:8-9): *Liberásti* and *laudábimur*: Augustine's *future
  under the figure of the past*; Aquinas's *in praesenti … in futuro*;
  Chrysostom's present pledges.
- **Alleluia and Offertory** (Ps 129:1-2): Hilary: God hears by nature, *"sed
  fidei nostrae est ut precemur audiri"*; the waiting Church cries. Rupert and
  Durandus read the Offertory as the plea for pardon of Joseph's brothers
  (supporting only).
- **Epistle** (1 Thess 1:9-10): *exspectáre Fílium eius de cælis (quem
  suscitávit ex mórtuis) Iesum, qui erípuit nos ab ira ventúra*: Chrysostom
  (resurrection, ascension, second coming, judgment seen together; hope
  required) and Aquinas (cause removed, penalty escaped).
- **Gospel** (Mt 13:31-35): the seed buried and the leaven hidden; *eructábo
  abscóndita a constitutióne mundi* (13:35); Jerome (the psalm in the Lord's
  person), Aquinas (things hidden from the world's foundation, now revealed),
  Augustine on Ps 77 (understanding asked of him who speaks in parables),
  Chrysostom (parables lead to inquiry); Jerome on 13:35 (col. 93): all that is
  written is to be understood *parabolice*, not only the letter but *abscondita
  sacramenta*. The parables' *donec* (until) is
  Chrysologus's *adveniente Christo*. Reading the parables' hiddenness
  through the Epistle's waiting is *(editorial)*.
- **Communion** (Mk 11:24): *crédite quia accipiétis*: a present faith in a
  future gift; Jerome's *Petite, et accipietis* at Jer 29:12-13 is the same
  logic, joined to the Communion *(editorial)*; Hilary on Ps 129: God hears
  by nature, *"sed fidei nostrae est ut precemur audiri"*, so the antiphon's
  *crédite* names faith's part *(editorial as to the Communion)*; Bede adds the
  condition of forgiving; on Matthew's parallel (Mt 21:21-22) Chrysostom reads
  the promise as given so that the disciples would cleave to prayer and faith
  and not fear the plots against them (scope section 7.7).
- **Collect** (*semper rationabília meditántes*): constancy, *semper*, in the
  time of waiting *(editorial)*.
- **Secret** (*gubérnet et prótegat*): governance and protection asked for the
  interval *(editorial)*.
- **Postcommunion** (*ut semper éadem … appetámus*): having been fed, to go on
  desiring; the Mass ends in desire, not possession *(editorial)*.

### The difficulty

*Erípuit nos ab ira ventúra* and *Liberásti* (scope section 7.5): how can a
deliverance be past when the wrath and the full return are future? Augustine:
the certainty of God's promise makes the future speakable as past; Aquinas:
the cause is already removed; Chrysostom: present pledges ground hope for what
is in the future. If accepted, the Mass's past tenses are neither naive
(as if the struggle were over) nor merely hopeful (as if nothing were given):
something is given now and something awaited. A study must not attribute to the
Missal the Greek present participle of 1:10, and must not say that Augustine
comments on this Epistle.

### Four senses

- **Literal.** Jeremiah writes to the exiles in Babylon that God's thoughts
  toward them are of peace and that he will bring them back, and tells them to
  pray (Jer 29:4-14); Paul recalls that the Thessalonians turned from idols to
  wait for God's Son from heaven, who delivers from the wrath to come (1 Thess
  1:9-10); Jesus speaks to the crowds in parables, as the psalm says, uttering
  things hidden from the world's foundation (Mt 13:34-35).
- **Allegorical.** The captivity is sin, and Christ has turned it away
  (Augustine on Ps 84); the psalm of the exodus is the Lord's own parable,
  and all that is written is to be read *parabolice* (Jerome, PL 26 col. 93);
  the Church is the place to which the exiles are brought back
  (Jerome on Jer 29).
- **Moral.** Pray for what is promised, since God commands prayer though he
  could give without it (Jerome); believe that you receive (Communion; Hilary:
  *fidei nostrae est ut precemur audiri*); forgive, that your prayer be heard
  (Bede).
- **Anagogical.** The Son awaited from heaven; the leaven's *until the whole*
  fulfilled at his coming (Chrysologus); the praise of *tota die*, eternity
  (Aquinas on Ps 43); after the confusion of this world, repentance received,
  the return to our place, the Church; God struck to heal, and will give the
  end of our labour and patience; we shall call, return, pray and be heard,
  and then he will appear to us (Jerome, cols. 893-894).

### Limits

- Augustine's loci are on the psalms, not on the Epistle or Gospel; Jerome's
  on Jeremiah and Matthew; no witness joins the tenses across the elements.
  The synthesis is the editor's.
- Augustine on the Psalms was read only in the abridged NPNF I.8 English; its
  words are the condensed English, not his continuous Latin, and must be
  quoted as such (scope sections 5, 12).
- Jerome's anagogy of Jer 29 has an order: the confusion of this world lasts
  until the rest is merited; then, repentance received, the promise is
  fulfilled. A study must not place the fulfilment within the confusion.
  *"Finem laboris nostri atque patientiae"* (col. 894) is his anagogy of the
  clause the antiphon omits and must be labelled so; *"invocabimus … et
  exaudiemur"* reads the antiphon's own words. The seventy years as a
  lifetime, *"nunc enim in umbra nos omnia et in imagine possidere"*, is a
  reading he reports of others (*Quidam*), not his own.
- Jerome's *parabolice sentienda* is at PL 26 col. 93, not col. 92.
- Rupert, Durandus and Sicard support only the chants' sequence; their reading of the
  chants as Israel's final conversion is reception of the chants and must not
  be extended to this Gospel.
- Chrysostom's NPNF English *which delivereth us* renders his Greek; the
  Missal's Latin is *erípuit*.

## Comparison of the three readings

| | `crushed-seed` | `hidden-leaven` | `already-and-awaited` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Key words | *mínimum … fit arbor* | *abscóndit … donec fermentátum est totum* | *erípuit … ab ira ventúra*; *abscóndita* |
| Question | How does the least become great? | What is leavened, and how far? | Why speak of deliverance as done while waiting? |
| Answer | Through crushing: Christ's Passion, faith under pressure | By charity, or the teaching that kindles it, hidden in the whole person until all is changed | God's promise is certain, its cause removed; pray and wait |
| Carrying | Hilary, Ambrose (Chrysostom) | Augustine (leaven: love; measures: heart, soul, mind), Bede (leaven: love, in Augustine's words; the Church commands love of heart, soul and strength; *donec*: love grows until it changes the whole mind), Jerome (knowledge of Scripture; spirit, soul, body), Ambrose (his stated preference, VII.190-191: the Church's spiritual doctrine; flesh, soul, spirit) | Augustine, Jerome (Chrysostom, Aquinas) |
| What the Epistle shows | Word received in tribulation with joy, spread everywhere (1:6-8) | Faith that works, labour, charity (1:3); the turning from idols (1:9) | Waiting for the Son, deliverance from wrath to come (1:10) |
| Chants used | Gradual (martyrs; glory when forsaken), Alleluia/Offertory (cry of the afflicted), Communion (mountain-moving faith) | Offertory (prayer that changes before it receives), Communion (the whole person at one obtains what it asks: Jerome, Ambrose 192-193; forgive: Bede) | Introit (promise, prayer), Gradual (past for future), Communion (believe you receive) |
| Weight among the senses | allegorical (Christ the seed) and moral (faith pressed) | moral | anagogical |

What they share: the kingdom is small and hidden now, and God's power, not the
Church's, makes it grow; all three use the Epistle as the place where the
Gospel's parable is seen in a real church. Where they part: the first places
the hiddenness in suffering and looks outward to the world; the second places
it in the heart and looks inward to the whole person; the third places it in
time and looks forward to the Son's coming. A reader can hold all three; the
studies should not merge them into one theme of "growth".

The real disagreements a study must keep: what the mustard seed is (Christ,
faith, the preaching, the apostles; scope 8.1); what the leaven and the three
measures are, including teaching (Jerome; Ambrose's own preference) against
love (Augustine; Bede), Law, Prophets and Gospel (Hilary, Chrysologus; congruent for
Ambrose, not his preference) against the whole person, and the Trinitarian
reading Jerome limits, Hilary rejects and Chrysologus uses (scope 8.2); who hid the leaven (scope 8.3); the Greek and
Latin construction of 1 Thess 1:3 (scope 8.4). The Communion's promise is
conditioned differently by Bede, Hilary, and Jerome with Ambrose (scope 7.7).
