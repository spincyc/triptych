# Research scope: Fourteenth Sunday after Pentecost

## Identity and research boundary

This brief governs the Claude guide to *Dominica Decima Quarta post
Pentecosten* in the 1962 Vatican typical edition of the *Missale Romanum*,
printed pp. 394--396, marginal nos. 1572--1581. It covers the Introit,
Collect, Epistle, Gradual, Alleluia, Gospel, Offertory, Secret, Communion,
and Postcommunion in their received ritual order. The 1962 facsimile controls
the Latin, boundaries, references, and sequence; the registered
Douay--Rheims/Challoner artifacts control English Scripture. No verified
public-domain English witness is available for the three orations, so the
guide may print their controlled Latin and describe their petitions, but may
not supply or imply an English prayer text for recitation (COV-001--COV-002).

The seven research lanes supplied textual context, patristic and later
reception, liturgical history, theological synthesis, citation and rights
controls, cultural afterlives, and a bounded precedent search. No research was
added at synthesis. The strongest evidence is the checked 1962 formulary and
its registered Scriptural controls, direct work-and-locus reception witnesses,
and page-inspected sacramentary, gradual, and missal witnesses. Historical OCR,
secondary reports of uninspected apparatus, and unsearched original-language
loci remain leads or explicit limits.

## Evidence-state inventory

### Verified textual observations and Scriptural context

- **Introit: Psalm 83:10--11, 2--3 (Vulgate numbering).** The psalm moves from
  longing for the Lord's courts, through blessed dwelling and ascent to Sion,
  to prayer for God's protector and anointed and confidence in divine good.
  The Introit places vv. 10--11 before the chant verse from vv. 2--3. Psalm
  131:7--17 is a material Psalter parallel for tabernacle, Sion, rest, the
  anointed, and bread. In Psalm 83's literal register *christus* means the
  anointed; direct identification with Jesus is a received typological move,
  not the psalm's unaided historical assertion (SCR-001).
- **Epistle: Galatians 5:16--24.** The pericope belongs to Paul's insistence
  that freedom from circumcision and law must become service through charity,
  not an occasion for the flesh (5:1--15). It opposes works of the flesh to the
  singular fruit of the Spirit, ends with the crucifixion of the flesh by
  those who belong to Christ, and continues immediately into walking by the
  Spirit, burden-bearing, and sowing (5:25--6:10). Romans 8:1--17 is the
  nearest checked Pauline parallel. Galatians' list itself prevents *flesh*
  from being reduced to bodily material: it includes idolatry, enmities,
  contentions, envy, and sects (SCR-002).
- **Gradual: Psalm 117:8--9 (Vulgate numbering; common modern Psalm 118).** The
  two trust verses occupy the centre of a thanksgiving psalm moving from a
  communal mercy refrain through distress and deliverance to the gates of
  justice, the rejected cornerstone, the Lord's day, the blessed entrant, and
  an altarward festal procession. Matthew 21:42 and Acts 4:10--12 explicitly
  apply the psalm's later cornerstone verses to Jesus Christ; that canonical
  relation belongs to the whole psalm but is not stated by the Gradual's two
  selected verses alone (SCR-003).
- **Alleluia: Psalm 94:1 (Vulgate numbering; common modern Psalm 95).** The
  opening summons to praise belongs to a psalm that names God Creator and
  shepherd before warning against present-day hardness and exclusion from
  divine rest. Hebrews 3:7--4:11 receives the latter movement as the Holy
  Ghost's continuing address and extends its rest into a promise requiring
  faith and perseverance; the warning remains complete-psalm and canonical
  context rather than wording sung by the Alleluia (SCR-004).
- **Gospel: Matthew 6:24--33.** The two-master saying grounds the ensuing
  teaching against solicitude. Matthew 6 has already opposed service before
  the Father to visible reward, and heavenly treasure and the single eye to
  earthly treasure; the Father's knowledge of need resolves the sequence in
  priority for his kingdom and justice. The appointed excerpt stops before
  Matthew's counsel concerning tomorrow at 6:34. Luke 12:22--31 supplies the
  closest Synoptic parallel and places the teaching between the rich fool and
  commands concerning almsgiving, treasure, and watchfulness; this comparison
  establishes a canonical relation, not a direction of literary dependence
  (SCR-005--SCR-006).
- **Offertory: Psalm 33:8--9 (Vulgate numbering; common modern Psalm 34).** A
  song of personal deliverance becomes instruction to fear, taste, seek,
  practice good, and trust the Lord's rescue. Its superscription points to
  David's escape in 1 Kings 21:10--15, where the changed countenance occurs
  before Achis, king of Geth, although the Douay superscription names
  Achimelech. The checked texts leave that name tension unresolved. First
  Peter 2:3 echoes tasting the Lord's sweetness and proceeds to Christ the
  living stone, believers as a spiritual house, Sion, and the rejected
  cornerstone, creating an explicit canonical bridge to the later part of
  Psalm 117 (SCR-007).
- **Communion: adaptation of Matthew 6:33.** Compared word for word with the
  Clementine Matthew and Luke, the antiphon promotes *Primum* to the head,
  omits *ergo*, *et iustitiam eius*, and *haec*, and adds *dicit Dominus*.
  Neither Gospel supplies that exact form. The antiphon therefore reprises the
  Gospel's kingdom-seeking climax without reproducing the biblical verse; the
  evidence does not establish why the form was made (SCR-008).
- **Cross-text observations.** Psalm 117 prefers trust in the Lord over humans
  and princes; Psalm 83 blesses trust in him; Psalm 33 joins hope, fear, and
  seeking with no lack of good; and Matthew orders necessities beneath the
  kingdom. Galatians and Matthew also employ different binary oppositions---
  Spirit and flesh, God and mammon---and both speak of the kingdom, without
  equating the two pairs (SCR-009--SCR-010).

The textual controls are the Catholic Church, *Missale Romanum*, Vatican
typical edition (1962), pp. 394--396; the English College at Douay and Rheims,
*Holy Bible*, Douay--Rheims, Challoner revision, Project Gutenberg eBook 1581
electronic edition, complete loci cited above; and the Catholic Church,
*Biblia Sacra iuxta Vulgatam Clementinam*, eBible LATVUC edition, corresponding
Latin loci. These are registered in the publication's source bindings
(COV-001).

### Documented liturgical history and ritual placement

1. H. A. Wilson's edition of the Old Gelasian tradition shows *Custodi* and
   *Concede* together in one *alia missa* (book III, section XI, pp. 229--230),
   while the exact generic *Purificent semper* circulates separately at no.
   699 (p. 237) and in a Saint Andrew adaptation (pp. 206--207). This is
   component circulation, not the later Sunday triad or its date of assembly
   (LIT-001).
2. The exact Collect, Secret, and Postcommunion occur consecutively at Sunday
   XV in ninth-century Gregorian-supplement witnesses: H. A. Wilson, ed.,
   *The Gregorian Sacramentary under Charles the Great*, Henry Bradshaw
   Society 49 (London, 1915), section CXXXIII, pp. 173--174. They remain at
   Sunday XV in Miquel dels Sants Gros i Pujol, ``El Sacramentari II de
   Gel·lona,'' *Miscel·lània Litúrgica Catalana* 20 (2012), p. 155, nos.
   1121--1123 (SupG 1171--1173), and in ``El Sacramentari de Santa Maria de
   Vilabertran,'' *Miscel·lània Litúrgica Catalana* 19 (2011), p. 109, nos.
   488--490. Lippe's edition of the 1474 Milan Roman-Curia Missal places the
   triad in the complete Sunday-XIV Mass, pp. 276--277. These witnesses bound
   the ordinal movement but do not supply a universal conversion rule, cause,
   or exact date (LIT-002).
3. René-Jean Hesbert, *Antiphonale Missarum Sextuplex*, no. 186, pp. 186--187,
   shows an early Sunday-XIV nucleus of *Protector noster*, *Immittet angelus
   Domini*, and *Panis quem ego dedero*, with variable Gradual and Alleluia
   states. Monza is incomplete, Rheinau has a lacuna, Compiègne's relevant
   leaves are lost and partly reconstructed editorially, and Senlis' printed
   Gradual is internally mixed. This evidence establishes neither *Venite* nor
   *Primum* in the surviving early state (LIT-003).
4. Cologne, Erzbischöfliche Diözesan- und Dombibliothek, Cod. 1001b, fols.
   184v--185v, the Gradual written and completed by Johannes von Valkenburg in
   1299, presents all five later chants together and in order: *Protector*,
   *Bonum*, *Venite*, *Immittet*, and *Primum*. It is an upper bound for the
   assembled sequence in this Franciscan witness, not universal Roman use
   (LIT-004).
5. The movement from *Panis* and variable Alleluias to *Primum* and *Venite*
   occurred between the checked early states and the 1299 witness. Prosper
   Guéranger reports that ancient liturgists and manuscripts consulted by
   Blessed Tommasi did not name *Primum* for the Sunday, but Tommasi's
   underlying apparatus was not inspected. The Guéranger artifact's catalog
   metadata also conflicts with its visible 1909 Stanbrook Abbey/London title
   page; its checked pages are usable, but bibliographic correction remains
   due (LIT-005).
6. G. H. Lippe, ed., *Missale Romanum Mediolani, 1474*, Henry Bradshaw Society
   17, vol. 1 (London, 1899), pp. 276--277, prints the complete ten-element
   core in the later order. The checked Mame 1922 Missal, pp. 444--446, and
   Vatican 1962 Missal, pp. 394--396, preserve it. Thus the formulary was not
   assembled by the reforms immediately preceding 1962; the checked books do
   not prove that every local use shared it (LIT-006).
7. Honorius of Autun, *Gemma animae* IV.72--79, PL 172:717--720, distributes
   target and neighbouring-Sunday elements in both directions across Sundays
   XIV and XV, warning against a simple one-number shift. At IV.74 he
   interprets *Immittet* through Tobias' angelic deliverance, Saint Michael,
   September Ember ordinations, Isaiah's seven gifts, and Tabernacles. This is
   a twelfth-century interpretation of contemporary convergences, not evidence
   of original selection or calendrical invariance (LIT-007--LIT-008).
8. The 1962 Missal reuses *Immittet* as the Offertory on Thursday after Lent I
   (p. 71, no. 477) and *Bonum* as the Gradual on Friday after Lent IV (p. 114,
   no. 735). Schuster also notes both, but recurrence alone determines no
   transfer direction (LIT-009).
9. The Sacred Congregation of Rites, *Variationes in Breviario et Missali
   Romano*, AAS 52 (1960), p. 709, part IV, no. 18, abolished the *Orationes
   pro diversitate Temporum*. A documented 1861 lay missal had directed
   seasonal additional Collects, Secrets, and Postcommunions under stated
   exceptions. The 1962 celebration therefore lost that older accompaniment
   while the core Sunday formulary remained (LIT-010).
10. The 1962 *Ordo Missae* places Introit through Offertory in the entrance and
    proclamation span, the Secret after the offering and *Orate fratres*, and
    Communion and Postcommunion in the communion span. Guéranger calls this
    the Sunday of the two masters; Schuster, under *Quarta post Sancti
    Laurentii*, reads the received movement through harvest, spiritual
    conflict, providence, oblation, and sacramental fruit. These are attributed
    later readings, not medieval compiler intent (LIT-011--LIT-012).

No checked witness identifies the first compiler of the complete formulary,
the exact cause or date of the prayer triad's movement, or the exact stages by
which *Panis* and the variable Alleluias yielded *Primum* and *Venite*.
Wilson, the Old Gelasian edition, Gellone II, Vilabertran, Hesbert's six
antiphoners, Honorius, the 1299 Valkenburg Gradual, the 1474 Roman-Curia
Missal, the checked 1861/1922/1962 missals, Guéranger, and Schuster bound this
negative. Deshusses' modern critical Gregorian edition, Tommasi's apparatus,
and a broader ninth- through thirteenth-century sacramentary and gradual
stemma remain uninspected (LIT-013, COV-009--COV-010).

## Strongest cross-proper argument

The synthesis commentary should be organized by the following five functional
units, not by a shortened procession through the ten propers.

### 1. Allegiance orders desire toward the kingdom

The Introit's longing for the courts, the Collect's movement away from harms
and toward saving goods, the Epistle's flesh--Spirit conflict, the Gospel's
God--mammon antithesis, and the Communion's promoted *Primum* together present
conversion as the reordering of desire under one final allegiance. The bodily
life and material necessities remain good: Theodoret rejects a condemnation of
the body, and Aquinas subordinates necessities to the final good rather than
denying them. The kingdom is both the present priority of Matthew and the
inheritance Galatians warns can be forfeited; the Postcommunion's everlasting
salvation supplies the eschatological horizon. This joins SCR-001--SCR-002,
SCR-005, SCR-008, SCR-010; PAT-004, PAT-007; and THE-001--THE-002, THE-007.

### 2. Providence summons responsible agency

The Gradual's trust, the Gospel's release from anxious mastery, the Collect's
petition for continual help, the Epistle's imperative to walk and crucify
vice-governed desire, and the Postcommunion's request that the sacraments
purify and fortify refuse both quietism and self-sufficient moralism.
Chrysostom preserves ordinary work within Christ's prohibition of solicitude;
Benedict makes Matthew 6:33 govern responsible care of souls amid scarcity;
Theodoret joins indwelling grace to the soul's response. Divine providence
orders action instead of cancelling it, and promises no immunity from temporal
deprivation (PAT-006--PAT-007; THE-003).

### 3. Created mediation serves without becoming a rival master

God is named protector in the Introit; the Gradual reserves ultimate trust to
him over humans and princes; the Offertory sings the Angel sent to guard those
who fear him; and the Gospel forbids divided mastery. Bellarmine distinguishes
creaturely assistance from confidence terminating in the creature. Augustine
instead identifies the Angel christologically, while Cassiodorus, Theodoret,
Aquinas, and Bellarmine preserve several created, ministerial, and ecclesial
readings. The unit must carry that genuine reception difference and may not
turn the Gradual into a rejection of all authority, expertise, human aid, or
angelic ministry (PAT-002, PAT-008; THE-004).

### 4. The oblation and sacraments carry the Church toward saving effect

The Collect asks perpetual propitiation and saving direction for the Church;
the Offertory's tasting receives explicit Eucharistic development in Augustine,
Cassiodorus, Cyril, and Ambrose; the Secret sets the saving Host in relation to
the purification of sins and the propitiation of divine power; the Communion
revoices the kingdom's priority after sacramental reception; and the
Postcommunion asks continuing purification and strengthening unto everlasting
salvation. Estius receives the Collect as the Church's prayer for persevering
grace. This is an arc of the received ritual order, not evidence that the
three prayers or all ten elements were composed together (PAT-009, PAT-011;
THE-005).

### 5. Grace transfigures appetite into worshipful delight

Longing for the courts, the Spirit's joy and peace, jubilation to the Savior,
freedom from solicitude, and tasting the Lord's sweetness move from restless
appetite toward worshipful delight and final salvation. Augustine and
Cassiodorus describe jubilation as joy too full for articulate speech;
Bellarmine moves from inward gladness to vocal praise and experiential
sweetness. These affects---*concupiscit*, *desideria*, *gaudium*, *iubilare*,
*sollicitudo*, and *gustare*---are related without becoming interchangeable
technical terms. Christian trust does not suppress grief, prudence, or
concrete care (PAT-003, PAT-009; THE-006).

### Frederick Douglass: rival masters become an ecclesial abolitionist test

- **Appointed text and locus:** Matthew 6:24, Gospel, ``You cannot serve God
  and mammon,'' in the registered Douay--Rheims/Challoner text.
