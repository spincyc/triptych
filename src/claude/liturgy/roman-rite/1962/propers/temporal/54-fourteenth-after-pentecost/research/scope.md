## 0. What this brief settles

### 0.1 The page-2 evidencing decision, which is the crux

The previous production of this target failed at exactly one point: its brief
carried no page-2 evidence, and the authoring stage, finding the gap,
retrieved evidence itself from a restricted source. That single decision cost
a full seven-lane research round. This brief therefore states the page-2
evidence position in full, and states it as fact rather than as
encouragement.

**The decision.** Page 2 is to be evidenced from what this repository already
holds or may already cite at an exact locus, in the two-witness form the
published collection uses — a named traditional orientation and a named
modern critical horizon, each at a locus, with the disagreement printed and
neither preferred [PRE-001, PRE-003]. Where one of the two witnesses is not
available to this brief, **the row prints a bounded negative naming what was
checked and what the limit is. The author does not go and get it.** Nothing
in this brief authorises retrieval, and the profile's page-2 rule is
satisfied by an honest bound as surely as by a witness.

**Two premises the last round got wrong, both now corrected.**

*(a) The Catholic Encyclopedia volumes are not unheld.* Both volumes on which
this dossier actually depends are registered as complete, hashed, page-counted
facsimile PDFs with `rights_status = "public-domain"`: volume 4 (New York
1908, sha256 `d1b059b1…8037f`, 896 pp.) carrying Corbett's "David, King", and
volume 8 (New York 1910, sha256 `ac62a7ca…a86a2`, 872 pp.) carrying Maas's
"Jesus Christ" [COV-002]. `storage = "remote"` on those records is a
*retention* decision about a 112 MB and a 136 MB file; it is not a rights
limitation. Both are quotable, and both passages are bound in the leaf at
states `[cataloged, acquired, inspected, verified]`.

*(b) The restricted Catholic Encyclopedia route is the New Advent per-article
HTML, not the volume.* New Advent responses are registered
`storage = "restricted"`, `rights_status = "restricted"`, because the site's
transcription, markup and presentation carry their own copyright notice over
the public-domain underlying article [COV-003]. The rule for authoring is
therefore exact and is easy to get backwards: **a Catholic Encyclopedia claim
read from a New Advent page may be summarised but not reproduced; the same
article read from the volume facsimile may be quoted.** They are the same
words under opposite rights, which is precisely the confusion that makes this
class of defect recur.

**What follows for the two Catholic Encyclopedia articles this formulary
would most want.** The repository's accepted locus for a Catholic Encyclopedia
citation is article title, URL, volume and year — not a held volume
[COV-008]. Twenty distinct proper leaves cite the encyclopedia that way, and
only four carry any binding; the identifier for "Gospel of St. Matthew"
(`cathen/10057a.htm`) is documented with retrieval dates in five committed
leaves [PRE-006], and "Epistle to the Galatians" is `cathen/06336a.htm`
[PRE-005]. So an unheld volume is not by itself a citation defect, and the
genuine defect in that class is a missing locus. **But confirming an
identifier is not confirming an article's content, and no lane in this run
retrieved either article.** The brief therefore supplies the *form* in which
those two articles could be cited and supplies **no content for them at all**.
The guide may not assert what either says. This is a bounded negative and it
is correctable at the cost of one retrieval by a stage empowered to retrieve.

**What follows for the modern critical horizon.** Every registered
USCCB/NABRE artifact in the repository is rights-restricted and must be
summarised rather than reproduced, and the only NABRE limb bearing on this
proper is the Galatians introduction registered 2026-08-21 [COV-005]. **There
is no registered NABRE artifact for Matthew, and none for NABRE Psalms 34,
84, 95 or 118 — the modern numbers of this proper's Vulgate Psalms 33, 83, 94
and 117.** For those five rows there is nothing this repository holds in any
form: neither citable nor summarisable, because there is no record to
summarise from. That is the exact shape of the earlier run's defect and it is
not closed by the Galatians registration. The Epistle row's handling of the
one registered limb — "restricted and summarised here, nowhere quoted" — is
correct and should be kept.

**What follows for the word "summarised".** Of the page-2 dossier's
non-scriptural sources, seven of the eight the repository holds are
registered public-domain and therefore quotable, yet the dossier labels every
one of them "summarised" [COV-017]. The rubric's definition ("verified here
but its bytes are not retained, so the wording is this guide's own") is
literally accurate in every case, but it silently conflates a retention
decision with a rights prohibition, and its worst effect is on the Epistle
row, where "the last two summarised" puts unregistered Jerome and
rights-restricted NABRE under one word. **The author should distinguish the
three states on the page**: quotable but summarised by choice; summarise-only
on unresolved rights (Haydock); summary compelled by rights (NABRE). Erring
toward summary is safe as a rights matter and the current tree reproduces
nothing it may not [COV-009]; the defect is legibility, and it trains the next
author to read "remote" as "restricted".

**Why this cannot be checked mechanically, and why the discipline is the
author's.** The only preflight check that touches quotation,
`unquoted-not-quoted`, is a self-consistency check between the leaf's own
References section and its own quotation environments; it never reads
`src/sources/` or any storage or rights field, and its `ATTRIBUTED` regex
matches only `\begin{namedtranslation}` and `\begin{sourcecard}`, so a
verbatim reproduction inside `\dossierprose` or ordinary prose is outside its
reach by construction [COV-001]. `tpt source-library validate` validates the
library against itself, not a publication's prose against the library's
rights fields [COV-009]. **A gate passing is not evidence that the tree is
rights-clean.** An authoring stage that reintroduced NABRE wording would pass
every mechanical check in this repository, exactly as it did before.

**The precedent form, and where it must live.** Leaf 53 evidences its page-2
rows through a dedicated `research/page-2-dossier-facts.md` recording, for
every cell, the exact article URL, the retrieval date, and short verbatim
strings fixing what each witness actually says; the target leaf has no
equivalent, and 48 and 49 have no page-2 research file at all [PRE-004]. That
form is what made leaf 53's rows checkable. **It cannot be reproduced as a
separate file by any later stage of this workflow**, because this stage is the
sole writer of the leaf's research record and no later stage may add to it.
The page-2 evidence is therefore carried *inside this brief*, at §2.8, in the
same per-cell form.

### 0.3 Adjudications this brief makes, and the corrections it carries

Seven matters needed deciding rather than recording.

**(a) The saint-adapted Postcommunion is Gelasianum Book II sect. LXVIII, not
LXIX.** `liturgical-history` corrected an inherited locus at first hand on a
page image: section LXIX is *Item in natali eiusdem*, the Mass of St Andrew's
feast on 30 November, and it begins *below* the Postcommunion in question,
which belongs to the preceding section, the Vigil Mass [LIT-003]. "LXIX" is
what the running head on printed p. 207 says, and **the running head names the
section the page reaches, not the one it opens in** — precisely the misread
that produced the "XLII" error the same lane corrected at [LIT-004]. This is
the second instance of the same defect in the same book. **Any locus in either
of Wilson's editions taken from a running head is suspect until checked
against the section heading itself**, and the hazard recurs in Wilson 1915,
where printed p. 174's running head reads "DOM. XVII. POST PENTECOSTEN" over
a continuation of section CXXXIII, *Dominica XV* [LIT-005].

**(b) The Collect-and-Secret displacement runs to eleven consecutive Masses,
and its limit is tighter than the prior statement of it.** Gelasianum Book III
sects. I–XI correspond to the 1962 Fifth through Fifteenth Sundays, and across
the whole run R/S/Gerbert and Menard stand uniformly two Sundays later and
Pamelius uniformly one, with no exception; each section's 1962 identity was
fixed from its own printed collect or Secret on the same page image, never
from position [LIT-028]. That both widens the claim (eleven, not eight) and
corrects an internal inconsistency in the prior statement, which named six
sections while claiming eight Masses. Simultaneously the limit tightened:
Wilson's footnote at sect. X covers **"The Collects and Secret"** only —
sect. X prints five prayers and neither of its two Postcommunions is the one
1962 prints [LIT-002, LIT-001] — and the 1962 Postcommunion has a materially
narrower Gelasian base, attested for R and Gerbert but **not** for S,
Pamelius or Menard, because S omits the whole appended series [LIT-003]. So
the displacement is a claim about two of the formulary's ten elements, and
prose treating the three orations as equally attested would be wrong.

**(c) `hope-formula-past-the-cut` keeps the Introit, on a criterion the
manifest's key already states.** `theological-synthesis` reports that the
`sper-` and `confid-` stems occur in the Gradual block only, twice each, and
**not at all** in the Introit, and therefore that its evidence does not reach
the Introit for this relation [THE-016, THE-026]. That count is correct and I
adopt it. But it tests the wrong criterion. `scripture-context` verified, by
reading all three psalms whole, that the formula *`beatus … qui sperat in eo`*
stands at the close of Ps. 33 (v. 23, `qui sperant in eo`) **and** at the
close of Ps. 83 (v. 13, `beatus homo qui sperat in te`), that it lies past the
Introit antiphon's cut at v. 11a *and* past its psalm verse's cut at v. 3a,
that it lies one clause past the Offertory's cut at v. 9a, and that the
Gradual alone sings it outright [SCR-016]. The relation is named *past the
cut*, not *contains*: the Introit belongs because the formula lies beyond both
of its boundaries, which is a fact about the appointed excerpt and its source
that SCR-016 established at first hand. **Membership retained as
`[introit, gradual, offertory]`; the criterion restated so that no later
reader mistakes it for a claim that the Introit uses hope vocabulary, because
it does not.** THE-026's own qualification anticipates this: it reports that
*its* evidence does not reach the member, "which is not the same as reporting
that they are wrong". The relation's own weakness travels with it: SCR-016
flags that the three cuts have other explanations and that intent was not
established.

**(d) `fruit-count-unsettled` is dropped as an interpretive proposal.** Three
lanes converge against it. At the level of the tracked texts the count is not
unsettled — it is 12 Latin against 9 Greek — so whatever is unsettled lies in
the reception [SCR-008]. The fruit list stands in the Epistle alone, and
nothing joins the Communion or the Gospel to a question about the count
[THE-020, THE-026], so the relation as the manifest declares it fails the
profile's requirement that a proposal join at least two precisely named
appointed elements. And `precedent-search` classifies the conjunction *not
located in the checked corpus* while noting that the singular-*fructus*
observation itself is demonstrably not novel [PRE-029]. **The material is
real and is retained — as documented reception in the detailed commentary
(§2.2), where Aquinas and Anthony expound the twelve-item Latin list and the
Greek witnesses cannot — but it is not an exploratory proposal and the
manifest's element membership for that relation is not supported by anything
this run gathered.**

**(e) `sunday-number-not-the-mass-own` is routed to source-grounded synthesis,
not to Interpretive Possibilities.** The precedent at leaf 53 is
documented-historical-orientation carried in commentary and scope — a
seven-row transmission table and the conclusion that "the formulary is stable
and its number is not" — and it is one Sunday earlier in the same collection
[PRE-030]. Published as an interpretive proposal here it would restate a
neighbouring guide's sourced finding, which the profile forbids as a recap;
published as source-grounded synthesis it has direct precedent. It is settled
as cross-proper claim **C4** at §7.4. It also does not join two appointed
elements by any mechanism of its own, so it could not have been retained as a
proposal in any case.

**(f) No Greek witness may be quoted on `homicidia` or on `castitas`.** The
Latin vice-list and fruit-list the missal prints are both longer than the
Greek lists the Greek Fathers expound — seventeen against fifteen and twelve
against nine — so two appointed vices (`luxuria`, `homicidia`) and three
appointed fruits (`modestia`, `continentia`, `castitas`) receive no comment
from a Greek witness commenting on his own text [PAT-051]. Aquinas closes the
gap and Chrysostom cannot: *Super Galatas* cap. 5 lect. 5 derives `veneficia`
from `venenum` and reads `homicidia` as of the heart as well as of the deed,
and lect. 6 defines all twelve fruits including the three the Greek lacks
[PAT-071, PAT-070]; Anthony of Padua defines all twelve individually but
passes over `homicidia` with no gloss at all [PAT-081, PAT-082]. **The
instruction to the author is narrow and firm: do not put a comment on
`veneficia … homicidia` or on `castitas` into a Greek Father's mouth on the
strength of a witness whose list does not contain the word.**

**(g) C3 joins the Gospel and the Communion; the guide's own apparatus is not
an element of anything.** Iteration 0 declared C3's members as "Gospel, and the
guide's own four-senses apparatus". That counted a feature of the publication
as an appointed element of the formulary and so left the claim standing on a
single ritual moment, and it contradicted this brief in three places at once:
§0.3(d) drops `fruit-count-unsettled` for failing exactly this floor, §9.6
refuses the Introit-incipit conjunction "because it joins only one appointed
element of this formulary and the profile requires at least two", and §10 row 3
asserts that all five §7 claims draw multiple ritual moments. **The floor is
upheld and C3 is repaired rather than withdrawn**, because the second element
was already in the evidence this brief holds and was simply not named. It is
the **Communion**. At the pericope's images Augustine forbids figure outright
[PAT-061]; at the one verse of that pericope the formulary lifts out, recasts
and appoints again as the Communion antiphon, he reads not an argument from a
lesser example but a doctrine of ordered ends — `primum` as an ordering of
importance and not of time [PAT-062]. **Two appointed elements carry Matthew 6
in this Mass and the same Father reads them in two different registers; that is
the claim.** The bearing on the guide's page-1 allegorical row is real and is
kept — as a **consequence** of the claim, at §7.3 and at §10 row 1, and not as
a member of it. Restated in full at §7.3; the rule it turns on is now stated at
the head of §7.

### 1.2 Text-critical facts established mechanically this run

Every appointed biblical text except the Communion is a **verbatim** quotation
of the tracked Clementine Vulgate. This was not assumed: each appointed Latin
block was normalised (accents, i/j, u/v, ae/oe, ii/i, ll/l, punctuation, case)
and compared word-for-word against the tracked Clementine chapter files, and
Introit antiphon, Introit psalm verse, Gradual, Alleluia, Offertory, Epistle
body and Gospel body all match; only the Communion differs [SCR-002]. This
matters because `guidance/bibles-for-agents.md` warns in terms that the Mass
chants must not be assumed to match any tracked psalter, and gives a
counter-example from the Sixth Sunday after Pentecost. Here the check was run
and they do match.

The normalisation deliberately absorbs real but non-verbal orthographic
differences: the missal prints `milia`, `adicere`, `adicientur`, `huiusmodi`,
`iubilemus` where the tracked Clementine prints `millia`, `adjicere`,
`adjicientur`, `hujusmodi`, `jubilemus`.

**The Communion departs from Mt. 6:33 in exactly four ways** [SCR-003],
verified against the Clementine, the Lucan parallel and the tracked
Robinson-Pierpont Greek: `ergo` is dropped and `Primum` promoted to head the
clause; `et iustitiam eius` is omitted; `haec` is dropped before `omnia`; and
`dicit Dominus` is added. `dicit Dominus` stands in none of Clementine
Mt. 6:33, Clementine Lk. 12:31, or Robinson-Pierpont Mt. 6:33. Luke, unlike
the antiphon, keeps `iustitiam eius`, so the omission is **not** explained by
conflation with the Lucan form.

**Two capitalisation differences fall exactly on the words that carry
typological weight** [SCR-020]: the missal prints `faciem Christi tui` where
the tracked Clementine prints lower-case `christi`, and `Immittet Angelus
Domini` where the Clementine prints lower-case `angelus`. **Firm caveat that
must travel with any use of this:** capitalisation in a modern electronic
Clementine is that e-text's editorial convention and capitalisation in the
1962 missal is that book's; neither is evidence of what the Latin intends,
and in the psalm's own sense `christus` is the anointed king. It would be easy
to build a typological claim on the missal's capital C as though the psalm
supplied it. It does not.

### 1.3 Psalm numbering, read from the concordance and not restated from memory

The appointed loci's modern English numbers are Ps. 84:9–10 with 84:1–2,
Ps. 118:8–9, Ps. 95:1 and Ps. 34:7–8, and the offset is produced by whether
the psalm's superscription is separately numbered [SCR-019]. This was read
directly from the tracked project-created concordance
(`…/challoner-gutenberg-1581/artifacts/psalm-numbering-ee3c7757/psalm-numbering.tsv`),
which `scripts/_psalms.py` declares the sole authority and which
`guidance/bibles-for-agents.md` forbids restating from memory.

Independent corroboration from a third series: Brenton's LXX carries exactly
the Clementine's verse numbers and exactly the same title treatment at all
four psalms — separately numbered at Pss. 33 and 83, folded into v. 1 at
Ps. 94, absent at Ps. 117 where only `Alleluia` stands — which confirms the
offset is a property of the **English** convention rather than of the Vulgate.
Brenton's superscription incidentally reads "Abimelech" where the Clementine
and Douay read "Achimelech".

**Bound the author must carry:** "modern English" is not "Masoretic". The
concordance's Hebrew column numbers the superscription, so under strict
Masoretic versification the Introit is 84:10–11 and the Offertory 34:8–9. The
parenthetical a reader needs is the English one. No Hebrew text of these
psalms is held in this repository, so the Masoretic half rests on the
concordance's declared columns and not on a Hebrew Bible.

**The same offset is a live hazard in the reception sweep**, and in a doubled
form. The New Advent/NPNF pages for Augustine's *Enarrationes* carry two
numbering systems at once: the page *title* uses the modern number, while the
inline verse tags run one verse **behind** the Clementine numbering the missal
cites, so every appointed psalm locus had to be shifted twice before the right
paragraph could be read [PAT-002]. The page at `…/1801083.htm` is not the
missal's Ps. 83 at all; the missal's Ps. 83 is served at `…/1801084.htm`. The
offset is absent from Ps. 117, which carries no counted superscription, and
absent from Ps. 94, whose title the Clementine folds into v. 1. **Every locus
in the reception matrix below was resolved by reading the quoted lemma against
the missal's Latin, never by trusting a verse tag.**

### 2.1 Introit — Ps. 83:10–11a with Ps. 83:2–3a

**Textual setting** [SCR-012]. Ps. 83 is a psalm of the sons of Korah about
desire for the sanctuary: the sparrow and turtle-dove finding a house at the
altars (v. 4), those who dwell in the house (v. 5), the ascents in the heart
through the vale of tears (vv. 6–7), `ibunt de virtute in virtutem` (v. 8).
**The two cuts have opposite effects on what the excerpt carries in**: cutting
v. 11 removes the psalm's explicit two-way choice between the house of God and
the tents of sinners; cutting v. 3 removes the rejoicing of heart and flesh
(`cor meum et caro mea exsultaverunt in Deum vivum`) — which is the only place
in the formulary's psalm sources where `caro` carries a positive sense
[THE-016]. Both dropped clauses are the psalm's, not the antiphon's, and the
antiphon asserts neither.

**Augustine reads the two halves as one movement of desire** [PAT-010]. On the
psalm verse (§6): "My soul longs and fails for the courts of the Lord. It is
not enough that it longs and fails: for what does it fail? For the courts of
the Lord." On the antiphon (§14): "one day there is better than a thousand
days. Men long for thousands of days … let them long for one day, which has
neither rising nor setting: one day, an everlasting day, to which no yesterday
yields, which no tomorrow presses." So on his reading the antiphon is the
answer to its own verse. Latin collated from a modern web transcription:
`contemnant millia dierum, desiderent unum diem, qui non habet ortum et
occasum; unum diem, diem sempiternum, cui non cedit hesternus, quem non urget
crastinus.` **Evidence state:** acquired, searched and inspected in the NPNF
English *and* corroborated in a Latin witness that agrees with it; **not**
collated against PL 37, so the Latin is corroboration and not verification,
and a guide quoting it must present the English as NPNF's.

**A material limit the author must not smooth over** [PAT-010]. Augustine's
§15 expounds the *rest* of Ps. 83:11 — "I have chosen to be cast away in the
house of the Lord" — which is exactly the clause the Introit cuts off after
`super milia`. **The Father's unit and the liturgy's unit do not coincide**,
and a guide should not imply Augustine is commenting on the antiphon as the
missal bounds it. A second trap the Latin collation caught: Augustine's
exposition speaks of `millia dierum`, thousands of *days*, and it is tempting
to conclude his psalter read a fuller text. It did not — his printed lemma is
the missal's Introit verbatim, and `millia dierum` is his **gloss** supplying
the noun the verse elides.

**On `respice in faciem Christi tui`, Augustine refuses the obvious reading**
[PAT-011]: "For when does God not look upon the face of His Christ? … Cause
Your Christ to become known to all." Latin: `Fac innotescere omnibus Christum
tuum … notus sit omnibus Christus tuus, ut possimus ire a virtutibus in
virtutem, ut possit superabundare gratia, quoniam abundavit peccatum.` This
is **the best-attested Augustinian quotation in the sweep and the safest to
publish in Latin**, because the argument is a grammatical one about `facies`
that survives translation. Note the reasoning is driven by Rom. 5:20 and by
the same psalm's v. 8, neither of which is in the appointed extract: the move
is Augustine's on the whole psalm, applied to the verse the missal took.

**Guéranger reads the same verse differently, and the two do not reconcile by
stacking** [PAT-090]. Where Augustine turns the petition outward and
missionary, Guéranger turns it inward and nuptially: "the Church, in
presenting her petitions to the Most High, begs of Him to look on the face of
His Christ, who is also hers", grounded on 1 Cor. 11:7, 11:3 and Eph. 5:23.
**Preserve both.** Guéranger is nineteenth-century, not patristic; his standing
is that of a recognised authority on the Roman liturgy and a guide must not let
his voice stand in for the tradition's.

**Guéranger also records the Sunday's popular names** [PAT-090]: "In the
western Church this Sunday is called that of the two masters, because of the
Gospel which is read upon it," and, of Byzantine usage, "The Greeks give it the
name of the Sunday of the invited to the marriage-feast, or, the fourteenth of
St. Matthew," with a displacement rule for the Exaltation of the Cross. **His
account of Greek naming is a claim about Byzantine usage checked against no
Greek source and must travel as his report.**

**A retrieval trap specific to this Introit, found and avoided** [PAT-096].
The incipit `Protector noster aspice, Deus` opens **both** this Mass's Introit
and the Fifth Sunday after Pentecost's Gradual, with different continuations —
`in faciem Christi tui` against `super servos tuos`. An incipit-driven search
for reception of the Introit returns material on a different chant of a
different Sunday. The searched missal shows the collision is wider still: the
same words open a verbatim psalm Introit, an adapted Gradual (four
occurrences), **and a composed collect that quotes no psalm at all**
(`Protector noster aspice, Deus: ut, qui malorum nostrorum pondere premimur…`)
[PRE-024]. That third case is precisely what the profile warns about at page 2
— "do not mislabel loose echoes in composed orations as direct quotations".

### 2.2 Epistle — Gal. 5:16–24

**The pericope's two boundaries** [SCR-007]. It begins by **replacing** Paul's
own connective: Clementine Gal. 5:16 reads `Dico autem: Spiritu ambulate`, and
the appointed Epistle reads `Fratres: Spiritu ambulate`. `Dico autem` ties
v. 16 back to the liberty of v. 13 — where Paul's own vocative `fratres`
stands, inside the sentence the pericope drops — and to the law fulfilled in
love at v. 14. The missal's `Fratres` **displaces** the connective rather than
supplementing it. And it ends two verses before the unit's own closing
inclusio: v. 25's `Si Spiritu vivimus, Spiritu et ambulemus` returns v. 16's
verb in the hortatory subjunctive. **In Greek the two verbs differ**
(περιπατεῖτε at v. 16, στοιχῶμεν at v. 25), so the inclusio is tighter in the
Latin than in the Greek — a fact about the Vulgate's rendering. The appointed
excerpt therefore ends on crucifixion (v. 24) rather than on walking (v. 25).

**The list lengths, and their mechanism** [SCR-008]. Latin: seventeen vices,
twelve fruits. Tracked Byzantine Greek: seventeen vices, nine fruits. The
critical text its apparatus records: fifteen and nine. Three precise
consequences:

- The Latin fruit list exceeds every tracked Greek form by exactly three: the
  pair `patientia`/`longanimitas` answers the single μακροθυμία, and `modestia`
  and `castitas` answer nothing in either the Byzantine text or its NA
  apparatus.
- `homicidia` corresponds to φόνοι, which the Byzantine text has and the
  apparatus marks as absent from NA — so "murders" stands in neither the
  Revised Version nor a modern critical-text English, and **the reason is a
  Byzantine/critical divergence, not a peculiarity of the Vulgate**.
- The Vulgate's list is nevertheless **not** simply the Byzantine list: the
  Byzantine μοιχεία (adultery) has no Latin counterpart, so the Clementine
  sides with NA at that word and with the Byzantine text at φόνοι.

*Uncertainty preserved:* the word-by-word alignment of `luxuria`/`impudicitia`
against ἀσέλγεια and its neighbours is a judgement about renderings, not a
count, and is held more loosely than the counts. *Bound:* no NA or UBS
edition is registered here; critical readings reached the lane only through
the inline `{NA …}` braces of the Robinson-Pierpont CSVs, read structurally
rather than by string search, and the artifact record itself warns that a raw
literal search can match apparatus wording as well as edition wording
[SCR-006, SCR-021].

**The asymmetry of the two lists** [THE-020]: the vice list is left explicitly
open (`et his similia`) and the fruit list is closed with a verdict
(`Adversus huiusmodi non est lex`). Textual observation for both counts; the
doctrinal asymmetry is source-grounded synthesis. **Any argument built on the
number twelve is an argument about the liturgical Latin, and saying so is not
optional.**

**Chrysostom's governing move is to refuse the reading that Paul condemns the
body** [PAT-050]: "by the flesh, he does not mean the body", proved from the
vice-list itself, since "hatred, variance, emulations, strife, heresies, and
witchcraft … arise merely from a depraved moral choice". His argument from
the verb holds in the appointed Latin: "If these things belonged to nature and
not to a bad moral choice, his expression, *they practise*, is inappropriate"
— and the missal prints `qui talia agunt`. His one purely verbal observation
also holds for the Latin: "He says not, the work of the Spirit, but, the
fruit", and the missal likewise prints plural `opera carnis` against singular
`Fructus autem Spiritus est`. **This is the fullest direct verse-by-verse
exposition of the appointed Epistle located, covering lemmata at vv. 16, 17,
18, 19–21, 22, 24 and continuing to v. 25, where the missal's pericope stops.
Evidence state: NPNF English only; PG 61 not collated.** The caveat of §0.3(f)
must travel with every use of it.

**Aquinas expounds the missal's own lists** [PAT-070, PAT-071], in Latin, and
is the only witness in the sweep that does. He orders the twelve fruits by
what each perfects — interior, exterior, what is above us, what is below us
(the body) — and reads the number as answering Apoc. 22:2 (`ex utraque parte
lignum vitae afferens fructus duodecim`); **the Apoc. 22 link is his own move
and must be attributed to him, not treated as the list's evident sense.** He
groups the seventeen vices by object, derives `veneficia` from `venenum`, and
reads `homicidia` "cordis et operis", citing 1 Jn. 4. He raises the objection
Chrysostom raises — that several listed vices are plainly not bodily — and
answers it by a **different route**: Augustine's definition, *De civ. Dei*
XIV.2, that whoever lives according to himself lives according to the flesh,
so `caro` here stands for the whole man. **Preserve the difference; do not
report a single patristic-scholastic consensus.** *Evidence state:* acquired,
searched, inspected **in the Latin** — materially better than the English-only
witnesses — but the Corpus Thomisticum is a machine transcription not collated
against a printed Leonine or Marietti page, so not verified. *Lead not
followed:* *De civ. Dei* XIV.2 was not opened, though the repository holds a
record for the work.

**Anthony of Padua left a sermon headed for this very Sunday, and it is
partly a false friend** [PAT-080]. He states his own Mass's texts explicitly:
his Introit is `Inclina, Domine, aurem tuam ad me` — the 1962 **Fifteenth**
Sunday's Introit — and his Gospel is Luke 17:11–19, the ten lepers. His whole
sermon is built on leprosy and cleansing, and his stated reason for pairing
Epistle with Gospel ("in evangelio fit mentio de leprosis … et in epistola
ponuntur vitia, ex quibus provenit lepra peccati") is a rationale for a
pairing the 1962 missal does not make. **What legitimately transfers is his
exposition of the Epistle's own words; what does not is any claim that the
Church pairs Gal. 5 with a cleansing narrative, or anything resting on
`Inclina, Domine`.** The artifact's identity is the strongest in the lane: the
downloaded PDF's sha256 reproduced exactly the digest already registered at
`…/anthony-of-padua/sermo-dominica-xiv-post-pentecosten/…/centro-studi-pdf-a6a9b93f/`.
**Rights posture:** that artifact is `storage = "restricted"` because of the
2021 modern apparatus. Anthony's thirteenth-century Latin is public domain by
age and is what may be quoted; **the modern apparatus must be paraphrased and
never reproduced**, and no bytes were written into the repository.

Anthony's substantive moves on the Epistle [PAT-081, PAT-082]: he glosses
`idolorum servitus` as **avarice** rather than cult of images; he moralises
`veneficia` into "suggestiones daemonum et adulationes peccatorum, quae nocere
non possunt nisi ad sanguinem, idest animae consensum, pervenerint"; and he
defines all twelve fruits individually. His definitions of the last two —
`continentia, quae a licitis abstinet; castitas, quae recte utitur licito` —
are **verbatim** the pair Aquinas independently attributes to the Gloss, from
two separately retrieved and separately hashed artifacts, which is real
evidence of a common medieval source. Three qualifications: his etymologies
are of their period and several are fanciful (`fornicatio, idest formae
necatio` is a pious paronomasia, not philology) and must be presented as his
interpretive move; his inner quotations from the Gloss and Isidore are
**leads**, neither having been opened; and his text prints three of the fruits
in a different order from the missal's (`patientia, longanimitas, bonitas,
benignitas, mansuetudo` against `patientia, benignitas, bonitas, longanimitas,
mansuetudo`), which is a fact about two texts and **not** evidence about which
Vulgate recension the missal descends from.

**The defeater that must be disclosed wherever Epistle and Gospel are joined**
[THE-031]. The library's sole registered treatment of Gal. 5:16–24 is
Anthony's sermon, and Anthony's Sunday numbering runs one ahead of the 1962
missal's — consistently across all three registered Anthony sermons. **A major
medieval preacher expounding this Epistle does so beside a different Gospel.**
So every cross-element candidate joining Epistle and Gospel is a claim about
how the 1962 formulary reads when taken as a whole, and **not** a claim about
how the tradition read these two texts together. Every such candidate must say
so.

### 2.3 Gradual — Ps. 117:8–9

**A negative worth recording**: unlike the Introit and the Offertory, the
Gradual takes complete verses and cuts nothing, so there is no omission to
report [SCR-013]. Context: vv. 6–7 twice call the Lord `adjutor` and name
`homo` as what need not be feared, so v. 8's `quam confidere in homine`
generalises a contrast the psalm has just made concrete; and v. 1's `quoniam
bonus` supplies the adjective the Gradual's `Bonum est` turns into a
comparative formula. The tracked Clementine prints `Alleluja.` at the head of
v. 1, which bears on any claim that Ps. 117 has no superscription.

**Augustine reads the two verses as a single escalating argument** [PAT-020]:
"let not my friend present himself unto me as a good man, so as to bid me
repose my hope in himself … Nor let any one, who may in a certain sense be
styled a good angel, be regarded by myself as one in whom I ought to put my
trust … For angels also are called princes, even as we read in Daniel,
Michael, your prince." The second term is progressively strengthened — bad
men, good men, good angels — and still loses. **This is the only witness in
the sweep whose verse tags needed no shift**, because Ps. 117 carries no
counted superscription. *Latin collated*, agreeing throughout, with one
variant reported as observed and not established: the Latin witness reads the
accusative `sperare in Dominum … in principes` where the missal prints the
ablative `in Dómino … in princípibus`. **One modern transcription cannot tell
whether that is Augustine's psalter or the transcriber's, and a guide should
not build on it without checking PL 37.**

*A cross-relation flagged as a lead and not as Augustine's claim:* his
escalation to good angels makes the Gradual's exclusion absolute rather than
moral, which is the Gospel's own logic at `Nemo potest duobus dominis
servire`. **He does not cite Mt. 6:24 here.**

### 2.4 Alleluia — Ps. 94:1

**Augustine takes the imperative `Venite` as the load-bearing word and denies
it means motion** [PAT-030]: "It is not by place, but by being unlike Him,
that a man is afar from God … If therefore by unlikeness we recede from God,
by likeness we approach unto God." And `exsultemus` is specified as joy *in
the Lord* precisely to exclude a worldly joy the same word would cover: "if
there were not in this life a wicked joy which is to be distinguished from a
righteous joy, it would be enough to say, Come, let us rejoice; but he has
briefly distinguished it."

**The Latin recovers something the translation loses entirely**, and it is
worth the guide's attention: Augustine closes the argument on a jingle built
from the appointed verb itself — `Pie debes Domino exsultare, si vis securus
mundo insultare` (you must piously *exult* in the Lord if you would safely
*exult over* the world). NPNF renders this "piously joy in the Lord, if you
dost wish safely to trample upon the world", and the play vanishes. **The play
depends on the missal's own word `exsultemus`.**

**A material limit** [PAT-030]. This enarratio's centre of gravity is
vv. 7–11, `Hodie si vocem eius audieritis`, which the Alleluia does not
appoint, and Augustine's §13 closes on the oath of exclusion from rest. **A
guide taking Augustine on this psalm must not import that ending into a chant
that stops at v. 1.** The same limit applies to the canonical relationship
`scripture-context` verified [SCR-014]: Hebrews quotes Ps. 94:8–11 at 3:7–11,
repeats the opening at 3:15, and expounds it again through 4:1–11 — real and
checkable, but the formulary appoints v. 1 and no more, and **that the
Alleluia's single verse carries the whole psalm's warning is an inference, not
something the appointed text states.** A precise textual detail verified and
worth carrying: the same Clementine edition renders the quotation differently
in the two places — Ps. 94:9 has `in irritatione` where Heb. 3:8 quoting it has
`in exacerbatione` — so the Vulgate's psalter and its Hebrews do not agree
verbally.

**A structural fact that constrains synthesis** [THE-030]. The Alleluia is the
**least lexically integrated element of the formulary**. Its only substantive
tie to any other element is `salutari`; `exsult-` and `iubil-` occur nowhere
else in the ten elements, and it shares no other content word beyond `Dominus`
and `Deus`. The joy of the Alleluia is a natural thing for a synthesis to
reach for as the resolution of the Gospel's anxiety, and **the appointed texts
do not supply the lexical warrant**: the Epistle's `gaudium` and the Alleluia's
`exsultemus` are different roots in different elements with nothing joining
them. A writer wanting the Alleluia in a joy-resolves-anxiety argument must
build it as an exploratory proposal or find reception support, and no lane
found any.

### 2.5 Gospel — Mt. 6:24–33

**The pericope is cut out of a continuous argument at both ends** [SCR-009].
It begins bare at v. 24, with no connective particle, immediately after the
treasure and eye sayings (`Ubi enim est thesaurus tuus, ibi est et cor tuum`,
v. 21), and stops at v. 33 without v. 34, the passage's own conclusion.
`Sollicitus` governs the discourse four times (vv. 25, 28, 31, 34) and the
pericope carries three of them, ending on the promise of v. 33 rather than on
the restriction to today. That this lets the Communion take the Gospel's last
verse as its own antiphon is a further textual fact; what the compiler meant
by it is not established.

**The pericope contains the second half of a repetition internal to Matthew 6
and not the first** [SCR-010]: the Father's foreknowledge of need at v. 32
(`Scit enim Pater vester`) restates v. 8 (`scit enim Pater vester, quid opus
sit vobis, antequam petatis eum`), which stands in the instruction on prayer
the pericope does not include — and the Lord's Prayer stands between the two
members, asking for the kingdom's coming and for bread. **Flagged as
inference:** any claim that the pericope is meant to be heard against the Our
Father is the lane's to flag, not the text's to assert; Matthew supplies the
proximity and nothing in the appointed formulary points to it.

**Both sayings have Lucan parallels in different settings, and Luke orders the
material the other way round** [SCR-011]. Luke places the two-masters saying
at 16:13, after the unjust steward, and adds `servus`; Matthew has it inside
the Sermon without `servus`. Luke's `corvos` and `cellarium, neque horreum`
differ from Matthew's `volatilia caeli` and `horrea`. And the ordering is
reversed: in Matthew treasure-heart precedes the anxiety discourse and in Luke
it follows — which bears directly on whether Mt. 6:24 is heard as the close of
the treasure unit or the head of the anxiety unit. **The appointed pericope's
boundary at v. 24 takes it as a head; whether that is the compiler's reading
or an artefact of where a pericope had to start is not established.**

**Augustine reads v. 24's asymmetry as deliberate** [PAT-060]: Christ says of
the second master `contemnet` and not `odio habebit` because "almost no one's
conscience can hate God; but he despises, i.e. he does not fear Him, as if
feeling himself secure in consideration of His goodness." **This is the
strongest single patristic move on this Gospel**, because it is a claim about
which of two verbs the text uses and the appointed Latin preserves exactly
that contrast. Two things in the same locus must be reported as his own and
not as established: the "mammon is Punic for gain" remark, which was checked
against no Semitic authority; and the importation of "the prince of this
world" (Jn. 12:31) to identify the second master, which the appointed verse
does not say.

**Augustine explicitly forbids allegorising the birds and lilies** [PAT-061]:
"these examples are not to be treated as allegories, so that we should inquire
what the fowls of heaven or the lilies of the field mean: for they stand here,
in order that from smaller matters we may be persuaded respecting greater
ones", with the unjust judge as his comparison case. **This is a live
constraint on the guide's own apparatus**: the profile requires an allegorical
row on page 1, and the principal Latin Father commenting on this very pericope
says the pericope's central images are not to be taken allegorically. **That
tension is real and must be preserved rather than resolved by quietly
allegorising the lilies anyway or by suppressing Augustine's rule.** The
restraint is specific — he bars allegorising *these examples*, not the
pericope as a whole, and he is at the same time willing to read "seek first
the kingdom" as a doctrine of ordered ends.

**Chrysostom reads the illustrations as rhetorically engineered** [PAT-064].
The birds are chosen *because* they are irrational, deliberately in place of
Moses, Elijah or John, "that He might touch them more to the quick … He has
cut off from them every excuse"; and the lilies are progressively degraded —
lilies, then "grass of the field", then "which today is", then "cast into the
oven" — so the a fortiori grows at every step. The appointed Latin fits word
for word: `fænum agri, quod hódie est, et cras in clíbanum míttitur … quanto
magis vos módicæ fídei?`, and Chrysostom calls the rebuke "not want, but
poverty, of faith". *A defect in the retrieved witness, recorded rather than
silently corrected:* the New Advent page for Homily 22 prints its lemma header
as "Matthew 5:28-29", which is wrong; the text beneath is Mt. 6:28–29. **A
lane resolving loci from that header would mis-cite the homily.**

**Where both Fathers comment they agree the images are arguments and not
figures, and differ in what the argument is** [PAT-065]. Augustine grounds the
inference in the **rank** of the creature ("a rational being such as man has a
higher rank in the nature of things than irrational ones"); Chrysostom grounds
it in the **vileness** of the creature and in the deliberate rhetorical
descent, and gives the anthropological ground in terms of gift rather than
rank ("you, to whom He gave a soul, for whom He fashioned a body … for whose
sake He gave up His only begotten Son"). **The difference is not a
contradiction, but the middle terms differ and the two yield different
homiletic conclusions from the same verses. A guide reporting only "the
Fathers read the birds as an argument from lesser to greater" has flattened the
more interesting half of the evidence.**

**Both Fathers independently set a limit against over-reading the pericope**
[PAT-063]. Augustine, *De serm. Dom.* II.57–58: Christ kept money bags, Paul
worked with his hands, the church at Antioch stored food against Agabus's
famine, and the whole precept reduces to the rule "that even in looking after
such things we should think of the kingdom of God, but in the service of the
kingdom of God we should not think of such things." Chrysostom reaches the same
limit by a different route on v. 26: "He said not, we must not sow, but we must
not take thought; neither that one ought not to work, but not to be
low-minded." **A guide should prefer the Chrysostom sentence when the claim
must rest inside the appointed bounds**, because Augustine's material there is
anchored partly in v. 34, which the lesson excludes, while Chrysostom's is
anchored in v. 26, which it includes.

**A documented *controversial* reception, which runs against the obvious
devotional reading** [PAT-073]. Certain monks quoted these very verses to
argue that they need not work, and Augustine wrote *De opere monachorum*
against them: "let these persons, who from perverse understanding of the Gospel
labor to pervert apostolic precepts, either take no thought for the morrow,
even as the birds of the air; or let them obey the Apostle"; "against the
Apostle of Christ, they recite a Gospel of Christ." The lead came from the
Catena and **was checked against the underlying work and confirmed**. *Not*
confirmed: the "Augustinus de Haeres." citation naming the Euchitae, since *De
haeresibus* was not retrieved — that identification remains an unverified lead
and must not be published as checked.

**The Catena aurea is a lead-map, and its "Chrysostom" is two authors**
[PAT-072]. The appointed Gospel occupies cap. 6 lect. 16–21, drawing on
Augustine, Jerome, Hilary, Chrysostom, Gregory, Rabanus, Remigius and the
Gloss. The Catena cites `Chrysostomus in Matth.` and `Chrysostomus super
Matth.` as **different things**: the first is the genuine *Homiliae in
Matthaeum* (cross-matched sentence for sentence against the independently
retrieved NPNF Homily 22); the second is the anonymous *Opus imperfectum in
Matthaeum*, a fifth-century work with Arian features whose attribution to
Chrysostom is universally rejected. **A guide that quotes "Chrysostom" on
Mt. 6:33 from the Catena's `super Matth.` formula will have attributed to a
Doctor of the Church a text that is not his.** Two of the Catena's attributions
were actually checked and are reported as checked (the lect. 19 Chrysostom and
the lect. 17 *De opere monachorum*); **Hieronymus, Hilarius, Gregorius,
Rabanus, Remigius, Glossa and every `super Matth.` excerpt remain unverified
leads and must not be cited from this Catena text.**

### 2.6 Offertory — Ps. 33:8–9a

**The psalm is anchored by its own superscription to a named deliverance
narrative** [SCR-015]: `Davidi, cum immutavit vultum suum coram Achimelech`.
The tracked Douay prints the cross-reference in the text as "[1 Kings 21.]",
which in Douay book-naming is modern **1 Samuel 21** — a point
`guidance/bibles-for-agents.md` warns about explicitly. So the angel who
encamps stands in a psalm whose superscription reads it off a concrete escape,
and `eripiet eos` at v. 8 repeats the `eripuit me` of v. 5. The antiphon stops
one clause short of the psalm's beatitude.

**NEGATIVE, BOUNDED, and the author must not assert it**: Ps. 33 (MT 34) is
commonly described as an alphabetic acrostic, which would bear on why vv. 8–9
sit where they do. **This could not be verified.** No Hebrew text of the
Psalms is held in this repository — the registered Kittel edition's retained
artifacts are Genesis and 1 Samuel page images — and neither tracked Latin nor
tracked English edition carries acrostic apparatus [SCR-015, SCR-021]. Note
that Bellarmine, the one registered witness who does note the alphabetical
structure, does so at vv. 2–3, and in an abridged Victorian English
translation from which such a remark is exactly the kind of thing an abridger
drops [COV-015].

**Augustine rejects a rival reading and defends the one the missal prints**
[PAT-040]: "So it is written, brethren, not as some bad copies have it, *The
Lord shall send His Angel round about them that fear Him* … but thus, *The
Angel of the Lord shall send round about them that fear Him*." He then
identifies that Angel as Christ, "the Angel of the great Counsel". The 1962
Offertory prints `Immittet Angelus Domini` — `Angelus Domini` nominative
subject of `immittet`, the very reading he defends. Latin collated:
`mendosi codices: Immittet Angelum Dominus … sed sic: Immitte[t] Angelus
Domini …`

**The Latin collation forced a correction and sharpened both the convergence
and its limit.** The locus is *Enarratio in Ps. XXXIII* **Sermo II**, not
Sermo I: the passage is absent from Sermo I entirely, and any citation to
Sermo I is wrong. And Augustine's text continues `et ERUET eos` where the
missal prints `et ERIPIET eos` — so the two **agree exactly on the point in
dispute** (which noun is the subject) and **differ on the following verb**.
That difference is evidence that the missal's text is not simply Augustine's,
and it must be reported alongside the agreement rather than suppressed.

**Two unproven steps the guide must not take** [PAT-040]: that the missal's
reading *descends* from Augustine's, and that the missal's capital `Angelus`
encodes his christological identification. The capital is an orthographic fact;
the transmission question belongs to a textual history no lane swept. **Report
a convergence between the appointed text and a Father's defended reading,
which is checkable; not dependence, which is not.**

**Augustine reads v. 9 as openly Eucharistic** [PAT-041], decoding the psalm's
superscription: the David who "was carried in his own hands" before Achish is
Christ holding his own Body ("cum commendaret ipsum corpus suum et sanguinem
suum, accepit in manus suas quod norunt fideles"), so "taste" is set against
the incomprehension of Jn. 6:52. **The limit that materially bears on this
formulary: his reading is anchored in the psalm's superscription, and the
Offertory antiphon prints no superscription** — so his reasoning is available
to a guide only as reception of the psalm, not as something a hearer of the
antiphon alone could recover.

**Two fourth-century mystagogical witnesses attest the verse as the Church's
own communion invitation, and the 1962 Mass does not use it that way**
[PAT-042]. Cyril of Jerusalem reports it actually being chanted at the
communion: "ye hear the chanter inviting you with a sacred melody to the
communion of the Holy Mysteries, and saying, O taste and see that the Lord is
good. Trust not the judgment to your bodily palate." Ambrose cites it to
identify what is eaten: "Taste and see that the Lord is good, blessed is the
man that hopes in Him. In that sacrament is Christ." **In the 1962 Fourteenth
Sunday the same verse stands at the OFFERTORY, before the consecration, and
this Mass's Communion is a different text entirely. That displacement should
not be smoothed over.** Note also that Ambrose quotes the verse **with** the
clause `beatus vir qui sperat in eo` which the Roman Offertory omits, which
sharpens rather than weakens the observation that the antiphon cuts at
`Dominus`. *Bounds:* both witnesses in nineteenth-century English only,
neither collated against PG 33 or PL 16; Cyril's authorship of the
Mystagogical Catecheses is disputed in modern scholarship (John of Jerusalem
being the principal alternative) and that question was not swept; and the
history of the Roman Offertory chant belongs to §7.4, with **no claim here
about Roman practice or about any transmission between Jerusalem and Rome**.

**The Offertory's argument runs close to the Gospel's two verses past its own
cut** [SCR-017]: Ps. 33:10–11 ("Timete Dominum … Divites eguerunt, et
esurierunt; inquirentes autem Dominum non minuentur omni bono") and
Mt. 6:32–33 both set seeking God against want of food and both promise that
those who seek will not lack. **Clearly flagged as inference:** this is a
similarity of argument, not of wording, and not a quotation in either
direction. The Latin verbs differ, and the Gospel's own `inquirunt` at v. 32
is used of the gentiles *negatively* where the psalm uses the participle
positively — which cuts against reading it as a verbal link. It is recorded
because the appointed excerpt does not contain the psalm verses in question at
all and an author could mistake it for something the formulary makes.

### 2.7 Communion — Mt. 6:33 recast

See §1.2 for the four departures and §7.5 for the settled cross-proper claim.

**Augustine's exegesis of the verse bears directly on the antiphon's word
order, and the two must be kept apart** [PAT-062]. On `primum` he argues an
ordering of **importance and not of time**: "in using that word *first*, He has
indicated that this is to be sought later, not in point of time, but in point
of importance"; and he makes the point by denying the sequence the word
invites — "He did not say, *Then seek such things*". He offers an inversion
test at II.55 and ties the phrase back to Mt. 6:24 at II.56. **The decisive
sentence is uniquely well attested in this sweep**: it is additionally
corroborated in Latin by a second, independent, thirteenth-century witness —
the Catena aurea, retrieved and hashed separately — whose `non tempore, sed
dignitate` answers the NPNF's "not in point of time, but in point of
importance" exactly. That is corroboration, not verification: a catena excerpt
is a lead as to the underlying work, and PL 34 was not opened. But the Latin
wording of the sentence a guide is most likely to quote no longer rests on a
translator alone.

**The line the author must not cross.** (1) Augustine's exegesis of Mt. 6:33 is
documented reception and is his. (2) The observation that the missal's
Communion antiphon **promotes `Primum` to first position** — where Augustine's
argument makes word order carry the doctrine — is an **editorial observation**,
and Augustine knows nothing of the antiphon. **A guide may state both; it must
not present the second as patristic.** Note also that Augustine's exposition
covers v. 34, which the appointed pericope excludes.

### 2.8 The three composed orations — and the page-2 evidence table

**Reception: a bounded negative.** No patristic or medieval commentary on the
Collect `Custodi, Domine`, the Secret `Concede nobis, Domine`, or the
Postcommunion `Purificent semper et muniant` was located [PAT-094]. The three
incipits and their distinctive phrases were searched as literal strings across
every artifact the reception lane retained — the Latin Corpus Thomisticum
files for *Super Galatas* and *Catena aurea* cap. 5–9; the four Latin
*Enarrationes* files; the Centro Studi Latin PDF of Anthony's sermon; and both
Guéranger volumes — on the terms `Custodi, Domine`, `propitiatione perpetua`,
`labitur humana mortalitas`, `abstrahatur a noxiis`, `Concede nobis`, `hostia
salutaris`, `purgatio delictorum`, `propitiatio potestatis`, `Purificent` and
`perpetuae salvationis`. No hit constituted commentary on any of the three.
**Why the negative is expected rather than surprising:** these are anonymous
composed Roman orations, and the patristic commentary genres swept — psalm
expositions, scriptural homilies, epistle commentaries — do not take liturgical
orations as their object.

**Bounds, each a live route for a later attempt:** a literal string search
cannot exclude paraphrase, a different incipit, or an oration cited by its
Sunday rather than its words. Specifically **not** searched: the ancient
sacramentaries' modern editions as commentary sources; Amalarius, Walafrid
Strabo, Rupert of Deutz, Durandus and the other medieval liturgical
expositors; and **Schuster's *Liber Sacramentorum*, whose volume 3 IS
registered in this repository** (sha256 `410f6d11…7239`) and which almost
certainly treats this Sunday — not retrieved because the artifact is 158 MB
and the return did not justify it inside the lane's bounds. **That is a
decision a later attempt may reverse cheaply, and it is the cheapest single
repair available for the orations' reception.**

**The one positive is thin, and its thinness is the finding** [PAT-091].
Guéranger's remarks on the Secret and the Postcommunion are one sentence each
and are paraphrase rather than exegesis. Only the Collect note makes an
interpretive move — that the "abyss of vice" the Church's children would fall
into without help is specifically the Epistle's catalogue, a cross-element
reading — and even that is an association he asserts rather than argues. **A
guide must not inflate a one-sentence editorial gloss into "documented
reception" in the profile's third claim class.** One genuine convergence worth
keeping: Guéranger construes `propitiatio potestatis` as the appeasing of
God's power, independently agreeing with `verified.md`'s own construal.

**Guéranger is a reception witness only and never a text witness** [PAT-093].
His printed Latin differs from the 1962 typical edition at four points: he
prints `millia` for `mília`; a **comma** where the typical edition prints the
**semicolon** that `verified.md` calls load-bearing; lower-case `angelus`;
and `Concede nobis, Domine, quaesumus:` with a colon and a capitalised
`Hostia`, plus the short conclusion `Per Dominum` at both Secret and
Postcommunion where the typical edition prints the longer form at the
Postcommunion. **A guide must not quote the appointed Latin from him.** The
last of the four is the weakest as evidence, since hand-missal presentations
abbreviate conclusions as a matter of course.

## 6. Competing historical judgments preserved

### 6.1 The displacement: the claim and the bound, which must travel together

**The claim, all verified this iteration at first hand on page images**
[LIT-037]: eleven consecutive Gelasian Sunday Masses at N+2 for R/S/Gerbert
and Menard and N+1 for Pamelius, with the run's regularity unbroken
[LIT-028]; the offset already present at the series' **first** section, so
nothing inside the series produces it [LIT-033]; the Gregorian Hadrianum
supplement independently at N+1 across **seven** sections, each identified from
its own printed collect [LIT-005]; and the Ottobonianus 313 chant cues
independently at N+2 across **six** sections in a different edition [LIT-006].
The break at sect. XII is fully explained: Wilson names **two** Masses that R,
S and Gerbert interpolate — the September Embertide Sunday and the "Vacant"
Sunday — which is exactly the +2 the arithmetic requires to move the offset
from N+2 to N+4, while Pamelius stays at N+1 [LIT-033].

**The bound, equally verified** [LIT-037]: none of the five books opened at the
locus [LIT-032]; Wilson's footnote at sect. X covering only "the Collects and
Secret" [LIT-002]; the Postcommunion attested for R and Gerbert only
[LIT-003]; and Honorius's Fourteenth Sunday agreeing with 1962 in four elements
and differing in four [LIT-035].

**Reporting the disagreement without the bound overstates it; dropping the
disagreement because of the bound understates it far worse** — and this
iteration shifts the balance slightly toward the claim. Wilson's apparatus is
not a rumour: it is a published critical collation, and for R and S
specifically it is Wilson's own reading of manuscripts physically in front of
him at the Bodleian. On top of that the numbering is not one footnote but
eleven consecutive ones, plus a twelfth that breaks the pattern exactly as an
interpolation predicts, plus a wholly separate chant apparatus in a different
edition reaching the same magnitude. **A single misreading by Wilson could not
produce that.**

**The one-sentence form a later writer should not simplify past** [LIT-037]:
*the Collect and Secret of this Mass stand two Sundays later in the Frankish
Gelasian books and one Sunday later in the Gregorian, uniformly across the
whole run, on a published critical collation two of whose manuscripts the
editor read himself — and no one has since opened any of the five books at the
locus, the assignment covers only two of the formulary's ten elements, and the
rest of the formulary was assembled on a different schedule again.*

### 6.2 The four books agreeing on the sixteenth Sunday are not four independent witnesses

Established on page images from Wilson's own preface and introduction
[LIT-029]. R and S were sent to the Bodleian and collated by Wilson himself.
Gerbert's principal manuscript T "cannot now be traced". Wilson holds that the
"Gelasian" portions of T, and so of Gerbert's printed text, "do not represent
the original text of the 'Gelasian' books, but a revision of that text,
apparently based upon the text of the Sacramentaries of the 'Gregorian' type",
and that "the readings of the later hand of S., followed by Gerbert's printed
text, are in agreement with the 'Gregorian' Sacramentaries edited by Pamelius
and by Menard". **So Gerbert does not merely fail to be a third Gelasian
voice: it leans toward the very Gregorian editions that are supposed to
corroborate.**

**The honest count** is therefore: two Frankish Gelasian manuscripts Wilson
collated himself (R and S), plus a printed edition largely dependent on a lost
manuscript copied from S and revised toward the Gregorian type (Gerbert), plus
one Gregorian edition (Menard); with the other Gregorian edition (Pamelius) at
the fifteenth. **And a caution in the other direction that prose must also
keep: R's and S's own Sunday headings are Wilson's own collation and do not
depend on Gerbert at all, so Gerbert's unreliability does not weaken them.**

**Four corrections to how Wilson has been quoted** [LIT-029]. (1) "Exceedingly
confused and misleading" is hedged with "at times", applies to Gerbert's
*handling and explanation of his materials* rather than to his text as such,
and stands immediately after Wilson's warm tribute — "students of Liturgy owe
so much as we owe to Gerbert". **Quoting the phrase bare overstates Wilson.**
(2) Wilson excepts "the latter part" of R from his criticism, and whether these
Sunday Masses fall inside that part is not established, so **the
unreliability cannot simply be assumed to reach them**. (3) "Now-lost
Turicensis" is fair; Wilson's own words are "it cannot now be traced". (4) The
prior statement *understated* its own case, per (2) above about Gerbert's
Gregorian lean.

**One further dependence prose must not double-count** [LIT-005]: the Gregorian
numbering and Pamelius's N+1 in Wilson's Gelasian apparatus **agree because
Pamelius is an edition of the Gregorian book. They are not two independent
witnesses to N+1.**

### 6.3 Honorius's Fourteenth Sunday is not the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday

By the early twelfth century the 1962 Sunday number and this Introit are
already paired: *Gemma animae* IV.72 is headed "Dominica decima quarta post
Pentecosten « Protector noster, » sub lege" — and the quotation is exact, as
the lane confirmed by actively trying to break it, since Migne's neighbouring
headings (CAP. LXVII, LXX) print no "post Pentecosten" and CAP. LXXII does
[LIT-034].

**But reading the whole chapter rather than its last sentence shows the
opposite half of the picture, and it must travel with the first** [LIT-035].
Within one and the same chapter Honorius's **Introit, Collect, Epistle and
Offertory are the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday's**, while his **Gospel is the 1962
Thirteenth Sunday's** (Luke 17:11–19, the ten lepers) — and that Gospel offset
is not a slip: it holds across three consecutive Sundays. His **Gradual**
(*Bonum est confiteri Domino*, Ps. 91) is not the 1962 Fourteenth's (*Bonum est
confidere in Domino*, Ps. 117:8–9); his **Alleluia** (*Quoniam Deus magnus*,
Ps. 94:3) is a different verse of the same psalm the 1962 Alleluia uses; and
his **Communion** is *Panis, quem ego dabo*.

**Why this matters to the whole question:** the earlier books do not simply
carry the 1962 formulary under a different number. **Different strands of the
Mass — orations, chants, Gospel — were numbered and paired independently and
settled at different times, and prose that reports "this Mass stood at a
different Sunday" as one clean fact will be reporting something no witness
actually says.**

*A caution on the printing:* in Migne's text the two parenthetical references
in the Gradual and Gospel sentences are **transposed** — `Bonum est confiteri
Domino (Luc. XVII)` and the ten lepers `commemorat (Psal. XCI, 2)` — whereas
*Bonum est confiteri Domino* is Ps. 91:2 and the ten lepers is Luke 17. **A
reader taking either reference at face value from this column will be misled.**

## 7. Cross-proper claims settled for the synthesis commentary

Five claims. Each draws together multiple ritual moments, scriptural contexts
and reception witnesses; **none is an abridged procession through the
propers.** Claim classes are given per claim, and where a claim mixes classes
the mixture is stated rather than averaged.

**The two-element floor governs this section exactly as it governs §9, and the
element line of each claim below names the members it joins.** Every claim
joins at least two precisely named **appointed elements of this formulary**.
The guide's own apparatus — its four senses, its page-2 sheet, its gallery — is
never a member of a conjunction: it is a feature of the publication and not a
ritual moment of the Mass, and where a claim bears on it that bearing is
recorded as a **consequence** of the claim. This is the same floor that drops
`fruit-count-unsettled` at §0.3(d) and refuses the Introit-incipit conjunction
at §9.6. It is stated here rather than left implied because iteration 0 of this
brief broke it at C3, and at C3 alone; see §0.3(g).

### 7.1 — C1. One phrase carries the Mass, and it is not the phrase Matthew usually uses

**Elements: Epistle, Gospel, Communion.** Classes 1 and 3.

`Regnum Dei` stands verbatim in three appointed elements and carries one
argument across them: the Epistle names who is **excluded** from the kingdom
(`qui talia agunt, regnum Dei non consequentur`), the Gospel commands that it
be **sought first**, and the Communion repeats that command as **the Lord's own
word at the moment of reception** [THE-001]. The negative and positive forms
are two faces of one claim about the kingdom, not two positions set against
each other.

Three textual facts sharpen it. (a) `Regnum Dei` is **rare in Matthew**: four
occurrences in the whole Gospel (6:33, 12:28, 21:31, 21:43) against thirty-four
occurrences of `regnum caelorum` across thirty-two verses, and Mt. 6:33 is the
first of the four [SCR-004]. (b) It is the **only** occurrence of the phrase in
Galatians [SCR-005]. (c) Across the fifteen 1962 Roman formularies this
repository has collated, `regnum Dei` occurs in **this formulary alone**, and
within it at exactly three loci, independently reproduced in a second
provider's collation [THE-008].

**Reception at the hinge:** Augustine's `non tempore, sed dignitate` — the
`primum` of Mt. 6:33 is an ordering of importance and not of time — is the
best-attested Latin sentence in the whole sweep, corroborated from two
independent witnesses [PAT-062]. Augustine himself ties the phrase back to
Mt. 6:24 at *De serm. Dom.* II.56.

**Limits.** The sample for (c) is fifteen collated formularies, which is small
and non-random; it is **emphatically not a claim about the missal as a whole**
and must name the sample [THE-008]. The count at (a) is of the tracked
Clementine's Latin and was not repeated in Greek [SCR-004]. And there is a
defeater for the spine's third leg: the Communion antiphon is drawn from the
Gospel of the day, which is ordinary practice, so if its `regnum Dei` were
shown to be a purely mechanical carry-over the three-element spine would
reduce to a two-element one plus an echo — **though that does not touch the
Epistle's independent occurrence, which comes from a different book and a
different pericope** [THE-001]. Finally, §2.2's defeater applies: a major
medieval preacher expounding this Epistle does so beside a different Gospel
[THE-031].

### 7.2 — C2. The flesh that is crucified is not the body that is fed, and the tradition says so twice, by two different routes

**Elements: Epistle, Gospel.** Classes 1 and 3.

The two elements are **lexically disjoint**: `caro`/`carn-` stands five times
in the Epistle and nowhere else in the formulary; `corp-` stands twice in the
Gospel and nowhere else [THE-011]. **This is one unit, not a contrast.** The
point is not that Epistle and Gospel disagree about the body but that they use
different words for different things, and that reading them together dissolves
the appearance of disagreement. **A writer who builds this as "Epistle
mortifies vs Gospel provides" has inverted it.**

**The doctrinal resolution is now documented reception and not merely
synthesis**, which is a change this brief makes deliberately.
`theological-synthesis` reached this claim on textual grounds alone and
recorded that the repository's registered library holds none of the witnesses
that would settle it at the Galatians 5 locus [THE-011, THE-032].
`patristic-reception` then reached two witnesses by direct retrieval:
Chrysostom's governing move that "by the flesh, he does not mean the body",
proved from the vice-list's own contents [PAT-050]; and Aquinas answering the
same objection through Augustine's *De civ. Dei* XIV.2, that `caro` here stands
for the whole man [PAT-071]. **Preserve both answers; they differ in kind.**
Augustine's *De opere monachorum* supplies the third leg from the other
direction: the ascetic misreading of the Gospel half **actually happened**, and
a whole treatise was written against it [PAT-073], with both Fathers
independently setting the same limit against over-reading the pericope
[PAT-063].

**Limits.** Gal. 5:24's `carnem suam crucifixerunt` is read by some as
including the body proper, and the Vulgate elsewhere uses `caro` for the body
simply; the disjunction is a fact about these two pericopes as printed, not a
lexical law [THE-011]. The Introit's psalm source has `caro` in a **positive**
sense at Ps. 83:3b, but that clause falls outside the appointed cut. Chrysostom
is NPNF English only, PG 61 uncollated; Aquinas is a machine transcription
uncollated against a printed edition. And §0.3(f) governs: **the Greek witness
must not be quoted on the words his list does not contain.**

### 7.3 — C3. The images are arguments and not figures, and the one sentence the Mass lifts out of them is read as doctrine

**Elements: Gospel, Communion.** Classes 1 and 3.

**The Gospel half.** Augustine explicitly **forbids** allegorising the birds
and lilies: "these examples are not to be treated as allegories … they stand
here, in order that from smaller matters we may be persuaded respecting greater
ones", with the unjust judge as his comparison case [PAT-061]. Chrysostom
independently reads them as arguments too, but as **rhetorically engineered**
ones — the birds chosen *because* irrational, in place of Moses, Elijah or
John, "that He might touch them more to the quick"; the lilies progressively
degraded through the sentence so the a fortiori grows at every step [PAT-064].
Both Fathers then set a limit against over-reading the pericope, and they reach
it by different routes [PAT-063].

**Where they agree they still differ, and the difference is the interesting
half** [PAT-065]. Augustine's middle term is the **rank** of the creature;
Chrysostom's is its **vileness**, and his anthropological ground is **gift**
rather than rank. The two yield different homiletic conclusions from the same
verses, and a guide reporting only "the Fathers read the birds as an argument
from lesser to greater" has flattened the evidence.

**The Communion half, which is this claim's second appointed element.** The
formulary appoints Mt. 6:33 twice: once as the Gospel's last verse and again,
recast, as the Communion antiphon — the seventh element standing on the sixth
locus, and the formulary's single substantive departure from its Bible text
(§1.1, §1.2) [SCR-001, SCR-002, SCR-003]. On that one verse Augustine does not
argue from a lesser example at all. He reads `primum` as an ordering of
**importance and not of time** — `non tempore, sed dignitate` — and makes the
point by denying the sequence the word invites: "He did not say, *Then seek
such things*" [PAT-062]. This is the other face of the same restraint, and the
restraint's specificity is what makes it one claim rather than two: he bars
allegorising *these examples*, not the pericope as a whole, and at the
pericope's closing imperative he reads a doctrine of ordered ends. **The two
appointed elements that carry Matthew 6 in this Mass therefore receive from one
witness two different kinds of reading — figure refused at the images, doctrine
read at the imperative — and the sentence the missal lifted out to say at the
moment of reception is the one on which he reads doctrine.**

**The evidence is unusually good at exactly this hinge.** Augustine's decisive
sentence is the best-attested Latin in the whole sweep: corroborated in Latin
by a second, independent, separately hashed thirteenth-century witness whose
`non tempore, sed dignitate` answers the NPNF wording exactly [PAT-062]. That
is corroboration and not verification, and §11.2 governs how it is stated.

**One editorial observation, and it must be kept apart from the reception.**
The missal's recasting promotes `Primum` to the head of the antiphon — exactly
where Augustine's argument makes word order carry the doctrine. **That is an
editorial observation and not a patristic one; Augustine knows nothing of the
antiphon.** A guide may state both and must never present the second as
patristic (§2.7).

**What follows for the guide's own page 1 — a consequence of the claim, not a
member of it.** The profile requires an allegorical row, and the principal
Latin Father commenting on this very pericope bars allegorising the pericope's
central images. **That tension is real and must be preserved rather than
resolved by quietly allegorising the lilies anyway or by suppressing
Augustine's rule.** §10 row 1 carries it there as a named constraint on that
row. Iteration 0 of this brief declared the four-senses apparatus itself as
C3's second element, which was wrong twice over: the apparatus is the guide's
and not the formulary's, and promoting a consequence to a member left the claim
resting on one ritual moment. See §0.3(g).

**Limits.**

- Both patristic witnesses are **NPNF English only**; neither is collated
  against PL 34 or PG 57 [PAT-001]. Chrysostom's observations are about
  wording, so their force depends on the sequence of words being the same in
  Greek, Latin and English at these points; **the Latin correspondences were
  checked and do hold, the Greek was not** [PAT-064].
- **The Communion membership carries C1's defeater, and the author must state
  it** [THE-001]. A Communion antiphon drawn from the day's Gospel is ordinary
  practice, so this second element may be a mechanical carry-over rather than a
  choice. That bears on how *interesting* the join is; it does not dissolve it,
  because the profile's criterion is two **ritual moments**, and the lesson and
  the reception are two ritual moments whatever produced the antiphon.
  **Unlike C1, this claim has no third element from a different book to fall
  back on, and that is its principal weakness.**
- **No compiler's intent is claimed.** That the pericope stops at v. 33 is what
  lets the Communion take the Gospel's last verse, and **what the compiler
  meant by it is not established** [SCR-009]. The claim is about what the two
  appointed elements are and how the Fathers read them, never about why the
  verse was chosen.
- **This claim and C5 share one datum and must not be merged.** Both notice the
  promotion of `Primum`. C5 puts it to the extent and antiquity of the
  recasting (§7.5); C3 puts it to the difference between the two registers in
  which the appointed Matthaean text is read. Neither restates the other, and
  each needs its own home in the guide rather than one merged passage.
- Augustine's exposition of Mt. 6:33 runs on into v. 34, which the appointed
  pericope excludes (§2.7). Where a claim must rest inside the appointed
  bounds, prefer Chrysostom's limit at v. 26, which the lesson includes
  [PAT-063].

### 7.4 — C4. The Sunday's number is the least stable thing about the Mass, and its strands were assembled on different schedules

**Elements: Collect, Secret, Postcommunion; and, at the second remove, the
chants and the Gospel.** Class 2, with class 4 in the conclusion.

The whole case, its bound and its honest one-sentence form are at §6.1–§6.3.
What makes it a synthesis claim rather than a chronology is the second half:
the earlier books do not carry the 1962 formulary under a different number.
**Orations, chants and Gospel were numbered and paired independently** — the
orations displaced uniformly by two Sundays in the Frankish Gelasian and one in
the Gregorian; the Ottobonianus chant cues attached two 1962 Sundays ahead of
the orations they sit beside, so that the cue set beside *Custodi, Domine* is
the 1962 **Sixteenth** Sunday's [LIT-006]; and Honorius, in one chapter,
agreeing with 1962 in four elements and differing in four [LIT-035].

**And the Communion of this Mass is attested in that apparatus at a different
Mass** [LIT-007]. Wilson's own index shows *Primum quaerite* occurring as a
Communion at exactly one locus, p. 171, whose chant set is the 1962 **Ninth**
Sunday's Introit, Gradual and Offertory; the *Protector noster* set's Communion
there is *Panis quem ego*. Setting that against the 1962 book shows a
**rotation** rather than a single replacement: in this apparatus the
1962-Ninth set takes *Primum quaerite*, the 1962-Fourteenth set takes *Panis
quem ego*, and the 1962-Fifteenth set takes *Qui manducat*, while 1962 assigns
*Qui manducat* to the Ninth, *Primum quaerite* to the Fourteenth and *Panis
quem ego* to the Fifteenth.

**Routing decision:** this is source-grounded synthesis and documented
historical orientation, **not** an interpretive proposal — see §0.3(e) and
§9.6.

**Limits.** Two of the three 1962 assignments in the rotation were verified
against repository text; the Fifteenth Sunday's Communion was **not**, because
no leaf for it exists here [LIT-007]. What the rotation establishes is **what
the older pairing was, not when the substitution happened**; Hesbert's
*Sextuplex*, the instrument that would test it, is registered at catalog level
only. The Ottobonianus evidence is about the cues of **one manuscript** and is
not a bound on the two-Sunday displacement as such, which rests on a wholly
different body of books [LIT-006]. And the cue placement is establishable at
**heading** precision from the page images even though the repository's own
passage record says page precision, because Wilson prints the footnote marks on
the section numbers [LIT-006].

### 7.5 — C5. The formulary makes exactly one substantive recasting, it is at the Communion, and it is older than this book

**Elements: Gospel, Communion.** Classes 1, 2 and 3.

The four departures are at §1.2 and were verified mechanically, in Latin and
against the Greek [SCR-002, SCR-003]. Two of them bear theologically
[THE-018]: adding `dicit Dominus`, which stands nowhere in Matthew at this
verse or its Lucan parallel, **turns a saying inside a sermon into an address
made to the communicant at the moment of reception**; and dropping `et
iustitiam eius` leaves the kingdom to be sought without the justice the Gospel
names beside it. A third — promoting `Primum` to the head — falls exactly where
Augustine's argument makes word order carry the doctrine, **which is an
editorial observation and not a patristic one** (§2.7).

**The recasting is not proper to the 1962 edition:** the same form is printed
in the Pustet Missal of Ratisbon, 1862, which this repository tracks as
public-domain text [THE-018].

**Three independent witnesses converge on the antiphon being secondary in this
position.** (a) The appointed text itself: it is the formulary's one
substantive departure from its Bible text. (b) Guéranger, p. 342: "The
Communion-anthem, taken from the Gospel which now is assigned to this Sunday,
was not the one primitively used; the ancient liturgists make no mention of it
in its present position, nor is it to be found there in any of the manuscripts
consulted by Blessed Thomasi" [PAT-092]. (c) The Ottobonianus rotation at §7.4
[LIT-007].

**And a control from within the same Office, which is the strongest single
thing here and which anyone can check in the same pages** [PAT-092]: Guéranger
prints this Sunday's Magnificat antiphon at Vespers as `Quaerite primum regnum
Dei, et justitiam ejus, et haec omnia adjicientur vobis. Alleluia.` **That
antiphon retains both `et iustitiam eius` and `haec` — exactly the two clauses
the Mass's Communion drops — and leaves `primum` in its Matthaean position
after the verb.**

**Limits.** Guéranger's judgment rests on Tommasi's Antiphonary and "the
ancient liturgists", neither consulted: a **checked report of an unchecked
claim**, to be attributed to him and never restated as established chant
history [PAT-092]. Antiphon texts are routinely shortened for chant and `dicit
Dominus` is a common formulaic tag, so both changes may be conventional rather
than expressive, and that count was not run [THE-018]. The Pustet witness shows
the recasting is old but says nothing about why it was made. And the
Communion's selecting the imperative rather than the pericope's three
prohibitions may be an artefact of taking the final verse rather than a choice
among the pericope's parts [THE-021] — **a real weakness in the third step of
that argument, though not in the first two, which rest on the Gospel's own
structure.**

### 11.2 Evidence-state discipline

**No patristic or medieval witness in this brief is *verified* in the
source-library sense**, because verification under this profile means
collating the locus against the controlling critical or Migne edition, and
Migne was opened for none of them [PAT-001]. The tiering used is: **acquired**
(exact bytes obtained and hashed), **searched** (query run over the retained
artifact), **inspected** (the identified passage read with surrounding
context), and, where a Latin witness independently agrees, **corroborated** —
which is short of collation. This is deliberate: the prior run's recorded
failure was reaching every witness through a summarising route and so being
unable to quote at all, and the correction is **to state the route and the
state per witness rather than to make one blanket claim of verification.**

Three witnesses are better attested than the rest and the guide may lean on
them accordingly: **Anthony of Padua** and **Guéranger**, whose downloaded
digests reproduced already-registered artifact records bit-for-bit; and
**Augustine on `primum`**, whose decisive sentence is corroborated in Latin
from a second, independent, separately hashed thirteenth-century witness.

**Every English quotation from a patristic or medieval witness must name the
translation it is quoting, on the page and not only in this brief.** The brief
states this at §2.1 for Augustine on the Introit — "a guide quoting it must
present the English as NPNF's" — and nothing about that locus is special: with
the sole exception of the Latin read from Aquinas, Anthony, the *Catena* and
the augustinus.it *Enarrationes* files, **every** patristic string this brief
supplies is somebody's English translation, reached by one of the routes at
§3.2. Naming the route is what keeps an *inspected* nineteenth-century
translation from reading on the page as the Father's own words, and it is the
page-level counterpart of the per-witness evidence states above. **The rule is
therefore stated once for the whole class rather than left at one locus.** The
translations to name, exactly as §3.2 records them and no more precisely than
that: **NPNF first series** for Augustine's *Enarrationes in Psalmos*, *De
sermone Domini in monte* and *De opere monachorum*, and for Chrysostom's
*Commentary on Galatians* and *Homilies on Matthew*; **NPNF second series
vol. 7** for Cyril of Jerusalem's *Mystagogical Catecheses*; **NPNF second
series vol. 10** for Ambrose's *De mysteriis*; and, for Guéranger, the **Duffy
1900 English edition** of *The Liturgical Year* vol. XI. **No lane established
who translated the Guéranger volume, so name the edition and never a
translator.** Latin quoted in Latin needs no translation attribution, but it
needs its route and state (§3.2) like everything else, and where this brief
gives an English rendering beside a Latin collation the English is still the
translator's and is still to be named as his.

### 11.3 Retrieval traps found this run — four instances of one species

All four are queries that **resolve successfully and wrongly**, returning
well-formed material about the wrong text. They are recorded because the next
worker will meet them.

1. **The doubled psalm-numbering offset on New Advent** [PAT-002]. The page
   title uses the modern number and the inline verse tags run one verse behind
   the Clementine, so a locus must be shifted **twice**. A lane resolving
   "Ps. 83:10" without shifting lands on Augustine expounding a different
   psalm and quotes a well-formed paragraph under the wrong verse. Absent at
   Ps. 117 and Ps. 94 for reasons peculiar to those psalms — **an agreement
   that is a coincidence and not a general property.**
2. **The running head that names the section the page reaches** [LIT-003,
   LIT-004, LIT-005]. Two loci in Wilson 1894 were misread this way, one
   corrected by the prior run and one only now; the same device recurs in
   Wilson 1915. **Any locus in either book taken from a running head is
   suspect until checked against the section heading itself.**
3. **The shared incipit** [PAT-096, PRE-024]. `Protector noster aspice, Deus`
   opens this Mass's Introit, the Fifth Sunday's Gradual, and a composed
   collect, with different continuations. **A search hit on the incipit proves
   nothing on its own.** Related: the augustinus.it file index is a running
   document number, not the psalm number — `esposizione_salmo_083` serves
   Ps. 66 — and the correct files were resolved through the host's own index
   and confirmed by each printed heading.
4. **OCR hyphenation and phrase-search degradation** [PRE-023, CUL-015,
   CUL-001]. `grep -i immittet` over the tracked missal misses a Lenten
   occurrence broken as `Immit-`/`tet`; a grep of 47 newspaper issues for
   "political kingdom" returned zero while the phrase was present as
   `poli-`/`tical`; and loc.gov's quoted-string search degrades to an AND of
   terms. **A search that finds nothing has not established that nothing is
   there; it has established what the search did.**

A fifth, from the Catena: **`Chrysostomus super Matth.` is not Chrysostom**
(§2.5, [PAT-072]).

### 11.4 What no gate can check
