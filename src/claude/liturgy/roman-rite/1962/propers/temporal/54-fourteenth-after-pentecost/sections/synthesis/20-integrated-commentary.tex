\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

\subsection*{1. What is this Mass asking for, and where does it say so?}

It asks to be looked at and kept, and it says so in two registers that never
meet lexically. The Introit asks for a glance --- \latin{réspice in fáciem
Christi tui} --- and the Collect answers the look with a keeping whose diagnosis
is not sin but mortality: \latin{quia sine te lábitur humána mortálitas}. The
Church is named there and \textbf{nowhere else in the ten elements}, though the
first-person plurals of Introit, Alleluia, Secret and Postcommunion are
corporate in their own way, which softens any contrast built on that count.

\textbf{Six readings now stand on that one clause and they do not stack.}
Augustine will not let the petition rest at its surface, since God never fails
to look on the face of his Christ: it must mean \latin{Fac innotescere omnibus
Christum tuum}. Cassiodorus formalises the same move as \emph{hypallage}.
Theodoret's Greek reads the face ecclesially, on 1~Cor.~12:27.
\textbf{Arnobius the Younger} reads it of the man seated at God's right hand ---
\latin{Cum respicit in faciem Christi, et hominem viderit sedentem ad dexteram
suam} --- with no figure of speech invoked. \textbf{Godfrey of Admont makes the
anointed the congregation itself}, \latin{Christum suum nobis dando, semper nos
unctos suos respiciat}, and he quotes the Introit as sung. Guéranger reads it of
the Church presenting the face of the Christ who is also hers. Guéranger is nineteenth-century, not patristic, and Godfrey
expounds this formulary's chants beside a different Gospel.

Against that, a second vocabulary partitions the formulary. \latin{Propitiátio}
stands twice, in the Collect and the Secret and nowhere else; \latin{semper} and
\latin{perpétu-} twice each, in the Collect and the Postcommunion and nowhere
else; and the \latin{salut-} and \latin{salv-} root four times, once each in the
Collect, the Alleluia, the Secret and the Postcommunion and in none of the other
six elements. \textbf{The people's texts state the problem as service, trust and
anxiety; the priest's prayers state it as expiation and rescue} --- and the
Alleluia's \latin{salutári} is the single word tying the day's one purely
jubilant chant into that thread. The orations' idiom is the standard vocabulary
of Roman collects, so the saturation may be unremarkable, and the four
\latin{salut-} words are one root in four senses: of a victim, of God, of things
that save, and of a state. The registered English drops the noun at the Collect
and both \latin{semper} and the noun at the Postcommunion, so those three words
do not survive into it.

\subsection*{2. What can a man add?}

Nothing to his own stature, and a Doctor of the Church reads that incapacity as
the pericope's own proof of providence. \latin{Adícere} stands twice in the
appointed Gospel: at Mt.\ 6:27 of the cubit no man can add to his stature, and
at Mt.\ 6:33 of what shall be added to those who seek first. \textbf{Both
occurrences arrive together as a matter of pericope choice:} the compiler
took Mt.\ 6:24--33 whole, and the Communion, which takes the last of those
verses, is the one reuse the liturgy makes of the word --- and even that is
inherited, being in print in 1862.

\textbf{Aquinas reads the cubit as an experimental proof of providence.} The
measure of a man's stature is not in his own power, so the verse demonstrates
from the hearer's own body what the birds demonstrate from outside him:
\latin{Hic probat experimento, quod sicut providet avibus, ita et nobis \dots\
Unde augmentum non est nobis ex nobis, sed ex Deo: unde de providentia Dei non
debetis desperare.} \textbf{Growth is from God and not from ourselves}, and on
that ground the anxiety the pericope forbids is demonstrably vain.

\textbf{And the verb the antiphon keeps is read by three witnesses.}
Pseudo-Bede: \latin{Non dixit nobis dari, sed adjici, quia nobis in intentione
aeternitas, in usu vero debet esse temporalitas}. Christian of Stavelot supplies
the elided noun with its limit, \latin{haec, subauditur necessaria, adjicientur
\dots\ Ad necessitatem, non ad superfluitatem}. Paschasius reads the promise
through use and enjoyment, \latin{his utamur in via, quatenus illo fruamur in
patria}. \textbf{So the promise is read as supplement rather than as gift, and
bounded to necessity} --- and none of the three comments on the antiphon as
such, all three reading Mt.\ 6:33 in its Gospel place.

\subsection*{3. Whose flesh is the Epistle talking about?}

Not the body, and four witnesses say so in four different ways. The two lessons
are \textbf{lexically disjoint} --- \latin{carn-} five times in the Epistle and
nowhere else in the formulary, \latin{corp-} twice in the Gospel and nowhere
else --- \textbf{so they are one unit and not a contrast}, and a reading that
sets a mortifying Epistle against a providing Gospel has inverted it.
\textbf{The Introit's cut keeps the division clean}: its psalm verse stops one
clause before \latin{cor meum et caro mea exsultaverunt in Deum vivum}.

\textbf{Chrysostom's Greek denies desire to the flesh} --- \latin{sarka
gar entautha, ou to soma kalei} --- and proves it from the vice-list's own
contents, since enmities, strifes and heresies belong only to a corrupted
choice; the body is an instrument, and \emph{we hate and punish not the iron but
the murderer}. \textbf{Marius Victorinus} argues the contrary from the elements,
which have impulses and no soul: \latin{Habet enim motus suos caro, habetque
sensus}. \textbf{The Glossa} makes the ascription a manner of speaking about a
joint act, \latin{ex utroque enim fit}. \textbf{Ambrosiaster} shifts the ground
to two laws, \latin{unam Dei, alteram peccati}, so the crucified flesh is the
world. \textbf{Chrysostom and Victorinus comment on the same appointed verse and
cannot both be right.} Jerome defends the missal's own word, \latin{vitia}
rendering \emph{pathemata}, \latin{desideria} added \latin{ut non naturam
corporis videretur in spiritualibus viris negare, sed vitia}; and Aquinas
answers the same objection through the definition he cites from Augustine's
\work{De civitate Dei} XIV.2, \textbf{that work standing unopened behind his
citation of it.} Augustine supplies a leg from the other side: the ascetic
misreading of the Gospel half \emph{actually happened}, and \work{De opere
monachorum} was written against monks who quoted these very verses, which the
\work{Catena aurea} points to and the work itself confirms. The identification
of those monks as the Euchitae stands in \work{De haeresibus}, which is
unopened.

\textbf{On the lists themselves:} the Latin prints seventeen vices and twelve
fruits, the tracked Byzantine Greek seventeen and nine, the critical text
fifteen and nine. \textbf{The twelve are a feature of the liturgical
Latin and not of the Greek behind it.} \textbf{Ambrosiaster's own list runs to
ten members and carries words the missal does not print}, so his exposition
counts a different pair of lists. Aquinas expounds the
missal's twelve, and Anthony of Padua defines all twelve individually while
\textbf{passing over \latin{homicidia} with no gloss at all}; the Glossa alone
gives the list a structure, making charity the head from which the members
follow, \latin{Caput praemisit, unde membra sequuntur}.

\textbf{The library's sole registered treatment of Gal.\ 5:16--24 is Anthony's
sermon, and his Sunday numbering runs one ahead of the 1962 missal's.} \textbf{A
major medieval preacher expounding this Epistle does so beside a different
Gospel} --- his own Mass reads the ten lepers --- so every cross-element claim
joining these two elements is a claim about how the 1962 formulary reads. The
\work{Liber Comitis} answers the other half: the two lessons were paired, at
these two extents, in the Roman lectionary tradition.

\subsection*{4. Are the birds and the lilies figures --- and what is the one verse the Mass says twice?}

\textbf{The Latin tradition is divided by name, and the division is not one of
emphasis.} Augustine says expressly that the images are not allegories, in the
English of the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, first series ---
``these examples are not to be treated as allegories \dots\ they stand here, in
order that from smaller matters we may be persuaded respecting greater ones'' ---
and the exposition printed under Bede's name repeats him; \textbf{Christian of
Stavelot} refuses allegory at the lilies on frankly aesthetic grounds, no linen
matching the lily nor any dye the rose; Bruno of Segni reads them literally.
\textbf{Godfrey of Admont stands at the far end}, making the birds the good
angels, the lilies the evil spirits and Solomon Christ, and then raising and
answering the objection his own allegory creates. Chrysostom reads them as
arguments too, but as rhetorically engineered ones --- the birds chosen
\emph{because} irrational, the lilies degraded through the sentence so the
\emph{a fortiori} grows at every step. \textbf{Where the Fathers agree they
still differ}: Augustine's middle term is the creature's \textbf{rank},
Chrysostom's its \textbf{vileness}, grounded in gift rather than rank.

\textbf{And the sentence the Latin tradition repeats most often at this Gospel's
first verse was withdrawn by the man who wrote it.} \latin{Nullius enim fere
conscientia Deum potest odisse} is retracted at \work{Retractationes} I.19,
Augustine naming his own exposition of the verse and giving Ps.\ 73:23 as his
reason. Five later Latin witnesses repeat it \emph{without} his qualifier
\latin{fere}, the Glossa alone flagging the retraction; and \textbf{Bruno of
Segni denies the proposition outright}, the covetous man serving idols and
therefore hating God (Eph.\ 5:5). \textbf{``No one can hate God'' is therefore
neither the tradition's settled reading here nor Augustine's own last word.}

\textbf{The Mass appoints Mt.\ 6:33 twice, and that second appointment is this
question's second element.} On that verse nobody argues from a lesser example.
The Augustine the compilations transmit reads \latin{primum} as an ordering of
importance and not of time, \latin{non témpore, sed dignitáte}; \textbf{and
Paschasius denies that it marks order at all}, \latin{magis ad quantitatem
referendum est}, calling the sequence \latin{praeposterus} because the kingdom
was put first to soothe the hearing before justice was brought in as the
laborious thing. \textbf{So the sentence the missal lifted out for the moment of
reception is the one on which the tradition reads doctrine and then divides
about a single adverb.} A Communion drawn from the day's Gospel is ordinary
practice, so this element may be a mechanical carry-over, and the claim has no
third element from another book to fall back on. Bruno of Segni restricts
the whole prohibition of anxiety to apostles and those aiming at perfection and
Godfrey preaches it to monks about \emph{lectio}, so two witnesses read the
pericope as addressed to a narrower audience than a Sunday congregation. \textbf{The recasting promotes \latin{Primum} to the
head of the antiphon}, and none of the witnesses read here comments on the
antiphon as such.

\textbf{The Latin Fathers commenting on this pericope are divided about whether
its central images may be allegorised at all} --- Hilary allegorises them,
Jerome and Augustine forbid it. And the \work{Catena aurea}, which supplies most
of what a reader would expect here, is a lead-map: its \latin{Chrysostomus super Matth.}
is the anonymous \work{Opus imperfectum} and not Chrysostom.

\subsection*{5. Who is the angel, and where does the tasting happen?}

The Fathers divide at both chants of this Mass. \textbf{At the Offertory the angel has four
accounts.} Augustine defends the very reading the 1962 book prints ---
\latin{Immíttet Angelus Dómini}, against what the English of the \work{Nicene
and Post-Nicene Fathers}, first series, calls ``some bad copies'' reading
\latin{Immittet Angelum Dominus} --- and identifies that Angel as Christ.
Cassiodorus's lemma is the reading Augustine denounced, and he turns instead to
the moral sense. \textbf{Arnobius the Younger makes him the angel of the
sepulchre}, \latin{revolvit lapidem a monumento} --- \textbf{a reading that
turns on repointing \latin{timentium eum} as those fearing \emph{for} his body,
which is not what the missal's Latin says.} \textbf{Godfrey makes him the gift
of grace itself.} \textbf{But Augustine's text continues \latin{et eruet eos}
where the missal prints \latin{et erípiet eos}: the two agree exactly on the
point in dispute and differ on the following verb, which is evidence that the
missal's text is not simply his.} \textbf{The missal's capital \latin{Angelus}
is an orthographic fact}, and no textual history joins the two readings by
descent.

\textbf{At the Gradual the princes have four positions and a question.}
Augustine escalates the excluded second term from bad men to good men to
\textbf{good angels} --- ``for angels also are called princes,'' in the English
of the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, first series; Cassiodorus lets
\latin{princeps} cover both the devil and the good angel; Theodoret, in Greek,
makes them earthly rulers whose authority is temporary; Godfrey makes them the
interior and exterior senses. \textbf{And Bruno the Carthusian asks whether the
verse is comparative at all}, answering first that the word is not set down as a
comparative and then that it is, but only if the princes are heavenly spirits. \textbf{So the angel who is the Mass's rescuer at
one chant is its excluded object of trust at the other, and the positions were
reached separately.} Peter Lombard puts the angelic reading under Augustine's
name and Prosper's exposition is largely an Augustine epitome, so what looks
like three witnesses to that reading may be one position with three carriers;
Augustine's own \latin{Enarratio} on this psalm would settle it and is
unopened.

On the tasting, Augustine reads Ps.\ 33:9 openly of the Eucharist, decoding the
psalm's superscription --- \textbf{and the antiphon prints no superscription},
so his reasoning is available as reception of the psalm rather than as something
a hearer of the antiphon alone could recover. \textbf{Cassiodorus reaches the
same reading from the appointed verbs themselves}, and \textbf{Arnobius reads
the verse eucharistically in place}, \latin{Gustate corpus vitae}. The
\work{Mystagogical Catecheses} report the verse actually being chanted at the
communion --- \textbf{their attribution to Cyril of Jerusalem is disputed in
modern scholarship} --- and Ambrose cites it to identify what is eaten.
\textbf{In this Mass the same verse stands at the Offertory, before the
consecration, and the Communion is a different text entirely.}

\textbf{And three excerpts stop short of the same formula while a fourth sings
the hope vocabulary outright.} \latin{Beatus \dots\ qui sperat} stands in
exactly two verses of the Clementine, and they are the two psalms this Mass
takes its Offertory and its Introit from: \latin{beatus vir qui sperat in eo} at
Ps.\ 33:9b and \latin{beatus homo qui sperat in te} at Ps.\ 83:13, the two
differing in what is hoped in. It lies past the
Introit antiphon's cut at v.~11a and past its psalm verse's cut at v.~3a, and
one clause past the Offertory's cut at v.~9a, while the Gradual is uncut and
carries \latin{sper-} and \latin{confid-} twice each, the only element that
carries either. Six Sundays earlier Ps.\ 33:9 is appointed whole, hope clause
included. Antiphon extents are inherited chant tradition, and nothing
establishes that anything was cut in order to suppress the beatitude.

\subsection*{6. What did this Mass inherit, and what did it alter?}

Almost everything, and one thing --- and the strands it inherited were counted
from different feasts. \textbf{The orations} stand one Sunday later in the
Gregorian books, confirmed across eleven consecutive Masses on page images, and
two later in the Frankish Gelasian at the section that carries them: across
Gelasianum Book~III sects.~I--XI, this Mass's sect.~X among them, Rheinau,
S.~Gallen, Gerbert and Menard stand two Sundays later, and across
sects.~XII--XVI four, while Pamelius stands one later across all sixteen without
a break and Menard never agrees with him. \textbf{The chants did not travel
with them:} the
ninth-century antiphonary formulary carrying this Introit, Gradual and Offertory
is numbered \emph{Dominica XIV}, the number 1962 gives it, and the Ottobonianus
margin reproduces those formularies one number above the sacramentary headings
they stand against. \textbf{The readings never moved at all:} the \work{Liber
Comitis} pairs this Epistle with this Gospel, cut at exactly the verses 1962
cuts, under \latin{Dominica v post sancti Laurentii}.

\textbf{Two mechanisms stand.} Wilson's is a numeration
base. \textbf{Schuster's is a Roman cycle built around the feast of
St~Lawrence}, whose count ``was not everywhere the same'' --- and the Comes and
Schuster themselves differ by one about where in that cycle this Sunday falls.
Four of the five Gelasian books --- Rheinau, S.~Gallen, Pamelius and Menard ---
are unopened at the locus, and for them the disagreement rests on Wilson's
apparatus alone; the fifth, Gerbert, stands open at his printed p.~173 in two
independently produced Internet Archive optical layers that agree verbatim,
with no page image behind them. Wilson's footnote at that section covers ``the
Collects and Secret'' only, and the books agreeing are not independent --- one printed edition leans toward
the Gregorian type it is supposed to corroborate and another is itself an
edition of the Gregorian book. \textbf{Schuster does not corroborate the
displacement:} his pages on this Mass name no station, no sacramentary siglum
and no manuscript.

\textbf{And the reception shows the displacement twice, independently.}
Honorius has this Sunday's Introit, Collect, Epistle and Offertory beside the
previous Sunday's Gospel and the following Sunday's Gradual, Alleluia and
Communion --- \textbf{three chants of five, not five} --- and Godfrey of Admont
expounds this formulary's chants and Collect beside the
Gospel of the ten lepers.

\textbf{The one alteration is at the Communion, it is older than this book, and
it broke a pair.} Mt.\ 6:33 is recast in four ways --- \latin{ergo} dropped and
\latin{Primum} promoted, \latin{et iustítiam eius} omitted, \latin{hæc} dropped,
\latin{dicit Dóminus} added, which stands nowhere in Matthew here or in Luke.
\textbf{But \latin{Primum quaerite} is not a late composition:} it is an ancient
Roman antiphon standing at the \emph{Ninth} Sunday in four ninth-century
antiphonaries, where this Sunday's Communion is \latin{Panis quem ego dedero}
--- \textbf{which travels with the Offertory \latin{Immittet Angelus} at both
its occurrences}, so the substitution separated two chants the older books kept
together. \textbf{The antiphon is secondary in this slot only and not in the
repertory.} Guéranger's report that it ``was not the one primitively used''
rests on Tommasi's Antiphonary and ``the ancient liturgists,'' neither of which
stands open here, and it is confirmed in substance from a source he did not
have. \textbf{When the exchange was made no witness
says}, only that it is later than the antiphonaries, the Ottobonianus margin and
Honorius, and earlier than the Pustet Missal of 1862.
