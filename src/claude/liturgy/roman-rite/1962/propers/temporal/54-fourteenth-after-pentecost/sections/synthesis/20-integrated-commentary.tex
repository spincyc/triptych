\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

\begingroup
\footnotesize

\subsection*{Allegiance orders desire toward the kingdom}

The Introit gives holy longing its object; the Collect asks movement away from
harms and toward saving goods; Galatians shows desire divided between fleshly
orientation and the Spirit; the Gospel names rival masters; and the Communion
places the kingdom first at reception. Aquinas reads the masters as rival
beatitudes, Augustine reads ``first'' as first in dignity, and Bellarmine
gathers the psalm's longing into desire for heaven. The Postcommunion's
perpetual salvation gives the sequence its eschatological horizon. Created
goods remain good, but they cannot become a rival final end.

\subsection*{Providence summons responsible agency}

The Gradual's trust, the Collect's plea for continual help, the Epistle's
imperative \latin{Spíritu ambuláte}, the Gospel's release from anxious mastery,
and the Postcommunion's request for sacramental purification and strength hold
divine gift and human response together. Chrysostom joins diligence to divine
kindness; Theodoret joins indwelling grace to cooperation; Benedict makes
Matthew~6:33 govern a superior's care of souls amid scarcity. Work and prudence
remain, but neither can master the heart. Providence orders action instead of
cancelling it and promises no immunity from deprivation.

Jerome, Chrysostom, and Theodoret refuse to make bodily nature the object of
Paul's condemnation. The Epistle's crucified \latin{caro} and the Gospel's
provided-for \latin{corpus} are not opposites: the body is good, while
vice-governed desire requires healing.

\subsection*{Created mediation serves without becoming a rival master}

The Gradual places confidence in the Lord above man and princes. Augustine and
Cassiodorus include angelic powers among those princes; Theodoret reads earthly
rulers; Bellarmine permits creaturely assistance only when hope passes through
it to God. The Offertory immediately presents an Angel who encamps around those
who fear the Lord. Augustine identifies the Angel with Christ; Cassiodorus and
Bellarmine read a ministering angel; Theodoret speaks of ministering angels;
Aquinas preserves created, Christological, and ecclesial senses.

The two chants do not cancel one another. They distinguish ministry from
mastery. The creature may serve God's protection without receiving the
ultimate confidence denied to man and princes. The Gospel's two masters gives
that distinction its moral edge, while the Introit's invocation and the
Postcommunion's fortification show the same protection before and after the
Eucharistic action. Psalm~33 still includes affliction and redemption;
protection is not physical immunity.

\subsection*{The oblation and sacraments carry the Church toward saving effect}

The Collect asks perpetual propitiation and saving direction for the Church.
The Offertory's ``O taste, and see'' has explicit Eucharistic reception in
Augustine and Cassiodorus, \work{Mystagogical Catechesis} V.20---traditionally
attributed to Cyril of Jerusalem though its authorship remains disputed---and
Ambrose's \work{De mysteriis} IX.58. The Secret names the saving Host as
purification and propitiation; the Communion revoices the kingdom's priority;
the Postcommunion asks continuing purification and strength unto perpetual
salvation. Estius receives the Collect as a prayer for persevering grace.
Offering, communion, and sacramental effect remain distinct acts in one
received ritual movement, not evidence of common composition.

\subsection*{Grace transfigures appetite into worshipful delight}

The Introit's longing, the Spirit's joy and peace, jubilation to the Saviour,
freedom from solicitude, and the Offertory's tasting move restless appetite
toward worshipful delight and final salvation. Augustine and Cassiodorus hear
jubilation as joy beyond articulate speech; Theodoret hears a victor's cry;
Bellarmine moves from inward gladness to vocal praise and experiential
sweetness. These affects are related without becoming interchangeable terms.
Christian trust suppresses neither grief, prudence, nor concrete care.

Continuity and selection mark the inherited history. Gelasian and Hadrianum
Gregorian sources preserve the prayers in differing arrangements, while
Hesbert's early chant comparison and Honorius preserve mixed constellations.
The five later chants stand together in Johannes of Valkenburg's Gradual of
1299; Lippe's 1474 Roman-Curia Missal prints the complete ten-element core,
which remains stable in element and order in the Venice 1570, Vatican 1604,
Mame 1922, and Vatican 1962 witnesses. The earlier Communion
\latin{Panis quem ego} gives way to \latin{Primum quǽrite} without a recovered
date or cause. Across these received forms, the later formulary places desire
under one allegiance, creaturely need under providence, assistance under
divine mastery, and sacramental action under the kingdom sought first.
