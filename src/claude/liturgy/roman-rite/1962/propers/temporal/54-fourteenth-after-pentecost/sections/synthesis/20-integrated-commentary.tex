\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

This edition omits the complete appointed texts and the element-by-element
sweep. What stands here is the same evidence redistilled: six questions a
reader of these ten texts actually has, each answered from several elements and
several witnesses at once. The complete Latin, the full per-element reception
and the collation records are in the canonical edition and in
\texttt{propers/verified.md}.

\textbf{The evidence discipline governing every witness below.} \textbf{Not one
reception witness in this guide is \emph{verified} in the source-library
sense}: verification means collating the locus against the controlling critical
or Migne edition, and \textbf{Migne was opened for none of them}. Witnesses are
\emph{acquired}, \emph{searched}, \emph{inspected}, and in three cases
\emph{corroborated} by a second independent witness in the original language.
Two Greek authors were reached and \textbf{neither was read in Greek}, against
four Latin and one nineteenth-century French, so nothing here is presented as an
even Greek--Latin comparison and no claim is made about the Fathers \emph{as a
body}. And because the Latin vice- and fruit-lists the missal prints are longer
than the lists the Greek Fathers expound, \textbf{no Greek witness is made to
comment on \latin{homicídia} or on \latin{cástitas}}, words his own list does
not contain. \textbf{Every English string quoted from a Father below is the
rendering of the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers} and is named as such and
not as the Father's own words}: first series for Augustine and Chrysostom,
second series vol.~7 for the \work{Mystagogical Catecheses}, second series
vol.~10 for Ambrose. Guéranger is quoted from the Duffy 1900 edition of
\work{The Liturgical Year} vol.~XI; \textbf{no lane established who translated
that volume, so the edition is named and no translator is.}

\subsection*{1. What is this Mass asking for, and where does it say so?}

It asks to be looked at and kept, and it says so in two registers that never
meet lexically. The Introit asks for a glance --- \latin{réspice in fáciem
Christi tui} --- and the Collect answers the look with a keeping whose diagnosis
is not sin but mortality: \latin{quia sine te lábitur humána mortálitas}. The
Church is named there and \textbf{nowhere else in the ten elements}, though the
first-person plurals of Introit, Alleluia, Secret and Postcommunion are
corporate in their own way, which softens any contrast built on that count.

Augustine will not let the Introit's petition stand at its surface, since God
never fails to look on the face of his Christ: it must mean \latin{Fac
innotescere omnibus Christum tuum}, make thy Christ known to all. \textbf{This
is the best-attested Augustinian quotation in the sweep and the safest to
publish in Latin}, because the argument is grammatical, about \latin{facies},
and survives translation. Guéranger reads the same clause the other way, of the
Church presenting the face of the Christ who is also hers; \textbf{the two do
not reconcile by stacking and both are kept}, and Guéranger is
nineteenth-century, not patristic. \textbf{Two limits travel with the
Augustine.} His reasoning is driven by Rom.\ 5:20 and by the psalm's v.~8,
neither of which is in the appointed extract. And his exposition of the
antiphon's other verse runs on into the half of v.~11 the Introit cuts off:
\textbf{the Father's unit and the liturgy's unit do not coincide.}

Against that, the orations carry a vocabulary the sung and read elements do not:
\latin{propitiátio} twice, the \latin{salut-} root four times, \latin{semper} and
\latin{perpétu-} twice each. \textbf{The people's texts state the problem as
service, trust and anxiety; the priest's prayers state it as expiation and
rescue.} \textbf{Bounds:} that is the standard vocabulary of Roman collects, so
the saturation may be unremarkable; the substantive \latin{propitiátio} stands
in no other of the fifteen formularies collated here while the wider
\latin{propiti-} family reaches eleven of them, so the claim is about the
substantive alone; and the registered English drops the noun at the Collect and
both \latin{semper} and the noun at the Postcommunion, \textbf{so the
measurement is conducted on the Latin.}

\subsection*{2. What can a man add?}

Nothing, and that is the point of the verb. \latin{Adícere} stands at Mt.\ 6:27
of the cubit no man can add to his stature and at Mt.\ 6:33 of what shall be
added to those who seek first, and the Communion repeats the promise alone.
\textbf{The reversal is in the morphology} --- active of the man who cannot,
passive of the God who does --- and the Collect had already made that impotence
the ground of its petition while the Epistle names it a third way, \latin{ut non
quæcúmque vultis, illa faciátis}.

\textbf{The join is doctrinal and not lexical at two of its four members},
because neither the Collect nor the Epistle uses the verb, and that is a weaker
join than the one at question~5. \textbf{And both Gospel occurrences arrive
together as a matter of pericope choice:} the compiler took Mt.\ 6:24--33 whole,
so only the Communion's reuse is an act of the liturgy upon the text --- and
even that is inherited, being in print in 1862.

\subsection*{3. Whose flesh is the Epistle talking about?}

Not the body, and the tradition says so twice by routes that differ in kind. The
two lessons are \textbf{lexically disjoint} --- \latin{carn-} five times in the
Epistle and nowhere else in the formulary, \latin{corp-} twice in the Gospel and
nowhere else --- \textbf{so they are one unit and not a contrast}, and a reading
that sets a mortifying Epistle against a providing Gospel has inverted it.

Chrysostom proves it from the vice-list's own contents: ``by the flesh, he does
not mean the body,'' since hatred, variance, emulations, strife, heresies and
witchcraft ``arise merely from a depraved moral choice'' --- and he presses the
verb the missal prints, \latin{qui tália agunt}. Aquinas raises the same
objection and answers it through the definition he cites from Augustine's
\work{De civitate Dei} XIV.2, that whoever lives according to himself lives
according to the flesh, so \latin{caro} stands for the whole man ---
\textbf{that work not opened here, so it is Aquinas's use of Augustine that is
reported and not Augustine's own wording.} \textbf{Preserve the difference; there is
no single patristic-scholastic consensus here.} Augustine supplies a third leg
from the other side: the ascetic misreading of the Gospel half \emph{actually
happened}, and \work{De opere monachorum} was written against monks who quoted
these very verses --- a lead taken from the \work{Catena aurea} and
\textbf{checked against the underlying work and confirmed}, though the
\work{De haeresibus} identification of the Euchitae was not and remains an
unverified lead.

\textbf{On the lists themselves:} the Latin prints seventeen vices and twelve
fruits, the tracked Byzantine Greek seventeen and nine, the critical text
fifteen and nine. \textbf{Any argument built on the number twelve is an argument
about the liturgical Latin, and saying so is not optional.}
\latin{Homicídia} is missing from a critical-text English because of a
Byzantine-against-critical divergence and \textbf{not} because of anything
peculiar to the Vulgate; and the Vulgate is not simply the Byzantine list
either, siding with the critical text at ``adultery.'' Aquinas is the only
witness in the sweep who expounds the missal's own twelve, and Anthony of Padua
defines all twelve individually while \textbf{passing over \latin{homicidia}
with no gloss at all.}

\textbf{The defeater that must be disclosed wherever Epistle and Gospel are
joined:} the library's sole registered treatment of Gal.\ 5:16--24 is Anthony's
sermon, and his Sunday numbering runs one ahead of the 1962 missal's. \textbf{A
major medieval preacher expounding this Epistle does so beside a different
Gospel} --- his own Mass reads the ten lepers --- so every cross-element claim
joining these two elements is a claim about how the 1962 formulary reads, not
about how the tradition read the two texts together.

\subsection*{4. Are the birds and the lilies figures --- and what is the one verse the Mass says twice?}

\textbf{Augustine says expressly that they are not}, and that is the most
consequential single sentence of reception on this Gospel: ``these examples are
not to be treated as allegories \dots\ they stand here, in order that from
smaller matters we may be persuaded respecting greater ones.'' Chrysostom reads
them as arguments too, but as rhetorically engineered ones --- the birds chosen
\emph{because} irrational, in place of Moses, Elijah or John, ``that He might
touch them more to the quick,'' and the lilies degraded through the sentence so
the \emph{a fortiori} grows at every step, the appointed Latin following him
word for word.

\textbf{Where they agree they still differ, and that is the interesting half.}
Augustine's middle term is the creature's \textbf{rank}; Chrysostom's is its
\textbf{vileness}, grounded in gift rather than rank. \textbf{A guide reporting
only that the Fathers read the birds as an argument from lesser to greater would
have flattened the evidence.} Both set the same limit against over-reading, and
Chrysostom's form of it stays inside the appointed bounds --- ``He said not, we
must not sow, but we must not take thought'' --- where Augustine's is anchored
partly in v.~34, which this lesson excludes.

\textbf{The Mass appoints Mt.\ 6:33 twice, and that second appointment is this
question's second element.} The verse stands as the Gospel's last and again,
recast, as the Communion antiphon --- the seventh element on the sixth locus,
and the formulary's one substantive departure from its Bible text. On that verse
Augustine argues from no lesser example at all: he reads \latin{primum} as an
ordering of importance and not of time, \latin{non témpore, sed dignitáte},
denying the sequence the word invites --- ``He did not say, \emph{Then} seek
such things.'' \textbf{So the two appointed elements carrying Matthew~6 in this
Mass receive from one witness two different kinds of reading --- figure refused
at the images, doctrine read at the imperative --- and the sentence the missal
lifted out for the moment of reception is the one on which he reads doctrine.}
That sentence is the best-attested Latin in the sweep, corroborated by an
independent, separately hashed thirteenth-century witness; \textbf{corroboration
is not verification}, and \work{Patrologia Latina}~34 was not opened.
\textbf{Limits:} a Communion drawn from the day's Gospel is ordinary practice,
so this element may be mechanical carry-over, which bears on how interesting the
join is and not on whether these are two ritual moments; the claim has no third
element from another book to fall back on; and \textbf{no compiler's intent is
claimed.} The promotion of \latin{Primum} to the head of the antiphon is
\textbf{an editorial observation of this guide's and not a patristic one} ---
Augustine knows nothing of the antiphon --- and question~6 puts the same datum
to the recasting's extent and antiquity rather than to its registers.

\textbf{One consequence for the guide's own apparatus, which is not an element
of this claim.} The profile requires an allegorical row on page~1, and the
principal Latin Father commenting on this pericope bars allegorising its central
images. \textbf{That tension is preserved on page~1 rather than resolved by
allegorising the lilies anyway.} The four senses are this publication's
apparatus and not a ritual moment of the Mass, so the bearing is recorded as a
consequence and never counted as a member of the conjunction. And the
\work{Catena aurea}, which supplies most of what a reader would expect here, is
a lead-map: its \latin{Chrysostomus super Matth.} is the anonymous \work{Opus
imperfectum} and not Chrysostom, and Jerome, Hilary, Gregory, Rabanus, Remigius
and the Gloss remain unverified leads cited from it nowhere in this guide.

\subsection*{5. Who is the angel, and where does the tasting happen?}

Both chants of this Mass carry documented divisions, and the formulary prints
both without adjudicating. At the Offertory Augustine defends the very reading
the 1962 book prints --- \latin{Immíttet Angelus Dómini}, against ``some bad
copies'' reading \latin{Immittet Angelum Dominus} --- and identifies that Angel
as Christ. \textbf{But his text continues \latin{et eruet eos} where the missal
prints \latin{et erípiet eos}: the two agree exactly on the point in dispute and
differ on the following verb, which is evidence that the missal's text is not
simply his.} \textbf{Two steps this guide does not take:} that the missal's
reading \emph{descends} from his, and that the missal's capital \latin{Angelus}
encodes his identification --- the capital is orthography, and the transmission
question belongs to a textual history no lane swept. At the Gradual Augustine
escalates the excluded second term from bad men to good men to \textbf{good
angels}, ``for angels also are called princes.'' \textbf{So the angel who is the
Mass's rescuer at one chant is its excluded object of trust at the other, and
the two positions were reached separately} --- he does not cite the Gospel's
\latin{Nemo potest} at either place, and the join between them is this guide's
observation.

On the tasting, Augustine reads Ps.\ 33:9 openly of the Eucharist, decoding the
psalm's superscription --- \textbf{and the antiphon prints no superscription},
so his reasoning is available as reception of the psalm rather than as something
a hearer of the antiphon alone could recover. The \work{Mystagogical Catecheses} report the verse
actually being chanted at the communion --- \textbf{their attribution to Cyril
of Jerusalem is disputed in modern scholarship and that question was not swept},
and they were read in the English of the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers},
second series, vol.~7, uncollated against \work{Patrologia Graeca}~33 --- and
Ambrose cites it to identify what is eaten. \textbf{In this Mass the same verse stands at the Offertory,
before the consecration, and the Communion is a different text entirely; that
displacement is not smoothed over here.} Ambrose also quotes the verse
\emph{with} the hope clause the Roman Offertory omits.

\textbf{And three excerpts stop one clause short of the same formula while a
fourth sings it.} \latin{Beatus \dots\ qui sperat in eo} closes both Ps.~33 and
Ps.~83; it lies past the Introit antiphon's cut and past its psalm verse's cut,
one clause past the Offertory's cut, and the Gradual alone sings it outright.
Six Sundays earlier Ps.\ 33:9 is appointed whole, hope clause included.
\textbf{The strongest limit is that antiphon extents are inherited chant
tradition: the cuts have other explanations and intent was not established.}

\subsection*{6. What did this Mass inherit, and what did it alter?}

Almost everything, and one thing. \textbf{The Collect and Secret stand two
Sundays later in the Frankish Gelasian books and one Sunday later in the
Gregorian, uniformly across eleven consecutive Masses}, on a published critical
collation two of whose manuscripts the editor read himself. Two further lines of
evidence reach the same magnitude independently --- the Gregorian supplement
across seven sections, the Ottobonianus chant cues across six in a different
edition --- and the one break is fully explained by two interpolated Masses.
\textbf{The offset is already present at the series' first section, so nothing
inside the series produces it}, and its origin lies outside that series.

\textbf{The bound is load-bearing and travels with the claim.} None of the five
books has been opened at the locus. The footnote covers ``the Collects and
Secret'' only, and the Postcommunion's Gelasian base is narrower again, attested
for two of the five books and not the rest. \textbf{So the displacement is a
claim about two of the formulary's ten elements, and treating the three orations
as equally attested would be wrong.} The books agreeing are not independent:
one printed edition leans toward the Gregorian type it is supposed to
corroborate, and another is itself an edition of the Gregorian book, so its
reading and the Gregorian numbering are not two witnesses. In the other
direction, two manuscripts' own Sunday headings are the editor's own collation
and do not depend on the unreliable edition at all.

\textbf{And the strands did not travel together.} Honorius, in one
twelfth-century chapter already headed for this Sunday and opening
\latin{Protector noster}, has this Sunday's Introit, Collect, Epistle and
Offertory but the previous Sunday's Gospel, a different Gradual and Alleluia,
and a different Communion. \textbf{The earlier books do not carry the 1962
formulary under a different number: orations, chants and Gospel were numbered
and paired independently and settled at different times.}

\textbf{The one alteration is at the Communion, and it is older than this book.}
Mt.\ 6:33 is recast in four ways --- \latin{ergo} dropped and \latin{Primum}
promoted, \latin{et iustítiam eius} omitted, \latin{hæc} dropped, \latin{dicit
Dóminus} added, which stands nowhere in Matthew here or in Luke. The tag turns a
saying inside a sermon into an address at the moment of reception; the omission
leaves the kingdom sought without the justice the Gospel names beside it; and
the promotion of \latin{Primum} falls exactly where Augustine's argument makes
word order carry the doctrine --- \textbf{which is an editorial observation and
not a patristic one, since Augustine knows nothing of the antiphon.} His
decisive sentence, \latin{non tempore, sed dignitate}, is corroborated in Latin
from a second independent thirteenth-century witness, which is corroboration and
not collation.

\textbf{Three witnesses agree the antiphon is secondary here}, and one control
is checkable in the same pages: Guéranger prints the day's Magnificat antiphon
at Vespers retaining \emph{both} clauses the Mass's Communion drops.
\textbf{But his judgment that the antiphon is not primitive rests on Tommasi's
Antiphonary and ``the ancient liturgists,'' neither consulted: it is a checked
report of an unchecked claim, his and never established chant history.} And the
chant rotation establishes \textbf{what the older pairing was, not when the
substitution happened.}
