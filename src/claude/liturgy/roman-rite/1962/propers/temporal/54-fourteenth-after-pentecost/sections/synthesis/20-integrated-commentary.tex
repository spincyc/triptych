\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

\begingroup
\footnotesize

\subsection*{Desire receives its object}

The Introit gives desire its positive beginning. Psalm~83 longs for God's
courts, prays for the anointed, and prefers one day with God above thousands.
Augustine and Cassiodorus read that one day as everlasting; Theodoret keeps
Israel's displacement and bodily rescue in view; Bellarmine makes the psalm a
pilgrim's longing for the heavenly dwelling. The voices differ in emphasis,
but none treats desire itself as the enemy.

Galatians names the conflict within desire. Paul opposes the works produced by
the fleshly orientation to the fruit borne under the Spirit. Augustine reads
the struggle through humility, grace, and a will that may refuse desire its
completion. Jerome, Chrysostom, and Theodoret deny that flesh here condemns
bodily nature. Their distinction prepares Matthew: Christ acknowledges food,
clothing, work, and the body while denying that anxiety or mammon may become a
master.

\subsection*{The kingdom governs service and use}

The Gospel brings the alternatives to an ultimate end. Aquinas reads the two
masters as rival beatitudes; Augustine reads the kingdom sought ``first'' as
first in dignity. The Communion then contracts that command and addresses it
anew at reception: \latin{Primum quǽrite regnum Dei}. The antiphon omits the
Gospel's justice clause and adds \latin{dicit Dóminus}; Augustine, Hilary,
Jerome, Chrysostom, Aquinas, and Benedict comment on the complete saying, not
on that exact recasting. Its liturgical force is direct. The
longing of the Introit and the conflict of the Epistle are ordered toward the
kingdom as the governing allegiance.

\subsection*{Guardianship, action, and providence}

The Collect confesses that human mortality slips without God and asks for
perpetual guardianship, withdrawal from harm, and direction toward saving
things. The Epistle's imperative, \latin{Spíritu ambuláte}, is heard within
that petition. Chrysostom joins human diligence to divine kindness, Theodoret
joins indwelling grace to cooperation, and Augustine preserves grace-assisted
freedom. Command and prayer describe the same Christian agency from opposite
sides.

The Gospel extends that agency to daily need. Jerome and Chrysostom retain the
birds and lilies as real creatures proving providence; Hilary reads the images
through resurrection and angelic glory. Their disagreement leaves the Father's
care intact. Work and prudence remain, but neither can master the heart. The
Postcommunion completes the Collect's petition after reception: the sacraments
purify, fortify, and lead toward perpetual salvation. What God directs is not
passive; what the disciple does is not autonomous.

\subsection*{Protection and the order of mediation}

The Gradual places confidence in the Lord above man and princes. Augustine and
Cassiodorus include angelic powers among those princes; Theodoret reads earthly
rulers; Bellarmine permits creaturely assistance only when hope passes through
it to God. The Offertory immediately presents an Angel who encamps around those
who fear the Lord. Augustine identifies the Angel with Christ; Cassiodorus and
Bellarmine read a ministering angel; Theodoret speaks of ministering angels;
Aquinas preserves created, Christological, and ecclesial senses.

The two chants do not cancel one another. They distinguish ministry from
mastery. The creature may serve God's protection without receiving the
ultimate confidence denied to man and princes. The Gospel's two masters gives
that distinction its moral edge, while the Introit's invocation and the
Postcommunion's fortification show the same protection before and after the
Eucharistic action. Psalm~33 still includes affliction and redemption;
protection is not physical immunity.

\subsection*{From saving praise to sacramental effect}

The Alleluia praises ``God our saviour.'' Augustine and Cassiodorus hear
jubilation beyond speech, Theodoret a victor's cry, and Bellarmine inward joy
becoming vocal praise. The complete psalm presses that joy toward hearing
God's voice today. The Collect asks to be directed toward saving things; the
Secret names the offering a saving victim and calls it purification and
propitiation; the Postcommunion asks the sacraments to produce the effect of
perpetual salvation.

The Offertory's ``taste and see'' carries this sequence to the altar. Augustine
and Cassiodorus receive the verse in Christ and the Church; Cyril of Jerusalem
and Ambrose use it in sacramental instruction; Aquinas and Bellarmine connect
tasting with experienced sweetness. Their reception illuminates the verse but
does not move it from its appointed place at the Offertory. Offering,
communion, and sacramental effect remain distinct acts joined by one saving
movement.

Continuity and selection mark the inherited history. Gelasian and Hadrianum
Gregorian sources preserve the prayers in developing arrangements; the
Hadrianum source separates the three target orations from a chant
constellation with another Communion; the verified Mame 1922 page images and
the Vatican 1962 facsimile carry the same later ten-element placement and
order. The original compiler and the causes of these rearrangements remain
historically undetermined. Across these received forms, the formulary places
desire under one allegiance, creaturely need under providence, assistance
under divine mastery, and sacramental action under the kingdom sought first.
