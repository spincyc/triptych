\section*{The Propers: Notable and Quotable}

Five afterlives of wording from the scriptural propers, each a use that moves
the phrase into a register its psalmist or evangelist did not occupy.
\textbf{Their evidence state is \emph{inspected}, and this gallery says so at
the head rather than in a closing paragraph: every entry below was read from a
digital surrogate --- optical text of a newspaper page, a digitised official
report, or a court-database record --- and \emph{no page image, printed volume
or manuscript was collated for any of them}. ``Retrieved and read in a digital
surrogate'' and ``verified'' are different states, and the distance between
them is exactly a page image. Nothing here is described as verified.} The loci
are given precisely enough that the collation remains cheap for whoever makes
it.

Straight exegesis, devotional reuse, an independently similar phrase, and a
bare quotation, title, motto or musical setting are excluded by the gallery
rule. The three orations are outside it too, since it is confined to wording
from the scriptural propers. \textbf{Three appointed elements were swept and
produced nothing that qualifies:} for the Introit, the Alleluia and the
Offertory, every use confirmed in the optical text was straight devotional,
liturgical or exegetical reception --- church-notice standing heads, sermon
texts, a responsive reading, devotional columns, a gospel-song text, and, for
the Alleluia, music programmes and service listings, which the rule excludes
outright. That is a searched negative and it is bounded: the American newspaper
corpus is thin after 1922 and effectively ends about 1963, only
English-language American papers were searched, and its quoted-string search
degrades to an AND of terms for stopword-heavy phrases, so every count here is
an AND count and every negative is bounded to the ranked results actually
checked.

\begin{afterlife}{Communion and Gospel, Mt. 6:33 --- the political kingdom}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} \latin{Ant.\ ad Communionem}, marginal
no.~1580: \latin{Primum quǽrite regnum Dei, et ómnia adiciéntur vobis, dicit
Dóminus} --- and the same verse closes the appointed Gospel.

\textbf{Later text and loci.} Kwame Nkrumah's motto, ``Seek ye first the
political kingdom and all other things shall be added unto you.''
\work{The Spark} (Accra), no.~159, 5~Nov 1965, on the Ghana Co-operative
Movement. UK Hansard, HC 3~Dec 1962, col.~1036, Humphry Berkeley, reporting the
inscription on the statue outside the Parliament building in Accra; and HL
19~Dec 1962, col.~1203, Lord Milverton.

\textbf{Relationship.} \textbf{Documented verbal dependence, not echo.} The
motto preserves Matthew's imperative, its object-plus-addition structure and
the King James diction, substituting only ``political kingdom'' for ``kingdom
of God'' and dropping ``and his righteousness.''

\textbf{The turn.} Eschatological counsel against anxiety becomes a nationalist
priority-of-sequence argument: take state power first, development follows. The
redirection was contested in its own time --- Milverton treats the motto as a
doctrine that ``has caused a lot of trouble.'' The Mass's closing antiphon has
become, by 1962, a monumental inscription in Accra and a political doctrine
debated in both Houses at Westminster.

\textbf{Wording check.} Three attestations give \textbf{three variants}: ``all
other things shall be added unto you'' in 1965; ``all things will be added unto
you'' from both 1962 speakers \emph{independently}, which is itself evidence
that they were reporting a circulating inscription rather than copying one
another. \textbf{No attestation collated here reads the form most often quoted
secondhand.}

\textbf{Limiting qualification.} \textbf{None of the three sources is
Nkrumah's own first utterance.} \work{The Spark} reports what ``Osagyefo has
repeatedly said''; the two 1962 speakers report an inscription. The claim is
bounded to the motto's \textbf{circulation and reception}, not to its coinage;
Nkrumah's own text is in a lending-restricted item whose search-inside was
closed and could not be acquired. Rights: public-domain optical text of an
openly readable item, and the UK official report.

\textbf{Material negative, and it cuts both ways.} The phrase returns nothing
in the American newspaper corpus --- expected and uninformative, since that
corpus ends about 1963. And a false negative was caught here by luck: a search
of forty-seven \work{Spark} issues for ``political kingdom'' returned zero,
because the optical text broke it as \texttt{poli-}/\texttt{tical} across a
line end.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel, Mt. 6:24 --- the rule against divided loyalty}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} Gospel, marginal no.~1577: \latin{Nemo
potest duóbus dóminis servíre: aut enim unum ódio habébit, et álterum díliget:
aut unum sustinébit, et álterum contémnet. Non potéstis Deo servíre, et
mammónæ.}

\textbf{Later text and loci.} \work{Everhart v. Searle}, 71 Pa.\ 256 (Pa.\
1872): ``We have the authority of Holy Writ for saying that `no man can serve
two masters\dots'\,''. \work{Carter v. Harris}, 4 Rand.\ 199 (Va.\ 1826), the
earliest in the corpus. \work{United States v. Mississippi Valley Generating
Co.}, 364 U.S.\ 520 (1961): ``in the light of the authoritative declaration
that no man can serve two masters.'' \work{SEC v. Capital Gains Research
Bureau}, 375 U.S.\ 180 (1963), quoting the same sentence. \work{Knights of
Pythias v. Withers}, 177 U.S.\ 260 (1900). \work{NLRB v. Health Care \&
Retirement Corp.}, 511 U.S.\ 571 (1994).

\textbf{Relationship.} \textbf{Explicit and self-declared.}
\work{Everhart} names the source --- ``the authority of Holy Writ'' --- and
quotes the whole antithesis; the Supreme Court calls it ``the authoritative
declaration.''

\textbf{The turn.} Institutional and legal. In Matthew the sentence claims the
impossibility of divided religious allegiance and is urged as a reason not to
be anxious. In law it becomes a \textbf{prophylactic rule of positive
obligation that operates without proof of wrongdoing}: \work{Everhart} states
in terms that ``the rule is not intended to be remedial of actual wrong'' where
``no fraud [was] meditated and no injury done.'' \textbf{A saying about God and
money is made to disqualify a dual agent who has in fact behaved honestly},
and it has done that work for two centuries, up to and including the Supreme
Court.

\textbf{Wording check.} The courts quote the King James against the Vulgate's
\latin{aut unum ódio habébit \dots\ aut unum sustinébit, et álterum contémnet}.
\textbf{Every cited use stops before \latin{Non potéstis Deo servíre, et
mammónæ}: the courts take the general maxim and drop the naming of the second
master.}

\textbf{Limiting qualification.} The corpus is \textbf{overwhelmingly
American}; no English or Commonwealth sweep was performed, so nothing may be
stated about Anglo-American law as a whole beyond the United States. The corpus
reports 331 matching opinions, earliest 1826; that count is the database's and
was not independently audited. The \work{Everhart} passage was
\textbf{reassembled from four overlapping search snippets} rather than read
continuously, so its internal ellipses are this research's and the ordering of
the two fragments within the opinion was not established. Nineteenth-century
text is optical recognition of the printed reporters. Rights: United States
court opinions; no protected text.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Epistle, Gal. 5:23 --- ``against such there is no law,'' by pun}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} Epistle, marginal no.~1574: \latin{Advérsus
huiúsmodi non est lex.}

\textbf{Later text and loci.} \work{Johnston v. Commonwealth}, 22 Pa.\ 102 (Pa.\
1853), construing a Sunday-observance statute: ``works both of charity and
necessity. Certain it is, that against such there is no law, --- and they may
be performed on any day.'' Its companion case \work{Omit v. Commonwealth}, 21
Pa.\ 426 (1853), supplies the statutory language. \work{Sullens v. State}, 191
Miss.\ 856, 4 So.\ 2d 356 (1941), is a second and independent redeployment, of
conduct improper but not punishable.

\textbf{Relationship.} \textbf{Strong verbal dependence but undeclared.}
Neither court names Galatians. The clause is reproduced word for word in the
King James form and is not a phrase that arises independently, so this is
documented borrowing --- \textbf{to be described as unattributed borrowing and
not as a citation.}

\textbf{The turn, and it is a pun.} \textbf{The pivot is on the word ``law.''}
Paul's point is that the fruit of the Spirit stands outside the
\emph{condemning} reach of the Mosaic law; the court's is that works of charity
and necessity stand outside the \emph{prohibiting} reach of a Pennsylvania
penal statute against Sunday labour. Paul's subject is twelve virtues; the
court's is a statutory term of art. A theological claim about justification is
converted into a canon of statutory construction, and \work{Sullens} pushes it
further, to conduct improper yet legally uncompellable. It is the gallery's
only Epistle entry.

\textbf{Wording check.} King James Gal.\ 5:23b, ``against such there is no
law''; \work{Johnston} reproduces it exactly, prefixed by ``Certain it is,
that.'' The Vulgate as appointed is \latin{Advérsus huiúsmodi non est lex.}

\textbf{Limiting qualification.} \textbf{Whether the court intended the
scriptural allusion or was reaching for a stock formula is not established by
anything retrieved, and is not asserted.} The exact-phrase query returns
exactly two opinions in that corpus. The \work{Johnston} passage was
reassembled from three overlapping snippets, its internal ellipses this
research's. \textbf{No claim, positive or negative, is made about the clause's
presence in the American newspaper corpus}: a reliable phrase search of it
could not be run for this string, and the full-text service that would have
answered refused the request outright. Rights: United States court opinions.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel, Mt. 6:28--29 --- Solomon was not arrayed}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} Gospel, marginal no.~1577:
\latin{Consideráte lília agri quómodo crescunt: non labórant, neque nent. Dico
autem vobis, quóniam nec Sálomon in omni glória sua coopértus est sicut unum ex
istis.}

\textbf{Later text and loci.} \work{The Progressive Farmer}
(Winston/Raleigh, N.C.), 15~Jan 1889, p.~8, ``Solomon and his glory,''
reprinting the rural editor of \work{Farm, Field and Stockman}: the soprano,
then bass, then alto, then tenor each declare in turn that ``Solomon \dots\ was
not arrayed,'' until ``the feelings of the congregation had been harrowed up
sufficiently and our sympathies all aroused for poor Solomon, whose numerous
wives allowed him to go about in such a fashion even in that climate'' --- and
when ``like one of these'' finally arrives, ``by `these' one naturally
concluded that the choir was designed.'' \work{The Spanish American} (Roy,
N.M.), 11~Apr 1908, p.~9, ``A freeze out,'' credited to the \work{Illustrated
Sunday Magazine}: a singer breaks down at ``Solomon in all his glory was not
arrayed,'' and a voice from the freezing audience remarks, ``Bad fix for
Solomon on a night like this.''

\textbf{Relationship.} \textbf{Explicit;} both quote the clause, and the 1889
piece names it ``that scriptural poem.''

\textbf{The turn.} \textbf{Humorous, and in both cases the humour is produced
by the wording itself rather than attached to it.} The 1889 sketch is a joke
about syntax: a comparative clause severed from its second term turns a
statement about Solomon's inferiority to a wildflower into a statement that
Solomon was \emph{unclothed}, and the delayed ``these'' is then mis-referred to
the choir, so the appointed verse ends by praising the tailoring of a
fashionable congregation. The setting is the occasion and the appointed wording
is what is played with, which is what keeps it clear of the rule's exclusion of
a bare musical setting.

\textbf{Wording check.} Both turn on the King James clause, and both depend on
its being interrupted before ``like one of these.''

\textbf{Limiting qualification.} \textbf{Both items are reprints} --- the 1889
piece credits \work{Farm, Field and Stockman}, the 1908 piece the
\work{Illustrated Sunday Magazine} --- and \textbf{neither original was
retrieved, so the wording checked is the reprinting paper's.} Whether the two
jokes are independent or descend from a common source was not established:
\textbf{they are two attestations of a joke-type, not two independent
inventions.} \textbf{Punctuation and italics in the passages above are
reconstructed from damaged optical text and must not be presented as the
printed pointing.} Rights: public-domain American newspapers.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gradual, Ps. 117:9 --- princes, or railroads}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} Gradual, marginal no.~1575: \latin{Bonum est
confídere in Dómino, quam confídere in hómine. \Vsign\ Bonum est speráre in
Dómino, quam speráre in princípibus.}

\textbf{Later text and locus.} \work{Semi-weekly interior journal} (Stanford,
Ky.), 19~Nov 1895, p.~2, a travel letter datelined ``Atlanta, Nov.\ 16'': ``It
is better to serve the Lord than to put confidence in princes --- or railroads,
especially the latter, I thought when I got on the Exposition Flyer. My trials
were ended, but alas for human hopes!'' The engine then broke down, a flagman
walked two and a half miles for another, ``and from bad it went to worse till
the Flyer was a misnomer.''

\textbf{Relationship.} \textbf{Explicit verbal dependence, unattributed.} The
writer quotes the second half of the appointed Gradual verse almost intact and
then adds to it --- which only works if the reader recognises where the
sentence was going to stop.

\textbf{The turn.} \textbf{Humorous and idiomatic, achieved by a single
appended noun.} The psalm's antithesis is between divine and human
reliability, with ``princes'' standing for the highest human power; the letter
keeps the grammar and slips a railway company into the princes' place, so a
verse about the vanity of political trust becomes a joke about a train that
stopped at nearly every telegraph station. ``Especially the latter'' is what
makes it a redirection rather than a quotation: it ranks the railroad below
princes. It is the entry that broadens the gallery beyond the Gospel, the
Gradual being otherwise the least quoted element of this formulary in the
corpora swept.

\textbf{Wording check.} The optical text reads ``serve'' --- \textbf{the verb
is not the King James ``trust''}, and whether that is the writer's slip, a
compositor's or the scanner's could not be determined. \textbf{It must not be
silently corrected if the sentence is quoted.} The rest matches King James
Ps.\ 118:9 word for word.

\textbf{Limiting qualification.} An unremarkable local newspaper, not a
canonical text; the entry's value is the neatness of the redirection, not the
standing of the writer. \textbf{A byline appears in the optical text near the
dateline, but it was not established that it belongs to this letter rather than
to the masthead, and it is therefore not printed here as the author.} The
optical text is badly damaged in adjacent matter. Rights: public-domain
American newspaper.
\end{afterlife}
