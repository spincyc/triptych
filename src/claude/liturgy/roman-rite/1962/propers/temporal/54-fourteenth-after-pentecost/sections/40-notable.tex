\section*{The Propers: Notable and Quotable}

Five afterlives of wording from the scriptural propers, each a use that moves
the phrase into a register its psalmist or evangelist did not occupy.

\begin{afterlife}{Communion and Gospel, Mt. 6:33 --- the political kingdom}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} \latin{Ant.\ ad Communionem}, marginal
no.~1580: \latin{Primum quǽrite regnum Dei, et ómnia adiciéntur vobis, dicit
Dóminus} --- and the same verse closes the appointed Gospel.

\textbf{Later text and loci.} Kwame Nkrumah's motto, in the United Kingdom's
official parliamentary report. \textbf{House of Commons, 3~December 1962,
vol.~668}, in the Central Africa debate, Humphry Berkeley speaking from the
printed time-marker 8.1~p.m.: ``Outside the Parliament building in Accra there
is a statue of President Nkrumah the inscription on which is couched in Biblical
language, which some people find affronting but it is the mode of address of
many Africans. The inscription reads: `Seek ye first the political kingdom and
all things will be added unto you.' That is what the Africans want.''
\textbf{House of Lords, 19~December 1962, vol.~245}, on British policy in
Central Africa, Lord Milverton from 6.27~p.m., listing among the causes of the
Federation's failure ``the growing popularity of the doctrine which is
inscribed, as we all know, in a famous place in Accra, `Seek ye first the
political kingdom and all things will be added unto you'. Set against the
inevitably slow movement of real progress, naturally, that motto, if accepted
fully, has caused a lot of trouble.'' \work{The Spark} (Accra), no.~159,
5~November 1965, reports the same maxim, in the form ``all other things shall be
added unto you,'' as what ``Osagyefo has repeatedly said.''

The motto is verbal dependence and not echo: it preserves Matthew's imperative,
its object-plus-addition structure and the King James diction, substituting only
``political kingdom'' for ``kingdom of God'' and dropping ``and his
righteousness,'' and it keeps the word \emph{first}, which the Lucan parallel
does not carry and which is what fixes the locus as Matthew's.
\textbf{Neither 1962 speaker reads ``all these things shall be added,''} which
is the King James's own wording and the form usually quoted secondhand: both
give ``all things will be added unto you,'' and they were in different Houses
sixteen days apart and describe the source differently --- Berkeley a statue
inscription, Milverton ``a famous place'' --- so their agreement is evidence
that each was reporting a circulating inscription and not copying the other.

\textbf{The turn.} Eschatological counsel against anxiety about food and
clothing becomes a nationalist priority-of-sequence argument: take state power
first and development follows. The redirection was contested in its own time ---
Berkeley reports the inscription approvingly, Milverton lists it among the
causes of the Federation's collapse --- and Berkeley's aside that the biblical
idiom is what ``some people find affronting'' registers the appropriation as it
was felt. The Mass's closing antiphon stands, by 1962, as a monumental
inscription in Accra and a political doctrine debated in both Houses at
Westminster. What these witnesses attest is the maxim's circulation and
reception in December 1962 and not its coinage, which none of them dates; its
wording rests on Berkeley's report of it. That the antiphon drops \latin{et iustítiam eius} where the motto drops
``and his righteousness'' is a coincidence.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel, Mt. 6:24 --- the rule against divided loyalty}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} Gospel, marginal no.~1577: \latin{Nemo
potest duóbus dóminis servíre: aut enim unum ódio habébit, et álterum díliget:
aut unum sustinébit, et álterum contémnet. Non potéstis Deo servíre, et
mammónæ.}

\textbf{Later text and loci.} \work{Everhart v. Searle}, 71 Pa.\ 256, 259
(Supreme Court of Pennsylvania, 13~May 1872), opening: ``We have the authority
of Holy Writ for saying that `no man can serve two masters; for either he will
hate the one and love the other, or else he will hold to the one and despise the
other.' All human experience sanctions the undoubted truth and purity of this
philosophy, and it is received as a cardinal principle in every system of
enlightened jurisprudence.'' \work{United States v. Mississippi Valley
Generating Co.}, 364 U.S.\ 520, 549 (9~January 1961), grounding 18 U.S.C.\
\S434: ``The moral principle upon which the statute is based has its foundation
in the Biblical admonition that no man may serve two masters, Matt.\ 6:24, a
maxim which is especially pertinent if one of the masters happens to be economic
self-interest.'' \work{Chapman v. Klemick}, 3 F.3d 1508, 1512 (11th Cir.,
12~October 1993), closing an ERISA appeal: ``As one widely-cited authority has
said: No man can serve two masters: for either he will hate the one, and love
the other; or else he will hold to the one, and despise the other. Ye cannot
serve God and mammon. Matthew 6:24 (King James). A trust fund is not exactly
`mammon,' but an attorney's duty of loyalty to his client is very nearly
sacred.''

The dependence is explicit and self-declared in all three: \work{Everhart} names
``the authority of Holy Writ,'' \work{Mississippi Valley} prints the Matthaean
locus, and \work{Chapman} block-quotes the verse whole and gives the citation.
\textbf{Identity is the governing risk:} the King James antithesis and the
mammon clause stand word for word at Lk.\ 16:13, so only an express Matthaean
locus secures a candidate --- which \work{Mississippi Valley} and \work{Chapman}
supply and \work{Everhart} does not, resting on ``Holy Writ'' and the Matthaean
form of the antithesis alone. All three quote the King James against the
appointed Vulgate, and their ``or else he will hold to the one, and despise the
other'' renders \latin{aut unum sustinébit, et álterum contémnet}, where the
Douay reads ``or he will sustain the one, and despise the other.''

\textbf{The turn.} Institutional and legal. In Matthew the sentence claims the
impossibility of divided religious allegiance and is urged as a reason \emph{not
to be anxious}. In law it becomes a \textbf{prophylactic rule of positive
obligation that operates without proof of wrongdoing}: the rule ``is not
intended to be remedial of actual wrong, but preventive of the possibility of
it'' where ``no fraud [was] meditated and no injury done.'' \textbf{A saying
about God and money is made to disqualify a dual agent who has in fact behaved
honestly}, and it does that work from \work{Everhart} in 1872 to the Supreme
Court in 1961. \textbf{\work{Chapman} completes the turn as a joke in two
moves}: an Evangelist introduced with the formula an appellate court reserves
for a leading treatise, with a King James parenthetical where a court would name
an edition; and then the concession that a trust fund is ``not exactly
`mammon,''' which treats a term of eschatological absolutes as though it
admitted of degree, before the register recovers with ``very nearly sacred.''

Two attributions inside those opinions belong to other mouths. The ``not
intended to be remedial of actual wrong'' sentence is \work{Everhart}, at 260,
\emph{quoting Hare and Wallace's Notes to} 1 \work{Leading Cases in Equity},
p.~210, not the court speaking in its own voice; and ``the authoritative
declaration'' in \work{Mississippi Valley}'s footnote is the \emph{Court of
Claims'} phrase in \work{Michigan Steel Box Co.\ v.\ United States}, 49 Ct.\ Cl.\
421, 439, quoted by the Supreme Court and not the Supreme Court's own.

\textbf{Two lines of use run side by side and are not the same line.} The
conflict-of-interest line from \work{Everhart} to \work{Mississippi Valley}
stops at the general maxim and leaves the second master unnamed; a separate body
of state, bankruptcy and fiduciary decisions quotes the mammon clause and does
name Matthew. \work{Chapman}'s wit is one judge's, and nothing shows a
line, a tradition or a spread of the joke.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Epistle, Gal. 5:23 --- ``against such there is no law,'' by pun}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} Epistle, marginal no.~1574: \latin{Advérsus
huiúsmodi non est lex.}

\textbf{Later text and loci.} \work{Johnston v. The Commonwealth}, 22 Pa.\ 102,
\textbf{108} (1853), construing the Sunday-observance act of 1794 and its
exception for works of charity and necessity: ``perhaps all would agree, that
visiting and administering to the sick and destitute, and labors for the
spiritual welfare of men, are works both of charity and necessity. Certain it
is, that against such there is no law, and they may be performed on any day.''
\work{Sullens v. State}, 191 Miss.\
856, \textbf{872}, 4 So.\ 2d 356 (10~November 1941), on the constructive
contempt of a newspaper editor: ``When one functioning within the circle of free
speech runs out thoughtlessly to the full length of his tether he is apt to be
brought up abruptly to his discomfiture, to find himself entangled amid the
brambles of bad taste which fringe the border lines of discretion. Against such
there is no law. Where the courts can not compel they can not condemn.''

Neither court names Galatians, and both reproduce the clause word for word in
the King James form --- ``against such there is no law,'' where the Vulgate as
appointed reads \latin{Advérsus huiúsmodi non est lex} --- which is unattributed
borrowing and not citation, and whether either court intended the allusion is
not established. \textbf{The identity is clean:} that English clause stands at
Gal.\ 5:23 and nowhere else in the King James or the Douay.

\textbf{The turn, and it is a pun.} \textbf{The pivot is on the word ``law.''}
Paul's point is that the twelve fruits of the Spirit stand outside the
\emph{condemning} reach of the Mosaic law; \work{Johnston}'s is that works of
charity and necessity stand outside the \emph{prohibiting} reach of a
Pennsylvania penal statute against Sunday labour; \work{Sullens} pushes it
further, to conduct improper yet legally uncompellable. A theological claim
about justification is converted into a canon of statutory construction. Two
opinions eighty-eight years and two states apart are two attestations of a usage
and not a line of descent, and \work{Sullens} shows no sign of having read
\work{Johnston}.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gradual, Ps. 117:9 --- princes, or railroads}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} Gradual, marginal no.~1575: \latin{Bonum est
confidere in Dómino, quam confidere in hómine. \Vsign\ Bonum est speráre in
Dómino, quam speráre in princípibus.}

\textbf{Later text and locus.} \work{Semi-weekly interior journal} (Stanford,
Ky.), 19~November 1895, p.~2, cols.~3--4, in an unsigned travel letter under the
heading ``The Atlanta Exposition / And Other Incongruvial Notes Picked Up There
and Thereabouts'' and datelined ``ATLANTA, Nov.\ 16'': ``It is better to serve
the Lord than to put confidence in princes or railroads, especially the latter.
I thought when I got on the `Exposition Flyer,' my trials were ended, but alas
for human hopes. The engine broke down midway between stations five miles apart
and refused to turn a wheel.''

The writer quotes the second half of the appointed Gradual verse nearly intact,
unattributed, and then adds to it --- which only works if the reader recognises
where the sentence was going to stop.

\textbf{The turn.} \textbf{Humorous and idiomatic, achieved by a single
appended noun.} The psalm's antithesis is between divine and human
reliability, with ``princes'' standing for the highest human power; the letter
keeps the grammar and slips a railway company into the princes' place, so a
verse about the vanity of political trust becomes a traveller's grumble about
the Exposition Flyer. ``Especially the latter'' is what makes it a redirection
rather than a quotation: it ranks the railroad below princes.

\textbf{The letter's verb is ``serve'' where the King James reads ``trust,''}
and it stands so on the printed page, the writer's or the compositor's
substitution. \textbf{There is no dash
before ``or railroads''} --- the line breaks inside ``prin-ces'' and runs
straight on --- and the sentence ends at ``especially the latter.'' The rest
matches King James Ps.\ 118:9 word for word, and the identity is clean, the
near-twin ``Put not your trust in princes'' being another psalm and reading
differently. The paper is an unremarkable local one and the writer is unnamed.
``Incongruvial'' is as the paper set it.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel, Mt. 6:28--29 --- the lilies' valuation inverted}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} Gospel, marginal no.~1577:
\latin{Consideráte lília agri quómodo crescunt: non labórant, neque nent. Dico
autem vobis, quóniam nec Sálomon in omni glória sua coopértus est sicut unum ex
istis.}

\textbf{Later text and loci.} \work{The Ranch} (Seattle, Wash.), 1~November
1910, p.~3, an unsigned editorial paragraph on the paper's masthead page: ``The
farmer is not lying awake nights with worry because Wall street is so badly in
the dumps. That bunch of gamblers who toil not neither do they spin, yet
`Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these,' has hoisted the
banner of distress and sees no ray of hope.'' \work{State v. Tracy}, 294 Mo.\
372, 390 (Supreme Court of Missouri, 8~June 1922), on a prosecutor's opening in
a whiskey-warehouse burglary, where the defendant ``neither toiled nor did he
spin'' and ``The assistant prosecuting attorney correctly quoted the Master's
words, `They toil not, neither do they spin.'\,'' \work{State v. Whiteaker}, 118
Or.\ 656, 661, 247 Pac.\ 1077 (Supreme Court of Oregon, decided 20~July 1926),
declining to lay down a test under the Blue Sky Law, because ``a certain class
of gentlemen of the `J. Rufus Wallingford' type --- `they toil not neither do
they spin' --- would lie awake nights endeavoring to conceive some devious and
shadowy way of evading the law.'' \work{State ex rel.\ Garrett v.\ McPeters},
256 Ala.\ 555, 556, 56 So.\ 2d 102 (Supreme Court of Alabama, 13~November 1951),
Brown~J.\ \textbf{dissenting}: ``This evidence presents a typical victim for the
jackals of greed and corruption who `toil not neither do they spin,' (Matthew
6:~28) except at the handle of a `one armed bandit,' and feed upon the take.''

\textbf{The dependence is documented in all four, and \work{McPeters} prints the
Matthaean locus expressly}; \work{Tracy} does more than borrow the clause, it
\emph{certifies} the borrowing, holding that the prosecutor ``correctly quoted
the Master's words.'' \textbf{The identity is secure on one word:} Matthew reads
``they toil not, \emph{neither do} they spin'' where Lk.\ 12:27 reads ``they
toil not, they spin not,'' and all four carry ``neither''; the Solomon clause
alone stands word for word at Lk.\ 12:27 and would not discriminate. All four
give the King James, where the Douay reads ``they labour not,
neither do they spin,'' and the 1910 paper's ``like one of these'' is the King
James against the Douay's ``as one of these.''

\textbf{The turn.} \textbf{A complete reversal of valuation.} In Matthew, not
toiling is the point in the lilies' \emph{favour} --- it is what shows the
Father's provision. Here it is the charge, and the men who toil not are
speculators, burglars, blue-sky promoters and ``jackals of greed and
corruption.'' \textbf{The 1910 paper sets the farmer, who does toil, against the
speculator, who does not, and hands Matthew's compliment to the wrong party on
purpose}; and \textbf{\work{McPeters} sharpens it by appending an exception that
destroys the figure} --- they toil not ``except at the handle of a `one armed
bandit.'\,'' The 1910 paper's own punctuation shows how far the borrowing had
travelled: it puts quotation marks round the \emph{Solomon} clause only and
leaves ``who toil not neither do they spin'' unmarked inside its own sentence,
so the first half was already idiom to that writer while the second was still
felt as quotation.

\textbf{Three independent stems, and no descent between them.} \work{Tracy}
precedes \work{Whiteaker} and cannot descend from it; \work{McPeters} cites
Matthew directly and carries no Wallingford; the Oregon line quoting
\work{Whiteaker} forward is a fourth and derivative stem. The 1910 paragraph is
earlier than all three and shows no sign of their wording beyond the verse
itself, \textbf{so what it establishes is that the inversion was available in
the popular press before it reached the reports, and not that the courts took it
from there.} \textbf{\work{McPeters} is a dissent and not the judgment}: the per
curiam majority ousted the sheriff, and Brown~J.'s figure casts the convicted
sheriff as the victim. The 1910 paragraph is unsigned, and the court supplies
no gloss on who or what the ``J. Rufus Wallingford'' of \work{Whiteaker} was.
\end{afterlife}
