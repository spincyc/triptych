\section*{The Propers: Notable and Quotable}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel --- Douglass makes divided service an abolitionist test}
\textbf{Appointed phrase.} ``You cannot serve God and mammon'' (Mt.~6:24).

\textbf{Later work and locus.} Frederick Douglass, ``Baptists,
Congregationalists, the Free Church, and Slavery,'' Belfast, 23~December 1845,
paragraph~7 in the \work{Frederick Douglass Papers} transcript.

\textbf{Turn in meaning.} Douglass quotes the dominical clause within a chain
of paired contraries and extends divided service to church fellowship with
slaveholders: justice stands against injustice and liberty against slavery.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel --- Butler lets the lilies answer Solomon}
\textbf{Appointed text.} Matthew~6:28--29, the lilies that neither labor nor
spin and Solomon in his glory.

\textbf{Later work and locus.} Samuel Butler, \work{Erewhon}, revised and
enlarged edition, ch.~XXVII, ``The Views of an Erewhonian Philosopher
Concerning the Rights of Vegetables,'' Project Gutenberg eBook~1906.

\textbf{Turn in meaning.} Butler's intelligent lilies dispute their supposed
passivity and make splendidly dressed human ``Solomons'' the idle creatures.
The natural example becomes a comic quasi-scientific counterargument through
direct allusion and syntactic reversal.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlifewhole}{Gospel --- Bosch makes divided service spatial}
\textbf{Appointed phrase.} ``serve God and mammon'' (Mt.~6:24).

\textbf{Later work and locus.} Hieronymus Bosch, \work{Death and the Miser},
c.~1485/1490, National Gallery of Art accession 1952.5.33, with the Gallery's
object record and essay.

\textbf{Turn in meaning.} The museum relates the panel to the God-and-mammon
choice: an angel and crucifix draw the dying miser upward while money and
demons pull elsewhere. Sequential verbal alternatives become simultaneous
claims on body and sight. The middle-ground figure's identity and the panel's
original setting remain uncertain.
\end{afterlifewhole}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel --- the Supreme Court turns two masters into public duty}
\textbf{Appointed phrases.} ``No man can serve two masters'' and ``You cannot
serve God and mammon'' (Mt.~6:24).

\textbf{Later work and locus.} \work{United States v. Mississippi Valley
Generating Co.}, 364~U.S.~520, 548--550, especially 549 (1961), opinion of the
Court.

\textbf{Turn in meaning.} The Court cites Matthew~6:24 while identifying the
two masters as a government agent's public duty and economic self-interest.
Divided service becomes the rationale for an objective conflict-of-interest
rule that does not depend on proof of corruption or financial loss.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Offertory --- Levertov carries tasting into the subway and the world}
\textbf{Appointed phrase.} ``O taste, and see'' (Ps.~33:9).

\textbf{Later work and locus.} Denise Levertov, ``O Taste and See,'' in
\work{O Taste and See: New Poems} (New Directions, 1964), reprinted in
\work{Poems 1960--1967}, p.~125; the subway-poster context is discussed by
Albert Gelpi, \work{American Poetry after Modernism}, pp.~177--178.

\textbf{Turn in meaning.} A Bible phrase encountered on a subway poster opens
into sensuous and mortal experience: grief, mercy, language, weather, fruit,
death, and ordinary movement through the city. Advertising space becomes a
threshold for receiving concrete life.
\end{afterlife}
