\section*{The Propers: Notable and Quotable}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel --- Bosch makes divided service spatial}
\textbf{Appointed phrase.} ``You cannot serve God and mammon'' (Mt.~6:24).

\textbf{Later work and locus.} Hieronymus Bosch, \work{Death and the Miser},
National Gallery of Art accession 1952.5.33, with the Gallery's object essay.

\textbf{Turn in meaning.} The museum relates the panel to the God-and-mammon
choice: an angel and crucifix draw the dying miser toward one end, while a
demon and money press the other. A sentence about incompatible service becomes
simultaneous spatial claims on the gaze and will. The work's coin-and-rosary
figure is only possibly an earlier image of the miser, and no statement by
Bosch establishes dependence on this proper. The Gallery identifies the image
media as public domain.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel --- Bradbury opposes the lilies to advertising}
\textbf{Appointed phrase.} ``Consider the lilies of the field'' (Mt.~6:28).

\textbf{Later work and locus.} Ray Bradbury, \work{Fahrenheit 451}, part~2,
``The Sieve and the Sand,'' p.~78 in the Simon \& Schuster 50th Anniversary
edition.

\textbf{Turn in meaning.} Montag tries to hold the verse in memory while a
Dentifrice advertisement repeatedly displaces it. Providential attention
becomes resistance to commercial distraction. Bradbury's continuation follows
King James diction rather than the appointed Douay wording; only the short
inherited phrase is reproduced here, and the protected scene is paraphrased.
No dependence on the 1962 formulary is claimed.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel --- Butler reverses lilies and Solomon}
\textbf{Appointed text.} Matthew~6:28--29, the lilies that neither labor nor
spin and Solomon in his glory.

\textbf{Later work and locus.} Samuel Butler, \work{Erewhon}, revised and
enlarged edition, ch.~XXVII, ``The Views of an Erewhonian Philosopher
Concerning the Rights of Vegetables.''

\textbf{Turn in meaning.} Butler's mock argument about plant labor and
vegetable rights reassigns the verse's predicates: the ``Solomons'' become the
ones that neither toil nor spin. The natural example is made to testify against
its original premise through unmistakable quotation and syntactic reversal.
The diction is King James rather than Douay, and the passage has not been
assigned to Butler's first edition.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Epistle --- courts turn Paul's ``law'' into jurisprudence}
\textbf{Appointed phrase.} ``Against such there is no law'' (Gal.~5:23).

\textbf{Later works and loci.} \work{Johnston v. Commonwealth}, 22~Pa.~102,
108 (Pa. 1853); \work{Sullens v. State}, 191~Miss.~856, 872, 4~So.~2d~356
(Miss. 1941).

\textbf{Turn in meaning.} Each opinion reproduces the six words but gives
``such'' a juridical antecedent. \work{Johnston} applies them to charitable and
necessary Sunday activity while affirming a driver's conviction;
\work{Sullens} distinguishes punishable obstruction from offensive journalism
and reverses a contempt judgment. Neither opinion supplies a biblical citation,
so the links are strong verbal echoes, not documented dependence on a
particular translation or on the Roman proper.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gradual --- a railroad joins the unreliable princes}
\textbf{Appointed text.} Psalm~117:8--9, confidence in the Lord rather than in
man or princes.

\textbf{Later work and locus.} W.~P.~W., ``The Atlanta Exposition: And Other
Incongruvial Notes,'' \work{Semi-Weekly Interior Journal} (Stanford,
Kentucky), 19~November 1895, p.~2, datelined 16~November.

\textbf{Turn in meaning.} The travel letter adds railroads to the psalm's
unreliable powers just before the Atlanta ``Flyer'' repeatedly breaks down.
The hierarchy of trust becomes a deadpan review of industrial infrastructure.
The wording is a strong multi-word adaptation rather than a copy of the Douay
form, and the writer is attributed only by the initials printed with the
piece.
\end{afterlife}
