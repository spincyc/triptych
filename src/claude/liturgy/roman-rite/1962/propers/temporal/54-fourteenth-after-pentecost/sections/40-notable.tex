\section*{The Propers: Notable and Quotable}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel --- Douglass makes divided service an abolitionist test}
\textbf{Appointed phrase.} ``You cannot serve God and Mammon'' (Mt.~6:24).

\textbf{Later work and locus.} Frederick Douglass, ``Baptists,
Congregationalists, the Free Church, and Slavery,'' Belfast, 23~December 1845,
paragraph~7 in the \work{Douglass Papers} transcript.

\textbf{Turn in meaning.} Douglass quotes the dominical clause within a chain
of paired contraries and extends the test of divided service to church
fellowship with slaveholders: justice stands against injustice and liberty
against slavery. His appeal is explicitly to Matthew, but the speech connects
it neither to this proper nor to one historical English translation.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel --- Bradbury opposes the lilies to advertising}
\textbf{Appointed phrase.} ``Consider the lilies of the field'' (Mt.~6:28).

\textbf{Later work and locus.} Ray Bradbury, \work{Fahrenheit 451}, part~2,
``The Sieve and the Sand,'' p.~78 in the Simon \& Schuster 50th Anniversary
edition.

\textbf{Turn in meaning.} Montag tries to hold the verse in memory while a
Dentifrice advertisement repeatedly displaces it. Providential attention
becomes resistance to commercial distraction. Bradbury's continuation follows
King James diction rather than the appointed Douay wording, and the novel
supplies no link between the quotation and the 1962 formulary.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel --- Butler reverses lilies and Solomon}
\textbf{Appointed text.} Matthew~6:28--29, the lilies that neither labor nor
spin and Solomon in his glory.

\textbf{Later work and locus.} Samuel Butler, \work{Erewhon}, revised and
enlarged edition, ch.~XXVII, ``The Views of an Erewhonian Philosopher
Concerning the Rights of Vegetables.''

\textbf{Turn in meaning.} Butler's mock argument about plant labor and
vegetable rights reassigns the verse's predicates: the ``Solomons'' become the
ones that neither toil nor spin. The natural example is made to testify against
its original premise through unmistakable quotation and syntactic reversal.
The diction is King James rather than Douay; the identified locus is the
revised edition, not Butler's 1872 first edition.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Epistle --- a court turns Paul's ``law'' into jurisprudence}
\textbf{Appointed phrase.} ``Against such there is no law'' (Gal.~5:23).

\textbf{Later work and locus.} \work{Johnston v. Commonwealth}, 22~Pa.~102,
108--109 (Supreme Court of Pennsylvania, 1853).

\textbf{Turn in meaning.} The opinion gives ``such'' a juridical antecedent,
applying the phrase to charitable and necessary Sunday activity before holding
that the driver's ordinary paid employment did not qualify. Spiritual fruit is
made to sound like a proposition about positive law's reach. Because the
opinion cites no biblical source, the six words remain a strong exact echo
rather than documented dependence on Galatians, a particular translation, or
the Roman proper.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel --- Bosch makes divided service spatial}
\textbf{Appointed phrase.} ``serve God and mammon'' (Mt.~6:24).

\textbf{Later work and locus.} Hieronymus Bosch, \work{Death and the Miser},
c.~1485/1490, National Gallery of Art accession 1952.5.33, with the Gallery's
object record and essay.

\textbf{Turn in meaning.} The museum relates the panel to the God-and-mammon
choice: an angel and crucifix draw the dying miser toward one end, while a
demon and money press the other. Sequential verbal alternatives become
simultaneous bodily and spatial claims. The Gallery presents the God-and-mammon
relation, while no statement by Bosch connects the panel to Matthew, a
translation, or the proper. Its account leaves the middle-ground figure's
identity and the panel's original setting uncertain.
\end{afterlife}
