\section*{The Propers: Notable and Quotable}

Five afterlives of wording from the scriptural propers, each a use that moves
the phrase into a register its psalmist or evangelist did not occupy.

\begin{afterlife}{Communion and Gospel, Mt. 6:33 --- the political kingdom}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} \latin{Ant.\ ad Communionem}, marginal
no.~1580: \latin{Primum quǽrite regnum Dei, et ómnia adiciéntur vobis, dicit
Dóminus} --- and the same verse closes the appointed Gospel.

\textbf{Later text and loci.} Kwame Nkrumah's motto, ``Seek ye first the
political kingdom and all other things shall be added unto you.'' UK Hansard,
HC 3~Dec 1962, debate ``Central Africa,'' Humphry Berkeley, reporting the
inscription on the statue outside the Parliament building in Accra; and HL
19~Dec 1962, col.~1203, Lord Milverton, calling it ``a famous place in
Accra.'' \work{The Spark} (Accra), no.~159, 5~Nov 1965, on the Ghana
Co-operative Movement.

The motto is verbal dependence and not echo: it preserves Matthew's imperative,
its object-plus-addition structure and the King James diction, substituting only
``political kingdom'' for ``kingdom of God'' and dropping ``and his
righteousness,'' and it keeps the word \emph{first}, which the Lucan parallel
does not carry. \textbf{None of the three attestations reads ``all these things
shall be added,''} which is the King James's own wording and the form most often
quoted secondhand: the two 1962 speakers, in different Houses sixteen days
apart, both give ``all things will be added unto you,'' and \work{The Spark}
gives ``all other things shall be added unto you.''

\textbf{The turn.} Eschatological counsel against anxiety becomes a nationalist
priority-of-sequence argument: take state power first, development follows. The
redirection was contested in its own time --- Berkeley reports the inscription
approvingly, Milverton lists it among the causes of the Federation's failure.
The Mass's closing antiphon has become, by 1962, a monumental inscription in
Accra and a political doctrine debated in both Houses at Westminster. \work{The
Spark} reports what ``Osagyefo has repeatedly said'' and the two 1962 speakers
report an inscription, so what these three witness is the motto's circulation
and not its coinage, which none of them dates. That the antiphon drops
\latin{et iustítiam eius} where the motto drops ``and his righteousness'' is a
coincidence.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel, Mt. 6:24 --- the rule against divided loyalty}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} Gospel, marginal no.~1577: \latin{Nemo
potest duóbus dóminis servíre: aut enim unum ódio habébit, et álterum díliget:
aut unum sustinébit, et álterum contémnet. Non potéstis Deo servíre, et
mammónæ.}

\textbf{Later text and loci.} \work{Everhart v. Searle}, 71 Pa.\ 256, 258
(1872), which opens on ``the authority of Holy Writ'' and quotes the whole
antithesis; \work{United States v. Mississippi Valley Generating Co.}, 364 U.S.\
520, 549--550 (1961), grounding 18 U.S.C.\ §434 in ``the Biblical admonition
that no man may serve two masters, Matt.\ 6:24, a maxim which is especially
pertinent if one of the masters happens to be economic self-interest.'' In the
same line: \work{Knights of Pythias v. Withers}, 177 U.S.\ 260 (1900);
\work{SEC v. Capital Gains Research Bureau}, 375 U.S.\ 180 (1963); \work{NLRB v.
Health Care \& Retirement Corp.}, 511 U.S.\ 571 (1994).

The dependence is explicit and self-declared: \work{Everhart} names the source,
``the authority of Holy Writ,'' and \work{Mississippi Valley} prints the
Matthaean locus itself. The courts quote the King James against the Vulgate's
\latin{aut unum ódio habébit \dots\ aut unum sustinébit, et álterum contémnet.}

\textbf{The turn.} Institutional and legal. In Matthew the sentence claims the
impossibility of divided religious allegiance and is urged as a reason not to
be anxious. In law it becomes a \textbf{prophylactic rule of positive
obligation that operates without proof of wrongdoing}: the rule ``is not
intended to be remedial of actual wrong, but preventive of the possibility of
it'' where ``no fraud [was] meditated and no injury done.'' \textbf{A saying
about God and money is made to disqualify a dual agent who has in fact behaved
honestly}, and it does that work from \work{Everhart} in 1872 to the Supreme
Court in 1961.

Two attributions inside those opinions belong to other mouths. The ``not
intended to be remedial of actual wrong'' sentence is \work{Everhart}
\emph{quoting Hare and Wallace}, not the court speaking in its own voice; and
``the authoritative declaration'' is the \emph{Court of Claims'} phrase, quoted
by the Supreme Court, and not the Supreme Court's own.

\textbf{Two lines of use run side by side and are not the same line.} The
conflict-of-interest line from \work{Everhart} to \work{Mississippi Valley}
stops at the general maxim; a separate and larger body of state and bankruptcy
decisions --- at least eighteen opinions, at least six of them printing the
Matthaean citation expressly --- quotes the mammon clause and names Matthew, so
the second master is not left unnamed in American law. \textbf{Identity is
delicate throughout:} the King James antithesis and the mammon clause are
word-identical at Lk.\ 16:13, so only an express Matthaean locus secures a
candidate, and at least one opinion prints ``Luke 16, 13'' instead.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Epistle, Gal. 5:23 --- ``against such there is no law,'' by pun}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} Epistle, marginal no.~1574: \latin{Advérsus
huiúsmodi non est lex.}

\textbf{Later text and loci.} \work{Johnston v. Commonwealth}, 22 Pa.\ 102, 111
(1853), construing a Sunday-observance act: ``visiting and administering to the
sick and destitute \dots\ Certain it is, that against such there is no law, and
they may be performed on any day.'' Its companion case \work{Omit v.
Commonwealth}, 21 Pa.\ 426 (1853), supplies the statutory language.
\work{Sullens v. State}, 191 Miss.\ 856 (1941), on the criminal contempt of a
newspaper editor: ``the brambles of bad taste which fringe the border lines of
discretion. Against such there is no law.''

Neither court names Galatians, and both reproduce the clause word for word in
the King James form --- ``against such there is no law,'' where the Vulgate as
appointed reads \latin{Advérsus huiúsmodi non est lex} --- which is unattributed
borrowing and not citation.

\textbf{The turn, and it is a pun.} \textbf{The pivot is on the word ``law.''}
Paul's point is that the twelve fruits of the Spirit stand outside the
\emph{condemning} reach of the Mosaic law; \work{Johnston}'s is that works of
charity and necessity stand outside the \emph{prohibiting} reach of a
Pennsylvania penal statute against Sunday labour; \work{Sullens} pushes it
further, to conduct improper yet legally uncompellable. A theological claim
about justification is converted into a canon of statutory construction. It is
the gallery's only Epistle entry, and the two opinions are the only two the
American case-law corpus carries.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel, Mt. 6:28--29 --- Solomon was not arrayed}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} Gospel, marginal no.~1577:
\latin{Consideráte lília agri quómodo crescunt: non labórant, neque nent. Dico
autem vobis, quóniam nec Sálomon in omni glória sua coopértus est sicut unum ex
istis.}

\textbf{Later text and loci.} \work{The Progressive Farmer}
(Winston/Raleigh, N.C.), 15~Jan 1889, p.~8, ``Solomon and his glory,''
reprinting the rural editor of \work{Farm, Field and Stockman}: the soprano,
then bass, then alto, then tenor each declare in turn that ``Solomon \dots\ was
not arrayed,'' until ``the feelings of the congregation had been harrowed up
sufficiently and our sympathies all aroused for poor Solomon, whose numerous
wives allowed him to go about in such a fashion even in that climate'' --- and
when ``like one of these'' finally arrives, ``by `these' one naturally
concluded that the choir was designed.'' \textbf{The joke does not end there:}
it goes on to ask whether Solomon wore ``a Prince Albert or cutaway coat \dots\
an eye-glass and mustache, his hair cut Pompadour,'' and answers ``No, most
decidedly.'' \work{The Spanish American} (Roy, N.M.), 11~Apr 1908, p.~9, ``A
freeze out,'' credited to the \work{Illustrated Sunday Magazine}: at Hayville a
singer breaks down at ``Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed,'' and a voice
from the freezing audience remarks, ``Bad fix for Solomon on a night like
this.''

Both quote the clause explicitly, the 1889 piece naming it ``that scriptural
poem,'' and both turn on the King James wording and on its being interrupted
before ``like one of these.'' The 1889 piece's ``the lilies of the field'' is
Matthew's phrase and not Luke's, which is what fixes the identity; the clause
itself is word-identical at Lk.\ 12:27, so the 1908 item is identity-neutral
standing alone.

\textbf{The turn.} \textbf{Humorous, and in both cases the humour is produced
by the wording itself rather than attached to it.} The 1889 sketch is a joke
about syntax: a comparative clause severed from its second term turns a
statement about Solomon's inferiority to a wildflower into a statement that
Solomon was \emph{unclothed}, the delayed ``these'' is then mis-referred to the
choir, and \textbf{the appointed verse finishes as a satire on the tailoring of
an aesthetic congregation.} In 1908 the printed em-dashes inside the singer's
quotation are the typographic mark of the interruption the joke turns on. The
setting is the occasion and the appointed wording is what is played with.

\textbf{Both items are reprints} --- the 1889 piece credits \work{Farm, Field
and Stockman} and the 1908 piece the \work{Illustrated Sunday Magazine}, and the
wording quoted above is the reprinting paper's and not either original's ---
and they are two attestations of one joke-type rather than two independent
inventions.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gradual, Ps. 117:9 --- princes, or railroads}
\textbf{Appointed text and locus.} Gradual, marginal no.~1575: \latin{Bonum est
confídere in Dómino, quam confídere in hómine. \Vsign\ Bonum est speráre in
Dómino, quam speráre in princípibus.}

\textbf{Later text and locus.} \work{Semi-weekly interior journal} (Stanford,
Ky.), 19~Nov 1895, p.~2, a travel letter datelined ``Atlanta, Nov.\ 16'': ``It
is better to serve the Lord than to put confidence in princes or railroads,
especially the latter. I thought when I got on the `Exposition Flyer,' my
trials were ended, but alas for human hopes.'' The engine then broke down
midway between stations five miles apart and refused to turn a wheel, a
flagman had to go two and a half miles afoot for another, and the writer
reached Chattanooga two and a quarter hours late.

The writer quotes the second half of the appointed Gradual verse almost intact,
unattributed, and then adds to it --- which only works if the reader recognises
where the sentence was going to stop.

\textbf{The turn.} \textbf{Humorous and idiomatic, achieved by a single
appended noun.} The psalm's antithesis is between divine and human
reliability, with ``princes'' standing for the highest human power; the letter
keeps the grammar and slips a railway company into the princes' place, so a
verse about the vanity of political trust becomes a traveller's grumble about
the Exposition Flyer. ``Especially the latter'' is what makes it a redirection
rather than a quotation: it ranks the railroad below princes. In the American
case-law reports and the American English-language newspapers the Gradual is
the least quoted element of this formulary.

\textbf{The letter's verb is ``serve'' where the King James reads ``trust,''}
and it stands so on the printed page, the writer's or the compositor's
substitution rather than a fault of transcription. The rest matches King James
Ps.\ 118:9 word for word, and the identity is clean, the near-twin ``Put not
your trust in princes'' being another psalm and reading differently. The paper
is an unremarkable local one and the writer is unnamed: the entry's value is the
neatness of the redirection, not the standing of its author.
\end{afterlife}
