\section*{Source-Grounded Synthesis Across the Propers}

These are conclusions, not fresh evidence: each rests on the appointed texts,
their documented history, and the witnesses checked in the commentary. Nothing
here is a proposal; the proposals are in the next section and are labelled as
such. Each claim names the appointed elements it joins and the claim classes it
mixes, and each ends on the strongest thing that stands against it.
\textbf{Every English string quoted from a Father below is the rendering of the
\work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers} and not the Father's own words}: first
series for Augustine and Chrysostom, second series vol.~7 for the
\work{Mystagogical Catecheses}, second series vol.~10 for Ambrose. Guéranger is
quoted from the Duffy 1900 edition of \work{The Liturgical Year} vol.~XI;
\textbf{no lane established who translated that volume, so the edition is named
and no translator is.}

\subsection*{C1. One phrase carries the Mass, and it is not the phrase Matthew usually uses}

\textbf{Elements: Epistle, Gospel, Communion. Textual observation and
documented reception.}

\latin{Regnum Dei} stands verbatim in three appointed elements and carries one
argument across them. The Epistle names who is \emph{excluded} from the
kingdom, \latin{qui tália agunt, regnum Dei non consequéntur}; the Gospel
commands that it be \emph{sought first}; and the Communion repeats that command
as \emph{the Lord's own word at the moment of reception}. The negative and the
positive are two faces of one claim about the kingdom, not two positions set
against each other.

Three textual facts sharpen it. \latin{Regnum Dei} is \textbf{rare in Matthew}:
four occurrences in the whole Gospel (6:33, 12:28, 21:31, 21:43) against
thirty-four of \latin{regnum caelorum} across thirty-two verses, with Mt.\ 6:33
the first of the four --- a count taken over the tracked Clementine's Latin and
\textbf{not repeated in Greek}. It is the \textbf{only} occurrence of the phrase
in Galatians. And across the fifteen 1962 formularies this repository has
facsimile-collated, \latin{regnum Dei} occurs in \textbf{this formulary alone},
at exactly three loci, independently reproduced in a second provider's
collation.

At the hinge stands the best-attested Latin sentence in the whole sweep.
Augustine on \latin{primum}: \latin{non témpore, sed dignitáte} --- the
ordering is one of importance and not of time. He makes the point by denying
the sequence the word invites, ``He did not say, \emph{Then} seek such
things,'' and ties the phrase back to Mt.\ 6:24 at \work{De sermone Domini in
monte} II.56.

\textbf{Limits and defeaters.} The sample for the cross-formulary count is
fifteen collated formularies, small and non-random; it is \textbf{emphatically
not a claim about the Missal} and is not made as one. A Communion antiphon
drawn from the day's own Gospel is ordinary practice, so if this one's
\latin{regnum Dei} were shown to be a purely mechanical carry-over the
three-element spine would reduce to a two-element one plus an echo --- though
that would not touch the Epistle's independent occurrence, which comes from a
different book and a different pericope. And the defeater of \S C2 applies
here too: the one medieval preacher the library registers on this Epistle
expounds it beside a different Gospel.

\subsection*{C2. The flesh that is crucified is not the body that is fed, and the tradition says so twice by two different routes}

\textbf{Elements: Epistle, Gospel. Textual observation and documented
reception.}

The two elements are \textbf{lexically disjoint}: \latin{caro}/\latin{carn-}
stands five times in the Epistle and nowhere else in the formulary;
\latin{corp-} stands twice in the Gospel and nowhere else. \textbf{This is one
unit, not a contrast.} The point is not that Epistle and Gospel disagree about
the body but that they use different words for different things, and that
reading them together dissolves the appearance of disagreement. \textbf{A
reading built as ``Epistle mortifies against Gospel provides'' has inverted
it.}

The doctrinal resolution is documented reception and not merely synthesis, and
it arrives by two routes that differ in kind. Chrysostom's governing move is to
refuse the reading that Paul condemns the body --- ``by the flesh, he does not
mean the body'' --- and he proves it from the vice-list's own contents, since
hatred, variance, emulations, strife, heresies and witchcraft ``arise merely
from a depraved moral choice.'' His argument from the verb holds in the
appointed Latin: ``if these things belonged to nature and not to a bad moral
choice, his expression, \emph{they practise}, is inappropriate'' --- and the
missal prints \latin{qui tália agunt}. Aquinas raises the same objection and
answers it by a \textbf{different route}: the definition he cites from
Augustine's \work{De civitate Dei} XIV.2, that whoever lives according to
himself lives according to the flesh, so that \latin{caro} here stands for the
whole man. \textbf{\work{De civitate Dei} XIV.2 was not opened}, though this
repository holds a record for the work, so what stands here is Aquinas's use of
Augustine and not Augustine's own wording. \textbf{Preserve
the difference; do not report a single patristic-scholastic consensus.}

Augustine supplies a third leg from the other direction, and it is documented
\emph{controversial} reception. Certain monks quoted these very verses to argue
that they need not work, and \work{De opere monachorum} was written against
them: ``let these persons, who from perverse understanding of the Gospel labor
to pervert apostolic precepts, either take no thought for the morrow, even as
the birds of the air; or let them obey the Apostle.'' That lead came from the
\work{Catena aurea} and \textbf{was checked against the underlying work and
confirmed}. \textbf{Not} confirmed, and therefore not published as checked: the
\work{De haeresibus} citation naming the Euchitae, that work not having been
retrieved.

\textbf{Limits.} Gal.\ 5:24's \latin{carnem suam crucifixérunt} is read by some
as including the body proper, and the Vulgate elsewhere uses \latin{caro} for
the body simply; the disjunction is a fact about these two pericopes as
printed, \textbf{not a lexical law}. The Introit's psalm source carries
\latin{caro} in a positive sense at Ps.\ 83:3b --- outside the appointed cut.
Chrysostom is read in the English of the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers},
first series, only, with \work{Patrologia Graeca}~61 uncollated, so the English
quoted from him is NPNF's; Aquinas in a machine transcription uncollated against a printed
edition. \textbf{And the rule of \S C2's own evidence governs every use of the
Greek witness: the Latin vice- and fruit-lists the missal prints are longer than
the lists the Greek Fathers expound, so no Greek witness is made to comment on
\latin{veneficia \dots\ homicídia} or on \latin{cástitas}}, words his own list
does not contain.

\subsection*{C3. The images are arguments and not figures, and the one sentence the Mass lifts out of them is read as doctrine}

\textbf{Elements: Gospel, Communion. Documented reception, with a textual
observation.}

\textbf{The Gospel half.} Augustine explicitly \textbf{forbids} allegorising the
birds and the lilies: ``these examples are not to be treated as allegories, so
that we should inquire what the fowls of heaven or the lilies of the field mean:
for they stand here, in order that from smaller matters we may be persuaded
respecting greater ones,'' with the unjust judge as his comparison case. The
restraint is \emph{specific}: he bars allegorising \emph{these examples}, not
the pericope as a whole --- and that specificity is what makes this one claim
rather than two, because at the pericope's closing imperative the same witness
reads a doctrine.

Chrysostom independently reads them as arguments too, but as
\textbf{rhetorically engineered} ones. The birds are chosen \emph{because} they
are irrational, deliberately in place of Moses, Elijah or John, ``that He might
touch them more to the quick \dots\ He has cut off from them every excuse'';
and the lilies are progressively degraded --- lilies, then ``grass of the
field,'' then ``which today is,'' then ``cast into the oven'' --- so the
\emph{a fortiori} grows at every step. The appointed Latin fits word for word:
\latin{fænum agri, quod hódie est, et cras in clíbanum míttitur \dots\ quanto
magis vos módicæ fídei?}, and he calls the rebuke not want but \emph{poverty}
of faith.

\textbf{Where they agree they still differ, and the difference is the
interesting half.} Augustine grounds the inference in the \textbf{rank} of the
creature --- a rational being stands higher in the nature of things than an
irrational one. Chrysostom grounds it in the creature's \textbf{vileness} and
in the deliberate rhetorical descent, and gives the anthropological ground in
terms of \emph{gift} rather than rank: ``you, to whom He gave a soul, for whom
He fashioned a body \dots\ for whose sake He gave up His only begotten Son.''
The difference is not a contradiction, but the middle terms differ and the two
yield different homiletic conclusions from the same verses. \textbf{A guide
reporting only that the Fathers read the birds as an argument from lesser to
greater has flattened the more interesting half of the evidence.}

Both Fathers also set a limit against over-reading the pericope, and by
different routes. Augustine at \work{De serm. Dom.} II.57--58: Christ kept
money bags, Paul worked with his hands, the church at Antioch stored food
against Agabus's famine, and the precept reduces to the rule that even in
looking after such things we should think of the kingdom of God, but in the
service of the kingdom of God we should not think of such things. Chrysostom
reaches the same limit on v.~26: ``He said not, we must not sow, but we must
not take thought; neither that one ought not to work, but not to be
low-minded.'' \textbf{Where the claim must rest inside the appointed bounds,
Chrysostom's is the better sentence}, because Augustine's material there is
anchored partly in v.~34, which this lesson excludes.

\textbf{The Communion half, and it is this claim's second appointed element.}
The formulary appoints Mt.\ 6:33 twice: once as the Gospel's last verse and
again, recast, as the Communion antiphon --- the seventh element standing on the
sixth locus, and the formulary's single substantive departure from its Bible
text. On that one verse Augustine does not argue from a lesser example at all.
He reads \latin{primum} as an ordering of \textbf{importance and not of time}
--- \latin{non tempore, sed dignitate} --- and makes the point by denying the
sequence the word invites: ``He did not say, \emph{Then} seek such things.''
\textbf{The two appointed elements that carry Matthew~6 in this Mass therefore
receive from one witness two different kinds of reading --- figure refused at
the images, doctrine read at the imperative --- and the sentence the missal
lifted out to say at the moment of reception is the one on which he reads
doctrine.} The evidence is unusually good at exactly this hinge: the decisive
sentence is corroborated in Latin by a second, independent, separately hashed
thirteenth-century witness whose \latin{non tempore, sed dignitate} answers the
NPNF wording exactly. \textbf{That is corroboration and not verification}, and
\work{Patrologia Latina}~34 was not opened.

\textbf{One editorial observation, and it is kept apart from the reception.} The
missal's recasting promotes \latin{Primum} to the head of the antiphon ---
exactly where Augustine's argument makes word order carry the doctrine.
\textbf{That is an editorial observation of this guide's and not a patristic
one: Augustine knows nothing of the antiphon}, and nothing here is offered as
his comment on it. \textbf{This claim and C5 share that single datum and are not
merged:} C5 puts the promotion to the extent and antiquity of the recasting,
C3 puts it to the difference between the two registers in which the appointed
Matthaean text is read.

\textbf{What follows for the guide's own page~1 --- a consequence of this claim
and not a member of it.} The profile requires an allegorical row; the principal
Latin Father commenting on this very pericope bars allegorising the pericope's
central images. \textbf{That tension is preserved on page~1 rather than resolved
by quietly allegorising the lilies anyway or by suppressing Augustine's rule.}
The four-senses apparatus is a feature of this publication and not a ritual
moment of the Mass, so it is recorded here as a consequence of the claim and is
never counted as one of the elements the claim joins.

\textbf{Limits.} Both patristic witnesses are read in the English of the
\work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, first series, only --- that rendering,
and not their Latin or Greek, is what is quoted --- neither collated against
\work{Patrologia Latina}~34 or \work{Patrologia Graeca}~57.
Chrysostom's observations are about wording, so their force depends on the
sequence of words being the same in Greek, Latin and English at these points:
\textbf{the Latin correspondences were checked and do hold; the Greek was
not.} \textbf{The Communion membership carries C1's defeater and it is stated
here.} A Communion antiphon drawn from the day's Gospel is ordinary practice, so
this second element may be a mechanical carry-over rather than a choice. That
bears on how \emph{interesting} the join is; it does not dissolve it, because
the lesson and the reception are two ritual moments whatever produced the
antiphon. \textbf{Unlike C1, this claim has no third element from a different
book to fall back on, and that is its principal weakness.} \textbf{No compiler's
intent is claimed:} that the pericope stops at v.~33 is what lets the Communion
take the Gospel's last verse, and what the compiler meant by it is not
established.

\subsection*{C4. The Sunday's number is the least stable thing about the Mass, and its strands were assembled on different schedules}

\textbf{Elements: Collect, Secret, Postcommunion; at second remove the chants
and the Gospel. Documented historical orientation, with source-grounded
synthesis in the conclusion.}

The one-sentence form a later writer should not simplify past: \emph{the
Collect and Secret of this Mass stand two Sundays later in the Frankish
Gelasian books and one Sunday later in the Gregorian, uniformly across the
whole run, on a published critical collation two of whose manuscripts the
editor read himself --- and no one has since opened any of the five books at
the locus, the assignment covers only two of the formulary's ten elements, and
the rest of the formulary was assembled on a different schedule again.}

Every limb of that was verified at first hand on page images. Gelasianum
Book~III sects.~I--XI correspond to the 1962 Fifth through Fifteenth Sundays,
and across the whole run Rheinau, S.~Gallen, Gerbert and Menard stand uniformly
two Sundays later and Pamelius uniformly one, with no exception; each section's
1962 identity was fixed from its own printed collect or Secret on the same page
image, never from position. The offset is \textbf{already present at the
series' first section}, so nothing inside the series produces it. The Gregorian
Hadrianum supplement stands independently at one Sunday later across
\textbf{seven} sections, each identified from its own printed collect; and the
Ottobonianus~313 chant cues stand independently at two Sundays later across
\textbf{six} sections in a different edition. The break at sect.~XII is fully
explained: Wilson names \textbf{two} Masses that R, S and Gerbert interpolate,
the September Embertide Sunday and the ``Vacant'' Sunday, which is exactly the
arithmetic required to move the offset from two to four while Pamelius stays at
one.

\textbf{And the strands do not travel together.} The Ottobonianus cue set
sitting beside \latin{Custodi, Domine} is the 1962 \emph{Sixteenth} Sunday's,
two ahead of the orations it sits next to. Honorius, in one and the same
twelfth-century chapter headed for this Sunday and opening \latin{Protector
noster}, has this Sunday's Introit, Collect, Epistle and Offertory but the 1962
Thirteenth Sunday's Gospel, a Gradual and Alleluia that are not this Sunday's,
and a different Communion again --- and that Gospel offset is not a slip, since
it holds across three consecutive Sundays. \textbf{So the earlier books do not
carry the 1962 formulary under a different number. Orations, chants and Gospel
were numbered and paired independently and settled at different times, and
prose reporting ``this Mass stood at a different Sunday'' as one clean fact
would be reporting something no witness says.}

\textbf{Limits, and they are load-bearing.} None of the five books was opened
at the locus; the whole disagreement rests on Wilson's apparatus. His footnote
at sect.~X covers \textbf{``the Collects and Secret'' only} --- sect.~X prints
five prayers and neither of its two Postcommunions is the one 1962 prints ---
and the 1962 Postcommunion has a materially narrower Gelasian base, attested
for Rheinau and Gerbert but \textbf{not} for S.~Gallen, Pamelius or Menard.
\textbf{The displacement is therefore a claim about two of the formulary's ten
elements, and prose treating the three orations as equally attested would be
wrong.} The four books agreeing are not four independent witnesses: Gerbert's
principal manuscript cannot now be traced, and Wilson holds that its
``Gelasian'' portions represent a revision based on the Gregorian type, so
Gerbert leans toward the very editions supposed to corroborate it --- while
Pamelius is itself an edition of the Gregorian book, so its reading and the
Gregorian numbering are \textbf{not two independent witnesses}. In the other
direction, R's and S's own Sunday headings are Wilson's own collation and do
not depend on Gerbert at all. And the origin of the offset is unresolved: it
lies outside Book~III's Sunday series, in how the two traditions count the
preceding Sundays, and a later sweep should look there rather than for a
further interpolation inside the run.

\subsection*{C5. The formulary makes exactly one substantive recasting, it is at the Communion, and it is older than this book}

\textbf{Elements: Gospel, Communion. Textual observation, documented
historical orientation, documented reception.}

The Communion departs from Mt.\ 6:33 in exactly four ways, verified against the
Clementine, the Lucan parallel and the tracked Robinson-Pierpont Greek:
\latin{ergo} is dropped and \latin{Primum} promoted to head the clause;
\latin{et iustítiam eius} is omitted; \latin{hæc} is dropped before
\latin{ómnia}; and \latin{dicit Dóminus} is added. That tag stands in none of
Clementine Mt.\ 6:33, Clementine Lk.\ 12:31 or Robinson-Pierpont Mt.\ 6:33; and
Luke, unlike the antiphon, keeps \latin{iustitiam eius}, so the omission is
\textbf{not} explained by conflation with the Lucan form.

Two of the four bear theologically. Adding \latin{dicit Dóminus} turns a saying
inside a sermon into \textbf{an address made to the communicant at the moment
of reception}. Dropping \latin{et iustítiam eius} leaves the kingdom to be
sought without the justice the Gospel names beside it. A third --- promoting
\latin{Primum} to the head --- falls exactly where Augustine's argument makes
word order carry the doctrine, \textbf{which is an editorial observation and
not a patristic one: Augustine knows nothing of this antiphon.}

\textbf{Three independent witnesses converge on the antiphon being secondary in
this position.} The appointed text itself, since this is the formulary's one
substantive departure from its Bible text. Guéranger, who reports that the
antiphon ``was not the one primitively used; the ancient liturgists make no
mention of it in its present position, nor is it to be found there in any of
the manuscripts consulted by Blessed Thomasi.'' And the Ottobonianus rotation:
Wilson's index shows \latin{Primum quaerite} occurring as a Communion at
exactly one locus, whose chant set is the 1962 \emph{Ninth} Sunday's, the
\latin{Protector noster} set there taking \latin{Panis quem ego} --- so in that
apparatus the 1962-Ninth set takes \latin{Primum quaerite}, the 1962-Fourteenth
set \latin{Panis quem ego}, and the 1962-Fifteenth set \latin{Qui manducat},
while 1962 assigns those three the other way about. \textbf{That is a rotation,
not a single replacement.}

The strongest single control comes from within the same Office and anyone can
check it in the same pages: Guéranger prints this Sunday's Magnificat antiphon
at Vespers as \latin{Quaerite primum regnum Dei, et justitiam ejus, et haec
omnia adjicientur vobis. Alleluia.} \textbf{That antiphon retains both
\latin{et iustítiam eius} and \latin{haec} --- exactly the two clauses the
Mass's Communion drops --- and leaves \latin{primum} in its Matthaean position
after the verb.}

\textbf{Limits.} Guéranger's judgment rests on Tommasi's Antiphonary and ``the
ancient liturgists,'' neither consulted: it is a \textbf{checked report of an
unchecked claim}, to be attributed to him and never restated as established
chant history. Antiphon texts are routinely shortened for chant and
\latin{dicit Dóminus} is a common formulaic tag, so both changes may be
conventional rather than expressive, and that count was not run. Two of the
three 1962 assignments in the rotation were verified against repository text;
the Fifteenth Sunday's Communion was not, no leaf for it existing here. What
the rotation establishes is \textbf{what the older pairing was, not when the
substitution happened} --- the instrument that would test it, Hesbert's
\work{Antiphonale Missarum Sextuplex}, is registered at catalog level only. And
the recasting is not proper to the 1962 edition: the same form is printed in
the Pustet Missal of Ratisbon, 1862, which this repository tracks as
public-domain text --- which shows the recasting is old but says nothing about
why it was made.

\subsection*{What this formulary does not say}

Each of these is bounded to the ten appointed elements as printed, and nothing
wider.

\textbf{The Holy Spirit is named in exactly one element.} The \latin{spiritu-}
stem occurs five times in the Epistle and nowhere else; the Collect, Secret and
Postcommunion never name the Spirit. \textbf{This must be stated with its bound
or it is simply false:} the printed rubric appoints the Preface of the Most
Holy Trinity, so the Mass \emph{as celebrated} names the Spirit. The negative
is about the proper, and it bounds how far a Spirit-centred reading can be
pressed. Beyond that: no parousia, resurrection or judgment vocabulary past the
Epistle's \latin{regnum Dei non consequéntur} and the Postcommunion's
\latin{perpétuæ salvatiónis}; no saint named and no Marian reference; no
penitential, fasting or almsgiving language, despite a seventeen-item vice
list; one angel named, in the Offertory, and no other created spirit; and the
Church named \textbf{once}, in the Collect --- though the first-person plurals
of Introit, Alleluia, Secret and Postcommunion are corporate in their own way,
which softens any contrast built on that count.

\subsection*{Two joints this guide leaves open}

\textbf{The Alleluia is not joined to the rest by its own vocabulary.} It is
the least lexically integrated element of the formulary: its only substantive
tie to any other element is \latin{salutári}, and \latin{exsult-} and
\latin{iubil-} occur nowhere else in the ten. The joy of the Alleluia is a
natural thing for a synthesis to reach for as the resolution of the Gospel's
anxiety, and \textbf{the appointed texts do not supply the lexical warrant}:
the Epistle's \latin{gaudium} and the Alleluia's \latin{exsultémus} are
different roots in different elements with nothing joining them. No reception
support for such a reading was found, and none is asserted.

\textbf{Whether the Epistle and the Gospel were ever read together in the
tradition.} The library's sole registered treatment of Gal.\ 5:16--24 is
Anthony of Padua's sermon, and his Sunday numbering runs one ahead of the 1962
missal's, consistently across all three of his registered sermons. \textbf{A
major medieval preacher expounding this Epistle does so beside a different
Gospel.} Every cross-element claim joining Epistle and Gospel is therefore a
claim about how the 1962 formulary reads when taken as a whole, and not a claim
about how the tradition read these two texts together, and every such claim
above says so.
