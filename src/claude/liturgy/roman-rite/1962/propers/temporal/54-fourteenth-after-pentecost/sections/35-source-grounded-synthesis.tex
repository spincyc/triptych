\section*{Source-Grounded Synthesis Across the Propers}

\begingroup
\footnotesize

\subsection*{Allegiance orders desire toward the kingdom}

The Introit's longing for the courts, the Collect's movement away from harms
and toward saving goods, the Epistle's opposed desires and kingdom warning,
the Gospel's rival masters, and the Communion's promoted \latin{Primum} order
the whole person under one final allegiance. The Postcommunion's perpetual
salvation gives that ordering its eschatological horizon. Augustine's account
of grace-assisted resistance, Aquinas's rival ultimate ends, and Bellarmine's
heavenly pilgrimage show why conversion reorders desire rather than suppressing
it. Created goods remain good; they cease to govern when desire is ordered
toward beatitude.

\subsection*{Providence summons responsible agency}

The Gradual's trust, the Collect's petition for continual help, the Epistle's
imperative to walk and crucify vice-governed desire, the Gospel's release from
anxious mastery, and the Postcommunion's request for sacramental purification
and strength refuse both quietism and self-sufficient moralism. Chrysostom
preserves ordinary work within Christ's prohibition of solicitude; Benedict
makes Matthew~6:33 govern responsible care of souls amid scarcity; Theodoret
joins indwelling grace to the soul's cooperation. Divine providence orders
action instead of cancelling it and promises no immunity from deprivation.

The Epistle's crucified \latin{caro} and the Gospel's provided-for
\latin{corpus} are not opposites. Jerome refuses to make bodily substance the
object of Paul's condemnation: the body is good, while the person governed by
disordered desire must be healed.

\subsection*{Created mediation serves without becoming a rival master}

Divine protection appears as invocation in the Introit, petition in the
Collect, confidence in the Gradual, fatherly provision in the Gospel, angelic
encampment in the Offertory, and sacramental fortification in the
Postcommunion. The patristic disagreements sharpen rather than dissolve this
unity. Augustine and Cassiodorus include angelic princes under the Gradual's
warning; Theodoret reads earthly rulers; Bellarmine allows creaturely help
when hope remains referred to God. Augustine identifies the Offertory's Angel
with Christ, while Cassiodorus, Theodoret, Aquinas, and Bellarmine also preserve
created or ecclesial senses.

These readings distinguish mediation from mastery. God may protect through
angels, persons, or sacraments without surrendering the final allegiance that
Matthew calls service. Providence remains compatible with affliction and does
not promise bodily immunity.

\subsection*{The oblation and sacraments carry the Church toward saving effect}

The Collect asks perpetual propitiation and saving direction for the Church;
the Offertory's tasting has explicit Eucharistic reception in Augustine,
Cassiodorus, the \work{Mystagogical Catechesis} traditionally attributed to
Cyril, and Ambrose; the Secret names the saving Host as purification and
propitiation; the Communion revoices the kingdom's priority after reception;
and the Postcommunion asks continuing purification and strength unto perpetual
salvation. Estius receives the Collect as the Church's prayer for persevering
grace. This is an arc of the received ritual order, not evidence that the
prayers or all ten elements were composed together.

The Alleluia's saving God, the Secret's saving victim, and the
Postcommunion's perpetual salvation also form a verbal chain whose members are
not interchangeable. Praise addresses God; the victim purifies and
propitiates; the sacraments fortify and lead. The sacramentaries attest the
prayer texts, while patristic and medieval interpretation unfolds the appointed
passages' doctrinal and scriptural language.

\subsection*{Grace transfigures appetite into worshipful delight}

Longing for the courts, the Spirit's joy and peace, jubilation to the Saviour,
freedom from solicitude, and tasting the Lord's sweetness move from restless
appetite toward worshipful delight and final salvation. Augustine and
Cassiodorus hear Psalm~94's jubilation as joy beyond articulate speech;
Theodoret hears a victor's cry; Bellarmine moves from inward gladness to vocal
praise and experiential sweetness. The related affects---\latin{concupiscit},
\latin{desideria}, \latin{gaudium}, \latin{iubilare}, \latin{sollicitudo}, and
\latin{gustare}---are not interchangeable technical terms. Christian trust
does not suppress grief, prudence, or concrete care.

\subsection*{The received formulary is continuous and selective}

The Old Gelasian tradition holds \latin{Custódi} and \latin{Concéde nobis}
together in an \latin{alia missa}, while \latin{Puríficent semper} appears
separately and in a Saint Andrew adaptation (Wilson, \work{The Gelasian
Sacramentary}, pp.~206--207, 229--230, 237). The Hadrianum Gregorian
supplement gives all three consecutively at Sunday~XV; Gellone~II and
Vilabertran preserve that same three-prayer sequence and ordinal
(Wilson, \work{The Gregorian Sacramentary}, pp.~173--174; Gellone~II,
nos.~1121--1123; Vilabertran, nos.~488--490). The prayer unit circulated
before its later Sunday-XIV placement, but these ordinal differences supply no
universal conversion rule or cause.

Hesbert's six-antiphoner comparison shows an early Sunday-XIV nucleus of
\latin{Protéctor noster}, \latin{Immíttet Angelus Dómini}, and \latin{Panis
quem ego dédero}, with variable Gradual and Alleluia states. The surviving
columns establish neither \latin{Veníte} nor \latin{Primum} at that stage.
Honorius later distributes elements now associated with the Fourteenth and
Fifteenth Sundays in both directions (\work{Gemma animae} IV.72--79,
PL~172:717--720), so the history cannot be reduced to a simple one-week shift.

The five later chants stand together and in order on fols.~184v--185v of the
Gradual completed by Johannes of Valkenburg in 1299: \latin{Protéctor},
\latin{Bonum}, \latin{Veníte}, \latin{Immíttet}, and \latin{Primum}. This is
an upper bound for their conjunction in that Franciscan witness, not proof of
universal Roman use. Lippe's edition of the 1474 Milan Roman-Curia Missal then
prints the complete ten-element core under Sunday~XIV. The same core remains
stable in element and order in the Venice 1570, Vatican 1604, Mame 1922, and
Vatican 1962 witnesses. Venice carries a different order in part of
Galatians~5:22; the 1570 and 1604 books also retain seasonal orations abolished
by the 1960 \work{Variationes}.

Guéranger, reporting the manuscript tradition through Tommasi, calls
\latin{Panis quem ego} the earlier Communion and \latin{Primum quǽrite} the
later one. This evidence reaches Tommasi only through Guéranger; the sequence's
first appearance and compiler remain unidentified, as do the Communion
substitution's date and cause.

Guéranger and Schuster receive the formulary as a liturgical movement through
entrance, petition, proclamation, oblation, communion, purification, and final
salvation. Their synthesis receives the later arrangement; the sacramentaries
and medieval witnesses preserve the earlier variety from which it must remain
distinct. Continuity and selective change are both facts of the received
formulary.
