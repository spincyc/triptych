\section*{Source-Grounded Synthesis Across the Propers}

\subsection*{C1. One phrase carries the Mass, and the element that repeats it is the element that cuts it}

\latin{Regnum Dei} stands verbatim in three appointed elements and carries one
argument across them. The Epistle names who is \emph{excluded} from the
kingdom, \latin{qui tália agunt, regnum Dei non consequéntur}; the Gospel
commands that it be \emph{sought first}; and the Communion repeats that command
as \emph{the Lord's own word at the moment of reception}. The negative and the
positive are two faces of one claim about the kingdom, not two positions set
against each other.

Four textual facts sharpen it. \latin{Regnum Dei} is \textbf{rare in Matthew}:
four verses in the whole Gospel (6:33, 12:28, 21:31, 21:43) against twenty-four
carrying \latin{regnum caelorum}, with Mt.\ 6:33 the first of the four. It is
the \textbf{only} occurrence of the phrase in Galatians, \textbf{but Paul uses
it elsewhere}: 1~Cor.~6:9--10, Eph.~5:5 and
1~Cor.~15:50 form a small closed set of Pauline parallels closing vice lists
with the same exclusion, \textbf{none of the three is appointed, and nothing in
the ten elements points to them}; their standing is as the canonical context of
the appointed wording. \textbf{The tie survives into the Greek}, both texts
reading \emph{basileia theou}, which the Epistle-to-Gospel \latin{servitus} /
\latin{servire} bridge does not. And across the fifteen 1962 formularies this
repository has facsimile-collated, \latin{regnum Dei} occurs in \textbf{this
formulary alone}, at exactly three loci, independently reproduced in a second
provider's collation.

\textbf{The formulation is about exact forms, and dropping those two words makes
it false.} \latin{Regnum} is the only exact form reaching three or more of the
ten elements that is neither a divine name nor a function word --- but at stem
level \latin{salut-} and \latin{salv-} reach three elements too, and are
substantive. The two roots partition the formulary rather than competing:
\latin{regnum} in the read and sung word, \latin{salut-} in the three orations
and one chant.

\textbf{And the element that repeats the phrase is the element that cuts it.}
\latin{Iustítia} stands \textbf{once} in the whole appointed Latin, in the
Gospel's last verse --- and the Communion, quoting that very verse, drops it. In
Matthew that word closes an arc the chapter opens with at 6:1, \textbf{though
the arc is a property of the Vulgate's reading there, the tracked Byzantine text
reading \emph{alms}.}

At the hinge stands Augustine's reading of \latin{primum}, carried in Latin by a
second and separately hashed thirteenth-century witness, and it is now contested.
The Augustine the compilations transmit reads \latin{primum}
as an ordering of importance and not of time --- \latin{non témpore, sed
dignitáte} --- making the point by denying the sequence the word invites, in the
English of the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, first series: ``He
did not say, \emph{Then} seek such things''; and he ties the phrase back to
Mt.\ 6:24 at \work{De sermone Domini in monte} II.56. \textbf{Paschasius
Radbertus denies that \latin{primum} marks order at all}: \latin{non ad ordinem
referendum puto \dots\ magis ad quantitatem referendum est, ut extra hoc nihil
quaerendum sit}. The
verb the antiphon keeps has its own readers --- pseudo-Bede's \latin{Non dixit
nobis dari, sed adjici}, Christian of Stavelot's \latin{subauditur necessaria
\dots\ Ad necessitatem, non ad superfluitatem}, Paschasius's \latin{his utamur
in via, quatenus illo fruamur in patria} --- and Godfrey of Admont reads
\latin{regnum Dei} at this verse as the heart, which ought always to be God's
kingdom. \textbf{Schuster, commenting on the antiphon itself, reaches
back for the clause the recasting removed} and glosses the justice from the
Gospel verse rather than from the antiphon.

What the omission does is remove the formulary's only occurrence of
\latin{iustítia}. The cross-formulary count runs over fifteen collated
formularies, a small and non-random set, and is a fact about them rather than
about the \work{Missale Romanum}. A Communion antiphon drawn from the day's own
Gospel is ordinary practice, so were this one's \latin{regnum Dei} a purely
mechanical carry-over the three-element spine would reduce to a two-element one
plus an echo --- which would still leave the Epistle's occurrence standing,
since it comes from a different book and a different pericope. And the one
medieval preacher the library registers on this Epistle expounds it beside a
different Gospel.

\subsection*{C2. The flesh that is crucified is not the body that is fed, and the tradition now says so four ways in two languages}

The two lessons are \textbf{lexically disjoint}: \latin{caro}/\latin{carn-}
stands five times in the Epistle and nowhere else in the formulary;
\latin{corp-} stands twice in the Gospel and nowhere else. \textbf{This is one
unit, not a contrast.} The point is not that Epistle and Gospel disagree about
the body but that they use different words for different things, and that
reading them together dissolves the appearance of disagreement. \textbf{A
reading built as ``Epistle mortifies against Gospel provides'' has inverted
it.} \textbf{And the Introit's cut is what keeps the division clean:} its psalm
verse stops at \latin{in atria Domini}, one clause before the Clementine's
\latin{cor meum et caro mea exsultaverunt in Deum vivum} --- the one appointed
element that would have given \latin{caro} a positive sense, severed exactly
before it, and Cassiodorus comments on that very clause outside the appointed
extract.

\textbf{Four positions now stand on what \latin{caro} is at Gal.\ 5:17, and
they are genuinely four.} \textbf{Chrysostom's Greek denies desire to
the flesh}: \latin{sarka gar entautha, ou to soma kalei} --- flesh here is the
earthbound, slothful reasoning; desire belongs to the soul, so a flesh that
desires cannot be the body; the body is an instrument, \latin{organon gar estin
he sarx}, and \emph{we hate and punish not the iron but the murderer}; the
conflict is between two reasonings and not \latin{ouch he psyche kai to soma}.
\textbf{Marius Victorinus} argues the contrary from the soulless elements:
\latin{Habet enim motus suos caro, habetque sensus, neque tantum ab anima
suscitantur}. \textbf{The Glossa ordinaria} makes the ascription a manner of
speaking about a joint act --- \latin{Caro dicta est concupiscere, quia hoc
secundum ipsam agit anima} --- with the cause in neither alone, \latin{ex
utroque enim fit}. \textbf{Ambrosiaster} shifts the ground entirely: not two
parts of man but two laws, \latin{unam Dei, alteram peccati}, with \latin{medius
homo} standing between them, the flesh's works so named by origin
(\latin{quia errores isti ex mundo sunt, ex quo et caro}), so that at v.~24 the
crucified flesh is the world. \textbf{Chrysostom and Victorinus comment on the
same appointed verse and cannot both be right.}

\textbf{And Jerome supplies the argument from the missal's own word.} Where
the Latin translator put \latin{vitia} the Greek reads \emph{pathemata},
\emph{passiones}, and the Apostle added \latin{desideria} \textbf{precisely so
as not to seem to deny the nature of the body in spiritual men, but its
vices}: \latin{ut non naturam corporis videretur in spiritualibus viris negare,
sed vitia}. \textbf{A Father defending the appointed word on exactly this
ground is better evidence for this claim than any of the others, because it is
anchored in the word the missal prints.} Chrysostom adds at the lesson's last
verse that those of Christ did not destroy the flesh --- \latin{epei pos
emellon zen?} --- and that the desires, though they trouble, rage in vain.
Aquinas reaches the same conclusion by a different route again, through the
definition he cites from Augustine's \work{De civitate Dei} XIV.2, that whoever
lives according to himself lives according to the flesh, so that \latin{caro}
here stands for the whole man --- \textbf{Aquinas's citation of that work, and
not Augustine's own wording, the \work{De civitate Dei} standing unopened
behind it.}

\textbf{And the pericope itself supplies the convergence these routes point
to.} The lesson names only \latin{caro} and \latin{spiritus} and leaves the
soul unmentioned; \textbf{Chrysostom in Greek and Jerome in Latin
independently raise that difficulty and answer it the same way} --- the soul
lies between and makes the body spiritual or earthly by what it does with it.
\textbf{The two agree, and nothing shows dependence.}

Augustine supplies a leg from the other direction, and the reception is
\emph{controversial}. Certain monks quoted these very verses to argue
that they need not work, and \work{De opere monachorum} was written against
them, in the English of the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, first series:
``let these persons, who from perverse understanding of the Gospel labor
to pervert apostolic precepts, either take no thought for the morrow, even as
the birds of the air; or let them obey the Apostle.'' The \work{Catena aurea}
points to that work, and the work itself carries the passage. The
identification of those monks as the Euchitae stands in \work{De haeresibus},
which is unopened, and remains an unverified lead.

Gal.\ 5:24's \latin{carnem suam crucifixérunt} is read by some
as including the body proper, and the Vulgate elsewhere uses \latin{caro} for
the body simply; the disjunction is a fact about these two pericopes as
printed, \textbf{not a lexical law}. \textbf{Ambrosiaster's Latin lemma is not
the missal's} --- his fruit-list has ten members, carrying \latin{spes} and
\latin{castitas} and lacking \latin{fides} and \latin{patientia}, and his
vice-list opens differently --- \textbf{so his exposition counts a different
pair of lists, and the missal's seventeen and twelve are not the lists he
expounds.} Marius Victorinus's text as Migne prints
it passes from v.~17 to 6:1, so he comments on none of vv.~18--24, and whether a
critical edition shows the same gap was not checked. Chrysostom's Greek is a TLG
transcription uncollated against \work{Patrologia Graeca}~61. The
Latin vice- and fruit-lists the missal prints are longer than the lists the
Greek Fathers expound, so no Greek witness here speaks to
\latin{luxúria}, \latin{modéstia} or \latin{cástitas}, words his own list does
not contain.

\subsection*{C3. The tradition is namedly divided about the Gospel's images, and its most-repeated sentence at the Gospel's first verse was withdrawn by the man who wrote it}

\textbf{The division is named, and it is not a difference of emphasis.} On the
anti-allegorical side: the Augustine the compilations transmit --- \latin{ista
documenta non sicut allegorica discutienda sunt} --- repeated by the exposition
printed under Bede's name; \textbf{Christian of Stavelot refusing allegory at
the lilies on frankly aesthetic grounds}, no linen matching the lily's
whiteness, no dye the rose, no purple the violet; Jerome forbidding the birds as
angels on a ground internal to the appointed verse, \latin{Simpliciter ergo
accipiendum}; and Bruno of Segni reading the lilies literally. On the
allegorical side, at its far end: \textbf{Godfrey of Admont makes the birds the
good angels, the lilies the evil spirits and Solomon Christ}, and then raises
and answers the objection his own allegory creates --- so he is a self-aware
instance and not a naive one. \textbf{Hilary allegorises every appointed image
into doctrine of the risen body}, holding that the literal reading does not
cohere, \latin{non satis propositionibus congruunt}. Chrysostom reads the images
as arguments too, but rhetorically engineered ones, the illustrations degraded
step by step so the \emph{a fortiori} grows at each --- and \textbf{where the
Fathers agree they still differ}, Augustine's middle term being the creature's
\textbf{rank} and Chrysostom's its \textbf{vileness}, given in terms of
\emph{gift} rather than of rank.

\textbf{And the sentence the Latin tradition repeats most often at this
Gospel's first verse was withdrawn by its author.} \latin{Nullius enim fere
conscientia Deum potest odisse} is retracted at \work{Retractationes} I.19,
where Augustine names \work{De sermone Domini in monte} II.14.48 --- his
exposition of the appointed Gospel's first verse --- and gives Ps.\ 73:23 as his
reason, \latin{Superbia eorum qui oderunt te}. \textbf{Five later Latin
witnesses repeat the sentence without his qualifier \latin{fere}} --- Rabanus,
the Glossa, Christian of Stavelot, Paschasius and the exposition printed under
Bede's name, one of them so damaged (\latin{scientia} for \latin{conscientia})
that the corruption can only be transmission --- \textbf{the Glossa alone flags
the retraction}, and \textbf{Bruno of Segni denies the proposition outright}:
the covetous man does hate God, because he serves idols, from Eph.\ 5:5.
\textbf{``No one can hate God'' is therefore neither the tradition's settled
reading at Mt.\ 6:24 nor Augustine's own last word on it.}

\textbf{The Communion half, and it is this claim's second appointed element.}
The formulary appoints Mt.\ 6:33 twice: once as the Gospel's last verse and
again, recast, as the Communion antiphon --- the seventh element standing on the
sixth locus. On that one verse nobody argues from a lesser example. The reading
the compilations transmit under Augustine's name makes \latin{primum} an
ordering of importance and not of time; \textbf{Paschasius denies that it orders
anything}, and elsewhere in the same place calls the sequence
\latin{praeposterus}, the kingdom put first to soothe the hearing before justice
is brought in as the laborious thing. \textbf{So the two appointed elements that
carry Matthew~6 in this Mass receive figure refused at the images and doctrine
read at the imperative --- and the sentence the missal lifted out to say at the
moment of reception is the one on which the tradition reads doctrine and then
divides about a single adverb.}

\textbf{Beneath it, a textual observation.} The Gospel opens on a conclusion
whose premises are not read --- the treasure and the eye at Mt.\ 6:19--23 ---
and closes one verse before Matthew's own close at v.~34; and \textbf{the
supplied incipit narrows the audience to the disciples where Mt.\ 5:1 has the
crowds present.}

\textbf{The missal's recasting promotes \latin{Primum} to the head of the
antiphon} --- exactly where the argument transmitted under Augustine's name
makes word order carry the doctrine, and no witness read here comments on the
antiphon as such. That promotion bears twice over: it measures the extent and
antiquity of the recasting, and it separates the two registers in which the
appointed Matthaean text is read.

\textbf{The Latin Fathers commenting on this very pericope are divided about
whether its central images may be allegorised at all} --- Hilary allegorises
them, Jerome and Augustine forbid it --- and page~1's allegorical row prints
that division under the names that hold it.

\textbf{Bruno
of Segni restricts the whole prohibition of anxiety to apostles, their
successors and those aiming at perfection, and Godfrey preaches it to monks
about \emph{lectio}}, so two of the witnesses read the pericope as addressed to
a narrower audience than the Sunday congregation. A Communion antiphon drawn
from the day's Gospel is ordinary practice, so
this second element may be a mechanical carry-over. That bears on how
\emph{interesting} the join is; it does not dissolve it, because the lesson and
the reception are two ritual moments whatever produced the antiphon.
\textbf{Both elements that carry this claim stand in Matthew~6}, and no third
element from another book stands behind it.

\subsection*{C4. Three strands, three schedules, four reckonings --- and the strand that never moved}

\emph{The orations of this Mass stand one Sunday later in the Gregorian books
and two later in the Frankish Gelasian; the chants carry this Sunday's own
number in the ninth-century antiphonaries; and the Epistle and Gospel stand
under a reckoning counted from St~Lawrence and were never cut anywhere but where
1962 cuts them.} \textbf{No witness says that this Mass stood at a different
Sunday}: what moved is one strand of it, and the three strands moved by
different amounts or not at all.

\textbf{The orations.} Gelasianum Book~III runs sixteen sections, and
sects.~I--XVI correspond to the 1962 Fifth through Twentieth Sundays on a
numeration base that counts from Trinity; each section's 1962 identity was fixed
from its own printed collect or Secret on the page image, never from position.
\textbf{Across sects.~I--XI Rheinau, S.~Gallen, Gerbert and Menard stand two
Sundays later, and across sects.~XII--XVI they stand four}, the offset surviving
a documented interpolation and resuming a new constant rather than dissolving;
\textbf{Pamelius stands one later across all sixteen without a break, and Menard
agrees with Rheinau, S.~Gallen and Gerbert at every readable section and never
with him.} This Mass's own orations are sect.~X, inside the run of two.
\textbf{Gerbert himself is open at his printed p.~173}, headed
\latin{Dominica XVI} --- in two independently produced Internet Archive optical
layers that agree verbatim, under eighteenth-century long-s type and with no
page image behind them ---
and the offset is \textbf{already present at the series' first section}, so
nothing inside the series produces it. The Gregorian Hadrianum heads this Mass
\latin{DOMINICA .XV. POST PENTECOSTEN}, one later than 1962, \textbf{confirmed
across eleven consecutive Masses on page images}, and \textbf{its sect.~XXXIII
carries all three orations of this Mass word for word.}

\textbf{The chants did not travel with them.} The ninth-century antiphonary
formulary carrying this Introit, Gradual and Offertory is numbered
\emph{Dominica XIV} --- \textbf{the same number 1962 gives it} --- and the
Ottobonianus margin reproduces those formularies \textbf{one number above} the
sacramentary headings they stand against, at twenty-five of twenty-five slots
across five consecutive Masses, the bracketed Alleluia cues matching nothing in
1962. \textbf{The cue block whose Introit is \latin{Protector noster} attaches
to Wilson's sect.~XXXI, \latin{DOMINICA .XIII. POST PENTECOSTEN}, whose Collect
is the 1962 Twelfth Sunday's:} an offset of two, read on a page image where the
superscript reference letters stand before the section numbers and not as part
of them.

\textbf{The readings never moved.} The \work{Liber Comitis} as Migne prints it
pairs \latin{Fratres, spiritu ambulate, usque concupiscentiis} with \latin{Nemo
potest duobus dominis servire, usque adiicientur vobis} under \latin{Dominica v
post sancti Laurentii} --- \textbf{the same incipits and the same termini the
1962 book prints.} So the Epistle--Gospel pair is not among the things that
moved.

\textbf{Two different mechanisms stand behind the instability.} Wilson's is a
numeration base, \latin{post Pentecosten} against
\latin{post Octavas Pentecostes}. \textbf{Schuster's is a Roman cycle built
around the feast of St~Lawrence}, whose count ``was not everywhere the same,''
the Lawrence cycle succeeded at Rome by cycles around St~Cyprian and St~Michael,
these feasts serving ``as so many milestones \dots\ and had no special
connection with the saint whose name they bore.'' \textbf{Four series are now
tracked and two of them disagree with each other by one:} the Comes calls this
Sunday the fifth after St~Lawrence and Schuster's title for the formulary is
\latin{Quarta post Sancti Laurentii}. \textbf{Neither mechanism has been shown
to produce the offsets Wilson collates, and the arithmetic joining them has not
been done.}

\textbf{And the reception shows the same displacement twice, independently.}
Honorius reads this formulary in one twelfth-century chapter whose Introit,
Collect, Epistle and Offertory are the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday's while his
Gospel is the Thirteenth's and his Gradual, Alleluia and Communion are the
Fifteenth's --- \textbf{three chants of five displaced, not five} --- and he
reads the whole formulary twice more, \latin{sub lege} under Ezechias and
\latin{sub gratia} under Theodosius, and a third time as calendar, tying the
Offertory to the September night office's \emph{historia} of Tobias and Raphael
and to Michaelmas. \textbf{Godfrey of Admont expounds this
formulary's Introit, Gradual, Alleluia psalm and Offertory in order and closes
by praying its Collect --- and his Gospel is the ten lepers.}

Four of the five Gelasian books are unopened at the locus, and for those four
the disagreement rests on Wilson's apparatus; the fifth, Gerbert, is open only
in optical text. His footnote
at sect.~X covers \textbf{``the Collects and Secret'' only}, and the 1962
Postcommunion has a materially narrower Gelasian base, attested for Rheinau and
Gerbert but \textbf{not} for S.~Gallen, Pamelius or Menard. \textbf{The
Gelasian displacement is therefore a claim about two of the formulary's ten
elements, and prose treating the three orations as equally attested from that
side would be wrong.} The four books agreeing are not four independent
witnesses: Gerbert's principal manuscript cannot now be traced and Wilson holds
its ``Gelasian'' portions to be a revision based on the Gregorian type, while
Pamelius is itself an edition of the Gregorian book --- \textbf{so its reading
and the Gregorian numbering are not two witnesses.} \textbf{Schuster does not
corroborate the displacement:} his three pages on this Mass name no station, no
sacramentary siglum and no manuscript, and do not say the Communion is
secondary. The antiphonary side is a modern database and not Hesbert's printed
pages, which are unreachable here for a recorded rights reason; the 1962-side
identifications are against a repository derivative and not a facsimile
collation; the \work{Liber Comitis}'s own date and provenance are \textbf{not
established}; and the run of the two-Sunday offset breaks at Dominica XXII and
XXIII for a reason nobody has established.

\subsection*{C5. The formulary makes exactly one substantive exchange, it is at the Communion, it is older than this book, and it broke a pair}

The Communion departs from Mt.\ 6:33 in exactly four ways, verified against the
Clementine, the Lucan parallel and the tracked Robinson-Pierpont Greek:
\latin{ergo} is dropped and \latin{Primum} promoted to head the clause;
\latin{et iustítiam eius} is omitted; \latin{hæc} is dropped before
\latin{ómnia}; and \latin{dicit Dóminus} is added. That tag stands in none of
Clementine Mt.\ 6:33, Clementine Lk.\ 12:31 or Robinson-Pierpont Mt.\ 6:33.
\textbf{Nor does the Lucan parallel supply the fronted \latin{primum}}: the
tracked Byzantine Luke carries neither \emph{proton} nor \emph{kai ten
dikaiosynen autou}, so any comparison with Luke stands on the Clementine's Latin
alone and is attributed to it.

Two of the four bear theologically. Adding \latin{dicit Dóminus} turns a saying
inside a sermon into \textbf{an address made to the communicant at the moment
of reception}. Dropping \latin{et iustítiam eius} leaves the kingdom to be
sought without the justice the Gospel names beside it.

\textbf{The antiphon is secondary in this slot only, and not in the
repertory.} \latin{Primum quaerite} is an ancient Roman antiphon standing at the
\textbf{Ninth} Sunday in four ninth-century antiphonaries, where the Fourteenth
Sunday's Communion is \latin{Panis quem ego dedero}. \textbf{And that Communion
travels with the Offertory \latin{Immittet Angelus} at both its occurrences} ---
the Lenten Thursday and this Sunday --- \textbf{so substituting \latin{Primum
quaerite} here broke a pair the older books kept together.} Five witnesses now
converge on the substitution: the appointed text itself, this being the
formulary's one substantive departure from its Bible text; Guéranger's report
that the antiphon ``was not the one primitively used; the ancient liturgists
make no mention of it in its present position''; the Ottobonianus cue block
whose Introit is this Mass's own and whose Communion is \latin{Panis quem ego};
Honorius, whose Communion here is \latin{Panis, quem ego dabo}; and the
antiphonary formularies themselves. \textbf{Guéranger's judgment is confirmed in
substance from a source he did not have.}

The strongest single control comes from within the same Office and anyone can
check it in the same pages: Guéranger prints this Sunday's Magnificat antiphon
at Vespers as \latin{Quaerite primum regnum Dei, et justitiam ejus, et haec
omnia adjicientur vobis. Alleluia.} \textbf{That antiphon retains both
\latin{et iustítiam eius} and \latin{haec} --- exactly the two clauses the
Mass's Communion drops --- and leaves \latin{primum} in its Matthaean position
after the verb.}

\textbf{No witness explains why the exchange was made, and no witness dates
it.} What is fixed is that it is later than the ninth-century antiphonaries,
later than the Ottobonianus margin and later than Honorius, and earlier than
the Pustet Missal of Ratisbon, 1862, which prints the recast form already.
Guéranger's judgment rests on Tommasi's Antiphonary and ``the ancient
liturgists,'' neither consulted. Antiphon texts are routinely shortened for
chant and \latin{dicit Dóminus} is a common formulaic tag, so both changes may
be conventional rather than expressive, and that count was not run.
\textbf{The antiphonary evidence is a modern database and not Hesbert's
pages.}

\textbf{Two further negatives, and they bound how the Communion may be read.}
First, \textbf{the Communion carries no eating vocabulary}: the appointed text
is \latin{Primum quaerite regnum Dei}, and the verbs of eating that the
Offertory's \latin{Gustate} makes central belong to the Offertory alone and to
the Eucharistic reception the antiphon accompanies; nothing in the
Communion's own wording invites a Eucharistic reading. Second, \textbf{the law
and \latin{adversus} language is confined to
the Epistle}: the Communion's one addition, \latin{dicit Dóminus}, is an address
to the communicant and not a legal exclusion. Both negatives are bounded to the
appointed elements.

\subsection*{C6. Two vocabularies partition the Mass, and the silence they produce is a genre fact everywhere but in the readings}

\latin{Salut-} and \latin{salv-} occur four times, once each in the Collect
(\latin{salutária}), the Alleluia (\latin{salutári}), the Secret
(\latin{salutáris}) and the Postcommunion (\latin{salvatiónis}), \textbf{and in
none of the six other elements}; \latin{propitia-} twice, Collect and Secret
only; \latin{semper} and \latin{perpétu-} twice each, Collect and Postcommunion
only. \textbf{At stem level \latin{salut-} and \latin{regnum} are the only
substantive ties reaching three or more elements, and the two partition the
formulary cleanly:} \latin{regnum} in the read and sung word, \latin{salut-} in
the three orations plus the one chant and in no reading at all. \textbf{The
people's texts state the problem as service, trust and anxiety; the priest's
prayers state it as expiation and rescue.}

\textbf{The silence about temporal goods is a fact about the genre.} No Collect,
Secret or Postcommunion of any of the fifteen 1962 identities this repository
has collated --- forty-five orations --- asks for food, drink or clothing, so
this Sunday's silence about the three goods its Gospel names is not a
distinctive of this formulary. \textbf{And Roman orations of this season are not
uniformly non-temporal:} the Fourth Sunday's Collect asks that \latin{mundi
cursus pacifice nobis tuo ordine dirigatur}, the Nuptial Mass's Postcommunion
asks \latin{longaeva pace}, Trinity Sunday's asks \latin{ad salutem corporis et
animae}, and the Eleventh Sunday's asks \latin{subsidium mentis et corporis}.
\textbf{So these orations are silent about temporal goods generally and not only
about the Gospel's three.}

\textbf{The singularity lies on the reading side.} Among the same fifteen
identities, the Fourteenth Sunday is the \textbf{only} one whose appointed texts
name eating, drinking \emph{and} clothing together --- \latin{manducetis},
\latin{manducabimus}, \latin{esca}, \latin{bibemus}, \latin{induamini},
\latin{operiemur}, \latin{vestimento}, all in the Gospel. \textbf{So the
objects of petition and the objects of promise differ, and what is distinctive
about this Mass is what it reads and not what it asks.}

\textbf{And the address differs with the vocabulary.} The three orations never
command the assembly, their material addressed to God being otherwise
subjunctival; \textbf{the one command the Mass repeats at the moment of
reception is the promise's own \latin{Quǽrite}.}

The fifteen identities behind that count are a small and non-random set, so it
is evidence about them and not about the \work{Missale Romanum}. \textbf{And
\latin{salutáris} of a victim, \latin{salutári} of God, \latin{salutária} of
things that save and \latin{salvatiónis} of a state are one root in four senses},
so the four-fold count is a fact about the root and not about a single idea.
``Temporal'' against ``spiritual'' is a judgment and not a measurement.

\subsection*{What this formulary does not say}

\textbf{The Holy Spirit is named in exactly one of the ten appointed elements.}
The \latin{spiritu-} stem occurs five times in the Epistle and nowhere else; the
Collect, Secret and Postcommunion never name the Spirit. The printed rubric
appoints the Preface of the Most Holy Trinity, so the Mass \emph{as celebrated}
names the Spirit where its proper does not, and that is how far into this
formulary a Spirit-centred reading reaches. Beyond that, across the same ten
elements: no parousia, resurrection or judgment vocabulary past the
Epistle's \latin{regnum Dei non consequéntur} and the Postcommunion's
\latin{perpétuæ salvatiónis}; no saint named and no Marian reference; no
penitential, fasting or almsgiving language, despite a seventeen-item vice
list; one angel named, in the Offertory, and no other created spirit; and the
Church named \textbf{once}, in the Collect --- though the first-person plurals
of Introit, Alleluia, Secret and Postcommunion are corporate in their own way,
which softens any contrast built on that count.

\textbf{And two apparent ties are not ties.} \latin{Conte-} joins the Epistle's
\latin{contentiónes} to the Gospel's \latin{contémnet} and \latin{oper-} joins
its \latin{ópera carnis} to \latin{quo operiémur}, in each case across two
different verbs; and the Introit's \latin{gloria} is the doxology cue and not
the psalm's word. \textbf{No further substantive tie joins two of the ten
elements at token level.}

\subsection*{Two joints the appointed texts do not make}

\textbf{The Alleluia is not joined to the rest by its own vocabulary.} Its only
substantive tie to any other element is \latin{salutári}, and \latin{exsult-}
and \latin{iubil-} occur nowhere else in the ten. \textbf{No lexical warrant
joins the Alleluia's joy to the Gospel's anxiety}:
the Epistle's \latin{gaudium} and the Alleluia's \latin{exsultémus} are
different roots in different elements with nothing joining them. \textbf{Nor
does the reception supply it}: the medieval and later readers of this psalm
reached here --- a homily of the Chrysostomic corpus, Arnobius the Younger,
Bruno the Carthusian and Peter Lombard --- all read it penitentially,
and every one of them reaches that reading through v.~2,
\latin{Praeoccupemus faciem eius in confessione}, which the Alleluia does not
appoint.

\textbf{Whether the Epistle and the Gospel were ever read together in the
tradition.} \textbf{The lectionary answers half of it and no witness answers the
rest.} The \work{Liber Comitis} pairs these two lessons at these two extents, so
the pairing is old; but the library's sole registered treatment of Gal.\
5:16--24 is Anthony of Padua's sermon, and his Sunday numbering runs one ahead
of the 1962 missal's, consistently across all three of his registered sermons.
\textbf{A major medieval preacher expounding this Epistle does so beside a
different Gospel.} Every cross-element claim joining Epistle and Gospel is
therefore a claim about how the 1962 formulary reads when taken as a whole, and
not a claim about how the tradition expounded these two texts together.
