\section*{Source-Grounded Synthesis Across the Propers}

\subsection*{C1. One phrase carries the Mass, and the element that repeats it is the element that cuts it}

\latin{Regnum Dei} stands verbatim in three appointed elements and carries one
argument across them. The Epistle names who is \emph{excluded} from the
kingdom, \latin{qui tália agunt, regnum Dei non consequéntur}; the Gospel
commands that it be \emph{sought first}; and the Communion repeats that command
as \emph{the Lord's own word at the moment of reception}. The negative and the
positive are two faces of one claim about the kingdom, not two positions set
against each other.

Four textual facts sharpen it. \latin{Regnum Dei} is \textbf{rare in Matthew}:
four verses in the whole Gospel (6:33, 12:28, 21:31, 21:43) against
thirty-two verses carrying \latin{regnum caelorum}, thirty-four occurrences in
all, with Mt.\ 6:33 the first of the four. It is
the \textbf{only} occurrence of the phrase in Galatians, \textbf{but Paul uses
it elsewhere}: 1~Cor.~6:9--10, Eph.~5:5 and
1~Cor.~15:50 form a small closed set of Pauline parallels closing vice lists
with the same exclusion, \textbf{none of the three is appointed, and nothing in
the ten elements points to them}; their standing is as the canonical context of
the appointed wording. \textbf{The tie survives into the Greek}, both texts
reading \emph{basileia theou}, which the Epistle-to-Gospel \latin{sérvitus} /
\latin{servíre} bridge does not. And across the fifteen 1962 formularies whose
appointed text this guide has collated, \latin{regnum Dei} occurs in
\textbf{this formulary alone}, at exactly three loci.

\textbf{\latin{Regnum} is the only exact form reaching three or more of the
ten elements that is neither a divine name nor a function word} --- but at stem
level \latin{salut-} and \latin{salv-} together reach four of the ten, and
\latin{salut-} alone reaches three; both are substantive. The two roots
partition the formulary rather than competing: \latin{regnum} in the read and
sung word, the \latin{salut-} and \latin{salv-} root in the three orations and
one chant.

\textbf{And the element that repeats the phrase is the element that cuts it.}
\latin{Iustítia} stands \textbf{once} in the whole appointed Latin, in the
Gospel's last verse --- and the Communion, quoting that very verse, drops it.
\textbf{The clause it drops is the second member of a Matthean inclusio}, the
first standing at Mt.\ 6:1, \latin{Attendite ne justitiam vestram faciatis
coram hominibus}, \textbf{though the arc is a property of the Vulgate's reading
there, Robinson-Pierpont's Byzantine text reading \emph{alms}}; and the added
\latin{dicit Dóminus} stands nowhere in Matthew.

At the hinge stands Augustine's reading of \latin{primum}, and it is contested.
\textbf{He reads it at his own locus} --- \work{De sermone Domini in monte}
II.53, in the English of the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, first
series: ``in using that word \emph{first}, He has indicated that this is to be
sought later, not in point of time, but in point of importance,'' which
Rabanus Maurus's compilation on these verses carries in Latin as \latin{non
tempore, sed dignitate} at \work{Patrologia Latina}~107:836B--839A --- so a
compilation's Latin answers the English, under sigla that are Migne's editorial
work and not the compiler's. He makes the point by denying the sequence the word
invites, ``He did not say, \emph{Then} seek such things,'' and at II.56 reads
the promise's own grammar as grace, the addition following ``if you seek the
former, without any hindrance on your part.'' \textbf{Paschasius
Radbertus denies that \latin{primum} marks order at all}: \latin{non ad ordinem
referendum puto \dots\ magis ad quantitatem referendum est, ut extra hoc nihil
quaerendum sit}. The
verb the antiphon keeps has its own readers --- pseudo-Bede's \latin{Non dixit
nobis dari, sed adjici}, Christian of Stavelot's \latin{subauditur necessaria
\dots\ Ad necessitatem, non ad superfluitatem}, Paschasius's \latin{his utamur
in via, quatenus illo fruamur in patria} --- and Godfrey of Admont reads
\latin{regnum Dei} at this verse as the heart, which ought always to be God's
kingdom. \textbf{Chrysostom rests the whole promise on that verb}, in Greek at
\work{Patrologia Graeca} 57:303: he said not \emph{shall be given} but
\emph{shall be added}, ``that you may learn that the things present are nothing
great, set beside the greatness of the things to come,'' in the \work{Nicene
and Post-Nicene Fathers} English, first series. \textbf{Schuster,
commenting on the antiphon itself, reaches back for the clause the recasting
removed} and glosses the justice from the Gospel verse rather than from the
antiphon.

What the omission does is remove the formulary's only occurrence of
\latin{iustítia}. \textbf{That the omission was deliberate is established by
nothing, and one fact cuts against reading it as significant:} Chrysostom's own
citation of Mt.\ 6:33 drops \latin{et iustítiam eius} exactly as the antiphon
does --- while keeping the kingdom \emph{of heaven} where the antiphon keeps
\latin{regnum Dei} and fronting no \latin{primum} at all --- \textbf{so an
abbreviation of this shape is ordinary, and nothing supports dependence in
either direction.} Fifteen 1962 formularies whose appointed text this guide has
collated stand behind that count. A
Communion antiphon drawn from the day's own Gospel is ordinary practice, so were
this one's \latin{regnum Dei} a purely mechanical carry-over the three-element
spine would reduce to a two-element one plus an echo --- which would still leave
the Epistle's occurrence standing, since it comes from a different book and a
different pericope. And the one medieval preacher located on this Epistle
expounds it beside a different Gospel.

\subsection*{C2. The flesh that is crucified is not the body that is fed, and the tradition says so in Greek and in Latin by three different middle terms}

The two lessons are \textbf{lexically disjoint}: \latin{caro}/\latin{carn-}
stands five times in the Epistle and nowhere else in the formulary;
\latin{corp-} stands twice in the Gospel and nowhere else. \textbf{This is one
unit, not a contrast.} The point is not that Epistle and Gospel disagree about
the body but that they use different words for different things, and that
reading them together dissolves the appearance of disagreement. \textbf{A
reading built as ``Epistle mortifies against Gospel provides'' has inverted
it.} \textbf{And the Introit's cut is what keeps the division clean:} its psalm
verse stops at \latin{in átria Dómini}, one clause before the Clementine's
\latin{cor meum et caro mea exsultaverunt in Deum vivum} --- the one appointed
element that would have given \latin{caro} a positive sense, severed exactly
before it, and Cassiodorus comments on that very clause outside the appointed
extract.

\textbf{Four positions now stand on what \latin{caro} is at Gal.\ 5:17, and
they are genuinely four.} \textbf{Chrysostom's Greek denies desire to
the flesh}: \emph{sarka gar entautha, ou to soma kalei} --- flesh here is the
earthbound, slothful reasoning; desire belongs to the soul, so a flesh that
desires cannot be the body; the body is an instrument, \emph{organon gar estin
he sarx}, and \emph{we hate and punish not the iron but the murderer}; the
conflict is between two reasonings and not \emph{ouch he psyche kai to soma}.
\textbf{Marius Victorinus} argues the contrary from the soulless elements:
\latin{Habet enim motus suos caro, habetque sensus, neque tantum ab anima
suscitantur}. \textbf{The Glossa ordinaria} makes the ascription a manner of
speaking about a joint act --- \latin{Caro dicta est concupiscere, quia hoc
secundum ipsam agit anima} --- with the cause in neither alone, \latin{ex
utroque enim fit}. \textbf{Ambrosiaster} shifts the ground entirely: not two
parts of man but two laws, \latin{unam Dei, alteram peccati}, with \latin{medius
homo} standing between them, the flesh's works so named by origin
(\latin{quia errores isti ex mundo sunt, ex quo et caro}), so that at v.~24 the
crucified flesh is the world. \textbf{Chrysostom and Victorinus comment on the
same appointed verse and cannot both be right.}

\textbf{Theodoret and Augustine reach the same conclusion by three further
middle terms, and no dependence between them is established.} Theodoret
comments verse by verse on the whole appointed pericope, and argues twice from
the content of the lists that Paul's flesh is not the body: idolatry and sorcery
are proper to the soul, so \emph{ou tes sarkos kategorei, alla tou rhathymou
phronematos}, he accuses not the flesh but the slothful cast of mind; and had
the works of v.~19 belonged to the body alone, a matching list of the soul's
works would have had to be set down beside them, and is not. \textbf{Augustine
reaches it by two others in \work{De civitate Dei} XIV.2}: a man who abstains
from bodily pleasure \emph{on account of} idolatry or heresy is convicted by
apostolic authority of damnable works of the flesh in the very act of that
abstinence, \latin{et in eo, quod abstinet a uoluptatibus carnis, damnabilia
opera carnis agere demonstratur}; and nobody says \latin{Malam carnem} to an
enemy, so Paul names the whole man by a part, \latin{eo locutionis modo, quo
totum significatur a parte}. \textbf{And Theodoret closes the appointed lesson
baptismally}, those buried with Christ having made the body dead to sin, which
sets the lesson's last verse and this Mass's own sacramental action on one axis.

\textbf{And Jerome supplies the argument from the missal's own word.} Where
the Latin translator put \latin{vitia} the Greek reads \emph{pathemata},
\emph{passiones}, and the Apostle added \latin{desideria} \textbf{precisely so
as not to seem to deny the nature of the body in spiritual men, but its
vices}: \latin{ut non naturam corporis videretur in spiritualibus viris negare,
sed vitia}. \textbf{A Father defending the appointed word on exactly this
ground is better evidence for this claim than any of the others, because it is
anchored in the word the missal prints.} Chrysostom adds at the lesson's last
verse that those of Christ did not destroy the flesh --- \latin{epei pos
emellon zen?} --- and that the desires, though they trouble, rage in vain.
Aquinas reaches the same conclusion by a different route again, through the
definition he cites from \work{De civitate Dei} XIV.2, that whoever lives
according to himself lives according to the flesh, so that \latin{caro} here
stands for the whole man.

\textbf{And the pericope itself supplies the convergence these routes point
to.} The lesson names only \latin{caro} and \latin{spiritus} and leaves the
soul unmentioned; \textbf{Chrysostom in Greek and Jerome in Latin
independently raise that difficulty and answer it the same way} --- the soul
lies between and makes the body spiritual or earthly by what it does with it.
\textbf{The two agree, and nothing shows dependence.}

Augustine supplies a leg from the other direction, and the reception is
\emph{controversial}. Certain monks quoted these very verses to argue
that they need not work, and \work{De opere monachorum} was written against
them, in the English of the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, first series:
``let these persons, who from perverse understanding of the Gospel labor
to pervert apostolic precepts, either take no thought for the morrow, even as
the birds of the air; or let them obey the Apostle.'' The \work{Catena aurea}
points to that work, and the work itself carries the passage. The
identification of those monks as the Euchitae rests on \work{De haeresibus},
the work that would name them, and no text of it stands behind the name.

Gal.\ 5:24's \latin{carnem suam crucifixérunt} is read by some
as including the body proper, and the Vulgate elsewhere uses \latin{caro} for
the body simply; the disjunction is a fact about these two pericopes as
printed, \textbf{not a lexical law}. \textbf{Ambrosiaster's Latin lemma is not
the missal's} --- his fruit-list has ten members, carrying \latin{spes} and
\latin{castitas} and lacking \latin{fides} and \latin{patientia}, and his
vice-list opens differently --- \textbf{so his exposition counts a different
pair of lists, and the missal's seventeen and twelve are not the lists he
expounds.} Marius Victorinus's text as Migne prints
it passes from v.~17 to 6:1, so he comments on none of vv.~18--24, and no critical
edition stands beside that printing. Chrysostom's Greek at these verses is a
transcription of \work{Patrologia Graeca}~61 and not the column itself, and Theodoret's
own lemma at v.~19 opens with the Greek for \emph{adultery}, which the missal's
list does not carry. \textbf{The appointed Latin gives twelve fruits where the
Greek witnesses give nine, and seventeen vices that are not the seventeen of the
King James: \latin{luxúria} is the Latin head-word with no distinct Greek
counterpart, and \latin{modéstia} and \latin{cástitas} are the two appointed
fruits that answer nothing in the Greek lists} --- so no Greek witness here
speaks to those three words, and the twelve and the nine are counts of two
different lists.

\subsection*{C3. The tradition is namedly divided about the Gospel's images, and its most-repeated sentence at the Gospel's first verse was withdrawn by the man who wrote it}

\textbf{The division is named, and it is not a difference of emphasis.} On the
anti-allegorical side: \textbf{Augustine at his own locus}, \work{De sermone
Domini in monte} II.52, in the English of the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene
Fathers}, first series --- the birds and the lilies ``are not to be treated as
allegories \dots\ for they stand here, in order that from smaller matters we may
be persuaded respecting greater ones,'' the sentence the compilations transmit
as \latin{ista documenta non sicut allegorica discutienda sunt} --- repeated by
the exposition printed under Bede's name; \textbf{Christian of Stavelot refusing allegory at
the lilies on frankly aesthetic grounds}, no linen matching the lily's
whiteness, no dye the rose, no purple the violet; Jerome forbidding the birds as
angels on a ground internal to the appointed verse, \latin{Simpliciter ergo
accipiendum}; and Bruno of Segni reading the lilies literally. On the
allegorical side, at its far end: \textbf{Godfrey of Admont makes the birds the
good angels, the lilies the evil spirits and Solomon Christ}, and then raises
and answers the objection his own allegory creates --- so he is a self-aware
instance and not a naive one. \textbf{Hilary allegorises every appointed image
into doctrine of the risen body}, holding that the literal reading does not
cohere, \latin{non satis propositionibus congruunt}. Chrysostom reads the images
as arguments too, but rhetorically engineered ones, the illustrations degraded
step by step so the \emph{a fortiori} grows at each --- and \textbf{where the
Fathers agree they still differ}, Augustine's middle term being the creature's
\textbf{rank} and Chrysostom's its \textbf{vileness}, given in terms of
\emph{gift} rather than of rank. \textbf{Chrysostom refuses a different
over-reading, and at three places}: not being anxious about these things is no
labour, being anxious is --- \emph{ou toinyn to me merimnan tauta ponos,
alla to merimnan} --- and Christ abolished not the sowing and the
spinning but the anxiety about them, \emph{ou ton sporon anetrepsen, alla ten
phrontida}, \emph{ou to ergon aneilen, alla ten merimnan}. \textbf{And Aquinas
reads the pericope's argument as three ascending motives} --- the greater gifts
already given, the care God takes of animals and plants without man's
assistance, and divine providence itself, ``through ignorance of which the
gentiles are solicitous in seeking temporal goods before all others''
(\work{Summa theologiae} II-II q.~55 a.~6, in the English of the Fathers of the
English Dominican Province). \textbf{Augustine's refusal is a
refusal about the birds and the lilies and not a general rule}, so it does not
become a Fathers-wide position; and whether the division is about method or
about this pericope only is not established, the two sides nowhere answering
each other.

\textbf{And the sentence the Latin tradition repeats most often at this
Gospel's first verse was withdrawn by its author.} \latin{Nullius enim fere
conscientia Deum potest odisse} is retracted at \work{Retractationes} I.19,
where Augustine names \work{De sermone Domini in monte} II.14.48 --- his
exposition of the appointed Gospel's first verse --- and gives Ps.\ 73:23 as his
reason, \latin{Superbia eorum qui oderunt te}. \textbf{Five later Latin
witnesses repeat the sentence without his qualifier \latin{fere}} --- Rabanus,
the Glossa, Christian of Stavelot, Paschasius and the exposition printed under
Bede's name, one of them so damaged (\latin{scientia} for \latin{conscientia})
that the corruption can only be transmission --- \textbf{the Glossa alone flags
the retraction}, and \textbf{Bruno of Segni denies the proposition outright}:
the covetous man does hate God, because he serves idols, from Eph.\ 5:5.
\textbf{``No one can hate God'' is therefore neither the tradition's settled
reading at Mt.\ 6:24 nor Augustine's own last word on it.}

\textbf{The Communion half, and it is this claim's second appointed element.}
The formulary appoints Mt.\ 6:33 twice: once as the Gospel's last verse and
again, recast, as the Communion antiphon --- the seventh scriptural element
standing on the fifth of the six appointed loci. On that one verse nobody argues
from a lesser example. Augustine, at his own locus above, makes \latin{primum} an
ordering of importance and not of time; \textbf{Paschasius denies that it orders
anything}, and elsewhere in the same place calls the sequence
\latin{praeposterus}, the kingdom put first to soothe the hearing before justice
is brought in as the laborious thing. \textbf{So the two appointed elements that
carry Matthew~6 in this Mass receive figure refused at the images and doctrine
read at the imperative --- and the sentence the missal lifted out to say at the
moment of reception is the one on which the tradition reads doctrine and then
divides about a single adverb.}

\textbf{Beneath it, a textual observation.} The Gospel opens on a conclusion
whose premises are not read --- the treasure and the eye at Mt.\ 6:19--23 ---
and closes one verse before Matthew's own close at v.~34; and \textbf{the
supplied incipit narrows the audience to the disciples where Mt.\ 5:1 has the
crowds present.} \textbf{The images have Old Testament texts behind them and
Lucan parallels beside them, and Matthew quotes none of them}: Is.\ 40:6--8,
Ps.\ 89:6 and Ps.\ 102:15--16 behind the grass, Ps.\ 146:9 and Job 38:41 behind
the birds, 3~Kings 10:4--7 and 10:23 behind Solomon, and Ps.\ 54:23 with
1~Pet.\ 5:7 behind the \latin{sollicitudo}. They stand as background and none
is a citation the appointed text makes.

\textbf{The missal's recasting promotes \latin{Primum} to the head of the
antiphon} --- exactly where the argument transmitted under Augustine's name
makes word order carry the doctrine, and none of the witnesses above comments
on the antiphon as such. That promotion bears twice over: it measures the extent and
antiquity of the recasting, and it separates the two registers in which the
appointed Matthaean text is read.

\textbf{The Latin Fathers commenting on this very pericope are divided about
whether its central images may be allegorised at all} --- Hilary allegorises
them, Jerome and Augustine forbid it.

\textbf{Bruno
of Segni restricts the whole prohibition of anxiety to apostles, their
successors and those aiming at perfection, and Godfrey preaches it to monks
about \emph{lectio}}, so two of the witnesses read the pericope as addressed to
a narrower audience than the Sunday congregation. A Communion antiphon drawn
from the day's Gospel is ordinary practice, so
this second element may be a mechanical carry-over. That bears on how
\emph{interesting} the join is; it does not dissolve it, because the lesson and
the reception are two ritual moments whatever produced the antiphon.
\textbf{Both elements that carry this claim stand in Matthew~6}, and no third
element from another book stands behind it.

\subsection*{C4. Three strands, three schedules, four reckonings --- and the strand that never moved}

\emph{The orations of this Mass stand one Sunday later in the Gregorian books
and two later in the Frankish Gelasian; the chants carry this Sunday's own
number in the ninth-century antiphonaries; and the Epistle and Gospel stand
under a reckoning counted from St~Lawrence and were never cut anywhere but where
1962 cuts them.} \textbf{No witness says that this Mass stood at a different
Sunday}: what moved is one strand of it, and the three strands moved by
different amounts or not at all.

\textbf{The orations.} Gelasianum Book~III runs sixteen sections, and
sects.~I--XVI correspond to the 1962 Fifth through Twentieth Sundays on a
numeration base and not by interpolation; each section's 1962 identity is that
of its own printed collect or Secret and never of its position. Wilson names
the mechanism in a footnote --- the \latin{post Pentecosten} / \latin{post
Octavas Pentecostes} equivalence, arising from a discrepancy between the first
and supplementary portions of Muratori's text --- and \textbf{which single
Sunday each tradition counts as its first is not established}, so the
decomposition of the two units of offset is not settled.
\textbf{Across sects.~I--XI Rheinau, S.~Gallen, Gerbert and Menard stand two
Sundays later, and across sects.~XII--XVI they stand four}, the offset surviving
a documented interpolation and resuming a new constant rather than dissolving;
\textbf{Pamelius stands one later across all sixteen without a break, and Menard
agrees with Rheinau, S.~Gallen and Gerbert at every readable section and never
with him.} This Mass's own orations are sect.~X, inside the run of two.
\textbf{Gerbert's own printed p.~173 heads this Mass \latin{Dominica XVI}} ---
an eighteenth-century printed text and not a manuscript, set in long-s type --- and the offset is \textbf{already present at the series'
first section}, so nothing inside the series produces it. The Gregorian
Hadrianum heads this Mass \latin{DOMINICA .XV. POST PENTECOSTEN}, one later than
1962, \textbf{confirmed across eleven consecutive Masses, each identified from
its own printed collect}, and \textbf{its sect.~XXXIII carries all three
orations of this Mass word for word.}

\textbf{And the two Gregorian editions that disagree about this Sunday's number
are editions of two different manuscripts.} Pamelius first published the
Grimoldus codex and Menard's base is the Rodradus codex, and Menard argues at
length in his preface that the two are separate compilations --- \textbf{so the
disagreement between them is between two books and not two editorial habits},
though he is an interested party arguing for his own manuscript's Gregorian
authenticity. \textbf{And the oration set does not travel as a unit
everywhere}: Menard's \latin{Hebdomada XVI} carries this Collect with a
different Super oblata and a different Ad complendum, while his very next
section carries all three of the 1962 Fifteenth Sunday's orations together.
\textbf{Menard's volume prints no occurrence of the 1962 Postcommunion at all};
that is a real divergence between two Gregorian witnesses, and the silence is
the printed book's and not Menard's manuscripts'.

\textbf{The chants did not travel with them, and the strands come apart inside
one book.} In the Roman antiphonary printed at \work{Patrologia Latina}~78 the
\textbf{Introit and Offertory series agree with 1962 at every week from the
seventh to the eighteenth} --- a run whose first week that book's own body marks
\latin{Ista hebdomada non est in Antiphonario Romano} --- while the
\textbf{Gradual series runs one week behind across a long run and breaks at
exactly the two weeks this Sunday and its neighbour occupy}, and \textbf{the
Alleluia agrees with 1962 at only two of twelve weeks --- and at both of those
the collated manuscripts disagree with the printed text}, so that agreement is
evidence about the editor's exemplar rather than about the ninth-century state.
\textbf{The week-numbering of that book is itself disturbed across the same
run}: its footnote a reports Theodericus's office of the seventh Sunday standing
at the eighth and the eighth's at the ninth, \latin{sicque consequenter}, while
footnotes on the same leaf report his Alleluia at week~$N$ as the printed book's
at $N{+}1$, \textbf{so a Theodericensian reading printed at a given week is not
established as Theodericus's own week.} The ninth-century antiphonary formulary
carrying this Introit, Gradual and Offertory is numbered
\emph{Dominica XIV} --- \textbf{the same number 1962 gives it} --- and the
Ottobonianus margin reproduces those formularies \textbf{one number above} the
sacramentary headings they stand against, at twenty-five of twenty-five slots
across five consecutive Masses, the bracketed Alleluia cues matching nothing in
1962. \textbf{The cue block whose Introit is \latin{Protector noster} attaches
to Wilson's sect.~XXXI, \latin{DOMINICA .XIII. POST PENTECOSTEN}, whose Collect
is the 1962 Twelfth Sunday's:} an offset of two, the superscript reference
letters on that page standing before the section numbers and not as part of
them.

\textbf{The readings never moved.} The \work{Liber Comitis} as Migne prints it
pairs \latin{Fratres, spiritu ambulate, usque concupiscentiis} with \latin{Nemo
potest duobus dominis servire, usque adiicientur vobis} under \latin{Dominica v
post sancti Laurentii} --- \textbf{the same incipits and the same termini the
1962 book prints.} So the Epistle--Gospel pair is not among the things that
moved. \textbf{But the two reading strands were not numbered alike even in the
seventh-century Roman books}: the Würzburg gospel capitulary numbers this Gospel
\latin{ebd.\ IIII post sci.\ Laur.}, the fourth after St~Lawrence, while
\textbf{the Würzburg epistle list numbers Gal.\ 5:16--24 not at all}, laying
its forty Pauline pericopes out in canonical order without titles, and Wilmart's
reconstruction makes it the fifth of the five post-Laurentian epistles.
\textbf{So the \work{Liber Comitis}'s ``fifth'' and Schuster's ``fourth'' are
two books counting differently and neither is wrong}, and the asymmetry between
the reading strands reaches back to the Roman books themselves. Alcuin's fifth
is a reconstruction keyed to Würzburg item numbers and not a heading read in a
manuscript.

\textbf{And a fourth tradition splits this formulary along exactly that line.}
The Sarum Missal gives the whole 1962 oration set and the 1962 Gospel at
\latin{Dominica XV post Trinitatem} and the 1962 Introit, Epistle and Offertory
at \latin{Dominica XIV} --- \textbf{two offsets that must be kept apart}, since
calendrically Trinity~$N$ is Rome's Sunday~$N{+}1$ while internally Sarum's
Collect, Gospel, Secret and Postcommunion at Trinity~$N$ are Rome's at
Sunday~$N{-}1$.

\textbf{Four mechanisms stand behind the instability and no two have been shown
to be the same.} Wilson's is a numeration base, \latin{post Pentecosten} against
\latin{post Octavas Pentecostes}. \textbf{Schuster's is a Roman cycle built
around the feast of St~Lawrence}, whose count ``was not everywhere the same,''
the Lawrence cycle succeeded at Rome by cycles around St~Cyprian and St~Michael,
these feasts serving ``as so many milestones \dots\ and had no special
connection with the saint whose name they bore.'' \textbf{Berno of Reichenau's
is a difference in the number of Masses}: the compiler of the chant-offices
provided twenty-three Sundays between the octave of Pentecost and Advent and the
compiler of the lectionary twenty-five, because one computed on the embolismic
years of the nineteen-year cycle and the other on the common years --- which is
the shape of a book whose strands run on different schedules. \textbf{And the
\work{Micrologus} names a fourth}, the vacant Sundays after the Ember fasts,
whose mishandling disturbs the order of the offices as far as Advent, the Romans
making up the shortfall by repeating a Sunday's office rather than deferring it. \textbf{Four numbering series run through these
books} --- 1962's \latin{post Pentecosten}, the Gregorian sacramentary's at
1962$+$1, the Frankish Gelasian's at 1962$+$2, and the \latin{post sancti
Laurentii} reckoning --- \textbf{and inside that fourth one two books disagree
by one:} the Comes calls this Sunday the
fifth after St~Lawrence and Schuster's title for the formulary is \latin{Quarta
post Sancti Laurentii}, and Schuster himself says the count varied between
books. \textbf{None of the four mechanisms has been shown to produce the
offsets Wilson collates, and none has been shown to be what any compiler
actually did}: Berno writes four centuries after the arrangements he explains
and offers a rationale rather than a record, and the \work{Micrologus}
prescribes against a practice it disapproves.

\textbf{And the pairing the 1962 book does not have is a lectionary tradition,
not one commentator's displacement.} Honorius reads this formulary in one
twelfth-century chapter whose Introit, Collect, Epistle and Offertory are the
1962 Fourteenth Sunday's while his Gospel is the Thirteenth's and his Gradual,
Alleluia and Communion are the Fifteenth's --- \textbf{three chants of five
displaced, not five} --- and that chapter, \latin{sub lege} under Ezechias, is
one of three: he reads the whole formulary again \latin{sub gratia} under
Theodosius, and a third time as calendar, tying the Offertory to the September
night office's \emph{historia} of Tobias and Raphael and to Michaelmas. \textbf{Godfrey of
Admont expounds this formulary's Introit, Gradual, Alleluia psalm and Offertory
in order and closes by praying its Collect --- and his Gospel is the ten
lepers.} \textbf{Beside those two stand seven further witnesses to the same
arrangement, across four centuries and three kinds of book, so Honorius is no
outlier.} \textbf{Rupert of Deutz, Sicard of Cremona and Durandus} all set
this Sunday's Epistle beside the ten lepers and put
Matthew~6 on the Fifteenth, with its Introit, Gradual and Offertory expounded
by Rupert and Sicard; \textbf{the eighth-century
Comes of Murbach has the same pairing}, as one instance of a whole systematic
pattern of epistle~$N$ with gospel~$N{-}1$ rather than as a peculiarity here;
\textbf{the Sarum Missal and the Book of Common Prayer of 1662 still carry
it}, the latter with the marginals \latin{Gal.\ 5.\ 16.} and \latin{S: Luke.\
17.\ 11.}; and \textbf{\work{Patrologia Latina}~78's Roman Mass antiphonary
carries it at the Communion alone}, giving this Sunday \latin{Panis quem ego
dedero}. \textbf{When or why
the Roman books moved to the pairing 1962 prints, none of them says.} Whether
the English books inherit from the Frankish comes, whether both descend from a
common Roman state, or whether Rome's own pairing is the innovation, is not
established. \textbf{And Durandus's own chant set for this Sunday is not the
Roman one but Sarum's and Compiègne's} --- Gradual \latin{Bonum est confiteri
Domino}, Alleluia \latin{Confitemini Domino et invocate nomen eius}, Communion
\latin{Panis quem ego dedero} --- against Rupert and Sicard, who have the Roman
Gradual; no filiation between the three is established, and Compiègne stands
with Rome at the Gradual and against it at the Alleluia.

For four of the five sacramentary books --- Rheinau, S.~Gallen, Pamelius and
Menard --- the disagreement stands on Wilson's apparatus and not on their own
pages; the fifth, Gerbert, prints the Mass himself. Wilson's footnote
at sect.~X covers \textbf{``the Collects and Secret'' only}, and the 1962
Postcommunion has a materially narrower Gelasian base, attested for Rheinau and
Gerbert but \textbf{not} for S.~Gallen, Pamelius or Menard. \textbf{The
Gelasian displacement is therefore a claim about two of the formulary's ten
elements, the Collect and the Secret, and not about all three orations.} The four books agreeing are not four independent
witnesses: Gerbert's principal manuscript cannot now be traced and Wilson holds
its ``Gelasian'' portions to be a revision based on the Gregorian type, while
Pamelius is itself an edition of the Gregorian book --- \textbf{so its reading
and the Gregorian numbering are not two witnesses.} \textbf{Schuster does not
corroborate the displacement:} his three pages on this Mass name no station, no
sacramentary siglum and no manuscript, and do not say the Communion is
secondary. The antiphonary readings are a modern database's report of Hesbert's
\work{Antiphonale Missarum Sextuplex} and not his printed pages, whose rights
record closes them; what stands on the 1962 side is that database's record and not a
facsimile; the \work{Liber Comitis}'s
own date and provenance are \textbf{not established}; and the run of the
two-Sunday offset breaks at Dominica XXII and XXIII for a reason nobody has
established. \textbf{Pamelius and the Maurists are the whole of what Compiègne,
Mont-Blandin and the Theodericensian codices say here}, and each of the three
speaks through an editor's footnote reporting a reading rather than through a
transcription of the manuscript, so what any of them carries at a given week is
that editor's report of it.

\subsection*{C5. The formulary makes exactly one substantive exchange, it is at the Communion, it is older than this book, and it broke a pair}

The Communion departs in exactly four ways from Mt.\ 6:33 as the Clementine,
the Lucan parallel and Robinson-Pierpont's Byzantine Greek carry it:
\latin{ergo} is dropped and \latin{Primum} promoted to head the clause;
\latin{et iustítiam eius} is omitted; \latin{hæc} is dropped before
\latin{ómnia}; and \latin{dicit Dóminus} is added. That tag stands in none of
Clementine Mt.\ 6:33, Clementine Lk.\ 12:31 or Robinson-Pierpont Mt.\ 6:33.
\textbf{Nor does the Lucan parallel supply the fronted \latin{primum}}:
Robinson-Pierpont's Byzantine Luke carries neither \emph{proton} nor \emph{kai ten
dikaiosynen autou}, so any comparison with Luke rests on the Clementine's Latin
alone.

Two of the four bear theologically. Adding \latin{dicit Dóminus} turns a saying
inside a sermon into \textbf{an address made to the communicant at the moment
of reception}. Dropping \latin{et iustítiam eius} leaves the kingdom to be
sought without the justice the Gospel names beside it.

\textbf{The antiphon is secondary in this slot only, and not in the
repertory.} \latin{Primum quaerite} is an ancient Roman antiphon standing at the
\textbf{Ninth} Sunday in four ninth-century antiphonaries, where the Fourteenth
Sunday's Communion is \latin{Panis quem ego dedero}. \textbf{And that Communion
travels with the Offertory \latin{Immittet Angelus} at both its occurrences} ---
the Lenten Thursday and this Sunday --- \textbf{so substituting \latin{Primum
quaerite} here broke a pair the older books kept together.}

\textbf{All three legs of that rotation stand in the body of a printed chant
book, and not in a margin.} The Roman Mass antiphonary printed at
\work{Patrologia Latina}~78 gives \latin{Primum quaerite regnum Dei, et omnia
adjicientur vobis, dicit Dominus} --- \textbf{including the \latin{dicit
Dominus} Matthew does not have} --- to \latin{Hebdomada nona}, \latin{Panis
quem ego dedero} to the fourteenth and \latin{Qui manducat carnem meam} to the
fifteenth. \textbf{1962 reverses all three.} Witnesses now converge on the
substitution: the appointed text itself, this being the formulary's one
substantive departure from its Bible text; Guéranger's report that the antiphon
``was not the one primitively used; the ancient liturgists make no mention of it
in its present position''; the Ottobonianus cue block whose Introit is this
Mass's own and whose Communion is \latin{Panis quem ego}; Honorius, whose
Communion here is \latin{Panis, quem ego dabo}; the Sarum Missal at Trinity~XIV;
and the antiphonary formularies themselves. \textbf{Three printed witnesses give
this Sunday \latin{Panis quem ego dedero} and none of them gives it
\latin{Primum quaerite}, and Guéranger's judgment is confirmed in substance from
sources he did not have.}

The strongest single control comes from within the same Office and anyone can
check it in the same pages: Guéranger prints this Sunday's Magnificat antiphon
at Vespers as \latin{Quaerite primum regnum Dei, et justitiam ejus, et haec
omnia adjicientur vobis. Alleluia.} \textbf{That antiphon retains both
\latin{et iustítiam eius} and \latin{haec} --- exactly the two clauses the
Mass's Communion drops --- and leaves \latin{primum} in its Matthaean position
after the verb.}

\textbf{No witness explains why the exchange was made, and no witness dates
it. That book dates the state and not the change.} What is fixed is that it is
later than the ninth-century antiphonaries, later than the Ottobonianus margin
and later than Honorius, and earlier than the Pustet Missal of Ratisbon, 1862,
which prints the recast form already.
Guéranger's judgment is his own reading of Tommasi's Antiphonary and of ``the
ancient liturgists,'' and carries no more than that. Antiphon texts are
routinely shortened for chant and \latin{dicit Dóminus} is a common formulaic
tag, so both changes may be conventional rather than expressive, and no count of
either habit across the repertory stands behind this.

\textbf{Two further negatives, and they bound how the Communion may be read.}
First, \textbf{the Communion carries no eating vocabulary}: the appointed text
is \latin{Primum quǽrite regnum Dei}, and the verbs of eating that the
Offertory's \latin{gustáte} makes central belong to the Offertory alone and to
the Eucharistic reception the antiphon accompanies; nothing in the
Communion's own wording invites a Eucharistic reading. Second, \textbf{the law
and \latin{adversus} language is confined to
the Epistle}: the Communion's one addition, \latin{dicit Dóminus}, is an address
to the communicant and not a legal exclusion. Neither negative reaches past the ten
appointed elements.

\subsection*{C6. Two vocabularies partition the Mass, and the silence they produce is a genre fact everywhere but in the readings}

\latin{Salut-} and \latin{salv-} occur four times, once each in the Collect
(\latin{salutária}), the Alleluia (\latin{salutári}), the Secret
(\latin{salutáris}) and the Postcommunion (\latin{salvatiónis}), \textbf{and in
none of the six other elements}; \latin{propitia-} twice, Collect and Secret
only; \latin{semper} and \latin{perpétu-} twice each, Collect and Postcommunion
only. \textbf{At stem level the \latin{salut-} and \latin{salv-} root and
\latin{regnum} are the only substantive ties reaching three or more elements,
and the two partition the formulary cleanly:} \latin{regnum} in the read and
sung word, that root in the three orations plus the one chant and in no reading
at all. \textbf{The
people's texts state the problem as service, trust and anxiety; the priest's
prayers state it as expiation and rescue.}

\textbf{No expositor stands on the two prayers this partition turns on.} No
patristic or medieval exegesis of the Secret \latin{Concéde nobis, Dómine} or of
the Postcommunion \latin{Puríficent semper et múniant} stands anywhere the
\work{Patrologia Latina}'s exact-phrase index reaches, every hit on their
distinctive phrases being a sacramentary printing the prayer; \textbf{the
Collect stands outside that negative}, Honorius citing it by incipit, Godfrey of
Admont glossing it word by word and Schuster commenting on it continuously.

\textbf{The silence about temporal goods is a fact about the genre.} No Collect,
Secret or Postcommunion of the fifteen collated 1962 formularies --- forty-five
orations --- asks for food, drink or clothing, so
this Sunday's silence about the three goods its Gospel names is not a
distinctive of this formulary. \textbf{And Roman orations of this season are not
uniformly non-temporal:} the Fourth Sunday's Collect asks that \latin{mundi
cursus pacifice nobis tuo ordine dirigatur}, the Nuptial Mass's Postcommunion
asks \latin{longaeva pace}, Trinity Sunday's asks \latin{ad salutem corporis et
animae}, the Eighth Sunday's asks \latin{reparatio mentis et corporis}, and the
Eleventh Sunday's asks \latin{subsidium mentis et corporis}.
\textbf{So these orations are silent about temporal goods generally and not only
about the Gospel's three.}

\textbf{The singularity lies on the reading side.} Among the same fifteen
identities, the Fourteenth Sunday is the \textbf{only} one whose appointed texts
name eating, drinking \emph{and} clothing together --- \latin{manducétis},
\latin{manducábimus}, \latin{esca}, \latin{bibémus}, \latin{induámini},
\latin{operiémur}, \latin{vestiménto}, all in the Gospel. \textbf{So the
objects of petition and the objects of promise differ, and what is distinctive
about this Mass is what it reads and not what it asks.}

\textbf{And the address differs with the vocabulary.} The three orations never
command the assembly, their material addressed to God being otherwise
subjunctival; \textbf{the one command the Mass repeats at the moment of
reception is the promise's own \latin{Quǽrite}.}

\textbf{The orations also withhold agency from the assembly grammatically.} In
all three the acting subject is God, his propitiation, the victim or the
sacraments, and the assembly stands as object or as the subject of a passive:
\latin{Custódi \dots\ Ecclésiam tuam}; \latin{lábitur humána mortálitas};
\latin{abstrahátur \dots\ dirigátur}; \latin{hæc hóstia salutáris \dots\ fiat
purgátio \dots\ et propitiátio}; \latin{Puríficent \dots\ et múniant tua
sacraménta nos \dots\ et ad perpétuæ ducant salvatiónis efféctum}.
\textbf{The assembly acts in its own right in six elements, and every one is a
reading or a chant} --- the Introit psalm verse's \latin{concupíscit, et déficit
ánima mea}, \latin{Spíritu ambuláte} with \latin{carnem
suam crucifixérunt}, \latin{Veníte, exsultémus \dots\ iubilémus}, the Gospel's
\latin{Respícite}, \latin{Consideráte}, \latin{Nolíte \dots\ esse} and
\latin{Quǽrite}, the Offertory's \latin{gustáte et vidéte}, and the
Communion's \latin{Quǽrite} again. \textbf{And the Gospel states the same morphology
of itself}, the active denied of the man --- \latin{Quis autem vestrum \dots\
potest adícere} --- and the passive promised to him, \latin{hæc ómnia
adiciéntur vobis}. \textbf{The Mass names the Church once and the nations once
and distinguishes them by what they seek}: the Collect asks that the Church be
drawn from what harms and steered to what saves, and the Gospel marks the
nations by seeking exactly the goods the Collect never asks for --- which is how
Aquinas reads \latin{gentes} there, of those who ``through ignorance of [divine
providence] \dots\ are solicitous in seeking temporal goods before all
others'' (\work{Summa theologiae} II-II q.~55 a.~6, in the English of the
Fathers of the English Dominican Province). \textbf{The passive with a divine subject is the settled grammar of
the Roman collect as a genre}, so this may be a fact about how Latin orations
are written rather than about what this Mass teaches. \textbf{No count of Roman
orations outside these fifteen stands behind the contrast.} \textbf{The Epistle's
\latin{crucifixérunt} is a human perfect active, so the claim is about the
orations and not about the formulary as a whole.}

Fifteen 1962 formularies whose appointed text this guide has collated stand
behind that count too. \textbf{And
\latin{salutáris} of a victim, \latin{salutári} of God, \latin{salutária} of
things that save and \latin{salvatiónis} of a state are one root in four
senses}, so the four-fold count runs over the root and not over a single idea.
\textbf{The Latin does not mark which of these petitions are temporal and which
spiritual}, so that division is a judgement and not a feature of the orations.

\subsection*{What this formulary does not say}

\textbf{The Holy Spirit is named in exactly one of the ten appointed elements.}
The \latin{spiritu-} stem occurs five times in the Epistle and nowhere else; the
Collect, Secret and Postcommunion never name the Spirit. The printed rubric
appoints the Preface of the Most Holy Trinity, so the Mass \emph{as celebrated}
names the Spirit where its proper does not, and that is how far into this
formulary a Spirit-centred reading reaches. Beyond that, across the same ten
elements: no parousia, resurrection or judgment vocabulary past the
Epistle's \latin{regnum Dei non consequéntur} and the Postcommunion's
\latin{perpétuæ salvatiónis}; no saint named and no Marian reference; no
penitential, fasting or almsgiving language, despite a seventeen-item vice
list; one angel named, in the Offertory, and no other created spirit; and the
Church named \textbf{once}, in the Collect --- though the first-person plurals
of Introit, Alleluia, Secret and Postcommunion are corporate in their own way,
which softens any contrast built on that count.

\textbf{And three apparent ties are not ties.} \latin{Conte-} joins the Epistle's
\latin{contentiónes} to the Gospel's \latin{contémnet} and \latin{oper-} joins
its \latin{ópera carnis} to \latin{quo operiémur}, in each case across two
different verbs; and \latin{glori-} joins the Introit's \latin{Glória Patri} to
the Gospel's \latin{in omni glória sua}, but the doxology belongs to the
Ordinary and stands outside the appointed psalm. \textbf{No further substantive tie joins two of the ten
elements at token level.}

\subsection*{Two joints the appointed texts do not make}

\textbf{The Alleluia is not joined to the rest by its own vocabulary.} Its only
substantive tie to any other element is \latin{salutári}, and \latin{exsult-}
and \latin{iubil-} occur nowhere else in the ten. \textbf{No lexical warrant
joins the Alleluia's joy to the Gospel's anxiety}:
the Epistle's \latin{gaudium} and the Alleluia's \latin{exsultémus} are
different roots in different elements with nothing joining them. \textbf{Nor
does the reception warrant that join}: the medieval and later readers of this
psalm reached here --- a homily of the Chrysostomic corpus, Arnobius the
Younger, Bruno the Carthusian and Peter Lombard --- all read it penitentially,
and every one of them reaches that reading through v.~2,
\latin{Praeoccupemus faciem eius in confessione}, which the Alleluia does not
appoint. \textbf{But the reception does supply a join, and Augustine draws it
from the appointed verse itself.} Expounding \latin{exsultémus}, he distinguishes
``a wicked joy which is to be distinguished from a righteous joy'' --- in the
\work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers} English, first series --- and asks
``What is it to rejoice aright? To rejoice in the Lord.''
\textbf{That is not joy against anxiety but right joy against wrong joy}, the
same disjunctive shape as the Gospel's two masters and the Gradual's two objects
of trust.

\textbf{Whether the Epistle and the Gospel were ever read together in the
tradition.} \textbf{The lectionary answers half of it and no witness answers the
rest.} The \work{Liber Comitis} pairs these two lessons at these two extents, so
the pairing is old; but Anthony of Padua's is the one medieval sermon on
Gal.\ 5:16--24 located for this Epistle, and his Sunday numbering runs one
ahead of the 1962 missal's, consistently across all three of his sermons for
these Sundays.
\textbf{A major medieval preacher expounding this Epistle does so beside a
different Gospel.}
