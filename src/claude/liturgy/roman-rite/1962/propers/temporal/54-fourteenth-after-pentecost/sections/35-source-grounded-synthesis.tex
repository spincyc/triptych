\section*{Source-Grounded Synthesis Across the Propers}

\begingroup
\footnotesize

\subsection*{Desire is converted by allegiance}

The Introit, Epistle, Gospel, and Communion describe one movement from desire
to dominion. Psalm~83 supplies holy longing; Galatians shows desires opposed
and made visible in works or fruit; Matthew names rival masters; the Communion
places the kingdom first at reception. Augustine's account of grace-assisted
resistance, Aquinas's rival ultimate ends, and Bellarmine's heavenly pilgrimage
show why the movement is conversion rather than suppression. Created goods are
not condemned. They cease to govern when desire is ordered toward beatitude.

\subsection*{Grace and embodied need belong together}

The Collect's petitions and the Epistle's imperative describe one life from
different sides. God guards, withdraws, and directs; the faithful walk in the
Spirit and crucify passions and desires. Chrysostom joins diligence to divine
kindness, Theodoret joins indwelling grace to the soul's cooperation, and
Jerome refuses to make bodily substance the object of Paul's condemnation.
Matthew then places food and clothing under the Father's care. The Epistle's
crucified \latin{caro} and the Gospel's provided-for \latin{corpus} are not
opposites: the body is good, while the person governed by disordered desire
must be healed.

The Postcommunion makes that healing sacramental. What the Collect asks as
guardianship becomes purification and fortification after reception. Divine
action does not displace human action, and prudent labor does not displace
providential trust.

\subsection*{Protection admits ministry but refuses mastery}

Divine protection appears as invocation in the Introit, petition in the
Collect, confidence in the Gradual, fatherly provision in the Gospel, angelic
encampment in the Offertory, and sacramental fortification in the
Postcommunion. The patristic disagreements sharpen rather than dissolve this
unity. Augustine and Cassiodorus include angelic princes under the Gradual's
warning; Theodoret reads earthly rulers; Bellarmine allows creaturely help
when hope remains referred to God. Augustine identifies the Offertory's Angel
with Christ, while Cassiodorus, Theodoret, Aquinas, and Bellarmine also preserve
created or ecclesial senses.

These readings distinguish mediation from mastery. God may protect through
angels, persons, or sacraments without surrendering the final allegiance that
Matthew calls service. Providence remains compatible with affliction and does
not promise bodily immunity.

\subsection*{The language of salvation becomes action}

The Collect's saving direction, the Alleluia's saving God, the Secret's saving
victim, and the Postcommunion's perpetual salvation form a verbal chain. Its
members are not interchangeable. Praise addresses God; direction orders the
Church; the victim purifies and propitiates; the sacraments fortify and lead.
Augustine and Cassiodorus hear Psalm~94's jubilation as joy beyond articulate
speech, Theodoret as a victor's cry, and Bellarmine as inward gladness becoming
vocal praise. That acclamation passes into offering and reception without
losing its own liturgical moment.

The sacramentaries attest the three prayer texts; patristic and medieval
interpretation unfolds the appointed passages' doctrinal and scriptural
language. Together, the prayers and appointed passages move from praise through
offering to sacramental effect.

\subsection*{The received formulary is continuous and selective}

The Old Gelasian tradition holds \latin{Custódi} and \latin{Concéde nobis}
together, while \latin{Puríficent semper} appears separately and in a
saint-adapted form (Wilson, \work{The Gelasian Sacramentary}, pp.~207,
229--230, 237). The Gregorian tradition gives all three consecutively at
Sunday~XV (Wilson, \work{The Gregorian Sacramentary}, pp.~173--174). Early
numbering varies, so the ordinal difference does not prove a simple one-week
transfer.

Honorius's Fourteenth Sunday joins the later Introit, Collect, Galatians
lesson, and Offertory to chants and a Gospel now found in neighboring Sundays;
his Fifteenth Sunday carries this formulary's Gradual and Matthew Gospel amid
another mixed constellation (\work{Gemma animae} IV.72--79,
PL~172:717--720). The circa-900 Albi index likewise gives \latin{Panis quem
ego} as the Communion and \latin{Confitemini} as the Alleluia beside several
chants retained later. Variation precedes the received ten-element sequence
and cannot be reduced to one ordinal conversion.

The complete later sequence appears under the Fourteenth Sunday in Venice 1570
and remains stable in element and order in Vatican 1604, Mame 1922, and Vatican
1962. Venice carries a different order in part of Galatians~5:22; the 1570 and
1604 books also retain seasonal orations abolished by the 1960
\work{Variationes}. Guéranger, reporting the manuscript tradition through
Tommasi, calls \latin{Panis quem ego} the earlier Communion and \latin{Primum
quǽrite} the later one. The sequence's first appearance and compiler remain
unidentified, as do the Communion substitution's date and cause.

Guéranger and Schuster receive the formulary as a liturgical movement through
entrance, petition, proclamation, oblation, communion, purification, and final
salvation. Their synthesis receives the later arrangement; the sacramentaries
and medieval witnesses preserve the earlier variety from which it must remain
distinct. Continuity and selective change are both facts of the received
formulary.
