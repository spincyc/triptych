\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

Each appointed element is named by its Latin incipit, its printed heading and
reference, and the missal's marginal number. Its complete text, Latin and
English together, has just been printed in \textit{The Appointed Formulary in
Full}; what stands here is the evidence. Latin is quoted again only where an
argument turns on the wording, and Scripture is read in its whole literary
context before any witness is allowed to speak about it.

\textbf{Where the English comes from.} Scriptural elements take the
Douay--Rheims as revised by Bishop Challoner, the English of the Clementine
Vulgate the missal prints. The three orations take the anonymous English of
\work{The Roman Missal translated into the English language for the use of the
laity}, first revised edition (Philadelphia: Eugene Cummiskey, 1861). Both are
public domain in the United States; neither is an official liturgical
translation. This guide has composed, translated, adapted and paraphrased
nothing of the appointed text.

\textbf{What may be said about the witnesses below, and what may not.}
\textbf{Not one reception witness in this guide is \emph{verified} in the
source-library sense.} Verification under this profile means collating the
locus against the controlling critical or Migne edition, and \textbf{Migne was
opened for none of them}. Four states are distinguished and each is stated
where it falls. \emph{Acquired} means the exact bytes were obtained and hashed.
\emph{Searched} means a query was run over the retained artifact.
\emph{Inspected} means the identified passage was read with its surrounding
context. \emph{Corroborated} means a second and independent witness
independently agrees in the original language --- which is short of collation
and is never called more than it is. \textbf{Three witnesses are better
attested than the rest and this guide leans on them accordingly:} Anthony of
Padua and Guéranger, whose downloaded digests reproduced already-registered
artifact records bit for bit, and Augustine on \latin{primum}, whose decisive
sentence is corroborated in Latin from a second, independent, separately hashed
thirteenth-century witness.

\textbf{Two rules govern the reception material throughout, and both are
narrow.} First, \textbf{no Greek witness is made to comment on words his own
list does not contain.} The Latin vice-list and fruit-list the missal prints
are longer than the Greek lists the Greek Fathers expound --- seventeen vices
and twelve fruits here against seventeen and nine in the Byzantine text and
fifteen and nine in the critical text --- so three appointed words with no
Greek counterpart (\latin{luxúria} among the vices, \latin{modéstia} and
\latin{cástitas} among the fruits) receive no comment here from a Greek witness
commenting on his own text; and Jerome is himself the witness that the Latin
lists differ by rendering, \latin{longanímitas} and \latin{patiéntia} standing
for one \emph{makrothymia} and \latin{continéntia} for \emph{enkrateia}.
Second, \textbf{the Greek side of this sweep is better than it was and is
still bounded.} Theodoret on Pss.~83, 94 and 117 and Chrysostom on Galatians
were \textbf{read in Greek}, each from a transcription with no
\work{Patrologia Graeca} page image collated and no registered passage record
here; Chrysostom on Matthew and Cyril remain \textbf{English only}, in the
\work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}. Nothing here supports a claim about
what the Fathers \emph{as a body} held: every claim names its witnesses.
\subsection*{The text: one book collated, and another book's text layer}

The Latin was read as page images off the CMAA facsimile of the Vatican
\latin{editio typica} (printed pp.~394--396, marginal nos.\ 1572--1581) at
300~dpi, with every disputed letter, accent and point re-read at 500~dpi, which
is the scan's native resolution. It was then compared against the 1962 Benziger
\latin{editio iuxta typicam}. \textbf{That comparison is weaker than the
Thirteenth Sunday's and the difference is declared rather than papered over:}
only the Internet Archive item's uncorrected OCR derivative was read,
and the sibling page-image artifact was not opened. Every Benziger reading
below is therefore evidence about a text layer, not a collated reading of the
Benziger book, and that holds for all thirteen rows.

Two of these are worth more than a footnote. The two conclusions run in
opposite directions inside one formulary --- the typical edition abbreviates
the Secret's and prints the Postcommunion's at length, Benziger's text layer
does the reverse --- which is a useful reminder that ``the 1962 Latin'' is not
one artefact. And the typical edition abbreviates \latin{Ant. ad Introitum}
here where on the Thirteenth Sunday it spelled \latin{Antiphona ad Introitum}
out: the book is not uniform with itself two pages apart.

One mark on these pages is damaged, and it is not text. The Gospel's marginal
reference number, printed at the outer edge of the scanned block on p.~395, has
lost its fourth digit to ink loss. It is 1577, because 1576 and 1578 are both
legible and the series 1572--1581 is unbroken. That is resolved by sequence and
not by the second witness, which prints no marginal numbers at all. Nothing in
the appointed text depends on it.

\subsubsection*{Eighteen places where the appointed text is not the Bible's text}

The consequential collation is against the Clementine Vulgate, the Bible whose
English this guide quotes. Eighteen divergences were verified directly and are
textual observations, not inferences. Most are orthography or pointing. The
ones that change what is said are the two cuts at the Introit, the cut at the
Offertory, the Alleluia's dropped psalm title, the Epistle's displaced incipit,
and --- the only substantive recasting in the formulary --- the Communion.

Three negative results belong with the table, and they are what throw the
Communion into relief. The Gradual is the Clementine's own wording
exactly, both verses, with nothing added, dropped or transposed. The Epistle
after its incipit matches word for word apart from the orthography and pointing
above. The Gospel after its incipit does the same. \textbf{The only appointed
element of this formulary that departs from its Bible text substantively is the
Communion} --- and, as the Communion's own treatment below shows, even that
departure is inherited rather than made here.

No \latin{Psalterium Romanum}, Gallican or Vetus Latina witness was collated
for this guide, so no derivation of any chant reading from a particular older
psalter is asserted anywhere in it. That boundary matters most at the Offertory
and the Communion, where an older Latin form is the likeliest explanation of
what the missal prints and this repository cannot presently distinguish it from
a scribal or editorial variant.

\subsubsection*{Two corrections this guide makes to its own source record}

Both are corrections to \texttt{propers/verified.md}, the leaf's own collated
text record, and both are recorded here rather than left silent because a
reader who checks that file will find the amended wording and should know why
it changed.

\correction{the psalm numbers in parentheses}{The record glossed the appointed
psalm loci with what it called ``the Hebrew numbering,'' and at two of the four
that gloss is not the number a reader will find in a modern English Bible.
Three series are in use here and two of them are loosely called modern.
\textit{Vulgate}, which the missal cites and the Douay carries.
\textit{Masoretic}, which numbers the psalm's superscription as a verse and is
used by the Nova Vulgata and the New American Bible. \textit{English}, the

superscription unnumbered and therefore runs a verse behind wherever there is
one. Vulg.\ Ps.\ 83:10--11 is Masoretic 84:10--11 but English \textbf{84:9--10};
Vulg.\ Ps.\ 33:8--9 is Masoretic 34:8--9 but English \textbf{34:7--8}. Ps.~117
has no title and Ps.~94 folds its title into v.~1, so for the Gradual and the
Alleluia all three series agree. The old glosses were defensible as Masoretic
and wrong as ``the common modern number'' the profile asks for; the record now
prints the English number and says which series it is. \textbf{The conversions
are not this guide's arithmetic}: they were checked against a tracked
project-created reference table, the concordance
\texttt{psalm-numbering-ee3c7757} registered as an artifact of the
Douay--Rheims Challoner edition this guide quotes, whose rows give an English
offset of one verse for Vulgate Psalms 33 and 83 and of none for 94 and 117,
each marked uniform through the psalm. \textbf{No Hebrew text of any appointed
psalm was read here}, so the Masoretic column above is reasoned from the tracked
editions' declared conventions rather than read off a Hebrew Bible.}

\subsection*{How this Mass came to be this Sunday's}

\subsubsection*{The three orations do not descend as a unit}

This is the load-bearing structural point of the oration dossier, and it cuts
against the natural assumption that a formulary's prayers travel together.

The \textbf{Collect and Secret stand together in one Mass} in the Gelasianum
Vetus --- Wilson's 1894 edition, Book~III sect.~X, \latin{Item alia Missa},
printed pp.~229--230 --- and that Mass stands in a series of Sunday Masses which
the base manuscript, Vatican Reginensis 316, numbers \emph{not at all}.

The \textbf{Postcommunion comes from somewhere else}. \latin{Purificent semper}
stands in a floating series of unassigned Postcommunions appended after the
Canon (printed p.~237), in exactly the form the 1962 book prints,
\latin{nos, Deus}; and separately in a saint-adapted recension in the Vigil
Mass of St~Andrew (Book~II sect.~\textbf{LXVIII}, p.~207), reading \latin{nos,
Domine} and adding the apostle's intercession. Which recension is prior is not
settled by anything read.

\correction{the section number of the Postcommunion's saint-adapted
recension}{An earlier form of this leaf printed \textbf{Book~II sect.~LXIX},
taken from the running head above printed p.~207. \textbf{That head names the
section the page \emph{reaches}, not the one it opens in.} Read on the page
image, sect.~LXIX is \latin{Item in natali eiusdem}, the Mass of St~Andrew's
feast on 30~November, and it begins \emph{below} the Postcommunion in question,
which belongs to the preceding section --- the Vigil Mass, \textbf{sect.~LXVIII}.
\textbf{This is the second instance of the same misreading in the same book:}
the Secret's penitential home was printed as sect.~XLII from the running head
above p.~257 and is in fact sect.~XLI, opening on p.~256. \textbf{Any locus in
either of Wilson's editions taken from a running head is suspect until checked
against the section heading itself}, and the hazard recurs in the 1915 volume,
where the running head on printed p.~174 reads \latin{DOM. XVII. POST
PENTECOSTEN} over a continuation of section CXXXIII, \latin{Dominica XV}.}

The \textbf{Secret has a second, penitential home} in the same book, Book~III
sect.~\textbf{XLI} (printed pp.~256--257), among votive and occasional material
--- a Mass whose collects beg God to turn his anger away from his people.
\textbf{And the page image narrows that claim materially.} The marginal at
sect.~XLI reads \latin{R.\,S.(alibi.)} --- Wilson's notation that R and S have
the prayer \emph{elsewhere}, that is, at the Sunday Mass. So the penitential
Mass \emph{as R and S transmit it does not contain this Secret}; the second home
is attested for the Vatican manuscript and for Menard, while the other two
references point back to the single Sunday occurrence. \textbf{The Secret's
``second, penitential home'' is therefore a feature of the Vatican manuscript's
arrangement and not of the Gelasian tradition at large.}

\textbf{And the Verona negative is replayable, and the positive half is the
more useful half.} Feltoe's 1896 \work{Leonian Sacramentary} is registered
here --- the work, the edition, and two Internet Archive OCR artifacts of it,
corpus members both --- so the search that found none of the three orations in
the Veronense can be repeated. It ran over seventeen distinctive strings,
including \latin{custodi domine}, \latin{propitiatione perpetua},
\latin{abstrahatur}, \latin{hostia salutaris}, \latin{purgatio delictorum} and
\latin{perpetuae salvationis}, after accent stripping and de-hyphenation across
line ends. \textbf{Four bounds travel with it:} it is a search of two
uncorrected OCR derivatives and not a reading of printed pages; Feltoe's is a
diplomatic edition printing the codex's contractions, so the zero counts for
\latin{custodi domine} and \latin{concede nobis domine} are weaker than the
rest; de-hyphenation was itself applied, with the risk that carries; and
\textbf{the Veronense has no ordered Sunday series at all} --- it runs by month
from April --- so absence there is much weaker evidence about a Sunday formulary
than absence from an ordered sacramentary would be. \textbf{The positive half:}
the Veronense does contain, twice, the prayer-shape the 1962 Postcommunion uses
--- \latin{Purificent} with holy things as subject and a second protective verb
yoked to it, the closer reading \latin{tua nos \dots\ sancta purificent, et ab
externis erroribus perpetua virtute defendant} --- which places the prayer's
\emph{idiom}, though not the prayer, in the oldest Roman euchological stratum.
\textbf{The claim is about diction and prayer-shape, not derivation}, and its
evidence state is an uncorrected OCR layer, no page image collated. A companion
negative, new to this revision: the same string sweep returned zero hits in
Férotin's \work{Liber mozarabicus sacramentorum}, Bannister's \work{Missale
Gothicum} and Lowe's \work{Bobbio Missal} --- \textbf{the three orations belong
to the Roman euchological stream and not, so far as six editions show, to the
non-Roman Latin rites}, with the same optical-layer bounds.

\subsubsection*{The Sunday number has moved, and it moved by a measured amount}

\textbf{Sixteen consecutive sections, not eleven.} Gelasianum Book~III
sects.~I--XI and XII--XVI correspond to the 1962 Fifth through Twentieth
Sundays, and \textbf{the offset survives a documented interpolation and resumes
a new constant rather than becoming noise}: sects.~I--XI stand at $N{+}2$ for
Rheinau, S.~Gallen, Gerbert \emph{and Menard}, and at $N{+}1$ for Pamelius;
sects.~XII--XVI stand at $N{+}4$ for the first four while Pamelius stays at
$N{+}1$ without a break. \textbf{Menard is present at every readable section
and always agrees with R/S/Gerbert, never with Pamelius} --- pairing the two
Gregorian editions together would be wrong. \textbf{A caution: Wilson's
per-section wording varies in scope} --- sect.~IX excepts the Secret, sect.~V
the Postcommunion, sect.~VI covers ``the two Collects and the Secret,'' sect.~XII
``the first Collect, Secret, and Postcommunion'' --- \textbf{the offset is
uniform while the set of prayers it covers is not, and that is not flattened
here.} Each section's 1962 identity was fixed from its own printed collect or
Secret and never from position, and the mapping checks against the tracked
calendar: sect.~XI prints the 1962 Fifteenth's Collect and sect.~IX the
Thirteenth's, with sect.~X printing this Mass's.

\begin{pairtable}{Number}{Books that give it}
\textbf{Fourteenth} & the 1962 Missal\\
\textbf{Fifteenth} & Pamelius; and, independently, the Gregorian Hadrianum supplement\\
\textbf{Sixteenth} & Rheinau, S.~Gallen, Gerbert, Menard\\
\end{pairtable}

\textbf{Two independent lines reach the same magnitude.} The supplement to the
Gregorian Hadrianum numbers this Mass \latin{DOMINICA .XV. POST PENTECOSTEN},
one Sunday later than 1962, and the offset was verified across \textbf{seven}
consecutive sections, each identified from its own printed collect rather than
extrapolated. In Cod.\ Ottobonianus~313 the marginal chant cues stand two
Sundays later than the orations beside them across \textbf{six} sections, in a
different edition again. \textbf{A single misreading by one editor could not
produce that.}

\textbf{Where the offset comes from, and Wilson states it himself.} His
Introduction (printed pp.~lxxi--lxxiii) places the start of Book~III's series
against the first part of the Gregorian's third book, which makes the offset
\textbf{a difference in where each tradition begins counting --- a numeration
base --- and not an interpolation}: he names the mechanism in a footnote, the
\latin{post Pentecosten} / \latin{post Octavas Pentecostes} equivalence
arising from a discrepancy between the first and supplementary portions of
Muratori's text. \textbf{What remains open:} which single Sunday each tradition
counts as its first was not established, so the decomposition of the two units
of offset is not settled here. The break at sect.~XII is doubly sourced ---
Wilson's Introduction and his footnotes agree that R, S and Gerbert interpolate
the two September Ember-week Sundays and rejoin at their twentieth --- which is
exactly the arithmetic that moves the offset from two to four while Pamelius
stays at one.

\textbf{The bound, and it has narrowed materially.} \textbf{One of the five
books is now open at the locus.} Gerbert prints at his printed p.~173, under
the heading \latin{Dominica XVI. post Pentecosten}, a five-item Mass whose
Collect, Secret \emph{and} Postcommunion are the three composed orations of
this formulary, plus a second Collect and a proper Preface; the printed page
number matches Wilson's independent marginal citation ``Gerb.~173'', and the
five incipits match his Appendix tabulation item for item. \textbf{And the
Gregorian Hadrianum carries all three orations as one Mass}: Wilson's 1915
section CXXXIII, \latin{DOMINICA .XV. POST PENTECOSTEN}, printed
pp.~173--174, gives the Collect \latin{Custodi domine quaesumus}, the Super
oblata \latin{Concede nobis domine quaesumus}, and the Ad complendum
\latin{Purificent semper et muniant tua sacramenta nos deus, et ad perpetuae
ducant salvationis effectum} --- \textbf{the 1962 Postcommunion word for word},
and the next section opens with the 1962 Fifteenth's Collect. \textbf{So in
both traditions the whole 1962 oration set travels as a unit, at $N{+}1$ in the
Gregorian and $N{+}2$ in the Frankish Gelasian}, and the narrow attestation of
the Postcommunion is a feature of how the Vatican manuscript arranges its
unassigned Sunday Masses, not of the tradition. \textbf{Two halves of the
transmission must be kept together:} the unit that moved in the older books is
a Mass-set of three orations plus, in Gerbert, a proper Preface, of which only
two are elements of the 1962 formulary --- prose that says ``two elements
moved'' is right about 1962 and wrong about the transmission, and prose that
says ``the Mass moved'' is right about the transmission and overstates 1962.

\textbf{A near-miss at sect.~X that is also a search trap.} The bracketed
Postcommunion there reads \latin{Purificent nos, Domine, sacramenta quae
sumpsimus, et a cunctis efficiant vitiis absolutos} --- \textbf{a different
prayer of the same incipit family}, which Wilson's own index of incipits lists
separately from this one, three printed occurrences each. \textbf{A search on
the incipit alone will conflate them}, and the Vatican manuscript wrongly
titles that prayer \latin{Secreta}, an error of the scribe by Wilson's note.

\textbf{``Four against one'' is not four independent witnesses.} R and S are
two Frankish ``Gelasian'' manuscripts Wilson collated himself, and they carry
the sixteenth-Sunday heading directly. Gerbert's principal manuscript
``cannot now be traced,'' and Wilson holds that the ``Gelasian'' portions of it,
and so of Gerbert's printed text, ``do not represent the original text of the
`Gelasian' books, but a revision of that text, apparently based upon the text of
the Sacramentaries of the `Gregorian' type.'' \textbf{So Gerbert does not merely
fail to be a third Gelasian voice: it leans toward the very Gregorian editions
supposed to corroborate it.} And Pamelius is itself an edition of the
\emph{Gregorian} book, so \textbf{Pamelius's ``fifteenth'' and the Gregorian
supplement's ``fifteenth'' are not two independent witnesses to one Sunday}.
The honest count is two collated Gelasian manuscripts, plus a printed edition
largely dependent on a lost manuscript copied from one of them and revised
toward the Gregorian type, plus one Gregorian edition --- with the other
Gregorian edition one Sunday earlier. \textbf{Opening Gerbert proves that
Wilson cited him accurately and excludes a mis-citation as the pattern's
explanation, but it adds no fourth independent voice: he is the least
independent of the five.} R's and S's own Sunday headings are Wilson's own
collation and do not depend on Gerbert at all.

\textbf{A caution in the other direction, which prose must also keep.} R's and
S's own Sunday headings are Wilson's own collation and do not depend on Gerbert
at all, \textbf{so Gerbert's unreliability does not weaken them.} And
\textbf{quoting Wilson's ``exceedingly confused and misleading'' bare overstates
him}: the phrase is hedged with ``at times,'' applies to Gerbert's
\emph{handling and explanation of his materials} rather than to his text as
such, and stands immediately after Wilson's warm tribute --- ``students of
Liturgy owe so much as we owe to Gerbert.'' Wilson also excepts ``the latter
part'' of R from his criticism, and whether these Sunday Masses fall inside that
part is not established, \textbf{so the unreliability cannot simply be assumed
to reach them.}

\textbf{A wording distinction in Wilson that must travel with the claim.} He
does \emph{not} say the same thing about all five books. Of R, S and Gerbert he
writes that the prayers ``\textbf{are assigned by}'' them to the sixteenth --- a
statement about those books' own Sunday headings. Of Pamelius and Menard he
writes only ``\textbf{see the \latin{Missa} for} the \dots\ Sunday in,'' which is
a cross-reference to a \emph{parallel} Mass and need not mean an identical
composition. This guide does not report all five with one verb.

\textbf{Reporting the disagreement without the bound overstates it; dropping the
disagreement because of the bound understates it far worse.} Wilson's apparatus
is not a rumour: it is a published critical collation, and for R and S
specifically it is his own reading of manuscripts physically in front of him.

\subsubsection*{Honorius's Fourteenth Sunday is not the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday}

By the early twelfth century the 1962 Sunday number and this Introit are
already paired. \work{Gemma animae} IV.72 is headed \latin{Dominica decima
quarta post Pentecosten « Protector noster, » sub lege}, and the quotation is
exact --- confirmed by actively trying to break it, since the neighbouring
chapter headings in the same column print no \latin{post Pentecosten} and the
following one does.

\textbf{But reading the whole chapter rather than its last sentence shows the
opposite half of the picture, and it must travel with the first.} Within one
and the same chapter Honorius's \textbf{Introit, Collect, Epistle and
Offertory are the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday's}, while his \textbf{Gospel is the
1962 Thirteenth Sunday's} --- Luke 17:11--19, the ten lepers --- and that Gospel
offset is not a slip, since it holds across three consecutive Sundays. His
\textbf{Gradual} (\latin{Bonum est confiteri Domino}, Ps.~91) is not the 1962
Fourteenth's (\latin{Bonum est confidere in Domino}, Ps.~117:8--9); his
\textbf{Alleluia} (\latin{Quoniam Deus magnus}, Ps.~94:3) is a different verse
of the same psalm the 1962 Alleluia uses; and his \textbf{Communion} is
\latin{Panis, quem ego dabo}.

\textbf{And his three differing chants are not three separate facts.} Collated
against the tracked 1962 registry --- \textbf{a repository derivative, not a
facsimile collation of pp.~396--398, which no lane ran} --- his Gradual,
Alleluia and Communion are \textbf{precisely the 1962 Fifteenth Sunday's chant
set}: within one chapter his chant strand stands one Sunday \emph{later} than
his oration strand while his Gospel stands one Sunday \emph{earlier}, a spread
of exactly two Sundays. \textbf{That converges in direction, though not in
magnitude, with the Ottobonianus cues below}, where the chant set beside
\latin{Custodi, Domine} also runs later than the orations: \textbf{two
independent witnesses in which the chants of this Mass run later than its
orations, at $+1$ and $+2$ --- they do not corroborate a single number.}

\textbf{A caution on the printing.} In Migne's text the two parenthetical
references in the Gradual and Gospel sentences are \textbf{transposed} ---
\latin{Bonum est confiteri Domino (Luc. XVII)} and the ten lepers
\latin{commemorat (Psal. XCI, 2)} --- whereas \latin{Bonum est confiteri Domino}
is Ps.~91:2 and the ten lepers is Luke~17. \textbf{A reader taking either
reference at face value from this column will be misled.} Honorius was read on
a rendered page image of a rights-restricted facsimile, hash-verified, with
nothing retained in this repository, and he is not quoted at length anywhere in
this guide.

\textbf{One convergence recorded as a lead and not as a demonstrated link.}
Honorius's Ember week and the Masses Wilson reports as interpolated may or may
not be the same disturbance; \textbf{the two witnesses number their Sundays
differently}, and nothing read settles it.

\textbf{The compiler of the Gregorian supplement stays unnamed.} Wilson
attributes it to Alcuin; the modern reattribution rests on scholarship this
repository does not hold and which was not consulted, so no name is given here.

\textbf{And one manuscript reading is observed and not interpreted.} The
Ottobonianus cue \latin{b} reads \latin{Bonum est confidere} where 1962 gives
the Fifteenth Sunday \latin{Bonum est confiteri Domino}. Manuscript reading,
shared respond, or error --- this research cannot decide, and no claim here
rests on it.

\textbf{One conflict is printed rather than settled.} The prior production,
reading the footnote reference marks on page images, placed the cue set beside
\latin{Custodi, Domine} as the 1962 \emph{Sixteenth} Sunday's, an offset of
$+2$; this revision's optical reading places that block at the section whose
Collect is \latin{Custodi domine}, which would make the cue offset zero. The
prior run's reading stands and the disagreement is \textbf{live}: the footnote
marks are exactly what an optical layer destroys, and \textbf{the page image
controls}. \textbf{What survives either reading, because it is a fact about
block content and not about attachment:} the cue set whose Introit is
\latin{Protector noster} --- this Mass's own --- has Communion \latin{Panis
quem ego} and not \latin{Primum quaerite}.

\elementguard
\subsection*{Introit \cue{Int.}}

\elementid{Protéctor noster, áspice, Deus}{\latin{Ant. ad Introitum} --- Ps. 83, 10-11, with \latin{Ps. ibid.,} 2-3}{1572}

\textbf{The whole psalm, first.} Psalm 83 is a psalm of the sons of Core about
desire for the sanctuary: the sparrow and the turtle-dove finding a house at
the altars (v.~4), those who dwell in the house (v.~5), the ascents in the
heart through the vale of tears (vv.~6--7), \latin{ibunt de virtute in
virtutem} (v.~8). \textbf{What those verses support is movement toward the
temple.} That the psalm is therefore \emph{for} pilgrims, or voices their
sentiments, is an interpretation the text does not state, and this guide does
not state it either.

\textbf{What the chant does to it, and the two cuts have opposite effects.} It
sings the psalm backwards: the antiphon from the end, vv.~10--11a, the psalm
verse from the beginning, vv.~2--3a. Cutting v.~11 removes the psalm's explicit
two-way choice between the house of God and the tents of sinners. Cutting v.~3
removes the rejoicing of heart and flesh, \latin{cor meum et caro mea
exsultaverunt in Deum vivum} --- \textbf{the only place in the formulary's psalm
sources where \latin{caro} carries a positive sense}, which bears directly on
the Epistle's use of the word. \textbf{Both dropped clauses are the psalm's,
not the antiphon's, and the antiphon asserts neither.}

\textbf{A warning about the capital, before any Father is quoted.} The missal
prints \latin{fáciem Christi tui} where the tracked Clementine prints lower-case
\latin{christi}. \textbf{Capitalisation in a modern electronic Clementine is
that e-text's editorial convention and capitalisation in the 1962 missal is that
book's; neither is evidence of what the Latin intends}, and in the psalm's own
sense \latin{christus} is the anointed king. The christological reading must be
argued from the Fathers, who do argue it, and never from the printer.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{Enarratio in Ps. LXXXIII} §6 and §14.} \emph{Acquired,
searched and inspected in the English of the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene
Fathers}, first series, retrieved through New Advent's transcription of that
series, and corroborated in a Latin witness that agrees with it; \textbf{not}
collated against \work{Patrologia Latina}~37, so the Latin below is
corroboration and not verification. \textbf{Every English string quoted from him
here is NPNF's rendering and not Augustine's Latin.} The NPNF text is public
domain; the New Advent transcription of it is registered restricted over that
public-domain text, and what is reproduced here is the NPNF wording.} \textbf{He reads the two
halves as one movement of desire.} On the psalm verse: ``My soul longs and fails
for the courts of the Lord. It is not enough that it longs and fails: for what
does it fail? For the courts of the Lord.'' On the antiphon: ``one day there is
better than a thousand days. Men long for thousands of days \dots\ let them long
for one day, which has neither rising nor setting.'' Latin as collated:
\latin{contemnant millia dierum, desiderent unum diem, qui non habet ortum et
occasum; unum diem, diem sempiternum, cui non cedit hesternus, quem non urget
crastinus.} \textbf{So on his reading the antiphon is the answer to its own
verse.}
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{A material limit that must not be smoothed over.} Augustine's §15
expounds the \emph{rest} of Ps.~83:11 --- in NPNF's English, ``I have chosen to
be cast away in the house of the Lord'' --- which is exactly the clause the
Introit cuts off after
\latin{super mília}. \textbf{The Father's unit and the liturgy's unit do not
coincide}, and this guide does not imply that he is commenting on the antiphon
as the missal bounds it. A second trap the Latin collation caught: his
exposition speaks of \latin{millia dierum}, thousands of \emph{days}, and it is
tempting to conclude that his psalter read a fuller text. \textbf{It did not} ---
his printed lemma is the missal's Introit verbatim, and \latin{millia dierum} is
his \emph{gloss}, supplying the noun the verse elides.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, on \latin{réspice in fáciem Christi tui}.} \emph{Same route
and state.} \textbf{He refuses the obvious reading:} ``For when does God not look
upon the face of His Christ? \dots\ Cause Your Christ to become known to all.''
Latin as collated: \latin{Fac innotescere omnibus Christum tuum \dots\ notus sit
omnibus Christus tuus, ut possimus ire a virtutibus in virtutem, ut possit
superabundare gratia, quoniam abundavit peccatum.} \textbf{This is the
best-attested Augustinian quotation in the sweep and the safest to publish in
Latin}, because the argument is a grammatical one about \latin{facies} that
survives translation. Note that the reasoning is driven by Rom.\ 5:20 and by the
same psalm's v.~8, \textbf{neither of which is in the appointed extract}: the
move is Augustine's on the whole psalm, applied to the verse the missal took.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{Guéranger reads the same verse differently, and the two do not reconcile
by stacking.} Where Augustine turns the petition outward and missionary,
Guéranger turns it inward and nuptial --- and every sentence quoted from him in
this guide is the English of the Duffy 1900 edition of \work{The Liturgical
Year} vol.~XI, \textbf{whose translator no lane established, so the edition is
named and no translator is}: ``the Church, in presenting her petitions
to the Most High, begs of Him to look on the face of His Christ, who is also
hers,'' grounded on 1~Cor.\ 11:7, 11:3 and Eph.\ 5:23. \textbf{Both are
preserved.} Guéranger is nineteenth-century and not patristic; his standing is
that of a recognised authority on the Roman liturgy, and \textbf{his voice does
not stand in for the tradition's.}

\textbf{Guéranger also records the Sunday's popular names.} ``In the western
Church this Sunday is called that of the two masters, because of the Gospel
which is read upon it''; and, of Byzantine usage, ``The Greeks give it the name
of the Sunday of the invited to the marriage-feast, or, the fourteenth of
St~Matthew,'' with a displacement rule for the Exaltation of the Cross.
\textbf{His account of Greek naming is a claim about Byzantine usage checked
against no Greek source, and it travels as his report and not as a finding.}

\textbf{A retrieval trap specific to this Introit, found and avoided.} The
incipit \latin{Protector noster aspice, Deus} opens \textbf{both} this Mass's
Introit and the Fifth Sunday after Pentecost's Gradual, with different
continuations --- \latin{in faciem Christi tui} against \latin{super servos
tuos} --- so an incipit-driven search for reception of this Introit returns
material on a different chant of a different Sunday. The searched Missal shows
the collision is wider still: the same words open a verbatim psalm Introit, an
adapted Gradual at four occurrences, \textbf{and a composed collect that quotes
no psalm at all} (\latin{Protector noster aspice, Deus: ut, qui malorum
nostrorum pondere premimur\dots}). \textbf{That third case is exactly what this
guide's own page-2 rule warns against --- mislabelling a loose echo in a
composed oration as a direct quotation --- and it is why the Fifth Sunday's
chant is described here as an adaptation and this one as a verbatim
quotation.}

\textbf{Negative results, and one of them has been lifted.} \textbf{Theodoret
on Ps.~83 was retrieved this revision and read in Greek}, at PG~80:1541--1545,
from a real text layer: he reads \latin{réspice in fáciem Christi tui}
ecclesially, the face of Christ being the saved people, on 1~Cor.~12:27 ---
\emph{hymeis de este somata Christou}, transliterated because this document
sets no Greek face. \textbf{That is a fourth reading of the verse that does not
stack with the other three}, and it removes the ground for treating the
inward-ecclesial reading as a nineteenth-century departure from the Fathers ---
though it stands on a different Pauline text and without the nuptial figure, so
it corroborates neither Augustine nor Guéranger. \textbf{His state travels with
him:} inspected in Greek, no PG page image collated, still no registered
passage record here. \textbf{Cassiodorus was also reached at last}, in Latin:
the complete \work{Expositio in Psalterium} was retrieved from Corpus Corporum,
a transcription of Migne PL~70, \textbf{inspected and not verified} --- no
PL~70 page image and no CCSL collation, the registered CCSL~98 being
summarise-only and running only Pss.~71--150. On \latin{réspice} he gives the
same outward reading as Augustine, formalised as \textbf{hypallage} --- the
face of Christ, he says, is the Church's face in her Head --- and on this
psalm's cut clause \latin{cor meum et caro mea} he comments at v.~3b,
\textbf{the clause the missal cuts}: a second witness that \latin{caro} here is
positive, outside the appointed extract. \textbf{At the Introit he is
corroboration and probably dependence on Augustine, not a second independent
vote.} And \textbf{no qualifying cultural afterlife of the Introit's wording
was found}: the corpus was swept and every use confirmed in the optical text
was straight devotional or liturgical reception, which the gallery rule
excludes.

\subsection*{Collect \cue{Coll.}}

\elementid{Custódi, Dómine, quǽsumus, Ecclésiam tuam}{\latin{Oratio}}{1573}

\textbf{The pointing is load-bearing.} The semicolon after \latin{mortálitas} is
a semicolon and not a comma, and it divides the prayer into its two real parts:
a causal clause, \latin{quia sine te lábitur humána mortálitas}, and the double
petition that clause grounds. The two limbs of the petition are yoked by an
\latin{et \dots\ et} straddling \latin{abstrahátur} and \latin{dirigátur}, and
the first \latin{et} sits before \latin{abstrahátur} rather than before
\latin{tuis}, which is what makes the pair read as a pair. By the same
\latin{auxíliis} the Church is drawn back from what harms and steered toward
what saves: withdrawal and direction, one instrument.

\textbf{Its object is the Church, and its diagnosis is mortality.} Not sin, not
the world, not the enemy: what the prayer says is wrong is that human mortality
slips when God is not holding it. \textbf{The Church is named here and nowhere
else in the ten elements} --- though the first-person plurals of Introit,
Alleluia, Secret and Postcommunion are corporate in their own way, which softens
any contrast built on that count. The formulary's only other words for sin are
this prayer's \latin{nóxiis} and the Secret's \latin{delictórum};
\latin{peccatum} occurs nowhere in the ten elements at all.

\textbf{The one positive in the reception record is thin, and its thinness is
the finding.} Guéranger makes one interpretive move on this Collect: that the
abyss of vice the Church's children would fall into without help is specifically
the Epistle's catalogue --- a cross-element reading. \textbf{But it is an
association he asserts rather than argues, and a one-sentence editorial gloss is
not inflated here into documented reception.} His remarks on the Secret and the
Postcommunion are one sentence each and are paraphrase rather than exegesis.

\textbf{What the English witness does not carry.} The Cummiskey rendering turns
\latin{propitiatióne perpétua} into ``by thy constant mercy'' and \latin{tuis
semper auxíliis} into ``may thy grace,'' so both the noun \latin{propitiatio} and
the adverb \latin{semper} disappear --- and the measurement in the synthesis
rests on both, which is why it is conducted on the Latin. Of the three orations
it is \textbf{this} one whose English is least attested: the Secret and the
Postcommunion are recorded as collated against a page image of the 1861 printing,
while the Collect carries only an agreement-between-two-scans detector, with no
printed page and no page image ever seen.

\subsection*{Epistle \cue{Ep.}}

\elementid{Fratres: Spíritu ambuláte}{\latin{Léctio Epístolæ beáti Pauli Apóstoli ad Gálatas} --- Gal. 5, 16-24}{1574}

\subsubsection*{Where the lesson is cut from, and what the cut costs}

The pericope \textbf{replaces} Paul's own connective. Clementine Gal.\ 5:16
reads \latin{Dico autem: Spiritu ambulate}, and the appointed Epistle reads
\latin{Fratres: Spíritu ambuláte}. \latin{Dico autem} ties v.~16 back to the
liberty of v.~13 --- where Paul's own vocative \latin{fratres} stands, inside
the sentence the pericope drops --- and to the law fulfilled in love at v.~14.
\textbf{The missal's \latin{Fratres} displaces the connective rather than
supplementing it}, and supplies at v.~16 an address the apostle had used three
verses earlier.

It also ends two verses before the unit's own closing inclusio. Verse 25's
\latin{Si Spiritu vivimus, Spiritu et ambulemus} returns v.~16's verb in the
hortatory subjunctive --- and \textbf{in Greek the two verbs differ}, so the
inclusio is tighter in the Latin than in the Greek, which is a fact about the
Vulgate's rendering. \textbf{The appointed excerpt therefore ends on crucifixion
rather than on walking}, and v.~25 is the incipit of the \emph{next} Sunday's
lesson, printed on the facing pages of the same book.

\subsubsection*{Two catalogues that are not built alike}

\latin{Ópera carnis} is plural with a plural verb; \latin{fructus Spíritus} is
singular with a singular verb, and \textbf{the asymmetry stands in the Greek as
well}, so it is not a Latin artefact. The vice list is left explicitly open
(\latin{et his simília}) and the fruit list is closed with a verdict
(\latin{Advérsus huiúsmodi non est lex}). The counts are textual observation;
the doctrinal asymmetry is source-grounded synthesis.

\textbf{The list lengths, and their mechanism.} Latin: seventeen vices, twelve
fruits. Tracked Byzantine Greek: seventeen vices, nine fruits. The critical text
its apparatus records: fifteen and nine. Three precise consequences follow.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=0.15em]
\item \textbf{The Latin fruit list exceeds every tracked Greek form by exactly
three.} The pair \latin{patiéntia}/\latin{longanímitas} answers the single Greek
word for long-suffering, and \latin{modéstia} and \latin{cástitas} answer
nothing in either the Byzantine text or its apparatus. \textbf{Any argument
built on the number twelve is an argument about the liturgical Latin, and saying
so is not optional.}
\item \textbf{\latin{Homicídia} stands in neither a Revised Version nor a modern
critical-text English}, because the Byzantine text has the word and the
apparatus marks it absent from the critical text --- so \textbf{the reason is a
Byzantine-against-critical divergence and not a peculiarity of the Vulgate.}
\item \textbf{But the Vulgate's list is not simply the Byzantine list either:}
the Byzantine word for adultery has no Latin counterpart, so the Clementine
sides with the critical text at that word and with the Byzantine text at
``murders.''
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Uncertainty preserved:} the word-by-word alignment of
\latin{luxúria}/\latin{impudicítia} against their Greek neighbours is a
judgement about renderings and not a count, and is held more loosely than the
counts are. \textbf{Bound:} no critical edition is registered here; critical
readings reached this research only through the inline apparatus braces of the
tracked Greek files, read structurally rather than by string search, and the
artifact's own record warns that a raw literal search can match apparatus
wording as well as edition wording.

\subsubsection*{What the commentators actually argue}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{John Chrysostom, \work{Commentarius in epistolam ad Galatas} cap.~V,
verse by verse.} \emph{Acquired, searched and inspected \textbf{in Greek} this
revision, from a real text layer (PG~61 content in the TLG digitisation), with
no \work{Patrologia Graeca} page image collated and still no registered passage
record here; NPNF's English, first series, was the route by which he was first
found, and every English string below is NPNF's rendering.} \textbf{His
governing move survives the Greek, and is sharper there:} \emph{horas hoti ou
phesi ten sarka entautha, alla ton geode logismon kai chamai syromenon} ---
\textbf{the object is \emph{sarx} and the contrast term \emph{logismos}}, an
earthly cast of mind dragged along the ground, \textbf{not \emph{soma}}; NPNF's
``by the flesh, he does not mean the body'' is a paraphrase that does not
misrepresent the sense, but \textbf{the Greek makes a sharper claim about the
word the Epistle actually uses}, and a guide arguing from the appointed
\latin{caro} should prefer it. Proved from the vice-list itself, since
``hatred, variance, emulations, strife, heresies, and witchcraft \dots\ arise
merely from a depraved moral choice.'' \textbf{His argument from the verb holds
in the appointed Latin:} ``If these things belonged to nature and not to a bad
moral choice, his expression, \emph{they practise}, is inappropriate'' --- and
the missal prints \latin{qui tália agunt}. \textbf{The Greek confirms two
wordings of the appointed Latin against rivals:} his \emph{Ouk eipe, to ergon
tou pneumatos, all', Ho karpos} is verbatim NPNF's ``He says not, the work of
the Spirit, but, the fruit,'' and his lemma at the list's close is \emph{Qui
autem sunt Christi} --- \textbf{the missal's reading, against Augustine's
\latin{Qui sunt in Christo Iesu}}, so on this clause the appointed Latin is the
better witness to Paul. \textbf{And at the lesson's last verse he insists the
crucifixion is not the destruction of the flesh}, \emph{Ou gar de ten sarka
aneilon: epei pos emellon zen?} --- exact discipline --- \textbf{the strongest
single support the sweep holds for reading the appointed v.~24 non-ascetically.}
\textbf{This is the fullest direct verse-by-verse exposition of the appointed
Epistle located}, covering lemmata at vv.~16, 17, 18, 19--21, 22 and 24 and
continuing to v.~25, where the missal's pericope stops.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{And the rule of this guide's own evidence applies here before anything
is built on him.} His own Greek vice lemma has seventeen items and includes
both \emph{pharmakeia} and \emph{phonoi}, so \textbf{he is not barred from
\latin{veneficia} or \latin{homicidia}}; \textbf{what he is barred from is
\latin{luxúria}, \latin{modéstia} and \latin{cástitas}}, the three appointed
words with no counterpart in his text, and no comment on them is put in his
mouth. His nine-item fruit lemma is the Greek's, in the same order Augustine's
and Jerome's lemmata carry.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{Expositio epistolae ad Galatas} ss.~45--53, continuous
on the whole appointed pericope.} \emph{Retrieved in Latin this revision from a
transcription, inspected, not collated against \work{Patrologia Latina}~35 or a
critical edition.} \textbf{His own lemma differs from the missal's at every
load-bearing point: thirteen vices} --- \latin{animositates} for the missal's
\latin{irae}, \latin{haereses} for \latin{sectae} --- \textbf{and nine fruits},
and at the exclusion \latin{regnum Dei non possidebunt} where the missal prints
\latin{non consequentur}. \textbf{So his systematic opposition of the two lists}
--- \latin{Recte igitur fornicationi opponitur caritas}, point for point ---
\textbf{cannot be mapped onto the missal's seventeen and twelve without
distortion}, and is reported here as his reading of his own text. \textbf{On
the counts he refuses the argument altogether}: \latin{Non enim hoc suscepit,
ut doceret, quot sint, sed in quo genere} --- and he reads \emph{both} lists as
open, grounding it on the missal's own word, \latin{Non enim ait: Adversus haec
non est lex, sed: Adversus huiusmodi}. \textbf{On v.~16 he makes the argument
turn on the mood of the verb}: Paul said not that you shall not \emph{have} the
desires of the flesh but that you shall not \emph{fulfil} them --- and though
his lemma reads \latin{concupiscentias carnis ne perfeceritis}, the argument
survives into the missal's \latin{non perficietis}, which is still about
fulfilling. \textbf{On v.~17 he denies that the verse removes free choice}, the
verse being addressed to those who will not hold the grace of faith --- an
anti-Pelagian-shaped argument drawing on Rom.~5--8, none of it appointed. At
the connective the pericope cuts he reads \latin{Spiritu ambulate} as the answer
to v.~15's biting and devouring, naming \latin{humilitas et mansuetudo} the
first and great gift of the Spirit --- \textbf{and \latin{mansuetúdo} is in the
appointed fruit list, so that link the appointed text can carry}. At the last
verse he grounds the crucifixion of the flesh in \textbf{chaste fear}, the fear
of the chaste wife against the adulteress's, through Ps.~118:120.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Jerome, \work{Commentarii in epistolam ad Galatas} lib.~III,
ad 5:16--24.} \emph{Retrieved in Latin this revision from a transcription,
inspected, not collated against \work{Patrologia Latina}~26 or a critical
edition.} \textbf{He rules on the missal's list count in his own voice:}
\latin{Sed sciendum non plus quam quindecim carnis opera nominata} --- fifteen
vices, with \latin{adulterium}, \latin{impudicitia} and \latin{homicidia}
recorded as additions found \emph{in Latinis codicibus}, and the items numbered
in his own text --- \textbf{so the 1962 Epistle prints two of the three he
rejects, in a list of seventeen}. The bound: this is Jerome on the Latin codices
he knew about 386--388, not a judgement about the Clementine's descent, and
nothing is said about how the 1962 text got its count. \textbf{On the missal's
own word \latin{vitiis} he is its defender, not its corrector:}
\latin{Ubi Latinus interpres vitia posuit, in Graeco pathemata, id est,
passiones leguntur}, and \latin{desideria} was added \latin{ut non naturam
corporis videretur in spiritualibus viris negare, sed vitia} --- \textbf{which
also explains Augustine's divergent lemma \latin{passionibus}}: two renderings
of one Greek noun, not two different texts. \textbf{His philology of the
vice-list is Greek-anchored and checkable}: \latin{aemulatio} = \emph{zelos};
\latin{rixae} renders \emph{eritheia} and not \emph{mache}; \latin{haeresis}
from \emph{helesthai}, choosing. \textbf{He distinguishes \latin{modestia} from
\latin{continentia}} --- the former belonging to the perfect, the latter to
those still on the way --- \textbf{which is not the medieval
\latin{continentia}/\latin{castitas} pair} Anthony and Aquinas share, and must
not be collapsed with it. On v.~17 he answers the free-choice objection by a
\textbf{tripartite anthropology}, the soul standing in the middle between flesh
and spirit --- \textbf{the same difficulty Chrysostom raises in Greek and the
same answer, a convergence with nothing showing dependence}. On v.~18 he tells
a Latin reader that \textbf{\latin{spiritus} there stands without the article
and so is the Holy Spirit}, a distinction Greek marks and Latin cannot ---
\emph{qui arthra penitus non habemus}; the 1962 book's lower-case \latin{spiritu}
at v.~18 and capital \latin{Spiritus} at v.~22 are the printers making visible
what he says the language cannot. \textbf{He transmits Origen verbatim and
partly withdraws from him}: at v.~24 he records Origen construing \latin{Christi}
with \latin{carnem} --- those without law crucified the flesh \emph{of Christ}
--- and declines it, \latin{ut non carnem Christi, sed suam eos crucifixisse
dicamus}; and on the fruit's being unnamed he transmits Origen's reading that
the works are \emph{manifest} and the fruit not, so it must be sought with
labour, while saying at 5:19--21 that on reflection the named vices seem to him
plain sense. \textbf{Origen reaches this guide only through Jerome, a checked
report of an unchecked source.} \textbf{And on the clause the list closes with,
he and Augustine converge on one proof-text}, 1~Tim.~1:8--10, that the law is
not laid down for the just --- \textbf{the firmest reception the sweep found
for \latin{Adversus huiúsmodi non est lex}, and convergence, not dependence}.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{Four accounts of the two lists' asymmetry, and they are not flattened
into one.} The textual reading: the vice list is left open (\latin{et his
simília}) and the fruit list closed with a verdict. Augustine, against the
argument from counts altogether, reads \emph{both} lists as open on the
missal's own \latin{huiúsmodi} --- pointing the opposite way, and both stand.
Origen through Jerome: the works are called \emph{manifest} and the fruit not,
so the fruit must be sought with labour. Chrysostom: evil works come from us
alone, while the good need God's kindness too --- \textbf{and Jerome's own
reason differs from his, vices ending in themselves where virtues sprout and
overflow, the Greek account being about grace and the Latin about fecundity.}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Thomas Aquinas, \work{Super Galatas} cap.~5 lect.~5 and 6.}
\emph{Acquired, searched and inspected \textbf{in the Latin} --- materially
better than the English-only witnesses --- but the transcription is a machine
transcription not collated against a printed Leonine or Marietti page, so not
verified.} \textbf{He expounds the missal's own lists, and is the only witness
in this sweep that does.} He orders the twelve fruits by what each perfects ---
interior, exterior, what is above us, what is below us --- and reads the number
as answering Apoc.\ 22:2, \latin{ex utraque parte lignum vitae afferens fructus
duodecim}; \textbf{the Apocalypse link is his own move and is attributed to him
here, not treated as the list's evident sense.} He groups the seventeen vices by
object, derives \latin{veneficia} from \latin{venenum}, and reads
\latin{homicidia} \latin{cordis et operis}, of the heart as well as of the deed,
citing 1~Jn.\ 4. \textbf{He raises the objection Chrysostom raises --- that
several listed vices are plainly not bodily --- and answers it by a different
route:} Augustine's definition at \work{De civitate Dei} XIV.2, that whoever
lives according to himself lives according to the flesh, so that \latin{caro}
here stands for the whole man. \textbf{Preserve the difference; this guide does
not report a single patristic-scholastic consensus.} \emph{Lead not followed:}
\work{De civitate Dei} XIV.2 was not opened, though the repository holds a
record for the work, so Aquinas's use of it is reported and Augustine's wording
is not.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Anthony of Padua, \latin{Sermo Dominica XIV post Pentecosten}.}
\emph{Acquired and read in the Latin; the downloaded file's digest reproduced
the already-registered artifact record bit for bit, which makes this the
strongest identity in the sweep. \textbf{Rights posture:} the artifact is
registered restricted because of its modern apparatus. Anthony's
thirteenth-century Latin is public domain by age and is what may be quoted; the
modern apparatus is paraphrased and never reproduced, and no bytes were written
into this repository.} \textbf{He left a sermon headed for this very Sunday, and
it is partly a false friend.} He states his own Mass's texts explicitly: his
Introit is \latin{Inclina, Domine, aurem tuam ad me} --- the 1962
\textbf{Fifteenth} Sunday's Introit --- and his Gospel is Luke 17:11--19, the ten
lepers. His whole sermon is built on leprosy and cleansing, and his stated reason
for pairing Epistle with Gospel (\latin{in evangelio fit mentio de leprosis \dots\
et in epistola ponuntur vitia, ex quibus provenit lepra peccati}) is a rationale
for a pairing \textbf{the 1962 missal does not make}. \textbf{What legitimately
transfers is his exposition of the Epistle's own words; what does not is any
claim that the Church pairs Gal.~5 with a cleansing narrative, or anything
resting on \latin{Inclina, Domine}.}
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{Anthony's substantive moves on the Epistle.} He glosses \latin{idolorum
servitus} as \textbf{avarice} rather than the cult of images; he moralises
\latin{veneficia} into \latin{suggestiones daemonum et adulationes peccatorum,
quae nocere non possunt nisi ad sanguinem, idest animae consensum, pervenerint};
and he defines all twelve fruits individually --- \textbf{passing over
\latin{homicidia} with no gloss at all.} His definitions of the last two,
\latin{continentia, quae a licitis abstinet; castitas, quae recte utitur licito},
are \textbf{verbatim} the pair Aquinas independently attributes to the Gloss,
from two separately retrieved and separately hashed artifacts --- \textbf{which
is real evidence of a common medieval source.} Three qualifications travel with
all of it. His etymologies are of their period and several are fanciful
(\latin{fornicatio, idest formae necatio} is a pious paronomasia and not
philology), and they are presented as his interpretive move. His inner
quotations from the Gloss and from Isidore are \textbf{leads}, neither having
been opened, and are attributed to \emph{his use} of them and not to Isidore or
the Gloss; the same holds for the definition of charity he ascribes to
Augustine, which the edition's own note transmits through Peter Lombard and
which reads differently in the printed Augustine, \textbf{so it is not published
here as Augustine's exact words.} And his text prints three of the fruits in a
different order from the missal's --- \latin{patientia, longanimitas, bonitas,
benignitas, mansuetudo} against \latin{patiéntia, benígnitas, bónitas,
longanímitas, mansuetúdo} --- which is a fact about two texts and \textbf{not
evidence about which Vulgate recension the missal descends from.}

\textbf{The defeater that must be disclosed wherever Epistle and Gospel are
joined.} The library's sole registered treatment of Gal.\ 5:16--24 is Anthony's
sermon, and \textbf{Anthony's Sunday numbering runs one ahead of the 1962
missal's, consistently across all three of his registered sermons.} A major
medieval preacher expounding this Epistle does so beside a different Gospel. So
every cross-element candidate joining Epistle and Gospel is a claim about how
the 1962 formulary reads when taken as a whole, and \textbf{not} a claim about
how the tradition read these two texts together --- and every such candidate in
this guide says so.

\textbf{Negative results and open gaps.} Both Fathers' Galatians commentaries
were reached this revision, but \textbf{as transcriptions uncollated against
Migne or a critical edition} --- the single most valuable repair a later attempt
could make is a collation of exactly these two. Theodoret's Galatians
commentary is registered only at 3:16--22 and so reaches no appointed verse.
Cassian, Ambrosiaster and Marius Victorinus on Galatians \textbf{were not swept
at all}.

\subsection*{Gradual \cue{Grad.}}

\elementid{Bonum est confídere in Dómino}{\latin{Graduale} --- Ps. 117, 8-9}{1575}

\textbf{A negative worth recording first.} Unlike the Introit and the Offertory,
\textbf{the Gradual takes complete verses and cuts nothing}, so there is no
omission to report --- one of only three places in this formulary where the
appointed wording is the Clementine's exactly. Context: vv.~6--7 twice call the
Lord \latin{adjutor} and name \latin{homo} as what need not be feared, so v.~8's
\latin{quam confídere in hómine} generalises a contrast the psalm has just made
concrete; and v.~1's \latin{quoniam bonus} supplies the adjective the Gradual's
\latin{Bonum est} turns into a comparative formula. The tracked Clementine
prints \latin{Alleluja.} at the head of v.~1, which bears on any claim that
Ps.~117 has no superscription --- it is a liturgical rubric-word rather than an
ascription, and it is not numbered separately.

\textbf{Two witnesses were reached this revision and they divide the element
three ways.} \textbf{Cassiodorus, in Latin} (transcription of PL~70, inspected,
uncollated), observes that \textbf{the two appointed verses are governed by
anaphora} --- \latin{Et nota quod in principiis amborum versuum eadem verba
geminavit. Quae figura dicitur anaphora} --- exactly on the appointed wording;
reads the pair as \textbf{carnal against spiritual} rather than a moral climb,
citing Jas.~4:13--15, Phil.~2:13 and Jer.~17:5 against presumption of the will;
and lets \latin{princeps} cover \textbf{both the devil and the good angel},
\latin{Sic et bonus angelus dictus est princeps, sicut in Daniele legitur}.
\textbf{Theodoret, in Greek} (PG~80:1812, same bounds as the Introit's Theodoret
material), has \emph{archontes} who are \textbf{earthly rulers whose authority
is temporary}: God is good, wills the good, can do what he wills and has
imperishable authority, while men are corruptible by nature and changeable in
purpose; his preface makes them the persecuting rulers, demagogues, kings and
generals whom the gentile believers outlasted. \textbf{His psalm heading is the
same \emph{Allelouia} the Clementine prints}, a small convergence, and
\textbf{his lemma reads the missal's ablative}, one data point against the
accusative variant below without settling it. \textbf{Augustine and Cassiodorus
both reach Michael in Daniel, so between those two the shared proof-text is
probably dependence and not independent invention} --- and \textbf{the
three-way division on what the princes are} is the strongest Greek--Latin
comparison the sweep supports.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{Enarratio in Ps. CXVII} §4, on vv.~8 and 9.}
\emph{Acquired, searched and inspected in the English of the \work{Nicene and
Post-Nicene Fathers}, first series, with the Latin collated and agreeing
throughout; \work{Patrologia Latina}~37 not opened, so \textbf{every English
string quoted from him below is NPNF's rendering and not Augustine's Latin.}
\textbf{This is the only witness in the sweep whose verse tags needed no
shift}, because Ps.~117 carries no counted superscription.} \textbf{He reads the two verses as a single
escalating argument:} ``let not my friend present himself unto me as a good man,
so as to bid me repose my hope in himself \dots\ Nor let any one, who may in a
certain sense be styled a good angel, be regarded by myself as one in whom I
ought to put my trust \dots\ For angels also are called princes, even as we read
in Daniel, Michael, your prince.'' The second term is progressively strengthened
--- bad men, good men, good angels --- and still loses.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{One variant reported as observed and not as established.} The Latin
witness reads the accusative \latin{sperare in Dominum \dots\ in principes} where
the missal prints the ablative \latin{in Dómino \dots\ in princípibus}.
\textbf{One modern transcription cannot tell whether that is Augustine's psalter
or the transcriber's, and nothing in this guide is built on it without a check
against \work{Patrologia Latina}~37.}

\textbf{A cross-relation flagged as a lead and not as Augustine's claim.} His
escalation to good angels makes the Gradual's exclusion absolute rather than
moral, which is the Gospel's own logic at \latin{Nemo potest duóbus dóminis
servíre}. \textbf{He does not cite Mt.\ 6:24 here}, and the join is this guide's
observation and not his.

\textbf{Negative results.} \textbf{Chrysostom's exposition of this psalm was not
retrieved}, so the only Greek reading of the Gradual is Theodoret's, with the
bounds stated above; \textbf{Bellarmine on this psalm was not retrieved
either.} The one Bellarmine passage this repository registers falls at Ps.~33
vv.~2--3, reaching no appointed verse of any element, and is in an abridged
Victorian English translation rather than the Latin.

\subsection*{Alleluia \cue{All.}}

\elementid{Veníte, exsultémus Dómino}{no separate heading; reference inline --- Ps. 94, 1}{1576}

\textbf{The whole psalm, and where its reception went.} Psalm 94 calls the
assembly to praise, names God king above all gods and shepherd of his flock, and
then turns abruptly to warning: today if you shall hear his voice, harden not
your hearts. \textbf{The chant takes v.~1 alone} and drops the psalm title
\latin{Laus cántici ipsi David}, which the Clementine prints as part of that
verse, pointing the clause with a comma where the Clementine has a semicolon.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{Enarratio in Ps. XCIV} §2--3.} \emph{Acquired,
searched and inspected in the English of the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene
Fathers}, first series, Latin collated; \work{Patrologia Latina}~37 not
opened, so \textbf{every English string quoted from him below, in this note
and in the discussion that follows it, is NPNF's rendering and not Augustine's
Latin.}} \textbf{He takes the imperative \latin{Venite} as the load-bearing
word and denies that it means motion:} ``It is not by place, but by being unlike
Him, that a man is afar from God \dots\ If therefore by unlikeness we recede
from God, by likeness we approach unto God.'' And \latin{exsultemus} is
specified as joy \emph{in the Lord} precisely to exclude a worldly joy the same
word would cover: ``if there were not in this life a wicked joy which is to be
distinguished from a righteous joy, it would be enough to say, Come, let us
rejoice; but he has briefly distinguished it.''
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{The Latin recovers something the translation loses entirely.} Augustine
closes the argument on a jingle built from the appointed verb itself ---
\latin{Pie debes Domino exsultare, si vis securus mundo insultare}: you must
piously \emph{exult} in the Lord if you would safely \emph{exult over} the world.
The English renders this ``piously joy in the Lord, if you dost wish safely to
trample upon the world,'' and the play vanishes. \textbf{The play depends on the
missal's own word \latin{exsultémus}.}

\textbf{A material limit.} This exposition's centre of gravity is vv.~7--11,
\latin{Hodie si vocem eius audieritis}, \textbf{which the Alleluia does not
appoint}, and Augustine's argument there closes on the oath of exclusion from
rest. \textbf{A guide taking Augustine on this psalm must not import that ending
into a chant that stops at v.~1}, and this one does not. The same limit governs
the canonical relationship: Hebrews quotes Ps.\ 94:8--11 at 3:7--11, repeats the
opening at 3:15, and expounds it again through 4:1--11 --- real and checkable,
but \textbf{the formulary appoints v.~1 and no more, and that the Alleluia's
single verse carries the whole psalm's warning is an inference and not something
the appointed text states.} A precise textual detail worth carrying: the same
Clementine renders the quotation differently in the two places --- Ps.\ 94:9 has
\latin{in irritatione} where Heb.\ 3:8 quoting it has \latin{in exacerbatione} ---
\textbf{so the Vulgate's psalter and its Hebrews do not agree verbally.}

\textbf{A structural fact that constrains synthesis.} The Alleluia is the
\textbf{least lexically integrated element of the formulary}. Its only
substantive tie to any other element is \latin{salutári} --- \textbf{and that
tie is to three composed orations rather than to a lesson}; \latin{exsult-} and
\latin{iubil-} occur nowhere else in the ten elements, and it shares no other
content word beyond \latin{Dominus} and \latin{Deus}. \textbf{The joy of the
Alleluia is a natural thing for a synthesis to reach for as the resolution of
the Gospel's anxiety, and the appointed texts do not supply the lexical
warrant}: the Epistle's \latin{gaudium} and the Alleluia's \latin{exsultémus}
are different roots in different elements with nothing joining them. No
reception support for such a reading was found, and none is asserted.

\textbf{Two witnesses were reached this revision, and they disagree about the
one word the chant contributes.} \textbf{Cassiodorus, in Latin} (PL~70
transcription, inspected, uncollated), reads \latin{iubilémus} as the psalm's
own invitation to the \textbf{jubilus} --- wordless praise beyond syllables ---
and takes \latin{Venite} through Is.~29:13, a people that draws near with the
mouth; he also glosses \latin{salutari} plainly of salvation, which quietly
underlines how little else the verse carries. \textbf{Theodoret, in Greek}
(PG~80, same bounds as the Introit's Theodoret material), hears in the same
verb \textbf{the shout of victors}, an epinician: the praise of those who have
already conquered, and he composes the psalm in the person of King Josiah
foreseeing his reform, from 4~Kgs 22--23 --- \textbf{his own reconstruction and
not what the psalm states}; he also reports the psalm \textbf{untitled among
the Hebrews}, against the Clementine's printed title. \textbf{They disagree
about what \latin{iubilémus} means}, and the disagreement is preserved rather
than adjudicated. \textbf{The \latin{Deo salutári nostro} resonance:} the
collocation is rare, and its only New Testament occurrence is the Magnificat's
\latin{Deus salutari meo} --- recorded as a lexical fact and not as a designed
link.

\textbf{Negative results.} No qualifying cultural afterlife of this chant's
wording was found; the uses confirmed in the corpus were music programmes and
service listings, \textbf{and a bare musical setting is expressly excluded by
the gallery rule.}

\subsection*{Gospel \cue{Gosp.}}

\elementid{Nemo potest duóbus dóminis servíre}{\latin{Sequéntia sancti Evangélii secúndum Matthǽum} --- Mt. 6, 24-33}{1577}

\subsubsection*{Where the lesson stands in Matthew 6}

\textbf{The pericope is cut out of a continuous argument at both ends.} It
begins bare at v.~24, with no connective particle, immediately after the
treasure and eye sayings (\latin{Ubi enim est thesaurus tuus, ibi est et cor
tuum}, v.~21), and stops at v.~33 without v.~34, the passage's own conclusion.
\latin{Sollícitus} governs the discourse four times, at vv.~25, 28, 31 and 34,
and \textbf{the pericope carries three of them, ending on the promise of v.~33
rather than on the restriction to today.} That this lets the Communion take the
Gospel's last verse as its own antiphon is a further textual fact; what the
compiler meant by it is not established.

\textbf{The pericope contains the second half of a repetition internal to
Matthew 6 and not the first.} The Father's foreknowledge of need at v.~32,
\latin{Scit enim Pater vester}, restates v.~8, which stands in the instruction on
prayer the pericope does not include --- and the Lord's Prayer stands between the
two members, asking for the kingdom's coming and for bread. \textbf{Flagged as
inference:} any claim that the pericope is meant to be heard against the Our
Father is this research's to flag and not the text's to assert; Matthew supplies
the proximity and nothing in the appointed formulary points to it.

\textbf{Both sayings have Lucan parallels in different settings, and Luke orders
the material the other way round.} Luke places the two-masters saying at 16:13,
after the unjust steward, and adds \latin{servus}; Matthew has it inside the
Sermon without \latin{servus}. Luke's \latin{corvos} and \latin{cellarium, neque
horreum} differ from Matthew's \latin{volatília cæli} and \latin{hórrea}. And the
ordering is reversed: in Matthew treasure-and-heart precedes the anxiety
discourse and in Luke it follows --- \textbf{which bears directly on whether
Mt.\ 6:24 is heard as the close of the treasure unit or the head of the anxiety
unit.} The appointed pericope's boundary at v.~24 takes it as a head; whether
that is the compiler's reading or an artefact of where a pericope had to start
\textbf{is not established.}

\subsubsection*{What the commentators actually argue}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{De sermone Domini in monte} II.47--58, continuous
direct commentary on the appointed pericope.} \emph{Acquired, searched and
inspected in the English of the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, first
series; \work{Patrologia Latina}~34 not collated, so \textbf{every English
string quoted from him below, in this note and in the comparative discussion
that follows it, is NPNF's rendering and not Augustine's Latin.}}
\textbf{He reads v.~24's asymmetry as deliberate:}
Christ says of the second master \latin{contemnet} and not \latin{odio habebit}
because ``almost no one's conscience can hate God; but he despises, i.e.\ he
does not fear Him, as if feeling himself secure in consideration of His
goodness.'' \textbf{This is the strongest single patristic move on this
Gospel}, because it is a claim about which of two verbs the text uses and the
appointed Latin preserves exactly that contrast. \textbf{Two things in the same
locus are reported as his own and not as established:} the remark that
``mammon'' is Punic for gain, which was checked against no Semitic authority;
and the importation of ``the prince of this world'' from Jn.\ 12:31 to identify
the second master, \textbf{which the appointed verse does not say.}
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Jerome, \work{Commentarii in Matthaeum}, on the appointed verses.}
\emph{Retrieved in Latin this revision from a transcription, inspected, not
collated against \work{Patrologia Latina}~26 or a critical edition.}
\textbf{He draws the distinction on which any non-ascetical reading of the
Gospel depends:} \latin{non dixit, qui habet divitias, sed qui servit divitiis.
Qui enim divitiarum servus est, divitias custodit, ut servus; qui autem
servitutis excussit iugum, distribuit eas, ut dominus} --- \textbf{Aquinas
repeats the same distinction in almost the same words}, \latin{aliud est habere
divitias ut dominus, aliud ut servus}, whether by drawing on him was not
traced. \textbf{He states the limit against over-reading in one sentence
anchored inside the appointed bounds:} \latin{labor exercendus est,
sollicitudo tollenda} (ad v.~25), with \latin{De praesentibus ergo concessit
debere esse sollicitos qui futura prohibet cogitare} at vv.~31ff --- \textbf{the
better citation where a claim must rest inside the lesson}, and a third
independent Father setting the same limit. \textbf{He expressly rebuts
allegorising the birds}, naming those who read them as angels and ministering
powers, and refutes them from the \emph{a fortiori} itself: \latin{Si hoc ita
est, ut intelligi volunt, quomodo sequitur dictum ad homines: Nonne vos magis
pluris estis illis? Simpliciter ergo accipiendum} --- \textbf{a ground internal
to the appointed verse, and better for this guide's purpose than Augustine's}.
\textbf{His comment on the lilies is aesthetic, not allegorical:}
\latin{quod sericum, quae regum purpura, quae pictura textricum potest floribus
comparari?} \textbf{He gives the other etymology of \latin{mammonae}:}
\latin{Mammona sermone Syriaco divitiae nuncupantur} --- \textbf{two Fathers,
two eastern languages, no check against a Semitic authority here; the finding
is that they disagree}. \textbf{And he records a variant the missal does not
print}, \latin{In nonnullis codicibus additum est neque quid bibatis} at
v.~25, the missal's shorter form being the one he treats as his own.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Hilary, \work{Commentarius in Matthaeum}, on the appointed verses.}
\emph{Retrieved in Latin this revision from a transcription, inspected, not
collated against \work{Patrologia Latina}~9 or a critical edition.}
\textbf{He is the Latin tradition's allegorist at this pericope, and he argues
for it:} the literal sense does not cohere with what precedes ---
\latin{Quae consequuntur, non satis propositionibus congruunt. Numquid et
unius domini servus non possit circa vestem esse cibumque sollicitus?} --- and
he reads the whole pericope as doctrine about the resurrection of the body,
allegorising every appointed image: \textbf{birds the unclean spirits; the
cubit the risen body raised to the measure of the perfect man (Eph.~4:13);
lilies the brightness of the angels; the hay burnt tomorrow the gentiles.}
\textbf{Two qualifications travel with him and must not be dropped:} the
hay-as-gentiles reading ends in \textbf{a bodily eternity of the damned no
other witness states}, and the birds-as-unclean-spirits reading is the one
place where his allegory works \emph{against} the pericope's consoling sense.
\textbf{At the verse the Mass lifts out he reads plainly}, grounding
\latin{Haec enim omnia gentes inquirunt} in unbelief rather than appetite ---
\latin{Gentium igitur est, infidelitatis istius cura angi} --- \textbf{the one
place where his resurrection reading and the plain sense converge}; his
exposition runs on to v.~34, which the lesson excludes. \textbf{His lemma lacks
the last step of Chrysostom's escalation}, reading \latin{cras in ignem
mittitur} where the missal prints \latin{cras in clíbanum} --- \textbf{the step
Chrysostom's rhetoric turns on is not in every ancient Latin witness}, and the
medieval Gloss knows the variant, \latin{Alii libri habent in ignem, vel in
acervum, qui habet speciem clibani}. \textbf{He is not refuted by Jerome:}
Jerome's target is a reading of the \emph{birds} as angels, Hilary's angels are
the \emph{lilies}, and that Jerome names Hilary among the Latin commentators he
had read is not evidence that he has him in view here.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Thomas Aquinas, \work{Super Matthaeum} cap.~6 lect.~3--5.}
\emph{Retrieved in Latin this revision from a transcription, inspected, not
collated against the Marietti or a critical edition; \textbf{the text is a
\emph{reportatio}}, which matters below.} \textbf{He fastens on the other
member of the same four-verb figure Augustine reads, and explains it from the
same asymmetry:} \latin{Et non dicit, diliget, quia Diabolus naturaliter diligi
non potest. Deus naturaliter diligitur; Diabolus vero sustinetur} ---
\textbf{complementary to Augustine's \latin{contemnet} observation, and both
checkable against the appointed Latin, which prints all four verbs.} He reads
the whole saying as a claim about ultimate ends, \latin{impossibile est quod
animus feratur ad duos fines simul et semel}, and on the cubit he gives
providence an experimental proof: the measure of stature, he argues, is not in
our power --- \textbf{a case where anxiety is demonstrably vain}. \textbf{He
independently names the sect Augustine's controversy leaves unnamed:}
\latin{destruit hic error Euticharum, qui dicebant quod viri apostolici non
debebant laborare}, answered from 2~Thess.~3:10 --- \textbf{which does not
close the lead}, since \work{De haeresibus} is still unopened and no claim that
Augustine names the Euchites may be published as checked; \latin{Euticharum} is
the text's spelling and is reproduced as printed. \textbf{And this text carries
a documented misattribution:} \emph{Super Matthaeum} ascribes the
lilies-as-angels reading \textbf{to Jerome}; the reading is \textbf{Hilary's},
and the \work{Catena aurea} --- Thomas's own compilation --- attributes it
correctly to \latin{Hilarius in Matth.} \textbf{The slip is most likely the
reporter's, and is reported as a misattribution in that text, not as an error
of Thomas.}
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, on the birds and the lilies.} \emph{Same route and state.}
\textbf{He explicitly forbids allegorising them:} ``these examples are not to be
treated as allegories, so that we should inquire what the fowls of heaven or the
lilies of the field mean: for they stand here, in order that from smaller
matters we may be persuaded respecting greater ones,'' with the unjust judge as
his comparison case. \textbf{This bears on this guide's own apparatus} --- the
profile requires an allegorical row on page~1, and one of the two principal
Latin Fathers commenting on this very pericope says its central images are not
to be taken allegorically. \textbf{Page~1 now resolves the constraint the
tradition's own way, by reporting the named division} --- Hilary allegorising,
Jerome and Augustine forbidding --- rather than by quietly allegorising the
lilies anyway or by suppressing his rule. The restraint is specific: he bars
allegorising \emph{these examples}, not the pericope as a whole, and he is at
the same time willing to read ``seek first the kingdom'' as a doctrine of
ordered ends.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{John Chrysostom, \work{Homiliae in Matthaeum} 21--22.} \emph{Acquired,
searched and inspected in the English of the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene
Fathers}, first series --- \textbf{which is the rendering reproduced by every
English string quoted from him below, in this note and in the comparative
discussion that follows it, and not his Greek};
\work{Patrologia Graeca}~57 not collated. \textbf{A defect in the retrieved
witness, recorded rather than silently corrected:} the page carrying Homily 22
prints its lemma header as ``Matthew 5:28-29,'' which is wrong --- the text
beneath is Mt.\ 6:28--29, and a worker resolving loci from that header would
mis-cite the homily.} \textbf{He reads the illustrations as rhetorically
engineered.} The birds are chosen \emph{because} they are irrational,
deliberately in place of Moses, Elijah or John, ``that He might touch them more
to the quick \dots\ He has cut off from them every excuse''; and the lilies are
progressively degraded --- lilies, then ``grass of the field,'' then ``which
today is,'' then ``cast into the oven'' --- so the \emph{a fortiori} grows at
every step. \textbf{The appointed Latin fits word for word:} \latin{fænum agri,
quod hódie est, et cras in clíbanum míttitur \dots\ quanto magis vos módicæ
fídei?}, and he calls the rebuke ``not want, but poverty, of faith.''
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{Where both Fathers comment they agree the images are arguments and not
figures, and they differ in what the argument is.} Augustine grounds the
inference in the \textbf{rank} of the creature --- ``a rational being such as
man has a higher rank in the nature of things than irrational ones.'' Chrysostom
grounds it in the \textbf{vileness} of the creature and in the deliberate
rhetorical descent, and gives the anthropological ground in terms of \emph{gift}
rather than rank: ``you, to whom He gave a soul, for whom He fashioned a body
\dots\ for whose sake He gave up His only begotten Son.'' \textbf{The difference
is not a contradiction, but the middle terms differ and the two yield different
homiletic conclusions from the same verses. A guide reporting only that ``the
Fathers read the birds as an argument from lesser to greater'' would have
flattened the more interesting half of the evidence.}

\textbf{Both Fathers independently set a limit against over-reading the
pericope.} Augustine at II.57--58: Christ kept money bags, Paul worked with his
hands, the church at Antioch stored food against Agabus's famine, and the whole
precept reduces to the rule ``that even in looking after such things we should
think of the kingdom of God, but in the service of the kingdom of God we should
not think of such things.'' Chrysostom reaches the same limit by a different
route on v.~26: ``He said not, we must not sow, but we must not take thought;
neither that one ought not to work, but not to be low-minded.'' \textbf{Where the
claim must rest inside the appointed bounds, Chrysostom's sentence is the better
one}, because Augustine's material there is anchored partly in v.~34, which this
lesson excludes, while Chrysostom's is anchored in v.~26, which it includes.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{De opere monachorum} §27, §30, §31 --- documented
\emph{controversial} reception.} \emph{Lead taken from the \work{Catena aurea},
\textbf{checked against the underlying work and confirmed}; acquired, searched
and inspected in the English of the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, first
series, which is the rendering quoted below.} \textbf{This runs against the obvious
devotional reading.} Certain monks quoted these very verses to argue that they
need not work, and Augustine wrote against them: ``let these persons, who from
perverse understanding of the Gospel labor to pervert apostolic precepts, either
take no thought for the morrow, even as the birds of the air; or let them obey
the Apostle''; ``against the Apostle of Christ, they recite a Gospel of Christ.''
\textbf{Not confirmed, and therefore not published as checked:} the
\latin{Augustinus de Haeres.} citation naming the Euchitae, since
\work{De haeresibus} was not retrieved. That identification remains an
unverified lead.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{The \work{Catena aurea} is now a checked map for four of its
authorities and a lead-map for the rest.} The appointed Gospel occupies
cap.~6 lect.~16--21, drawing on Augustine, Jerome, Hilary, Chrysostom, Gregory,
Rabanus, Remigius and the Gloss. \textbf{The Catena cites \latin{Chrysostomus
in Matth.} and \latin{Chrysostomus super Matth.} as different things:} the
first is the genuine \work{Homiliae in Matthaeum}, cross-matched sentence for
sentence against the independently retrieved Homily 22; \textbf{the second is
the anonymous \work{Opus imperfectum in Matthaeum}, a fifth-century work with
Arian features whose attribution to Chrysostom is universally rejected.} A
guide that quoted ``Chrysostom'' on Mt.\ 6:33 from the \latin{super Matth.}
formula would have attributed to a Doctor of the Church a text that is not his.
\textbf{Four attributions are checked and are reported as checked} --- the
Chrysostom of lect.~19, the \work{De opere monachorum} of lect.~17, and
\textbf{every \latin{Hilarius} and \latin{Hieronymus} excerpt on the appointed
Gospel, verified sentence by sentence against the independently retrieved
underlying works}, which reproduces them faithfully. \textbf{The check was made
against the same Migne recensions the Catena drew on}, so it establishes
faithful transmission and not the Migne text's own correctness.
\textbf{Gregory, Rabanus, Remigius and the Gloss remain unverified leads and
are cited from this Catena nowhere in this guide.}

\textbf{Negative results.} \textbf{The source library holds no registered
witness for Mt.\ 6:24--33 at all}: Chrysostom's homilies are registered only at
23 and 49, and the appointed pericope falls in 21--22, so the homilies above
were reached by direct retrieval rather than through a registered passage ---
and the same is true of the new Latin witnesses, Jerome, Hilary and Aquinas,
reached this revision. Gregory, Rabanus, Remigius and the Gloss \textbf{remain
unchecked leads}.

\subsection*{Offertory \cue{Off.}}

\elementid{Immíttet Angelus Dómini}{\latin{Ant. ad Offertorium} --- Ps. 33, 8-9}{1578}

\textbf{The psalm is anchored by its own superscription to a named deliverance
narrative --- and the narrative does not fit the name.} \latin{Davidi, cum
immutavit vultum suum coram Achimelech}. The tracked Douay prints the
cross-reference in the text as ``[1 Kings 21.],'' which in Douay book-naming is
modern \textbf{1~Samuel~21}. \textbf{Read whole, that chapter shows Achimelech
the priest at Nobe in vv.~1--9 and the change of countenance at v.~13 before
Achis, king of Geth}; the tracked CPDV reads \latin{Abimelech} at the
superscription. \textbf{The divergence is recorded and left unexplained}: a
common scholarly explanation exists but no source for it is registered here, so
it is not stated. The divergence bears materially, because Augustine's
Eucharistic reading below is anchored in David \emph{before Achis} while the
Latin superscription names Achimelech. So the angel who encamps stands in a
psalm whose superscription reads it off a concrete escape, and \latin{eripiet
eos} at v.~8 repeats the \latin{eripuit me} of v.~5. \textbf{The antiphon stops
one clause short of the psalm's beatitude.}

\textbf{The alphabetic acrostic can now be stated --- as documented reception,
and not as a fact about the Hebrew.} Cassiodorus's \latin{Divisio psalmi}
states it and locates the appointed verses within it: \latin{Per totum psalmum
verba prophetae sunt, alphabeti Hebraei litteras, excepta sexta, in capitibus
versuum per ordinem ponentis}, tagging every verse with its letter ---
\textbf{HETH at the Offertory's first verse and TETH at its second} --- and on
the skipped letter, \latin{Ego enim non inveni Patrum definitam de hac parte
sententiam}. \textbf{Two bounds:} this is a sixth-century Latin reader
reporting about the Hebrew, and no Hebrew psalter is held here, so what stands
is his report and no statement about the Hebrew text; Bellarmine, the one other
registered witness noting the structure, does so at vv.~2--3, reaching no
appointed verse, in an abridged Victorian English translation.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{Enarratio in Ps. XXXIII} \textbf{Sermo II}.}
\emph{Acquired, searched and inspected in the English of the \work{Nicene and
Post-Nicene Fathers}, first series, with the Latin collated;
\work{Patrologia Latina}~36 not opened, so \textbf{every English string quoted
from him below is NPNF's rendering and not Augustine's Latin.}
\textbf{The locus is Sermo II and not
Sermo I: the passage is absent from Sermo I entirely, and any citation to
Sermo~I is wrong.}} \textbf{He rejects a rival reading and defends the one the
missal prints:} ``So it is written, brethren, not as some bad copies have it,
\emph{The Lord shall send His Angel round about them that fear Him} \dots\ but
thus, \emph{The Angel of the Lord shall send round about them that fear Him}.''
He then identifies that Angel as Christ, ``the Angel of the great Counsel.'' The
1962 Offertory prints \latin{Immíttet Angelus Dómini} --- \latin{Angelus Dómini}
nominative subject of \latin{immíttet} --- \textbf{the very reading he defends.}
Latin as collated: \latin{mendosi codices: Immittet Angelum Dominus \dots\ sed
sic: Immitte[t] Angelus Domini \dots}
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{The Latin collation sharpened both the convergence and its limit.}
Augustine's text continues \latin{et ERUET eos} where the missal prints
\latin{et ERIPIET eos} --- \textbf{so the two agree exactly on the point in
dispute, which noun is the subject, and differ on the following verb.} That
difference is evidence that \textbf{the missal's text is not simply Augustine's},
and it is reported here alongside the agreement rather than suppressed.

\textbf{Two unproven steps this guide does not take.} That the missal's reading
\emph{descends} from Augustine's; and that the missal's capital \latin{Angelus}
encodes his christological identification. \textbf{The capital is an
orthographic fact, and the transmission question belongs to a textual history no
lane swept.} What is reported is a convergence between the appointed text and a
Father's defended reading, which is checkable --- not dependence, which is not.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Cassiodorus, \work{Expositio in Psalterium}, at Ps.~33 vv.~8--9.}
\emph{Retrieved in Latin this revision from a transcription of PL~70
(Corpus Corporum), inspected, not collated against a printed page; the
registered CCSL~98 runs only Pss.~71--150 and so does not reach this psalm.}
\textbf{His lemma reads \latin{Immittet angelum Dominus} --- exactly the
reading Augustine denounced as belonging to faulty copies.} \textbf{So the
variant Augustine fought was still standard in the principal medieval Latin
psalm commentary, and the 1962 Offertory follows Augustine against
Cassiodorus} --- a convergence and not a descent, nothing tracing
transmission. \textbf{He does not identify the angel with Christ, and that is a
disagreement to preserve rather than stack:} he reads \latin{Immittet} as
deliberately covert --- \latin{propter insolentiam humanae fragilitatis non
palam facit, sed occultis immissionibus operatur} --- and turns to the moral
sense, \latin{si vis te angelum fieri, fac quod praecipit \dots\ Tunc enim
spiritu angeli sumus, quando ministri supernae voluntatis efficimur}.
\textbf{He is positive evidence that a Latin reader could take the verse
otherwise}, which is what the orthographic-capital warning above asserts only
negatively.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Cassiodorus, on \latin{gustáte et vidéte} in the same exposition.}
\emph{Same route and state.} \textbf{His eucharistic reading is anchored in the
appointed verbs themselves, where Augustine's is anchored in the superscription
the antiphon does not print:} \latin{Gustate non pertinet ad palatum, sed
animae suavissimum sensum, qui divina contemplatione saginatur. Nam ut ipsum
gustum intelligeres, sequitur, videte, quod utique non ad os pertinet} --- and
he reads it eucharistically from the verb, citing Jn.~6:53 and the life-giving
flesh taken from the Virgin. \textbf{So the reading is available to a hearer of
the antiphon alone}, lifting the limit Augustine's route imposes; and note the
convergence with Cyril of Jerusalem's ``trust not the judgment to your bodily
palate'', reached in Latin and without dependence on the Jerusalem catechesis.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, on \latin{gustáte et vidéte} in the same exposition.}
\emph{Same route and state.} \textbf{He reads v.~9 as openly Eucharistic},
decoding the psalm's superscription: the David who ``was carried in his own
hands'' before Achish is Christ holding his own Body --- \latin{cum commendaret
ipsum corpus suum et sanguinem suum, accepit in manus suas quod norunt fideles}
--- so ``taste'' is set against the incomprehension of Jn.\ 6:52. \textbf{The
limit that materially bears on this formulary: his reading is anchored in the
psalm's superscription, and the Offertory antiphon prints no superscription}, so
his reasoning is available to this guide as reception of the psalm and not as
something a hearer of the antiphon alone could recover.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{Two fourth-century mystagogical witnesses attest the verse as the
Church's own communion invitation, and the 1962 Mass does not use it that way.}
Cyril of Jerusalem reports it actually being chanted at the communion: ``ye hear
the chanter inviting you with a sacred melody to the communion of the Holy
Mysteries, and saying, O taste and see that the Lord is good. Trust not the
judgment to your bodily palate.'' Ambrose cites it to identify what is eaten:
``Taste and see that the Lord is good, blessed is the man that hopes in Him. In
that sacrament is Christ.'' \textbf{In the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday the same verse
stands at the Offertory, before the consecration, and this Mass's Communion is a
different text entirely. That displacement is not smoothed over here.} Note also
that \textbf{Ambrose quotes the verse \emph{with} the clause \latin{beatus vir
qui sperat in eo} which the Roman Offertory omits}, which sharpens rather than
weakens the observation that the antiphon cuts at \latin{Dóminus}.
\emph{Bounds:} both witnesses were read in English only and neither was
collated against \work{Patrologia Graeca}~33 or \work{Patrologia Latina}~16 ---
\textbf{Cyril in the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, second series,
vol.~7, and Ambrose in the same series, vol.~10, which are the renderings quoted
above}; \textbf{Cyril's authorship of the Mystagogical Catecheses is disputed
in modern scholarship and that question was not swept}; and no claim is made
here about Roman practice or about any transmission between Jerusalem and Rome.

\textbf{And the displacement is attested as living practice in a surviving
Western rite, so it is well precedented and is not presented as a discovery.}
The Mozarabic \work{Missale mixtum} prints \latin{Gustate et videte quam
suavis est Dominus} as the \latin{ad accedentes} chant at communion time,
cued by incipit thirty-four times across the book --- \textbf{as liturgical
fact}, in a searched OCR layer, with the \latin{quam}/\latin{quoniam}
difference noted and poor OCR cautioned.

\textbf{The Offertory's argument runs close to the Gospel's two verses past its
own cut.} Ps.\ 33:10--11 (\latin{Timete Dominum \dots\ Divites eguerunt, et
esurierunt; inquirentes autem Dominum non minuentur omni bono}) and
Mt.\ 6:32--33 both set seeking God against want of food and both promise that
those who seek will not lack. \textbf{Clearly flagged as inference:} this is a
similarity of argument, not of wording, and not a quotation in either direction.
The Latin verbs differ, and \textbf{the Gospel's own \latin{inquírunt} at v.~32
is used of the gentiles negatively where the psalm uses the participle
positively}, which cuts against reading it as a verbal link. It is recorded
because the appointed excerpt does not contain the psalm verses in question at
all, and a reader could otherwise mistake it for something the formulary makes.

\textbf{The chant's other Roman use.} Ps.\ 33:8--9 has \textbf{exactly two Roman
uses in the searched Missal}, the second appointed immediately after the
Canaanite-woman Gospel ending \latin{O mulier, magna est fides tua}. \textbf{That
second use was found only by reading a recorded line range}, because a
whole-word search over the tracked Missal misses it entirely: the optical text
breaks \latin{Immittet} across a line end. \textbf{Any cross-Mass negative
produced by a whole-word search over that artifact is weak evidence of absence},
here and everywhere else in this guide.

\subsection*{Secret \cue{Sec.}}

\elementid{Concéde nobis, Dómine, quǽsumus}{\latin{Secreta}}{1579}

\textbf{One victim, two genitives.} The two \latin{et} clauses are parallel
predicates of the one subject \latin{hæc hóstia salutáris}, and the second
genitive is objective in a different way from the first: the victim is
\latin{purgátio} \emph{of our offences} and \latin{propitiátio} \emph{of thy
power} --- \textbf{the appeasing of God's power, not a power belonging to the
propitiation.} At the offering, the protection the Introit and Collect asked for
becomes propitiation. \textbf{One genuine convergence in the reception record
is worth keeping:} Guéranger construes \latin{propitiatio potestatis} the same
way, independently agreeing with this leaf's own collated construal.

\textbf{Transmission.} The Secret stands with the Collect in Gelasianum
Book~III sect.~X and has a second, penitential home at sect.~XLI --- with the
narrowing recorded above, that the second home is a feature of the Vatican
manuscript's arrangement rather than of the Gelasian tradition at large.

\subsection*{Communion \cue{Comm.}}

\elementid{Primum quǽrite regnum Dei}{\latin{Ant. ad Communionem} --- Mt. 6, 33}{1580}

\subsubsection*{The one substantive act this formulary performs on its Bible text}

\textbf{The Communion departs from Mt.\ 6:33 in exactly four ways}, verified
against the Clementine, the Lucan parallel and the tracked Greek: \latin{ergo}
is dropped and \latin{Primum} promoted to head the clause; \latin{et iustítiam
eius} is omitted; \latin{hæc} is dropped before \latin{ómnia}; and \latin{dicit
Dóminus} is added. \textbf{That tag stands in none of Clementine Mt.\ 6:33,
Clementine Lk.\ 12:31, or the tracked Greek at Mt.\ 6:33.} And \textbf{Luke,
unlike the antiphon, keeps \latin{iustitiam eius}, so the omission is not
explained by conflation with the Lucan form.}

Two of the four bear theologically. Adding \latin{dicit Dóminus} \textbf{turns a
saying inside a sermon into an address made to the communicant at the moment of
reception}; and dropping \latin{et iustítiam eius} leaves the kingdom to be
sought without the justice the Gospel names beside it.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{De sermone Domini in monte} II.53, II.55, II.56, on
\latin{primum}.} \emph{Acquired, searched and inspected in the English of the
\work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, first series, which is the rendering
quoted below;
\textbf{and the decisive sentence is additionally corroborated in Latin by a
second, independent, separately hashed thirteenth-century witness, whose
\latin{non tempore, sed dignitate} answers the English ``not in point of time,
but in point of importance'' exactly.} That is corroboration and not
verification: a catena excerpt is a lead as to the underlying work, and
\work{Patrologia Latina}~34 was not opened --- but the Latin wording of the
sentence a guide is most likely to quote no longer rests on NPNF's English
alone.}
\textbf{He argues an ordering of importance and not of time:} ``in using that
word \emph{first}, He has indicated that this is to be sought later, not in
point of time, but in point of importance,'' and he makes the point by denying
the sequence the word invites --- ``He did not say, \emph{Then} seek such
things.'' He offers an inversion test at II.55 and ties the phrase back to
Mt.\ 6:24 at II.56.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{The line this guide does not cross.} Augustine's exegesis of Mt.\ 6:33
is documented reception and is his. \textbf{The observation that the missal's
Communion antiphon promotes \latin{Primum} to first position --- where
Augustine's argument makes word order carry the doctrine --- is an editorial
observation, and Augustine knows nothing of the antiphon.} Both may be stated;
the second is not presented as patristic. Note also that his exposition covers
v.~34, which the appointed pericope excludes.

\subsubsection*{And the Communion is a later substitution at this Mass}

\textbf{Three independent witnesses converge on the antiphon being secondary in
this position.} First, the appointed text itself: this is the formulary's one
substantive departure from its Bible text. Second, Guéranger: ``The
Communion-anthem, taken from the Gospel which now is assigned to this Sunday,
was not the one primitively used; the ancient liturgists make no mention of it
in its present position, nor is it to be found there in any of the manuscripts
consulted by Blessed Thomasi.'' Third, the ninth-century chant apparatus:
Wilson's index shows \latin{Primum quaerite} occurring as a Communion at exactly
one locus, whose chant set is the 1962 \emph{Ninth} Sunday's, the
\latin{Protector noster} set there taking \latin{Panis quem ego}. Setting that
against the 1962 book shows \textbf{a rotation rather than a single
replacement}: in that apparatus the 1962-Ninth set takes \latin{Primum
quaerite}, the 1962-Fourteenth set \latin{Panis quem ego}, and the
1962-Fifteenth set \latin{Qui manducat}, while 1962 assigns \latin{Qui manducat}
to the Ninth, \latin{Primum quaerite} to the Fourteenth and \latin{Panis quem
ego} to the Fifteenth.

\textbf{A control from within the same Office, and it is the strongest single
thing here because anyone can check it in the same pages.} Guéranger prints this
Sunday's Magnificat antiphon at Vespers as \latin{Quaerite primum regnum Dei, et
justitiam ejus, et haec omnia adjicientur vobis. Alleluia.} \textbf{That antiphon
retains both \latin{et iustítiam eius} and \latin{haec} --- exactly the two
clauses the Mass's Communion drops --- and leaves \latin{primum} in its Matthaean
position after the verb.} The \work{Breviarium Romanum} itself was not opened;
this is a within-one-book control, the same editor printing both forms.

\textbf{Limits.} \textbf{Guéranger's judgment rests on Tommasi's Antiphonary and
``the ancient liturgists,'' neither consulted: it is a checked report of an
unchecked claim, attributed to him and never restated as established chant
history.} Antiphon texts are routinely shortened for chant and \latin{dicit
Dóminus} is a common formulaic tag, so both changes may be conventional rather
than expressive, and that count was not run. Two of the three 1962 assignments
in the rotation were verified against repository text; \textbf{the Fifteenth
Sunday's Communion was not}, no leaf for it existing here. What the rotation
establishes is \textbf{what the older pairing was, not when the substitution
happened} --- the instrument that would test it, Hesbert's \work{Antiphonale
Missarum Sextuplex}, is registered at catalog level only. \textbf{And the
recasting is not proper to the 1962 edition:} the same form is printed in the
Pustet Missal of Ratisbon, 1862, which this repository tracks as public-domain
text --- which shows the recasting is old and says nothing about why it was
made. One further weakness is recorded rather than left to be discovered: that
the Communion selects the imperative rather than the pericope's three
prohibitions \textbf{may be an artefact of taking the final verse} rather than a
choice among the pericope's parts.

\textbf{One reading this guide is warned off and does not make.} That the
omission of \latin{et iustítiam eius} should be read against the Epistle's
release from law is \textbf{probably defeated}: the omission is far more likely
metrical, no witness connects it to Galatians, and Paul's ``not under law'' and
Matthew's ``his justice'' are not the same concept.

\subsection*{Postcommunion \cue{Postcomm.}}

\elementid{Puríficent semper et múniant}{\latin{Postcommunio}}{1581}

\textbf{The figure, and the noun held to the end.} \latin{Ad perpétuæ \dots\
ducant salvatiónis efféctum} splits the genitive around the verb, exactly as the
Thirteenth Sunday's Postcommunion split \latin{redemptiónis ætérnæ \dots\
augméntum} around its own --- the same figure, one Sunday apart, and in both
cases the noun the prayer is reaching for is held back to the end. This is also
\textbf{the formulary's only reach past this life}: \latin{perpétuæ
salvatiónis} is the one phrase in the ten elements that looks beyond the
present.

\textbf{\latin{Semper}, a second time.} The Collect asked \latin{tuis semper
auxíliis}; this prayer opens \latin{Puríficent semper}. Of the fifteen
formularies collated in this repository, three carry \latin{semper} in an
oration and \textbf{this is the only one that carries it twice} --- a statement
about fifteen collated formularies and not about the Missal.

\textbf{Transmission, and it is the sharpest structural point in the oration
dossier.} This prayer does not come from the same place as the other two, and
\textbf{its Gelasian base is materially narrower than theirs}: it is attested
for Rheinau and Gerbert but \textbf{not} for S.~Gallen, Pamelius or Menard,
since S.~Gallen omits the whole appended series. \latin{Purificent semper}
stands in the floating series of unassigned Postcommunions appended after the
Canon, in exactly the form the 1962 book prints, and separately in a
saint-adapted recension in the Vigil Mass of St~Andrew at Book~II
sect.~\textbf{LXVIII}. Which recension is prior is not settled by anything read,
and the pages carrying the floating series were read in text layers only.

\textbf{One measured fact about its vocabulary.} \latin{Salvatio} as a noun
occurs \textbf{once} in the forty-five orations of the fifteen collated
formularies, and it is here. The bound on that count, and what may and may not
be built on it, are stated in the synthesis.

\subsection*{The three orations have no exegetical reception, and the negative is bounded}

\textbf{No patristic or medieval commentary on the Collect \latin{Custodi,
Domine}, the Secret \latin{Concede nobis, Domine}, or the Postcommunion
\latin{Purificent semper et muniant} was located.} The three incipits and their
distinctive phrases were searched as literal strings across every artifact this
research retained --- the Latin files for \work{Super Galatas} and the

\textbf{Why the negative is expected rather than surprising:} these are
anonymous composed Roman orations, and the patristic commentary genres swept ---
psalm expositions, scriptural homilies, epistle commentaries --- do not take
liturgical orations as their object.

\textbf{Bounds, each a live route for a later attempt.} A literal string search
cannot exclude paraphrase, a different incipit, or an oration cited by its
Sunday rather than by its words. \textbf{Specifically not searched:} the ancient
sacramentaries' modern editions as commentary sources; Amalarius, Walafrid
Strabo, Rupert of Deutz, Durandus and the other medieval liturgical expositors;
\textbf{Schuster's \work{Liber Sacramentorum}}, whose third volume is
registered complete, hashed and inspected at p.~123 with registered passages at
the adjacent Sundays --- \textbf{the cheapest single repair available for the
orations' reception, and left to a later attempt}; and the Usuarium Corpus
Orationum concordance, registered with a proven per-entry route and never
consulted. \textbf{The negative survives a corpus 5.5~MB larger than the one it
was first stated over}, including the Mozarabic, Gallican and Bobbio books
named above.

\textbf{And Guéranger is a reception witness only and never a text witness.} His
printed Latin differs from the 1962 typical edition at four points: he prints
\latin{millia} for \latin{mília}; a \textbf{comma} where the typical edition
prints the \textbf{semicolon} this leaf's collation calls load-bearing;
lower-case \latin{angelus}; and \latin{Concede nobis, Domine, quaesumus:} with a
colon and a capitalised \latin{Hostia}, plus the short conclusion \latin{Per
Dominum} at both Secret and Postcommunion where the typical edition prints the
longer form at the Postcommunion. \textbf{The appointed Latin is nowhere quoted
from him in this guide.} The last of the four is the weakest as evidence, since
hand-missal presentations abbreviate conclusions as a matter of course.
