\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

\elementguard
\subsection*{The courts, the Anointed, and the one day \cue{Int.}}
\elementid{Protéctor noster, áspice, Deus}{Ps. 83:10--11a; 2--3a}{1572}

Psalm~83 is a song of the sons of Korah ordered around dwelling and ascent.
The complete psalm moves from longing for the Lord's courts, through the
strength of those whose heart is set on ascent, to prayer for the anointed and
the judgment that one day in God's courts is better than thousands. The
antiphon takes the prayer for the protector and anointed from late in the
psalm, while its verse returns to the opening desire. The chant therefore
places the object of longing and the divine protection of the pilgrim within a
single entrance action.

The immediate psalmic horizon joins temple and kingship. The Latin
\latin{christi tui} names the Lord's anointed in that horizon; its direct
identification with Christ belongs to canonical and ecclesial reception rather
than to the noun alone. Augustine reads the face of Christ as the sign under
which the pilgrim Church is protected, and makes the one day the everlasting
day that neither yesterday yields nor tomorrow presses. Cassiodorus similarly
turns the courts toward the Church and the everlasting homeland, while his
missionary Christological reading develops the psalm's ecclesial reach
(\work{Expositio Psalmorum}, Ps.~LXXXIII, division and vv.~2, 10--12).

Theodoret preserves another accent. His exposition reads the longing through
Israel's displacement and bodily rescue and does not collapse the one day into
Augustine's timeless day (\work{Interpretatio in Psalmos}, Ps.~LXXXIII,
PG~80:1537--1545). Bellarmine later gathers the desire into the pilgrim soul's
longing for the heavenly dwelling (\work{Explanatio in Psalmos}, Ps.~83,
printed pp.~253--255). The voices converge on desire directed beyond present
possession, but their historical and anagogical emphases remain distinct.

The Introit's relation to the rest of the Mass begins here. Galatians will
distinguish objects and fruits of desire; Matthew will name the kingdom as the
first end; the Collect will ask that mortality be directed to saving things.
The entrance chant supplies the positive anthropology: desire is not merely a
danger to be suppressed but a capacity whose beatitude depends on its object.

\subsection*{Guarded mortality and saving direction \cue{Coll.}}
\elementid{Custódi, Dómine, quǽsumus, Ecclésiam tuam}{composed oration}{1573}

The Collect begins with the Church, not the isolated soul. Because human
mortality slips without God, she asks to be kept by perpetual propitiation.
The paired petition sets \latin{auxíliis} with two passive verbs:
\latin{abstrahátur} withdraws the Church from what harms, and
\latin{dirigátur} directs her toward what saves. Protection is not enclosure
or mere preservation. It is a governing mercy that both rescues and orders.

The Old Gelasian tradition places \latin{Custódi} beside the Secret
\latin{Concéde nobis}, though not yet as the complete three-prayer sequence of
this formulary (Wilson, \work{The Gelasian Sacramentary}, pp.~207,
229--230). The Gregorian tradition prints Collect, Secret, and Postcommunion
consecutively at its Sunday XV (Wilson, \work{The Gregorian Sacramentary},
pp.~173--174). Estius later quotes the paired petitions for withdrawal from
harm and direction toward saving things as the Church's prayer for the special
gift of perseverance (\work{Commentarius in Libros Sententiarum} II,
dist.~26, ch.~43). Guéranger and Schuster receive the same guardianship within
the Mass's movement from petition through proclamation and oblation to
Communion and continuing sacramental effect. The Collect's theological force
is explicit in its own grammar. The Epistle's
imperative, \latin{Spíritu ambuláte}, answers a Church that has first confessed
that she cannot stand or advance without God. The Gospel's providence prevents
that confession from becoming fear, while the Postcommunion's purification and
fortification give sacramental form to the Collect's \latin{auxíliis}.
Grace does not merely forgive a past deviation; it guards the
subject who walks and gives that walk its saving direction.

\subsection*{The works of the flesh and the fruit of the Spirit \cue{Ep.}}
\elementid{Fratres: Spíritu ambuláte}{Gal. 5:16--24}{1574}

The pericope stands within Paul's account of freedom. Charity and mutual
service precede it (Gal.~5:13--15), and the summons to live and walk by the
Spirit follows it (5:25). Within that frame, flesh and Spirit desire against
one another, the works of the flesh are manifest, and the Spirit bears a
singular fruit. Those who practise the former do not inherit the kingdom;
those who belong to Christ crucify flesh with its passions and desires.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{Expositio Epistolae ad Galatas} 45--53,
PL~35:2138--2144.} Augustine treats the conflict through humility and
grace-assisted choice. The presence of desire does not itself mean that the
will has completed the work desired; resistance is real, and its efficacy is
received rather than self-generated.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Jerome, \work{Commentaria in Galatas} III, on 5:16--24,
PL~26:500--515.} Jerome insists that flesh does not name bodily nature as evil.
His explanation of passions and desires directs the condemnation toward vice,
and his textual discussion preserves the fluidity of the ancient lists rather
than forcing every witness into the Missal's expanded Latin form.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Chrysostom, Homily~5 on Galatians.} Chrysostom likewise refuses the
identification of flesh with the body. He joins the good fruit to human
diligence and divine kindness and reads the crucifixion of the flesh as the
discipline of a life governed by the Spirit.
\end{witnessnote}

Theodoret describes indwelling grace as cooperating with the soul
(\work{Interpretatio ad Galatas} 5:16--24, PG~82:495--498). Aquinas orders the
fruits in the expanded Latin list according to the person's relation to God,
self, and neighbor (\work{Super Galatas} 5, lect.~6, no.~330, and lect.~7,
no.~331).
Other Latin and Greek lists differ from that twelvefold form, so their number
cannot bear an ecclesiological or spiritual calculation. Anthony of Padua
later brings Galatians~5:22--23 into his Fourteenth-Sunday sermon, but that
sermon's Gospel is Luke~17:11--19 rather than Matthew~6; it witnesses medieval
reuse of the fruits, not the intention of the later target formulary
(\work{Dominica XIV post Pentecosten} 15--18).

The Epistle's kingdom warning meets the Gospel's kingdom command. Paul's
catalogue does not equate mammon with every vice, nor does ``flesh'' make every
embodied need suspect. It discloses the fruit by which allegiance becomes
visible. Matthew will address food, clothing, and work as creaturely goods;
Galatians asks what master governs their use.

\subsection*{Hope in God and the place of creatures \cue{Grad.}}
\elementid{Bonum est confidere in Dómino}{Ps. 117:8--9}{1575}

The Gradual's two comparisons stand inside a great thanksgiving. Fear gives
way to confidence; the gates of justice open; the rejected stone becomes the
cornerstone; the Lord makes the day; the festal procession approaches the
altar. The appointed verses are therefore neither an isolated proverb nor a
condemnation of social dependence. They place the highest human power beneath
the Lord who delivers and opens the gate.

Augustine proceeds from bad men to good men and then to good angels: even
angelic princes cannot become the final object of confidence
(\work{Enarratio in Ps. CXVII} 4). Cassiodorus also includes angelic rulers
in his reading. Theodoret, by contrast, takes the princes as mutable earthly
rulers (\work{Interpretatio in Psalmos}, Ps.~CXVII vv.~8--9,
PG~80:1811--1812). Bellarmine preserves a further distinction: human or
angelic assistance may be received, provided hope rests finally in God
(\work{Explanatio in Psalmos}, Ps.~117:8--9, printed p.~370).

That distinction prepares the Offertory. The Angel of the Lord may encamp and
rescue without becoming the master whom the Gospel forbids or the prince in
whom the Gradual refuses ultimate trust. Created mediation and final allegiance
are not rivals when the creature ministers under God. The Gospel's two masters
make the boundary absolute at the level of the governing end.

Matthew~21:42 places Psalm~117:22 on Christ's lips concerning the rejected
stone; Acts~4:10--12 identifies Jesus Christ as the stone rejected by the
builders and the only name of salvation. These verses belong to the complete
psalm's Christological reception, not to the words appointed in the Gradual;
their canonical context does not turn them into additional chanted verses.

\subsection*{Jubilation and the saving God \cue{All.}}
\elementid{Veníte, exsultémus Dómino}{Ps. 94:1}{1576}

Psalm~94 opens with a communal summons to rejoice before ``God our saviour.''
It proceeds through creation and shepherd imagery to the warning at Meriba:
the people must hear God's voice today and not harden their hearts. Hebrews
3:7--4:11 receives that latter half as a present summons toward God's rest.
The Alleluia carries only the jubilant opening, but the complete psalm gives
the acclamation its obedient urgency.

Augustine and Cassiodorus hear \latin{iubilémus} as joy exceeding articulated
speech (Augustine, \work{Enarratio in Ps. XCIV} 2--3; Cassiodorus,
\work{Expositio Psalmorum}, Ps.~XCIV v.~1). Theodoret hears the victor's cry
and proposes a setting under Josiah; that reconstruction belongs to his
exposition, not to the psalm's stated occasion
(\work{Interpretatio in Psalmos}, PG~80:1639--1642). Bellarmine joins inward
joy to the outward voice that praises the Lord as the hope of salvation
(\work{Explanatio in Psalmos}, Ps.~94:1, printed pp.~298--299).

The Alleluia's \latin{salutári} anticipates the Secret's saving victim and the
Postcommunion's perpetual salvation without making praise, victim, and
sacrament synonyms. The first is acclamation, the second the offering at the
altar, and the third the petition for the sacramental effect. The common root
allows the liturgical movement to be heard across distinct actions.

\subsection*{One master and the Father's providence \cue{Gosp.}}
\elementid{Nemo potest duóbus dóminis servíre}{Mt. 6:24--33}{1577}

Matthew~6:24 closes the teaching on treasure and the sound eye by naming the
impossibility of serving two masters. The repeated ``therefore'' carries that
claim into the teaching on anxiety. Birds neither sow nor reap; lilies neither
labor nor spin; the Father feeds and clothes. The argument neither mocks work
nor treats bodily need as unreal. It denies that anxious acquisition may claim
the service owed to God.

Augustine links the two masters to the single intention of the heart
(\work{De sermone Domini in monte} II.47--58). He reads the kingdom sought
``first'' as first in dignity rather than in temporal sequence. Aquinas names
the masters as rival ultimate ends and identifies the kingdom with beatitude
(\work{Super Matthaeum} 6, lect.~5). Under that judgment, possession itself
is not condemned; service to riches is.

Jerome rejects an angelological reading of the birds because it breaks
Christ's argument from lesser creatures to human beings
(\work{Commentaria in Matthaeum} I, on 6:24--34). Chrysostom likewise retains
the ordinary creaturely demonstration while stressing the Father's care
(Homilies~21--22 on Matthew). The created witnesses establish providence
without condemning work or making material provision the disciple's end.

Benedict gives the full kingdom verse a pastoral reuse when he instructs the
abbot to seek first the kingdom and justice and entrust necessities to the
Father (\work{Rule} 2.35). The reuse preserves governance and provision
together. Luke~12 offers a close parallel, but its placement beside the rich
fool and almsgiving gives it a context different from Matthew's discourse on
treasure, sight, and rival masters.

\subsection*{The encamped Angel and the taste of goodness \cue{Off.}}
\elementid{Immíttet Angelus Dómini}{Ps. 33:8--9a}{1578}

Psalm~33 bears a title that names David before Achimelech, while 1~Kings~21
names Achis at Geth as the king before whom David changed his behavior. The
name-level disagreement remains unresolved. The complete psalm moves from the
poor man's cry and divine deliverance through the fear of the Lord, truthful
speech, peace, affliction, and redemption. Angelic encampment and tasting
therefore belong to a discipline of trust rather than to a promise that the
faithful escape every affliction.

The Angel receives several patristic senses. Augustine identifies him with
Christ and connects the singular Angel to the Lord who rescues
(\work{Enarratio in Ps. XXXIII}, sermo II.10--13). Cassiodorus reads a
ministering angel; Theodoret emphasizes the plurality of angelic ministers
(\work{Interpretatio in Psalmos}, PG~80:1103--1106). Aquinas records created,
Christological, and ecclesial possibilities at Ps.~33:8--9, and Bellarmine
describes the protection as an army-like security under God
(\work{Explanatio in Psalmos}, printed pp.~91--92). The Missal's capital
\latin{Angelus} does not by itself decide among them.

The command to taste receives an explicitly sacramental afterlife. Augustine
and Cassiodorus bring it into the Church's participation in Christ. Cyril of
Jerusalem uses it in mystagogical teaching on Eucharistic reception, though
the attribution of the \work{Mystagogical Catecheses} remains disputed; Ambrose
uses it in his account of the mysteries
(\work{Mystagogical Catechesis} V.20; \work{De mysteriis} IX.58). Theodoret
and Aquinas relate tasting to experiential knowledge, while Bellarmine speaks
of divine sweetness known by experience. These witnesses illuminate the verse;
their readings concern the biblical verse rather than its later placement as
this Sunday's Offertory.

At the presentation of gifts, protection and tasting lead toward the Secret's
\latin{hóstia salutáris}. The movement is sacramental without relocating the
chant to Communion: the words prepare an action whose offering, reception, and
effect remain distinct.

\subsection*{The saving victim \cue{Sec.}}
\elementid{Concéde nobis, Dómine, quǽsumus}{composed oration}{1579}

The Secret asks that the saving victim be at once purification from offenses
and propitiation of divine power. Both nouns are parallel predicates of the
same \latin{hóstia salutáris}. Purification names what is removed; propitiation
names the restored relation to God. The prayer does not reduce the offering to
moral resolve, nor does its compact grammar supply a complete theory of
sacrifice.

Miquel dels Sants Gros i Pujol's \work{El Sacramentari II de Gellona},
item~1122 (SupG~1172), p.~155, and Corpus Orationum CO~675a transmit the
Secret's wording. The Gregorian sacramentary places it between the target
Collect and Postcommunion, while the verified Mame 1922 page images carry the
same complete ten-element placement and order later found in the Vatican 1962
facsimile. Guéranger and Schuster later interpret its saving Host and
propitiation within the movement from oblation toward Communion and continuing
sacramental effect. The prayer itself binds that language to the
Eucharistic action: the Alleluia praises the saving God, the Secret names the
saving victim, and the Postcommunion asks for the effect of perpetual salvation.

The prayer also turns trust into action. The Lord invoked as protector is not
merely contemplated; his altar receives the victim through which offenses are
purified. The Offertory's taste and the Postcommunion's sacraments stand on
either side of this petition without becoming grammatically identical with
the victim named here.

\subsection*{The kingdom command at reception \cue{Comm.}}
\elementid{Primum quǽrite regnum Dei}{Mt. 6:33, adapted}{1580}

The Communion recasts Matthew~6:33. Compared with the Clementine Gospel, it
moves \latin{primum} to the front, omits
\latin{ergo} and \latin{et iustítiam eius}, drops \latin{hæc} before
\latin{ómnia}, and adds \latin{dicit Dóminus}. The Douay--Rheims translates
the complete biblical verse, including its justice clause, and has no English
form corresponding exactly to this adapted antiphon.

Augustine, Jerome, Chrysostom, Aquinas, and Benedict interpret the complete
dominical saying at the named loci, not the antiphon's exact omissions,
rearrangement, and added speech formula. Cantus records 643078 and 668777
attest the contracted chant form in the Bellelay Gradual and Einsiedeln Codex
121; they establish its medieval use without interpreting its contraction.
The exegetes explain why the kingdom is first, but their teaching receives the
Gospel verse rather than this particular recasting.

The Gospel proclaims Christ's command to seek the kingdom and God's justice;
the Communion contracts v.~33 to the kingdom command and divine addition,
omits the justice phrase, and speaks the imperative as \latin{dicit Dóminus}.
The antiphon therefore does not independently carry the Gospel's phrase about
justice. At sacramental reception, its retained kingdom and priority address
desire anew and connect the command with the Collect's direction and the
Postcommunion's leading.

\subsection*{Purified, fortified, and led to salvation \cue{Postcomm.}}
\elementid{Puríficent semper et múniant}{composed oration}{1581}

The Postcommunion prays that the sacraments continually purify and fortify and
lead to the effect of perpetual salvation. The plural \latin{sacraménta}
names what has been received; its three verbs make reception active in the
life of the Church. Purification answers the Secret's purification of offenses,
fortification answers the day's repeated language of protection, and leading
answers the Collect's petition for direction toward saving things.

Miquel dels Sants Gros i Pujol's \work{El Sacramentari II de Gellona},
item~1123 (SupG~1173), p.~155, and Corpus Orationum CO~4791 transmit the
Postcommunion's wording. An antecedent also appears separately in the Old
Gelasian tradition, including a saint-adapted form, while the Gregorian
tradition incorporates the prayer into the three-prayer sequence. Guéranger
and Schuster later interpret it through continuing sacramental purification,
protection, perseverance, and salvation (Schuster, \work{The Sacramentary},
vol.~III, pp.~136--138). The prayer's original composition and its entry into
the three-prayer sequence remain historically undetermined.

The final petition keeps the horizon proportionate. The sacraments purify and
strengthen toward perpetual salvation; the formula does not promise immediate
security from bodily harm. The Gospel's provident Father and the psalms'
protector remain the God who leads through creaturely need, moral struggle,
affliction, offering, and communion toward the kingdom sought first.
