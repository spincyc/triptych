\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

\subsection*{The typical edition against the Benziger printing}

The Vatican \latin{editio typica} prints this formulary at pp.~394--396 under
marginal nos.\ 1572--1581. The Benziger \latin{editio iuxta typicam} of the same
year prints the same ten elements and differs from it in thirteen places --- all
of them accidentals of orthography, accentuation, pointing, abbreviation and
layout, and none of them a different word. The table carries all thirteen, two
of its rows taking a pair at once; the Collect stands there because over it the
two books agree to the point, and the Epistle's vice-list because the Benziger
layer misrecognises its own book. \textbf{Three of the readings in the Benziger
column were read on the Internet Archive item's own single-page JP2 derivative
of printed pp.~389--390, an unregistered derivative of a registered item}: the Introit's \latin{míllia}, the \latin{fi}-ligature
accents and the acute in \latin{adiícere} at Mt.\ 6:27. Every other Benziger
reading in the table stands in that item's uncorrected optical text layer and
not on any page of the book.

Introit close & \latin{℣. Glória Patri.} and stop & \latin{℣. Glória Patri. Protéctor.} --- the antiphon-repetition cue, which the typical edition leaves to the general rubrics\\
Int., Ps. 83:11 & \latin{super mília} & \latin{super míllia}, printed with two \latin{l}s at the head of the right column of p.~389, read on that unregistered JP2 page image --- Benziger keeps the older double \latin{l} the typical edition drops\\

Two of these are worth more than a footnote. The two conclusions run in
opposite directions inside one formulary --- the typical edition abbreviates
the Secret's and prints the Postcommunion's at length, Benziger's text layer
does the reverse --- which is a useful reminder that ``the 1962 Latin'' is not
one artefact. And the typical edition abbreviates \latin{Ant. ad Introitum}
here where on the Thirteenth Sunday it spelled \latin{Antiphona ad Introitum}
out: the book is not uniform with itself two pages apart.

One mark on these pages is damaged, and it is not text. The Gospel's marginal
reference number, printed at the outer edge of the scanned block on p.~395, has
lost its fourth digit to ink loss. It is 1577, because 1576 and 1578 are both
legible and the series 1572--1581 is unbroken. That is resolved by sequence and
not by the second witness, which prints no marginal numbers at all. Nothing in
the appointed text depends on it.

\subsubsection*{Twenty-one places where the appointed text is not the Bible's text}

The Clementine Vulgate is the text that matters for the comparison, being the
Latin behind the Douay--Rheims Challoner English printed with these propers. Twenty-one divergences stand between the two texts,
and each stands at both. Most are orthography or pointing. The ones that
change what is said are the two cuts at the Introit, the cut at the Offertory,
the Alleluia's dropped psalm title, the Epistle's displaced incipit, and ---
the only substantive recasting in the formulary --- the Communion.

Three elements depart from the Clementine in nothing beyond the table, and that
is what throws the Communion into relief. The Gradual is the Clementine's own
wording exactly, both verses, with nothing added, dropped or transposed. The
Epistle after its incipit matches word for word apart from the i/j orthography,
the one capital at Gal.\ 5:18 and the pointing tabled above. The Gospel after
its incipit matches word for word apart from the i/j orthography, the æ for œ at
Mt.\ 6:30, the sentence capital at Mt.\ 6:32 and the pointing tabled above.
Three further differences are the arithmetic of quoting two Clementine verses
continuously rather than departures the missal makes: at Ps.\ 83:10--11 the
antiphon runs \latin{Christi tui: quia mélior} where the Clementine ends a
sentence and capitalises \latin{Quia}; at Ps.\ 83:2--3 it runs
\latin{virtútum! concupíscit} for the Clementine's \latin{virtutum ! Concupiscit};
and at Ps.\ 33:8--9 the Offertory runs \latin{eos: gustáte} for the Clementine's
\latin{eos. Gustate}. \textbf{The only appointed
element of this formulary that departs from its Bible text substantively is the
Communion} --- and, as the Communion's own treatment below shows, even that
departure is inherited rather than made here.

\textbf{No \latin{Psalterium Romanum}, Gallican or Vetus Latina witness stands
behind any chant reading above.} At the Offertory and the Communion an older
Latin form is the likeliest explanation of what the missal prints, and without
such a witness it cannot be told from a scribal or editorial variant.

\subsubsection*{Psalm numbering, and the rules behind the orations and the colour}

\correction{Which psalm numbering the parentheses give}{At two of the four
appointed psalms the Masoretic number is not the number a reader will find in a
modern English Bible. Vulg.\ Ps.\ 83:10--11 is Masoretic 84:10--11 but English
\textbf{84:9--10}; Vulg.\ Ps.\ 33:8--9 is Masoretic 34:8--9 but English
\textbf{34:7--8}. Ps.~117 has no title and Ps.~94 folds its title into v.~1, so
at the Gradual and the Alleluia the three series agree. The superscription is
what divides them: the Vulgate numbering the missal cites and the Douay carries
counts it with the psalm's first verse, the Masoretic numbers it as a verse of
its own, and the English convention of the King James leaves it unnumbered and so runs a verse behind wherever a psalm has one. No
Hebrew psalter stands behind the Masoretic column, which follows those
editions' own declared conventions.}

\correction{Which instrument abolished the seasonal orations, and which rule
prescribes green}{The second and third orations older missals print at this Mass
were abolished not by the 1960 \latin{Rubricae generales} but by
\work{Variationes in Breviario et Missali Romano}, cap.~IV, n.~18,
\latin{Orationes pro diversitate Temporum abolentur}, in \work{Acta Apostolicae
Sedis} 52 (1960), p.~709 --- an instrument the 1962 Missal does not reprint.
Inside the code itself the same result follows for this Sunday from RG 434~b
with RG 435. And a citation hazard runs through it: the 1960 promulgation contains two
different provisions numbered 18, RG~18 on the transfer of impeded Sundays
after the Epiphany and \work{Variationes} cap.~IV n.~18, so a bare ``n.~18''
cited for this Sunday names neither of them unambiguously. Colour is
governed not by a temporal-season rule of part three of the 1960 code but by
\textbf{part one}, at \textbf{RG 127~b}: \latin{Color viridis adhibetur in Officio et Missa de
Tempore: \dots\ b) a feria II post dominicam I post Pentecosten, usque ad
sabbatum ante Adventum}, particularising the general rule at RG~117. \work{AAS} 52 (1960) p.~619 prints
it, and \work{Missale Romanum} 1962, \latin{Rubricae generales} p.~XX, prints
it again without a single verbal difference.}

\correction{Which rubrics printed in this formulary are Tridentine and which
are not}{\textbf{One of the two rubrics printed inside this formulary is four
centuries older than the other.} \textbf{The \latin{Credo} rubric is
Tridentine and unchanged}: the 1570 book prints \latin{Symbolum, dicitur post
Euangelium in omnibus Dominicis per annum}, with \latin{Credo} printed in red in
the formulary itself. \textbf{The Preface rubric is not.} The 1570 book heads
the Trinity preface \latin{Sequens Praefatio cum suo cantu dicitur in festo
Sanctae Trinitatis} and its \latin{Rubricae generales} contain no clause
assigning a preface to the Sundays after Pentecost; the clause exists by 1889
and 1920 --- \latin{In Dominicis per annum \dots\ dicitur Praefatio de Trinitate}
--- and the 1960 code restates it as a preface \latin{de Tempore} at n.~494.
\textbf{Which instrument extended it, no document above names.} \work{Decreta authentica}
vol.~II, covering 1706--1870, carries sixteen decrees mentioning the Trinity and
a hundred and eleven mentioning a preface, and \textbf{none of them pairs a
preface grant with the Sundays after Pentecost.} No primary text quoted above carries the
attribution commonly made to Clement~XIII in 1759, and the grant may
lie in the companion volume or in a commentator. \textbf{So a
Trinitarian reading of the whole Mass cannot be pressed backwards past the
sixteenth century on this book's own evidence.}}

\correction{What could displace this Mass, and when that changed}{\textbf{The
protection this Sunday enjoys is Pius~X's and not John~XXIII's.} Under the
Tridentine rubrics any double of any grade took the Mass. From 1912 only a feast
of the Lord, a double of the first or second class, or a day within an octave of
a feast of the Lord could (\work{Divino afflatu} rubrics, Tit.~III n.~2,
\work{AAS} 3 (1911) p.~642). From 1961 only a first-class day or a first- or
second-class feast of the Lord can (\latin{Rubricae generales} n.~16,
\work{AAS} 52 (1960) p.~598, with n.~14, \latin{Officium et Missa dominicae
impeditae nec anticipantur nec resumuntur}). \textbf{The 1962 heading conceals
the size of what Pius~X did}: the Sunday's rite was still \latin{semiduplex} in
1920, after the change that actually protected it, and the 1955 decree that
renamed the rite \latin{duplex} changed nothing about displacement.
\textbf{Three moments, and they are not one}: a protection without a rank change
in 1911, a rank change without a protection change in 1955, and a further
protection change with a new rank name in 1960. \textbf{And the seasonal second
and third orations were abolished twice, five years apart and in nearly the same
words} --- \latin{Orationes pro diversitate temporum assignatae abolentur} at
\work{Cum nostra hac aetate} Tit.~V a) n.~1, \work{AAS} 47 (1955) p.~224, and
\latin{Orationes pro diversitate Temporum abolentur} at \work{Variationes}
cap.~IV n.~18. \textbf{Both are true of different moments}: the abolition dates
from 1~January 1956, and the law in force from 1~January 1961 is the 1960 text,
which \latin{Rubricarum instructum} n.~2 records as having assumed the 1955
decree's content when that decree ceased to be in force. The Missal's own intervening printings, from
1956 to 1960, stand outside the editions collated above.}

\subsection*{How this Mass came to be this Sunday's}

\subsubsection*{The three orations do not descend as a unit}

A formulary's prayers need not travel together, and these three do not.

The \textbf{Collect and Secret stand together in one Mass} in the Gelasianum
Vetus --- Wilson's 1894 edition, Book~III sect.~X, \latin{Item alia Missa},
printed pp.~229--230 --- and that Mass stands in a series of Sunday Masses which
the base manuscript, Vatican Reginensis 316, numbers \emph{not at all}.

The \textbf{Postcommunion comes from somewhere else}. \latin{Purificent semper}
stands in a floating series of unassigned Postcommunions appended after the
Canon (printed p.~237), in exactly the form the 1962 book prints,
\latin{nos, Deus}; and separately in a saint-adapted recension in the Vigil
Mass of St~Andrew (Book~II sect.~\textbf{LXVIII}, p.~207), reading \latin{nos,
Domine} and adding the apostle's intercession. Which recension is prior is settled by no
witness above.

\correction{Which section the Postcommunion's saint-adapted recension stands
in}{The running head above Wilson's printed p.~207 reads \textbf{LXIX}, and
\textbf{that head names the section the page \emph{reaches}, not the one it
opens in.} On that page sect.~LXIX is \latin{Item in natali eiusdem}, the
Mass of St~Andrew's feast on 30~November, and it begins \emph{below} the
Postcommunion in question, which belongs to the preceding section --- the Vigil
Mass, \textbf{sect.~LXVIII}. The same book sets the same trap at the Secret's
penitential home, where the running head above p.~257 stands over
sect.~\textbf{XLI}, which opens on p.~256.}

\correction{What the Hadrianum's printed section heading does with its
numeral}{The heading in Wilson's 1915 volume reads \latin{cXXXIII.}, where the
\latin{c} is a small raised footnote reference letter standing before the
numeral and no part of it. The section is \textbf{XXXIII}: \textbf{the running
section series XXVIIII--XLI across printed pp.~172--176 leaves no room for a
section CXXXIII at all}, and the extra hundred is that raised \latin{c} taken
for part of the number. A citation to
\textbf{CXXXIII} finds nothing on Wilson's pages.}

The \textbf{Secret has a second, penitential home} in the same book, Book~III
sect.~\textbf{XLI} (printed pp.~256--257), among votive and occasional material
--- a Mass whose collects beg God to turn his anger away from his people.
\textbf{And Wilson's own marginal narrows that claim materially.} The marginal at
sect.~XLI reads \latin{R.\,S.(alibi.)} --- Wilson's notation that R and S have
the prayer \emph{elsewhere}, that is, at the Sunday Mass. So the penitential
Mass \emph{as R and S transmit it does not contain this Secret}; the second home
is attested for the Vatican manuscript and for Menard, while the other two
references point back to the single Sunday occurrence. \textbf{The Secret's
``second, penitential home'' is therefore a feature of the Vatican manuscript's
arrangement and not of the Gelasian tradition at large.}

\textbf{None of the three orations stands in the Veronense.} Feltoe's 1896 \work{Leonian Sacramentary},
a diplomatic edition printing the codex's contractions, carries none of the
three; and \textbf{the
Veronense has no ordered Sunday series at all} --- it runs by month from April
--- so absence there is much weaker evidence about a Sunday formulary than
absence from an ordered sacramentary would be. \textbf{But the Veronense does
contain, twice, the prayer-shape the 1962 Postcommunion uses}
--- \latin{Purificent} with holy things as subject and a second protective verb
yoked to it, the closer reading \latin{tua nos \dots\ sancta purificent, et ab
externis erroribus perpetua virtute defendant} --- which places the prayer's
\emph{idiom}, though not the prayer, in the oldest Roman euchological stratum.
\textbf{The likeness is one of diction and prayer-shape and not of derivation},
and that likeness too stands on what Feltoe's
edition prints. Nor do the three orations stand in Férotin's
\work{Liber mozarabicus sacramentorum}, Bannister's \work{Missale Gothicum} or
Lowe's \work{Bobbio Missal} --- \textbf{they belong to the Roman
euchological stream and not, so far as six editions show, to the non-Roman Latin
rites}.

\subsubsection*{The Sunday number has moved, and it moved by a measured amount}

\textbf{Sixteen consecutive sections carry the correspondence.} Gelasianum Book~III
sects.~I--XI and XII--XVI correspond to the 1962 Fifth through Twentieth
Sundays, and \textbf{the offset survives a documented interpolation and resumes
a new constant rather than becoming noise}: sects.~I--XI stand at $N{+}2$ for
Rheinau, S.~Gallen, Gerbert \emph{and Menard}, and at $N{+}1$ for Pamelius;
sects.~XII--XVI stand at $N{+}4$ for the first four while Pamelius stays at
$N{+}1$ without a break. \textbf{Menard is present at every readable section
and always agrees with R/S/Gerbert, never with Pamelius} --- so the two
Gregorian editions do not agree with each other. \textbf{Wilson's per-section wording
varies in scope} --- sect.~IX excepts the Secret, sect.~V
the Postcommunion, sect.~VI covers ``the two Collects and the Secret,'' sect.~XII
``the first Collect, Secret, and Postcommunion'' --- \textbf{so the offset is
uniform while the set of prayers it covers is not.} Each section's 1962 identity is that of its own printed collect or
Secret and never of its position, and the mapping agrees with the
calendar: sect.~XI prints the 1962 Fifteenth's Collect and sect.~IX the
Thirteenth's, with sect.~X printing this Mass's.

\begin{pairtable}{Number}{Books that give it}
\textbf{Fourteenth} & the 1962 Missal\\
\textbf{Fifteenth} & Pamelius; and, independently, the Gregorian Hadrianum supplement\\
\textbf{Sixteenth} & Rheinau, S.~Gallen, Gerbert, Menard\\
\end{pairtable}

\textbf{Two independent lines reach the same magnitude.} The supplement to the
Gregorian Hadrianum numbers this Mass \latin{DOMINICA .XV. POST PENTECOSTEN},
one Sunday later than 1962, and the offset holds across \textbf{eleven}
consecutive Masses, \latin{Dominica XI} through
\latin{Dominica XXI post Pentecosten}, each identified from its own printed
collect rather than extrapolated. \textbf{Where it breaks:} at
\latin{Dominica XXII} the cues are the 1962 Seventh Sunday's set and at
\latin{Dominica XXIII} the received Twenty-third's, an offset of one, and
\textbf{nobody has established why.} In Cod.\ Ottobonianus~313 the marginal
chant cues stand two Sundays later than the orations beside them, and that
offset too holds across the same run. \textbf{A single
misreading by one editor could not produce that.} \textbf{The chant side is
uneven in two systematic ways:} the match is on Introit, Gradual,
Offertory and Communion, the bracketed Alleluia cues matching the 1962
assignment at none of the eleven; and where the count is three or two of four,
the odd element is always a Communion or Gradual displaced by one Sunday inside
the same stretch.

\textbf{Where the offset comes from, and Wilson states it himself.} His
Introduction (printed pp.~lxxi--lxxiii) places the start of Book~III's series
against the first part of the Gregorian's third book, which makes the offset
\textbf{a difference in where each tradition begins counting --- a numeration
base --- and not an interpolation}: he names the mechanism in a footnote, the
\latin{post Pentecosten} / \latin{post Octavas Pentecostes} equivalence
arising from a discrepancy between the first and supplementary portions of
Muratori's text. \textbf{Which single Sunday each tradition counts as its first
is not established}, so the decomposition of the two units of offset is not
settled here. The break at sect.~XII is doubly sourced ---
Wilson's Introduction and his footnotes agree that R, S and Gerbert interpolate
the two September Ember-week Sundays and rejoin at their twentieth --- which is
exactly the arithmetic that moves the offset from two to four while Pamelius
stays at one.

\textbf{One of the five books prints the Mass itself at the locus.}
Gerbert's printed p.~173, under
the heading \latin{Dominica XVI. post Pentecosten}, gives a five-item Mass whose
Collect, Secret \emph{and} Postcommunion are the three composed orations of
this formulary, plus a second Collect and a proper Preface; the printed page
number matches Wilson's independent marginal citation ``Gerb.~173'', and the
five incipits match his Appendix tabulation item for item. \textbf{His is an
eighteenth-century printed text and not a manuscript}, set in long-s type.
\textbf{And the
Gregorian Hadrianum carries all three orations as one Mass}: Wilson's 1915
section XXXIII, \latin{DOMINICA .XV. POST PENTECOSTEN}, printed
pp.~173--174, gives the Collect \latin{Custodi domine quaesumus}, the Super
oblata \latin{Concede nobis domine quaesumus}, and the Ad complendum
\latin{Purificent semper et muniant tua sacramenta nos deus, et ad perpetuae
ducant salvationis effectum} --- \textbf{the 1962 Postcommunion word for word},
and the next section opens with the 1962 Fifteenth's Collect. \textbf{So the
whole 1962 oration set travels as a unit in Wilson's Hadrianum at its
\latin{Dominica XV} and in Gerbert at his \latin{Dominica XVI} --- $N{+}1$ in the
Gregorian and $N{+}2$ in the Frankish Gelasian --- and it does not travel as a
unit in Menard}, whose \latin{Hebdomada XVI} carries this Collect with a
different Super oblata, \latin{Hostiam sacrificii singularis}, and a different
Ad complendum, \latin{Praesta nobis, misericors Deus}. \textbf{And the
Postcommunion's attestation is the narrowest of the three}: how the Vatican
manuscript arranges its unassigned Sunday Masses bears on it, and so does
Menard's volume, which prints no occurrence of the prayer at all. \textbf{The unit that moved in
the older books is a Mass-set}, and all three of its orations are elements of
the 1962 formulary: what travels in those books is a whole Mass --- with, in
Gerbert, a second Collect and a proper Preface beside the three --- and what it
amounts to in this one is three elements of ten, the second Collect and the
Preface being no part of the 1962 formulary at all. \textbf{Wilson's own
footnote at Gelasianum sect.~X covers the Collects and the Secret only}, so on
the Gelasian side alone the assignment reaches two of the ten and not three.

\textbf{A near-miss at sect.~X.} The bracketed
Postcommunion there reads \latin{Purificent nos, Domine, sacramenta quae
sumpsimus, et a cunctis efficiant vitiis absolutos} --- \textbf{a different
prayer of the same incipit family}, which Wilson's own index of incipits lists
separately from this one, three printed occurrences each. \textbf{The two part
company only past the incipit}, and the Vatican manuscript wrongly
titles that prayer \latin{Secreta}, an error of the scribe by Wilson's note.

\textbf{``Four against one'' is not four independent witnesses.} R and S are
two Frankish ``Gelasian'' manuscripts Wilson collated himself, and they carry
the sixteenth-Sunday heading directly. Gerbert's principal manuscript
``cannot now be traced,'' and Wilson holds that the ``Gelasian'' portions of it,
and so of Gerbert's printed text, ``do not represent the original text of the
`Gelasian' books, but a revision of that text, apparently based upon the text of
the Sacramentaries of the `Gregorian' type.'' \textbf{So Gerbert does not merely
fail to be a third Gelasian voice: it leans toward the very Gregorian editions
supposed to corroborate it.} And Pamelius is itself an edition of the
\emph{Gregorian} book, so \textbf{Pamelius's ``fifteenth'' and the Gregorian
supplement's ``fifteenth'' are not two independent witnesses to one Sunday}.
The count is two collated Gelasian manuscripts, plus a printed edition
largely dependent on a lost manuscript copied from one of them and revised
toward the Gregorian type, plus one Gregorian edition --- with the other
Gregorian edition one Sunday earlier. \textbf{Gerbert's own printed section heading agrees with what Wilson cites
from him, which excludes a mis-citation without adding a fourth independent
voice: he is the least independent of the five.} R's and S's own Sunday headings are Wilson's own
collation and do not depend on Gerbert at all.

\textbf{And Gerbert's unreliability does not weaken R and S}, whose Sunday
headings Wilson collated himself. His ``exceedingly confused and misleading'' is
hedged with ``at times,'' applies to Gerbert's
\emph{handling and explanation of his materials} rather than to his text as
such, and stands immediately after his warm tribute --- ``students of
Liturgy owe so much as we owe to Gerbert.'' Wilson also excepts ``the latter
part'' of R from his criticism, and whether these Sunday Masses fall inside that
part is not established, \textbf{so the unreliability cannot simply be assumed
to reach them.}

\textbf{Wilson does not say the same thing about all five books.} Of R, S and
Gerbert he writes that the prayers ``\textbf{are assigned by}'' them to the
sixteenth --- a
statement about those books' own Sunday headings. Of Pamelius and Menard he
writes only ``\textbf{see the \latin{Missa} for} the \dots\ Sunday in,'' which is
a cross-reference to a \emph{parallel} Mass and need not mean an identical
composition. \textbf{The two formulas are two different strengths of claim.}

\textbf{And his apparatus is not a rumour}: it is a published critical
collation, and for R and S specifically it is his own reading of manuscripts
physically in front of him.

\subsubsection*{Honorius's Fourteenth Sunday is not the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday}

By the early twelfth century the 1962 Sunday number and this Introit are
already paired. \work{Gemma animae} IV.72 is headed \latin{Dominica decima
quarta post Pentecosten « Protector noster, » sub lege}, and the quotation is
exact: the neighbouring
chapter headings in the same column print no \latin{post Pentecosten} and the
following one does.

\textbf{But the rest of the chapter shows the opposite half of the picture.} Within one
and the same chapter Honorius's \textbf{Introit, Collect, Epistle and
Offertory are the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday's}, while his \textbf{Gospel is the
1962 Thirteenth Sunday's} --- Luke 17:11--19, the ten lepers --- and that Gospel
offset is not a slip, since it holds across three consecutive Sundays. His
\textbf{Gradual} (\latin{Bonum est confiteri Domino}, Ps.~91) is not the 1962
Fourteenth's (\latin{Bonum est confidere in Domino}, Ps.~117:8--9); his
\textbf{Alleluia} (\latin{Quoniam Deus magnus}, Ps.~94:3) is a different verse
of the same psalm the 1962 Alleluia uses; and his \textbf{Communion} is
\latin{Panis, quem ego dabo}.

\textbf{And his three differing chants are not three separate facts.} Set
against the 1962 chant sets as a modern registry of them gives them, and not as
pp.~396--398 print them, his Gradual,
Alleluia and Communion are \textbf{precisely the 1962 Fifteenth Sunday's chant
set}: within one chapter his chant strand stands one Sunday \emph{later} than
his oration strand while his Gospel stands one Sunday \emph{earlier}, a spread
of exactly two Sundays. \textbf{That converges in direction, though not in
magnitude, with the Ottobonianus cues below}, where the chant set beside
\latin{Custodi, Domine} also runs later than the orations: \textbf{two
independent witnesses in which the chants of this Mass run later than its
orations, at $+1$ and $+2$ --- they do not corroborate a single number.}

\textbf{And he reads this formulary three times over, not once.} \work{Gemma
animae} IV.72 reads it \latin{sub lege}, under the type of Ezechias sick to
death; IV.73 reads it a second time \latin{sub gratia}, under the type of
Theodosius the Great, the council of his reign standing in the Epistle's
catalogue of vices and virtues and Ambrose's penance behind the Communion; and
IV.74 is not a typology at all but \textbf{a liturgical-calendar explanation},
tying the Offertory to the September night office's \emph{historia} of Tobias
and Raphael and to Michaelmas, and then explaining the autumn Ember
ordinations. \textbf{The paired \latin{sub lege} / \latin{sub gratia} structure
is the book's method and not a one-off}, chapters LXX and LXXI doing the same at
the Thirteenth Sunday.

\textbf{His September reading is one of five, and no two of them agree.}
\textbf{Sicard of Cremona} ties the Offertory to Tobias and Raphael
independently of him and on his own September calendar --- \latin{quae quidem
offerenda, bene simul cum Tobia cantatur, cum et ejus filius per angeli
obsequium liberetur} --- putting Tobias in the second week of September;
\textbf{Durandus} reads the Sunday under Judith instead and assigns Tobias to
his Thirteenth; \textbf{Rupert} has a scheme of his own; and
\textbf{Amalarius}, who is the authority usually reached for here, gives Job
weeks one to three and Tobias week four, for a medicinal reason. \textbf{John
Beleth} is named beside them as a sixth, and no September locus of his
\work{Rationale divinorum officiorum} stands behind that name. \textbf{The
reading is not Amalarius's}: \textbf{his \work{Patrologia Latina} volume nowhere
connects this Offertory to Tobias.} \textbf{So the September \emph{historia} is not one thing:
Tobias names one scheme of the five and not the tradition's.}

\textbf{And the September Ember days do not reliably follow this Sunday.} The
rule changed between the 1920 and the 1962 typical editions, from \latin{post
Festum Exaltationis sanctae Crucis} to \latin{post dominicam tertiam
septembris}. \textbf{Over a thousand years the September Ember days fall in the
week following this Sunday in ninety-three years under the 1962
rule and in a hundred and seventy-seven under the older one} --- fewer than one
year in ten, and fewer than one in five. The pre-1962 arithmetic assumes that a 14~September
falling on a Wednesday sends the Ember days to the following week, a reading
that is usual and that no rubric of either book states, and about one year in
seven turns on it.
\textbf{No instrument prescribing the change of formula stands in \work{Acta
Apostolicae Sedis} 52 (1960)}: \latin{Exaltationis} occurs there twice, both
times as a calendar entry for the feast, and no \latin{Quatuor Tempora} formula
stands in the volume at all.

\textbf{What survives of the Ember connection is about the psalm text and the
Gradual and not about the Introit chant, which the Ember days do not have.} \latin{Protector noster, aspice, Deus}
is the Gradual at the Monday of the first week of Lent, the second Gradual at
the Lenten and September Ember Saturdays, the Gradual at the Fifth Sunday after
Pentecost, and the Introit here; and this Sunday's Gradual \latin{Bonum est
confidere} stands among the September Ember Saturday responsories. \textbf{Two
of those three attestations are qualified}: the September Ember \latin{Bonum est
confidere} stands inside the editor's brackets, which he explains as marking a
codex's additions judged less authentic, while a footnote concedes that
Compiègne exhibits them; and the March Ember \latin{Protector} reaches the page
only through a footnote reporting what Mont-Blandin implies and what the
\work{Micrologus} recommends. \textbf{Honorius explains this formulary by the
September night office and the autumn Ember ordinations, and the book's own
arrangement puts the September Ember days between \latin{Hebdomada} XVII and
XVIII while two of this Mass's chant texts stand in the Ember repertory.} \textbf{His \latin{sub lege} reading is coherent only
with the Gospel he has}, the grateful leper, so it does not transfer to the 1962
formulary. And Migne prints
\latin{plusquam octoginta millia} for the number the angel slew where 4~Kings
19:35 has a hundred and eighty-five thousand --- his printed column carries
that figure, so it is the edition's and not a recognition error, and no source
above explains it.

\textbf{And the printing misplaces three references, not two.} In Migne's text
\latin{(Luc. XVII)} stands against the Gradual and
\latin{(Psal. XCI, 2)} against the Gospel --- each is the other's, since
\latin{Bonum est confiteri Domino} is Ps.~91:2 and the ten lepers is Luke~17 ---
and \textbf{a third reference, \latin{(Psal. LXXXIII, 10)}, stands against the
Collect sentence where it belongs to the Introit words quoted just before it.}
\textbf{A reader taking any of the three at face value from this column will be
misled.} \textbf{Migne's chapter is a rights-restricted facsimile}, and its heading, its
figure for the slain and its three misplaced references are short strings from
it and not its exposition.

Honorius's Ember week and the Masses Wilson reports as interpolated may or may
not be the same disturbance; \textbf{the two witnesses number their Sundays
differently}, and neither settles it.

\textbf{The compiler of the Gregorian supplement stays unnamed.} Wilson
attributes it to Alcuin.

The Ottobonianus cue \latin{b} reads \latin{Bonum est confidere} where 1962
gives the Fifteenth Sunday \latin{Bonum est confiteri Domino}. Manuscript
reading, shared respond, or error: no witness above settles which.

\textbf{The Ottobonianus cue attachment stands at an offset of two.} The cue
block whose Introit is \latin{Protector noster} attaches to
Wilson's sect.~\textbf{XXXI}, \latin{DOMINICA .XIII. POST PENTECOSTEN}, whose
Collect is the 1962 \emph{Twelfth} Sunday's --- an offset of $+2$ and not zero.
On Wilson's printed page \textbf{the superscript reference
letters \latin{a}, \latin{b}, \latin{c} stand immediately before the section
numbers and the three lettered footnotes stand at the foot of the same page},
so a text that fuses the letter with the numeral attaches the block to
the wrong section and yields a zero offset. At either attachment,
the cue set whose Introit is \latin{Protector noster} --- this
Mass's own --- has Communion \latin{Panis quem ego} and not \latin{Primum
quaerite}.

\subsubsection*{Schuster's three pages: an ancient title, a mechanism, and four absences}

Schuster's \work{The Sacramentary} vol.~III gives this formulary three pages,
pp.~136--138.
\textbf{He gives the formulary an ancient title:}
\latin{Quarta post Sancti Laurentii}, the fourth Sunday after St~Lawrence.
\textbf{He gives the unstable numbering a mechanism that is not Wilson's:} the
post-Pentecost series was interrupted at Rome by a cycle counted from
St~Lawrence, and ``the sequence of these Sundays after St~Lawrence was not
everywhere the same, for, whilst the Roman documents of the Frankish period
usually reckon five Sundays after the natalis of the Staurophoros, the Calendar
of Fronteau has only four''; the Lawrence cycle was succeeded at Rome by cycles
around St~Cyprian and then St~Michael, these feasts serving ``as so many
milestones \dots\ and had no special connection with the saint whose name they
bore.'' \textbf{The two mechanisms stand side by side}: neither has been shown to
produce the offsets Wilson collates, and the arithmetic joining them has not
been done.
\textbf{And his own title is a fourth reckoning}: the \work{Liber Comitis}
calls this Sunday the \emph{fifth} after St~Lawrence where Schuster calls it the
fourth --- \textbf{an instance of the variation he himself describes, and not an
error in either.}

He notes a progressive Introit-psalm series across these Sundays, two Lenten
cross-references for the chants, and comments continuously on all three
orations, at more length than Guéranger. \textbf{He reads the Secret's
\latin{tuæ propitiátio potestátis} as the appeasing of the divine
omnipotence}, where Guéranger arrives independently at the same construction;
and \textbf{his Communion paragraph supplies the \latin{et iustítiam eius} the
antiphon drops}, glossing the justice from the Gospel verse rather than from the
antiphon.

\textbf{Four absences on those same pages, and they bear on whether he
corroborates the displacement independently.} \textbf{He names no
station church for this Sunday} --- where his next page does name one for the
Fifteenth --- \textbf{no sacramentary siglum, no manuscript, and no note that
the Communion antiphon is secondary.} \textbf{On these pages he does not
corroborate the Gelasian and Gregorian displacement}, which therefore still
rests on Wilson's apparatus and Gerbert. Those are absences from his
pp.~136--138 and not from the whole volume, whose introduction and whose index of
sacramentary references lie elsewhere. \textbf{His paraphrases are English and
are not text witnesses.}

\subsubsection*{The chants and the readings: the two strands that did not move with the orations}

\textbf{The chant books number this formulary as 1962 numbers it.} The
ninth-century antiphonary formulary carrying this Introit, Gradual and Offertory
stands as \emph{Dominica XIV}, and the Ottobonianus margin reproduces those
formularies \textbf{one number above} the sacramentary headings they stand
against --- twenty-five of twenty-five slots across five consecutive Masses,
\textbf{the bracketed Alleluia cues matching nothing in 1962.} \textbf{And the
Communion of that formulary is not this one:} \latin{Primum quaerite} stands at
the \emph{Ninth} Sunday in four of those books, while the Fourteenth Sunday's
Communion is \latin{Panis quem ego dedero} --- which \textbf{travels with the
Offertory \latin{Immittet Angelus} at both its occurrences}, the Lenten
Thursday and this Sunday. \textbf{So the exchange at the Communion
separated two chants the older books kept together}, and the antiphon it
brought in is an ancient Roman antiphon of another slot rather than a late
composition. \textbf{Those four antiphonaries' readings are a modern database's record of
Hesbert's \work{Antiphonale Missarum Sextuplex} and not the \work{Sextuplex}'s
own printed pages.}

\textbf{The readings, alone of the three strands, never moved.} The \work{Liber
Comitis} as Migne prints it pairs \latin{Fratres, spiritu ambulate, usque
concupiscentiis} with \latin{Nemo potest duobus dominis servire, usque
adiicientur vobis} under \latin{Dominica v post sancti Laurentii} ---
\textbf{the same two incipits and the same two termini the 1962 book prints}, so
the Epistle--Gospel pair is not among the things that moved, and the pericopes
were already cut where 1962 cuts them. \textbf{The Comes's own date and
provenance are not established.}

\subsubsection*{The strands come apart inside one printed book}

\textbf{The Roman Mass antiphonary printed in \work{Patrologia Latina}~78 ---
Pamelius's edition collated against the Compiègne antiphoner and with variants
from Mont-Blandin --- lets the three strands be watched week by week in one
book.} \textbf{Its Introit and Offertory series agree with 1962 at every week
from the seventh to the eighteenth}, this Sunday's Introit standing at
\latin{Hebdomada decima quarta} with its psalm verse word for word as 1962
prints them. \textbf{Its Gradual series runs one week behind 1962 across a long
run and breaks at exactly the two weeks this Sunday and its neighbour occupy.}
\textbf{And its Alleluia agrees with 1962 at only two weeks of twelve --- at both
of which the collated manuscripts disagree with the printed text}, so that
agreement is evidence about the editor's exemplar rather than about the
ninth-century state. At this Sunday the witnesses part in opposite directions:
Compiègne, the oldest and best of them, agrees with 1962 at the Gradual and
disagrees at the Alleluia, and the printed text does the reverse.
\textbf{No witness here has 1962's chant set entire.}

\textbf{But the book disagrees with itself about which week is which, and it
disagrees at the week that series opens on.} At col.~717 its body prints, as a
rubric of its own, \latin{Hebdomada septima. Ista hebdomada non est in
Antiphonario Romano} --- and \latin{Antiphonarium Romanum} is nowhere defined on
that page. Footnote a there records that in Theodericus the office of that
Sunday stands at the eighth and the eighth's at the ninth, \latin{sicque
consequenter}: a displacement of one running forward through exactly the run of
weeks tabulated above, and beginning at the week that run opens on. Footnotes
d, f and g on the same leaf run the other way, reporting Theodericus's Alleluia
at week~$N$ as the printed book's at $N{+}1$. \textbf{The two statements cannot
both be read as they stand}, and nothing in the book resolves them, \textbf{so a
Theodericensian reading printed at a given week is not established as
Theodericus's own week.} On that bound the Theodericensian codices agree with
1962 at neither the Gradual nor the Alleluia \emph{where this book prints
them}, and what they carried at their own fourteenth is open.
\textbf{This edition is a route to the Compiègne and
Mont-Blandin readings and not a substitute for a critical apparatus}: Pamelius
prints an edited text with variants in footnotes where a critical edition would
print the manuscripts in parallel, and a footnote reporting a reading is not a
transcription.

\textbf{And the two Gregorian editions that disagree about this Sunday's number
are editions of two different manuscripts.} Pamelius first published the
Grimoldus codex; Menard's base is the Rodradus codex; and \textbf{Menard argues
at length in his preface that the two are separate compilations}, so their
disagreement is a disagreement between two books rather than between two
editorial habits. He is an interested party arguing for his own manuscript's
Gregorian authenticity, and whether Wilson's siglum \latin{Pam.} cites
Pamelius's Grimoldus text is not established.

\textbf{Menard's book also breaks the unit.} His \latin{Hebdomada XVI post
Pentecosten} carries this formulary's Collect word for word --- and with it a
\emph{different} Super oblata, \latin{Hostiam sacrificii singularis}, and a
\emph{different} Ad complendum, \latin{Praesta nobis, misericors Deus} --- while
his very next section carries all three of the 1962 Fifteenth Sunday's orations
together. \textbf{So the three orations do not travel as a unit everywhere},
and his sections carry proper Prefaces, episcopal blessings and Super populum
prayers throughout, which are Supplement and Frankish features. \textbf{The
Secret has no Sunday home in that book at all}: it stands there twice, as the
Super oblata of the Lenten station at St~Chrysogonus and in a penitential votive
series, \textbf{and the 1962 Passion Monday Secret is the first of those
verbatim while this Sunday's is the second with one word changed} ---
\latin{delictórum} for the votive series' \latin{peccatorum} --- so the
doublet the 1962 Missal prints is inherited and not a modern accident, and this
Sunday's form is the votive one. No stemma is established.
\textbf{And the 1962 Postcommunion does not occur anywhere in that
volume}, neither in the sacramentary nor in its appendix, notes or antiphonary;
that silence is the printed book's and does not reach
his manuscripts; what stands is a real divergence between two
Gregorian witnesses, Wilson's Hadrianum having this Postcommunion at its
\latin{Dominica XV} and Menard's book appearing not to have it at all.

\subsubsection*{Two more mechanisms for the numbering, and neither is the other two}

\textbf{Berno of Reichenau explains the instability by a difference in the
number of Masses, not in where the counting starts.} In his \work{Libellus} he
reports that the compiler of the chant-offices provided twenty-three Sundays
between the octave of Pentecost and Advent while the compiler of the Lectionary
provided twenty-five, \textbf{because one computed on the embolismic years of
the nineteen-year cycle and the other on the common years.} \textbf{That is the
shape of a book whose strands run on different schedules}, which is what the
evidence above shows. \textbf{He writes four centuries after the
arrangements he explains and offers a rationale rather than a record}; his
twenty-three is not the 1962 count; and he never reaches this Sunday.

\textbf{The \work{Micrologus} names a fourth mechanism}, and it is the only one
of the four that names an instrument still visible in the 1962 book: the vacant
Sundays after the Ember fasts, whose orations, mishandled, disturb the order of
the offices as far as Advent, the Romans making up the shortfall by repeating a
Sunday's office rather than deferring it.
\textbf{It speaks of the orations and cannot be transferred to the chant or
reading strands}, and it is a late eleventh-century book prescribing against a
practice it disapproves.

\subsubsection*{The pairing 1962 does not have is a lectionary tradition}

\textbf{The arrangement in which this Sunday's Epistle stands beside the ten
lepers is held by seven witnesses across four centuries and three kinds of
book}, so Honorius is no outlier and the Roman pairing is not simply the ancient
one. The eighth-century \textbf{Comes of Murbach} has it, at items
CXXVIII--CXXVIIII in Wilmart's edition --- \textbf{and there it is one instance
of a whole systematic pattern of epistle~$N$ with gospel~$N{-}1$, not a
peculiarity at this Sunday.} \textbf{Rupert of Deutz}, \textbf{Sicard of
Cremona} and \textbf{Durandus} all set this Sunday's Epistle beside the ten
lepers and put Matthew~6 on the Fifteenth; \textbf{the Introit, Gradual and
Offertory of this Sunday are expounded by Rupert and Sicard, and Durandus's
chant set is not the Roman one but the one Sarum has and the one Compiègne
has} --- Gradual \latin{Bonum est confiteri Domino}, Alleluia
\latin{Confitemini Domino et invocate nomen eius}, Communion \latin{Panis quem
ego dedero} --- against Rupert and Sicard, who have the Roman Gradual. The two
early printings of the \work{Rationale} agree on it, so the narrow difference
between his \latin{Bonum est confiteri} and the Roman \latin{Bonum est
confidere} is not a printer's slip. \textbf{No
filiation between Durandus, Sarum and Compiègne is established}, and Compiègne
stands with Rome at the Gradual and against it at the Alleluia, so none of the
three is simply the older reading. \textbf{Durandus attaches \latin{in
quibusdam ecclesiis} to the Epistle, the Gospel, the Alleluia and the Communion
of this Sunday but not to its Introit or Offertory}, which is the strongest
single evidence that the pairing was contested in his own day --- and the
Alleluia and the Communion he so marks are his own chants and not the 1962
texts. \textbf{The Sarum Missal} has it at
\latin{Dominica XIV post Trinitatem}, and \textbf{the Book of Common Prayer of
1662} still prints the marginals \latin{Gal.\ 5.\ 16.} and \latin{S: Luke.\ 17.\
11.} \textbf{The seventh witness carries it at one element only:} \work{Patrologia
Latina}~78's Roman Mass antiphonary gives this Sunday the Communion \latin{Panis
quem ego dedero}, and says nothing about its Epistle or its Gospel.
\textbf{When or why the Roman books moved to the pairing 1962 prints, none
of them says}, and whether the English books inherit from the Frankish comes,
whether both descend from a common Roman state, or whether Rome's own pairing is
the innovation is not established.

\textbf{And the two reading strands were not numbered alike even in the
seventh-century Roman books.} The Würzburg gospel capitulary numbers this Gospel
\latin{ebd.\ IIII post sci.\ Laur.}, the fourth after St~Lawrence; \textbf{the
Würzburg epistle list numbers Gal.\ 5:16--24 not at all}, laying its forty
Pauline pericopes out in canonical order without titles; and Wilmart's
reconstruction of the Murbach series makes this Epistle the fifth of the five
post-Laurentian ones. \textbf{So the \work{Liber Comitis}'s ``fifth'' and
Schuster's ``fourth'' are two books counting differently, and neither is wrong.}
Alcuin's fifth is a reconstruction keyed to Würzburg item numbers and not a
heading read in a manuscript, and the sanctoral adjacency at 11 and 14 September
places this Mass in the book's sequence without stating a date it fell on.

\textbf{The Sarum Missal splits this formulary across two consecutive Masses,
along exactly the line the strands divide on.} The whole 1962 oration set and
the 1962 Gospel stand at \latin{Dominica XV post Trinitatem}; the 1962 Introit,
Epistle and Offertory at \latin{Dominica XIV}. \textbf{Two offsets must be kept
apart here}: calendrically Trinity~$N$ is Rome's Sunday~$N{+}1$, while internally
Sarum's Collect, Gospel, Secret and Postcommunion at Trinity~$N$ are Rome's at
Sunday~$N{-}1$. \textbf{And this formulary's Collect survives in English as the
Book of Common Prayer's collect for the Fifteenth Sunday after Trinity} ---
``Keep we beseech thee, O Lord, thy Church with thy perpetual mercy.''
\textbf{No edition
consulted prints a source apparatus for the collects, and none says the collect
is Cranmer's rendering of the Sarum Latin.}

\elementguard
\subsection*{Introit \cue{Int.}}

\elementid{Protéctor noster, áspice, Deus}{\latin{Ant. ad Introitum} --- Ps. 83, 10-11, with \latin{Ps. ibid.,} 2-3}{1572}

\textbf{Psalm 83 is a psalm of the sons of Core about desire for the
sanctuary}: the sparrow and the turtle-dove finding a house at
the altars (v.~4), those who dwell in the house (v.~5), the ascents in the
heart through the vale of tears (vv.~6--7), \latin{ibunt de virtute in
virtutem} (v.~8). \textbf{What those verses support is movement toward the
temple.} \textbf{The text does not state that the psalm is
\emph{for} pilgrims or that it voices their sentiments.}

\textbf{What the chant does to it, and the two cuts have opposite effects.} It
sings the psalm backwards: the antiphon from the end, vv.~10--11a, the psalm
verse from the beginning, vv.~2--3a. Cutting v.~11 removes the psalm's explicit
two-way choice between the house of God and the tents of sinners. Cutting v.~3
removes the rejoicing of heart and flesh, \latin{cor meum et caro mea
exsultaverunt in Deum vivum} --- \textbf{the only place in the formulary's psalm
sources where \latin{caro} carries a positive sense}, which bears directly on
the Epistle's use of the word. \textbf{Both dropped clauses are the psalm's,
not the antiphon's, and the antiphon asserts neither.}

\textbf{The capital is the printer's.} The missal
prints \latin{fáciem Christi tui} where the Clementine prints lower-case
\latin{christi}. \textbf{Capitalisation in a modern electronic Clementine is
that e-text's editorial convention and capitalisation in the 1962 missal is that
book's; neither is evidence of what the Latin intends}, and in the psalm's own
sense \latin{christus} is the anointed king. The christological reading rests on
the Fathers, who do argue it, and never on the printer.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{Enarratio in Ps. LXXXIII} §6 and §14.} \textbf{He reads the two
halves as one movement of desire.} On the psalm verse, in the \work{Nicene and
Post-Nicene Fathers} English: ``My soul longs and fails
for the courts of the Lord. It is not enough that it longs and fails: for what
does it fail? For the courts of the Lord.'' On the antiphon: ``one day there is
better than a thousand days. Men long for thousands of days \dots\ let them long
for one day, which has neither rising nor setting.'' His Latin runs:
\latin{contemnant millia dierum, desiderent unum diem, qui non habet ortum et
occasum; unum diem, diem sempiternum, cui non cedit hesternus, quem non urget
crastinus.} \textbf{So on his reading the antiphon is the answer to its own
verse.}
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{The Father's unit and the liturgy's unit do not coincide.} Augustine's
§15 expounds the \emph{rest} of Ps.~83:11 --- in the English of the
\work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, first series, ``I have chosen to
be cast away in the house of the Lord'' --- which is exactly the clause the
Introit cuts off after
\latin{super mília}: he comments on the psalm, not on the antiphon as the missal
bounds it. And his
exposition speaks of \latin{millia dierum}, thousands of \emph{days}, which
invites the conclusion that his psalter read a fuller text. \textbf{It did not} ---
his printed lemma is the missal's Introit verbatim, and \latin{millia dierum} is
his \emph{gloss}, supplying the noun the verse elides.

\textbf{Eight readings stand on \latin{réspice in fáciem Christi tui}, and they
do not stack:} Augustine's, Guéranger's, Arnobius's, Godfrey's, Theodoret's and
Cassiodorus's, and beside those six the \work{Selecta in Psalmos}' and the
\work{Expositiones in Psalmos}', two Greek readings of contested attribution
that run directly against each other.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, on \latin{réspice in fáciem Christi tui}.} \textbf{He refuses the obvious reading}, in the English of the
\work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, first series: ``For when does God not look
upon the face of His Christ? \dots\ Cause Your Christ to become known to all.''
His Latin runs: \latin{Fac innotescere omnibus Christum tuum \dots\ notus sit
omnibus Christus tuus, ut possimus ire a virtutibus in virtutem, ut possit
superabundare gratia, quoniam abundavit peccatum.} \textbf{The argument is a
grammatical one about \latin{facies}, and it survives translation.} The reasoning
is driven by Rom.\ 5:20 and by the
same psalm's v.~8, \textbf{neither of which is in the appointed extract}: the
move is Augustine's on the whole psalm, applied to the verse the missal took.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{Guéranger reads the same verse differently, and the two do not reconcile
by stacking.} Where Augustine turns the petition outward and missionary,
Guéranger turns it inward and nuptial --- in \textbf{Dom Laurence Shepherd's}
English, the Duffy 1900 edition of \work{The Liturgical Year} vol.~XI: ``the
Church, in presenting her petitions to the Most High, begs of Him to look on the
face of His Christ, who is also hers,'' grounded on 1~Cor.\ 11:7, 11:3 and
Eph.\ 5:23. Guéranger is nineteenth-century and not patristic; his standing is
that of a recognised authority on the Roman liturgy, and \textbf{his voice does
not stand in for the tradition's.}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Arnobius the Younger (\emph{attributed}), \work{Commentarii in
Psalmos}, in Ps.~LXXXIII, PL~53:446C--447D.} \textbf{His reading of \latin{réspice in fáciem Christi tui} is none
of the others:} God looking on the face of his Christ is
God seeing the man seated at his own right hand --- \latin{Cum respicit in
faciem Christi, et hominem viderit sedentem ad dexteram suam} --- and the day
better than thousands follows from that man, \latin{per quem melior dies una,
quia aeterna erit, et noctem non habebit}, with \latin{millia} glossed as years
consumed on earth. \textbf{His agreement with Augustine and Cassiodorus about
the everlasting day is reached by a different route --- through Christ rather
than through the day's own properties --- and his \latin{millia} differs from
Cassiodorus's, so his is agreement reached separately and not a further vote for
their account.} He also reads the psalm's
opening allegorically: the sparrow is Christ, the turtle-dove the Church, the
altars \latin{mysteria tua}, and the house of God \latin{non cemento constat et
calce, sed in homine credente}. \textbf{The ascription of these commentaries is
the traditional one, and they stand as well under \latin{Auctor incertus}.}
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Godfrey of Admont, \work{Homilia} LXXXII, \latin{In Dominicam XIV post
Pentecosten prima}, PL~174:575A--580C.} \textbf{He
alone among the Latin witnesses read above makes the anointed the congregation}:
\latin{Petimus itaque in Introitu missae ut ille, qui protector noster est,
Christum suum nobis dando, semper nos unctos suos respiciat}; the courts are
\latin{in corporibus et in animabus nostris, quae per atria Domini possunt
intelligi}; and \latin{melior est una dies cognitionis et illuminationis eius
super millia, id est antecellit et superexcellit universa opera et merita
nostra}. \textbf{The \work{Expositiones} under Athanasius's name reach the same
sense in Greek, and Theodoret an ecclesial one beside it.}
\textbf{He quotes the Introit as sung} --- \latin{Sic enim cantamus}
--- \textbf{and the psalm verse with it.} \textbf{His Fourteenth Sunday is not
the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday.} His Gospel is the ten lepers of Luke~17 and his Communion is
\latin{Panis, quem ego dedero}; the 1962 Gospel is his \textbf{Fifteenth}.
\textbf{So the chants and the Collect he expounds are this formulary's and the
Mass he expounds them in is not} --- which is the displacement Honorius shows,
met independently in a different author.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{\work{Selecta in Psalmos} under Origen's name, \work{Patrologia Graeca}
12:1061--1062.} \textbf{It quotes the Introit's own two spans in succession as
one argument} --- the psalm verse first and then \latin{Protector noster,
aspice, Deus, et respice in faciem Christi tui: quia melior est dies una in
atriis tuis super millia} --- \textbf{cut at exactly the words the antiphon ends
on} --- and glosses the clause \emph{hos kai onomasti Christou memnetai}, that
the psalmist has made mention of Christ by name. \textbf{The attribution is the
controlling caution}: Migne's own running head over the neighbouring fragments
reads \latin{ex Origene}, these are catena excerpts, and a substantial part of
the \work{Selecta} is now assigned to Evagrius Ponticus, so the reading is the
\work{Selecta}'s and not Origen's flatly. \textbf{And the Latin quoted above is Migne's
facing translation, whose psalm wording is the Vulgate's because the translator
used it}, so its agreement with the Introit is a fact about that Latin column
and no evidence about the Greek; what the Greek carries is the pairing, the cut
and the \emph{onomasti}.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{\work{Expositiones in Psalmos} under Athanasius's name, \work{Patrologia
Graeca} 27:369--370.} \textbf{It takes \latin{Christi tui} corporately, of the
people}: at 83:10b, \emph{Christon entautha ton keklemenon laon eis basileion
hierateuma phesi} --- Christ here means the people called to a royal priesthood.
At 83:11a it makes the one day in the courts \textbf{the Saviour's resurrection
day, set against the thousand years of the worship under the Law}, reckoned from
Solomon's foundation of the temple to the Saviour's coming; at 83:2 the
\emph{skenomata} are the holy churches; and at 83:11b --- \textbf{the half-verse
the Introit cuts off} --- it prefers to be small and of no account in the Church
than great among a sinful people. \textbf{The authenticity of these
\work{Expositiones} is contested and they are widely held to be catena material
under his name}, so the reading is the \work{Expositiones}' and not
Athanasius's. \textbf{A
running-head hazard stands beside it, unexplained}: the leaf carrying this
exposition heads its columns \latin{Expositio in Psalmum LXXXIV} although the
Greek beneath is unmistakably the Septuagint's Ps.~83, so the column and the
Septuagint number are the reference that holds, and never the running head's
figure.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{These two Greek readings are directly opposed, and both stand.} The
\work{Selecta} make \latin{faciem Christi tui} name Christ explicitly; the
\work{Expositiones} make ``thy Christ'' the people. \textbf{Both are of
contested attribution, so neither is a vote of the author whose name stands over
it.}

\textbf{Three Roman texts open on the same words, and they stand in three
different relations to Ps.~83.} The incipit \latin{Protector noster aspice,
Deus} opens \textbf{both} this Mass's
Introit and the Fifth Sunday after Pentecost's Gradual, with different
continuations --- \latin{in faciem Christi tui} against \latin{super servos
tuos} --- so the two part company only past the incipit. In the 1862 Missal
the collision is wider still: the same words open a verbatim psalm Introit, an
adapted Gradual at four occurrences, \textbf{and a composed collect that quotes
no psalm at all} (\latin{Protector noster aspice, Deus: ut, qui malorum
nostrorum pondere premimur\dots}). \textbf{This Introit quotes the psalm
verbatim, the Fifth Sunday's Gradual adapts it, and the collect only echoes
it}: a loose echo in a composed oration is not a quotation of the psalm at all.
\textbf{And the older books widen it again}: the responsory of the Monday of the
first week of Lent and of the Lenten Ember Saturday opens on the same words and
continues \latin{et respice super servos tuos}, with \latin{Domine Deus virtutum,
exaudi preces servorum tuorum} as its verse; and two orations of the same
incipit are neither chant. \textbf{At least three distinct Latin texts stand
under that incipit in the Roman books, and the two chants share not only the
incipit but a psalm.} \textbf{So a note in an older book that names an
\latin{Oratio Protector noster aspice, Deus} --- as one does at the ordination of
exorcists --- names an oration and not this Introit}, and an argument built from
a search index on those words alone will attach this Sunday's chant to a rite it
has nothing to do with.

\textbf{The Roman Mass antiphonary prints this Introit at
\latin{Hebdomada decima quarta}, with its psalm verse, word for word as 1962
has them} --- and prints the same psalm text again as the second Responsory of
\latin{Hebdomada duodecima}. \textbf{And a late-thirteenth-century Roman
ceremonial has the cardinal priests sing the clause to a newly elected pope}: at
\work{Ordo Romanus} XIII \S11, when a pope consecrated or elected outside Rome
comes to St~Peter's, \latin{cantant coram ipso cardinales presbyteri: Protector
noster aspice Deus, et respice in facie Christi tui}, after which he is led to
the altar and the canons sing the \latin{Te Deum}. \textbf{Nothing in that book
says the chant was taken from this Sunday's Introit}; the ceremonial reads the ablative \latin{in facie} against the
missal's \latin{in faciem}; and the ceremonial is Migne's reprint of
Mabillon, one printed edition standing for the manuscripts, with no earlier
\work{Ordo} beside it. \textbf{What it shows is that \latin{Christi tui} --- ``thy
anointed'' --- was sung to the face of a man just elected.}

\textbf{Theodoret's Greek at PG~80:1541--1545 reads \latin{réspice in fáciem
Christi tui} ecclesially}, the face of Christ being the saved people, on
1~Cor.~12:27 ---
\emph{hymeis de este somata Christou}, in transliteration. \textbf{His
expositions of Pss.~83, 94 and 117 stand in a Greek transcription of
\work{Patrologia Graeca} 80 and not in the columns themselves.} \textbf{That reading
does not stack with the others}, and it removes the ground for treating the
inward-ecclesial reading as a nineteenth-century departure from the Fathers ---
though it stands on a different Pauline text and without the nuptial figure, so
it corroborates neither Augustine nor Guéranger.

\textbf{And on the antiphon's own clause he argues the other way from both
Latins.} Augustine and Cassiodorus read one everlasting day that has neither
rising nor setting; \textbf{Theodoret reads an argument from profit} at
\work{Interpretatio in Ps.} LXXXIII, \work{Patrologia Graeca}~80:1541--1545 ---
what a man may reap in a single day in those courts he could not gather
elsewhere though he spent thousands of days at it --- and he sets the psalm
historically among the Babylonian captives, \textbf{his own reconstruction} and
not something the psalm states. \textbf{So the antiphon's one day is contested
and not agreed}: the Antiochene historical method stands against the Latin
spiritual reading on the same words, and this reading too stands in a Greek
transcription of \work{Patrologia Graeca} 80 and not in the columns themselves.

\textbf{Cassiodorus reads the psalm in Latin.} On \latin{réspice} he refuses the
surface reading exactly as Augustine does and names the figure: \latin{Haec
figura dicitur hypallage, Latine permutatio, quoties dicitur respici magis ille
qui respicit} --- the one said to be looked upon is rather the one who looks ---
and the petition accordingly turns outward to the gentiles, with Ps.~13:2 and
Ps.~5:2 as his parallels. And on this
psalm's cut clause \latin{cor meum et caro mea} he comments at v.~3b,
\textbf{the clause the missal cuts}: a second witness that \latin{caro} here is
positive, outside the appointed extract. \textbf{At the Introit he is
corroboration and probably dependence on Augustine, not a second independent
vote.} And \textbf{no later use redirects the Introit's wording}: every located use is
straight devotional or liturgical reception.

\subsection*{Collect \cue{Coll.}}

\elementid{Custódi, Dómine, quǽsumus, Ecclésiam tuam}{\latin{Oratio}}{1573}

\textbf{The pointing is load-bearing.} The semicolon after \latin{mortálitas} is
a semicolon and not a comma, and it divides the prayer into its two real parts:
a causal clause, \latin{quia sine te lábitur humána mortálitas}, and the double
petition that clause grounds. The two limbs of the petition are yoked by an
\latin{et \dots\ et} straddling \latin{abstrahátur} and \latin{dirigátur}, and
the first \latin{et} sits before \latin{abstrahátur} rather than before
\latin{tuis}, which is what makes the pair read as a pair. By the same
\latin{auxíliis} the Church is drawn back from what harms and steered toward
what saves: withdrawal and direction, one instrument.

\textbf{Its object is the Church, and its diagnosis is mortality.} Not sin, not
the world, not the enemy: what the prayer says is wrong is that human mortality
slips when God is not holding it. \textbf{The Church is named here and nowhere
else in the ten elements} --- though the first-person plurals of Introit,
Alleluia, Secret and Postcommunion are corporate in their own way, which softens
any contrast built on that count. The formulary's only other words for sin are
this prayer's \latin{nóxiis} and the Secret's \latin{delictórum};
\latin{peccatum} occurs nowhere in the ten elements at all.

\textbf{This prayer is received three ways, and they are three kinds of
reception rather than three votes on a meaning.} \textbf{Honorius} cites the
prayer by incipit in \work{Gemma animae} IV. \textbf{Godfrey of Admont} glosses
it word by word inside his homily on this Mass, by interpolation: \latin{quia
sine te labitur humana mortalitas in elatione, tuis semper auxiliis abstrahatur
a noxiis superbiae et extollentiae mentis, et ad salutaria, vere humilitatis
bona, te ducente dirigatur}. And \textbf{Schuster} comments on the prayer
continuously, at more length than Guéranger. Godfrey's Mass, as at the Introit,
is not this one.

\textbf{An exact-phrase index over the \work{Patrologia Latina} returns this
Collect's clauses at two further places}: inside a prayer of Alcuin, \work{De
usu Psalmorum} VII, and inside a letter of Wibald of Corvey to Eugenius III,
\work{Epistola} CCCLXXV. \textbf{What either writer makes of the Collect's
clauses is not established}, and both stand as unverified leads.

Guéranger makes one interpretive move on this Collect: that the abyss of vice
the Church's children would fall into without help is specifically the Epistle's
catalogue --- a cross-element reading. \textbf{But it is an association he
asserts in one sentence rather than argues, with no witness behind it.} His
remarks on the Secret and the Postcommunion are one sentence each and are
paraphrase rather than exegesis.

\textbf{What the English witness does not carry.} The Cummiskey rendering turns
\latin{propitiatióne perpétua} into ``by thy constant mercy'' and \latin{tuis
semper auxíliis} into ``may thy grace,'' so both the noun \latin{propitiatio} and
the adverb \latin{semper} disappear from the English. The English itself
stands on the printed page: at p.~425 of the 1861 hand missal this Collect
reads word for word and point for point as it is printed here, as do the Secret
and the Postcommunion at p.~427. The book prints no translator's name.

\subsection*{Epistle \cue{Ep.}}

\elementid{Fratres: Spíritu ambuláte}{\latin{Léctio Epístolæ beáti Pauli Apóstoli ad Gálatas} --- Gal. 5, 16-24}{1574}

\subsubsection*{Where the lesson is cut from, and what the cut costs}

The pericope \textbf{replaces} Paul's own connective. Clementine Gal.\ 5:16
reads \latin{Dico autem: Spiritu ambulate}, and the appointed Epistle reads
\latin{Fratres: Spíritu ambuláte}. \latin{Dico autem} ties v.~16 back to the
liberty of v.~13 --- where Paul's own vocative \latin{fratres} stands, inside
the sentence the pericope drops --- and to the law fulfilled in love at v.~14.
\textbf{The missal's \latin{Fratres} displaces the connective rather than
supplementing it}, and supplies at v.~16 an address the apostle had used three
verses earlier.

It also ends two verses before the unit's own closing inclusio. Verse 25's
\latin{Si Spiritu vivimus, Spiritu et ambulemus} returns v.~16's verb in the
hortatory subjunctive --- and \textbf{in Greek the two verbs differ}, so the
inclusio is tighter in the Latin than in the Greek, which is a fact about the
Vulgate's rendering. \textbf{The appointed excerpt therefore ends on crucifixion
rather than on walking}, and v.~25 is the incipit of the \emph{next} Sunday's
lesson, printed on the facing pages of the same book.

\subsubsection*{Two catalogues that are not built alike}

\latin{Ópera carnis} is plural with a plural verb; \latin{fructus Spíritus} is
singular with a singular verb, and \textbf{the asymmetry stands in the Greek as
well}, so it is not a Latin artefact. The vice list is left explicitly open
(\latin{et his simília}) and the fruit list is closed with a verdict
(\latin{Advérsus huiúsmodi non est lex}).

\textbf{The list lengths, and their mechanism.} Latin: seventeen vices, twelve
fruits. The Byzantine Greek: seventeen vices, nine fruits. The critical text
its apparatus records: fifteen and nine. Three precise consequences follow.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=0.15em]
\item \textbf{The Latin fruit list exceeds both Greek forms by exactly
three.} The pair \latin{patiéntia}/\latin{longanímitas} answers the single Greek
word for long-suffering --- \textbf{Jerome says so himself}, as he says that
\latin{continéntia} renders \emph{enkrateia} --- and \latin{modéstia} and
\latin{cástitas} answer nothing in either the Byzantine text or its apparatus.
\textbf{Any argument built on the number twelve is an argument about the
liturgical Latin.}
\item \textbf{\latin{Homicídia} stands in neither a Revised Version nor a modern
critical-text English}, because the Byzantine text has the word and the
apparatus marks it absent from the critical text --- so \textbf{the reason is a
Byzantine-against-critical divergence and not a peculiarity of the Vulgate.}
\item \textbf{But the Vulgate's list is not simply the Byzantine list either:}
the Byzantine word for adultery has no Latin counterpart, so the Clementine
sides with the critical text at that word and with the Byzantine text at
``murders.''
\end{enumerate}

The word-by-word alignment of
\latin{luxúria}/\latin{impudicítia} against their Greek neighbours is a
judgement about renderings and not a count, and it stands more loosely than the
counts do. Every critical-text reading in this comparison is an apparatus note beside the
Byzantine text and not a critical edition's own text.

\subsubsection*{What the commentators actually argue}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{John Chrysostom, \work{Commentarius in epistolam ad Galatas} cap.~V,
verse by verse.} \textbf{His
governing move survives the Greek, and is sharper there:} \emph{horas hoti ou
phesi ten sarka entautha, alla ton geode logismon kai chamai syromenon} ---
\textbf{the object is \emph{sarx} and the contrast term \emph{logismos}}, an
earthly cast of mind dragged along the ground, \textbf{not \emph{soma}}; NPNF's
``by the flesh, he does not mean the body'' is a paraphrase that does not
misrepresent the sense, but \textbf{the Greek makes a sharper claim about the
word the Epistle actually uses}, and it is the Greek that bears on the appointed
\latin{caro}. Proved from the vice-list itself, which the
\work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers} English gives as ``hatred, variance,
emulations, strife, heresies, and witchcraft \dots\ arise merely from a
depraved moral choice.'' \textbf{His argument from the verb holds
in the appointed Latin:} ``If these things belonged to nature and not to a bad
moral choice, his expression, \emph{they practise}, is inappropriate'' --- and
the missal prints \latin{qui tália agunt}. \textbf{The Greek confirms two
wordings of the appointed Latin against rivals:} his \emph{Ouk eipe, to ergon
tou pneumatos, all', Ho karpos} is verbatim NPNF's ``He says not, the work of
the Spirit, but, the fruit,'' and his lemma at the list's close is \emph{Qui
autem sunt Christi} --- \textbf{the missal's reading, against Augustine's
\latin{Qui sunt in Christo Iesu}}, so on this clause the appointed Latin is the
better witness to Paul. \textbf{And at the lesson's last verse he insists the
crucifixion is not the destruction of the flesh}, \emph{Ou gar de ten sarka
aneilon: epei pos emellon zen?} --- exact discipline --- and \textbf{no other
expositor above states so directly that the appointed v.~24 is not ascetical.}
\textbf{No other exposition follows the appointed Epistle verse by verse as this
one does}, with lemmata at vv.~16, 17, 18, 19--21, 22 and 24 and
continuing to v.~25, where the missal's pericope stops.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{Chrysostom's Greek on the first half of the pericope} (PG~61:670--673)
reads \latin{Sarka gar entautha, ou to soma kalei}: flesh here is the earthbound, slothful, neglected reasoning;
\textbf{desire belongs to the soul, not to flesh, so a flesh that
\emph{desires} cannot be the body}; the body is an instrument, \latin{organon
gar estin he sarx}, and \emph{we hate and punish not the iron but the murderer};
the conflict is between two reasonings, virtue and vice, \latin{ouch he psyche
kai to soma}. \textbf{And at v.~18 he calls the dismissal of the law a great and
wonderful praise of it}, set aside not as bad but as less than the philosophy
given by the Spirit. \textbf{The opponents he answers are unnamed in the text.}

\textbf{Chrysostom's own Greek vice lemma has seventeen items and includes both
\emph{pharmakeia} and \emph{phonoi}, so he speaks to \latin{veneficia} and to
\latin{homicidia}}; \latin{luxúria}, \latin{modéstia} and \latin{cástitas} have
no counterpart in the text he expounds, and he says nothing of them. His
nine-item fruit lemma is the Greek's, in the same order Augustine's and
Jerome's lemmata carry.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Theodoret of Cyrus, \work{Interpretatio Epistolae ad Galatas} cap.~V,
\work{Patrologia Graeca} 82:495--498, on the whole appointed pericope.}
\textbf{He comments verse by verse across the entire lesson.} At v.~16, if you would master
the passions of the flesh, follow the Spirit's directions; at v.~17,
\emph{sarka legei ten epi ta cheiro tes gnomes ropen; pneuma de ten enoikousan
charin} --- flesh is the mind's inclination to the worse and spirit the indwelling
grace. \textbf{He argues twice, from the lists themselves, that this flesh is not
the body}: idolatry and sorcery \emph{ou tes sarkos, alla tes psyches estin
idia}, so \emph{ou toinyn tes sarkos kategorei, alla tou rhathymou phronematos},
he accuses not the flesh but the slothful cast of mind; and had the works of
v.~19 belonged to the body alone, \emph{edei deichthenai kai ta tes psyches
erga; all' ou tetheike} --- a matching list of the soul's works would have had to
be set down, and is not. He answers v.~23b from 1~Tim.\ 1:9. \textbf{And he
closes the appointed lesson baptismally}, \emph{to Christo syntaphentes nekron
apephenan te hamartia to soma}. \textbf{His own lemma is not the missal's}: at
v.~19 it opens with the Greek for \emph{adultery}, which the appointed Latin
does not carry, and his fruit-list has the Greek nine. Migne's facing Latin is
the Renaissance translation and controls his sense, not the missal's wording.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{De civitate Dei} XIV.2, in Hoffmann's critical edition,
CSEL~40.2 pp.~3--4.} \textbf{He reaches the same conclusion by a sharper route,
and by two middle terms of his own.} Because idolatry, sorcery, enmity,
dissension, heresy and envy are vices of the mind, \textbf{a man may abstain
from bodily pleasure precisely \emph{on account of} idolatry or heresy and be
convicted by apostolic authority of living according to the flesh in the very
act of that abstinence} --- \latin{et in eo, quod abstinet a uoluptatibus
carnis, damnabilia opera carnis agere demonstratur}. Then the test from ordinary
speech: nobody says \latin{Malam carnem} to an enemy. So Paul calls them all
works of the flesh \latin{eo locutionis modo, quo totum significatur a parte,
ipsum hominem uult nomine carnis intellegi}. \textbf{The Latin is the critical
edition's own wording, carried by a transcription of that edition.}
\textbf{Theodoret and Augustine reach one conclusion by three different middle
terms, one in Greek and two in Latin, and nothing shows that either knew the
other.}
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Ambrosiaster, \work{Commentaria in Epistolam ad Galatas}, PL
17:367D--368D.} \textbf{His
reading is governed by two laws rather than by two parts of man.} At v.~17,
\latin{Duas leges proponit \dots\ unam Dei, alteram peccati}, with \latin{medius
homo} standing between them; at vv.~19--21 the works are called the flesh's by
origin, \latin{quia errores isti ex mundo sunt, ex quo et caro}; and at v.~24
\latin{qui sunt Christi} is glossed \latin{christos, id est, unctos} while the
crucified flesh is the world, \latin{Hi carnem, id est, mundum crucifigunt},
proved from 1~Jn.\ 2:15. \textbf{His lemma is not the missal's Vulgate.} His fruit-list
has \textbf{ten} members, carrying \latin{spes} and \latin{castitas} while
lacking \latin{fides} and \latin{patientia}, and his vice-list opens
\latin{adulterium, fornicatio, impuritas, obscenitas} where the missal opens
\latin{fornicátio, immundítia, impudicítia, luxúria}. \textbf{So his exposition
counts a different pair of lists, and the missal's seventeen and twelve are not
the lists he expounds.}
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Marius Victorinus, \work{In epistolam Pauli ad Galatas}, PL
8:1192B--D.} \textbf{He is half a negative.} His
text as Migne prints it passes straight from v.~17 to 6:1, so \textbf{he
comments on none of vv.~18--24.} What he does say at v.~17 is the exact contrary
of Chrysostom's move: \latin{Habet enim motus suos caro, habetque sensus, neque
tantum ab anima suscitantur}, argued from water, fire, earth and the other
elements, which have their own impulses and no soul at all. \textbf{The two
comment on the same appointed verse and cannot both be right.} \textbf{The lacuna
is in the commentary as Migne prints it}, the text standing continuous across the join; no critical edition stands
beside it, so it is a gap in this printing and not established as Victorinus's
own.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Glossa ordinaria on Galatians, PL 114:584B--585B.} \textbf{A third position on the clause Chrysostom and Victorinus divide
over:} the flesh is \emph{said} to desire by the same idiom that says the ear
hears and the eye sees --- \latin{Caro dicta est concupiscere, quia hoc secundum
ipsam agit anima} --- and the cause is in neither alone, \latin{ex utroque enim
fit, quia sine utroque delectatio nulla sentitur}. It reads \latin{non
perficietis} precisely (\latin{non ait non feceritis \dots\ sed non
perficietis, id est non opera eorum consensu voluntatis implebitis}), defines
being under the law by fear rather than love, and \textbf{organises the fruit
list by making charity its head from which the members follow},
\latin{Caput praemisit, unde membra sequuntur}. \textbf{It is a compilation carried
under the name \latin{Anselmus Laudunensis et schola}, which
is the modern attribution of the redaction and not a claim that Anselm wrote
these sentences; no sentence in it stands under a named Father's name.}
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{Expositio epistolae ad Galatas} ss.~45--53, continuous
on the whole appointed pericope.} \textbf{His own lemma differs from the missal's at every
load-bearing point: thirteen vices} --- \latin{animositates} for the missal's
\latin{irae}, \latin{haereses} for \latin{sectae} --- \textbf{and nine fruits},
and at the exclusion \latin{regnum Dei non possidebunt} where the missal prints
\latin{non consequentur}. \textbf{So his systematic opposition of the two lists}
--- \latin{Recte igitur fornicationi opponitur caritas}, point for point ---
\textbf{cannot be mapped onto the missal's seventeen and twelve without
distortion}. \textbf{On
the counts he refuses the argument altogether}: \latin{Non enim hoc suscepit,
ut doceret, quot sint, sed in quo genere} --- and he reads \emph{both} lists as
open, grounding it on the missal's own word, \latin{Non enim ait: Adversus haec
non est lex, sed: Adversus huiusmodi}. \textbf{On v.~16 he makes the argument
turn on the mood of the verb}: Paul said not that you shall not \emph{have} the
desires of the flesh but that you shall not \emph{fulfil} them --- and though
his lemma reads \latin{concupiscentias carnis ne perfeceritis}, the argument
survives into the missal's \latin{non perficietis}, which is still about
fulfilling. \textbf{On v.~17 he denies that the verse removes free choice}, the
verse being addressed to those who will not hold the grace of faith --- an
anti-Pelagian-shaped argument drawing on Rom.~5--8, none of it appointed. At
the connective the pericope cuts he reads \latin{Spiritu ambulate} as the answer
to v.~15's biting and devouring, naming \latin{humilitas et mansuetudo} the
first and great gift of the Spirit --- \textbf{and \latin{mansuetúdo} is in the
appointed fruit list, so that link the appointed text can carry}. At the last
verse he grounds the crucifixion of the flesh in \textbf{chaste fear}, the fear
of the chaste wife against the adulteress's, through Ps.~118:120.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Jerome, \work{Commentarii in epistolam ad Galatas} lib.~III,
ad 5:16--24.} \textbf{He rules on the missal's list count in his own voice:}
\latin{Sed sciendum non plus quam quindecim carnis opera nominata} --- fifteen
vices, with \latin{adulterium}, \latin{impudicitia} and \latin{homicidia}
recorded as additions found \emph{in Latinis codicibus}, and the items numbered
in his own text --- \textbf{so the 1962 Epistle prints two of the three he
rejects, in a list of seventeen}. The bound: this is Jerome on the Latin codices
he knew about 386--388, not a judgement about the Clementine's descent, and
nothing is said about how the 1962 text got its count. \textbf{On the missal's
own word \latin{vitiis} he is its defender, not its corrector:}
\latin{Ubi Latinus interpres vitia posuit, in Graeco pathemata, id est,
passiones leguntur}, and \latin{desideria} was added \latin{ut non naturam
corporis videretur in spiritualibus viris negare, sed vitia} --- \textbf{which
also explains Augustine's divergent lemma \latin{passionibus}}: two renderings
of one Greek noun, not two different texts. \textbf{His philology of the
vice-list is Greek-anchored and checkable}: \latin{aemulatio} = \emph{zelos};
\latin{rixae} renders \emph{eritheia} and not \emph{mache}; \latin{haeresis}
from \emph{helesthai}, choosing. \textbf{He distinguishes \latin{modestia} from
\latin{continentia}} --- the former belonging to the perfect, the latter to
those still on the way --- \textbf{which is not the medieval
\latin{continentia}/\latin{castitas} pair} Anthony and Aquinas share, but a
different distinction. On v.~17 he answers the free-choice objection by a
\textbf{tripartite anthropology}, the soul standing in the middle between flesh
and spirit --- \textbf{the same difficulty Chrysostom raises in Greek and the
same answer, a convergence with nothing showing dependence}. On v.~18 he tells
a Latin reader that \textbf{\latin{spiritus} there stands without the article
and so is the Holy Spirit}, a distinction Greek marks and Latin cannot ---
\emph{qui arthra penitus non habemus}; the 1962 book's lower-case \latin{spiritu}
at v.~18 and capital \latin{Spiritus} at v.~22 are the printers making visible
what he says the language cannot. \textbf{He transmits Origen verbatim and
partly withdraws from him}: at v.~24 he records Origen construing \latin{Christi}
with \latin{carnem} --- those without law crucified the flesh \emph{of Christ}
--- and declines it, \latin{ut non carnem Christi, sed suam eos crucifixisse
dicamus}; and on the fruit's being unnamed he transmits Origen's reading that
the works are \emph{manifest} and the fruit not, so it must be sought with
labour, while saying at 5:19--21 that on reflection the named vices seem to him
plain sense. \textbf{Origen is present here only in Jerome's report of him, and that report
stands beside no text of Origen's own.} \textbf{And on the clause the list closes with,
he and Augustine converge on one proof-text}, 1~Tim.~1:8--10, that the law is
not laid down for the just --- \textbf{the firmest reception \latin{Advérsus
huiúsmodi non est lex} has here, and convergence rather than dependence}.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{Six accounts of the two lists' asymmetry stand, and they are six.} The
textual reading: the vice list is left open (\latin{et his
simília}) and the fruit list closed with a verdict. Augustine, against the
argument from counts altogether, reads \emph{both} lists as open on the
missal's own \latin{huiúsmodi} --- pointing the opposite way.
Origen through Jerome: the works are called \emph{manifest} and the fruit not,
so the fruit must be sought with labour. Chrysostom: evil works come from us
alone, while the good need God's kindness too --- \textbf{and Jerome's own
reason differs from his, vices ending in themselves where virtues sprout and
overflow, the Greek account being about grace and the Latin about fecundity.}
\textbf{The Glossa alone gives the list a structure}, and it
answers the question the others leave open: charity is the head from which the
members follow, so the fruit list is not a list of coordinates at all.
\textbf{And Ambrosiaster grounds \latin{fructus} rather than \latin{opera} in
the law of the spirit that fructifies them} --- a reason different again from
Chrysostom's and from Jerome's --- \textbf{but his own lists are not the
missal's, so his reason stands while his count is of other lists.}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Thomas Aquinas, \work{Super Galatas} cap.~5 lect.~5 and 6.}
\textbf{He expounds the missal's own lists, and he and Anthony of Padua are the
two witnesses read here that do.} He orders the twelve fruits by what each perfects ---
interior, exterior, what is above us, what is below us --- and reads the number
as answering Apoc.\ 22:2, \latin{ex utraque parte lignum vitae afferens fructus
duodecim}; \textbf{the Apocalypse link is his own move.} He groups the seventeen vices by
object, derives \latin{veneficia} from \latin{venenum}, and reads
\latin{homicidia} \latin{cordis et operis}, of the heart as well as of the deed,
citing 1~Jn.\ 4. \textbf{He raises the objection Chrysostom raises --- that
several listed vices are plainly not bodily --- and answers it by a different
route:} Augustine's definition at \work{De civitate Dei} XIV.2, that whoever
lives according to himself lives according to the flesh, so that \latin{caro}
here stands for the whole man --- \textbf{and Augustine's own wording at that
place, read above, grounds it on the ascetic idolater and on ordinary speech
rather than on the definition alone.} \textbf{And he classes \latin{idolorum
servitus} among the vices against God}, as that \latin{per quod impeditur ab
hostibus Dei cultus divinus}, warranting it from 1~Cor.\ 10:7 and Wisd.\ 14:27,
\textbf{and cites Eph.\ 5:5 at this pericope for \latin{fornicatio} and
\latin{immunditia} and not for the idolatry clause} --- \textbf{so the
identification of avarice with idolatry is not made here by the appointed text's
own principal Latin commentator.} At vv.~22--23 he defines \latin{fides} as
\latin{cognitio quaedam invisibilium cum certitudine}, from Gen.\ 15:6 and
Heb.\ 11:6, and holds the works of the Spirit called fruits \latin{non ut adepti
sive acquisiti, sed ut producti}.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Anthony of Padua, \latin{Sermo Dominica XIV post Pentecosten}.}
\textbf{He left a sermon headed for this very Sunday, and
it is partly a false friend.} He states his own Mass's texts explicitly: his
Introit is \latin{Inclina, Domine, aurem tuam ad me} --- the 1962
\textbf{Fifteenth} Sunday's Introit --- and his Gospel is Luke 17:11--19, the ten
lepers. His whole sermon is built on leprosy and cleansing, and his stated reason
for pairing Epistle with Gospel (\latin{in evangelio fit mentio de leprosis \dots\
et in epistola ponuntur vitia, ex quibus provenit lepra peccati}) is a rationale
for a pairing \textbf{the 1962 missal does not make}. \textbf{His exposition of
the Epistle's own words stands on the appointed text}; the pairing of Gal.~5
with a cleansing narrative, and everything resting on \latin{Inclina, Domine},
belongs to his Mass and not to this one.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{Anthony's substantive moves on the Epistle.} He glosses \latin{idolorum
servitus} as \textbf{avarice} rather than the cult of images; he moralises
\latin{veneficia} into \latin{suggestiones daemonum et adulationes peccatorum,
quae nocere non possunt nisi ad sanguinem, idest animae consensum, pervenerint};
and he defines all twelve fruits individually --- \textbf{passing over
\latin{homicidia} with no gloss at all.} His definitions of the last two,
\latin{continentia, quae a licitis abstinet; castitas, quae recte utitur licito},
are \textbf{verbatim} the pair Aquinas independently attributes to the Gloss,
in two texts that stand independently of each other --- \textbf{which is real
evidence of a common medieval source.} His etymologies carry the moral
sense he draws from a word rather
than its descent: \latin{fornicatio, idest formae necatio} reads the sin as the
killing of the form, which is his gloss on the appointed word and not its
philology. His inner
quotations from the Gloss and from Isidore stand at second hand and are
attributed to \emph{his use} of them and not to Isidore or the Gloss; the same holds for the definition of charity he ascribes to
Augustine, which the edition's own note transmits through Peter Lombard and
which reads differently in the printed Augustine, \textbf{so it is not
Augustine's exact words.} And his text prints three of the fruits in a
different order from the missal's --- \latin{patientia, longanimitas, bonitas,
benignitas, mansuetudo} against \latin{patiéntia, benígnitas, bónitas,
longanímitas, mansuetúdo} --- which is a fact about two texts and \textbf{not
evidence about which Vulgate recension the missal descends from.}

\textbf{And one fact bears on every join of Epistle and Gospel.} Anthony's
sermon is the one medieval treatment of Gal.\ 5:16--24 located for this Epistle,
and \textbf{his Sunday numbering runs one ahead of the 1962 missal's,
consistently across all three of his sermons for these Sundays.} A major
medieval preacher expounding this Epistle does so beside a different Gospel. So
every cross-element candidate joining Epistle and Gospel is a claim about how
the 1962 formulary reads when taken as a whole, and \textbf{not} a claim about
how the tradition read these two texts together.

Augustine's and Jerome's Galatians commentaries stand \textbf{in
transcriptions, with no Migne column or critical edition beside them}, and
Theodoret's exposition stands at its own Migne columns. Cassian, Pelagius's
\work{Expositio}, Nicholas of Lyra, Denis the Carthusian and Cornelius a Lapide
on Galatians add nothing to the question, so \textbf{no second Latin witness
stands to whether \latin{fides} at Gal.\ 5:22 is faith or fidelity.}

\subsection*{Gradual \cue{Grad.}}

\elementid{Bonum est confidere in Dómino}{\latin{Graduale} --- Ps. 117, 8-9}{1575}

Unlike the Introit and the Offertory,
\textbf{the Gradual takes complete verses and cuts nothing}, so there is no
omission at all --- one of only three places in this formulary where the
appointed wording is the Clementine's exactly. Context: vv.~6--7 twice call the
Lord \latin{adjutor} and name \latin{homo} as what need not be feared, so v.~8's
\latin{quam confidere in hómine} generalises a contrast the psalm has just made
concrete; and v.~1's \latin{quoniam bonus} supplies the adjective the Gradual's
\latin{Bonum est} turns into a comparative formula. The Clementine
prints \latin{Alleluja.} at the head of v.~1, which bears on any claim that
Ps.~117 has no superscription --- it is a liturgical rubric-word rather than an
ascription, and it is not numbered separately.

\textbf{The princes are read four ways, and a question stands beside the four.}
\textbf{Cassiodorus, in Latin at PL~70}, observes that \textbf{the two
appointed verses are governed by anaphora} --- \latin{Et nota quod in principiis amborum versuum eadem verba
geminavit. Quae figura dicitur anaphora} --- exactly on the appointed wording;
reads the pair as \textbf{carnal against spiritual} rather than a moral climb,
citing Jas.~4:13--15, Phil.~2:13 and Jer.~17:5 against presumption of the will;
and lets \latin{princeps} cover \textbf{both the devil and the good angel},
\latin{Sic et bonus angelus dictus est princeps, sicut in Daniele legitur}.
\textbf{Theodoret, in Greek at PG~80:1812}, has \emph{archontes} who are
\textbf{earthly rulers whose authority is temporary}: God is good, wills the good, can do what he wills and has
imperishable authority, while men are corruptible by nature and changeable in
purpose; his preface makes them the persecuting rulers, demagogues, kings and
generals whom the gentile believers outlasted. \textbf{His psalm heading is the
same \emph{Allelouia} the Clementine prints}, a small convergence, and
\textbf{his lemma reads the missal's ablative}, one data point against the
accusative variant below without settling it. \textbf{Augustine and Cassiodorus
both reach Michael in Daniel, so between those two the shared proof-text is
probably dependence and not independent invention} --- and \textbf{the
division on what the princes are} sets a Greek reading against three
Latin ones on the same two verses.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{Enarratio in Ps. CXVII} §4, on vv.~8 and 9.}
\textbf{He reads the two verses as a single
escalating argument}, in the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers} English:
``let not my friend present himself unto me as a good man,
so as to bid me repose my hope in himself \dots\ Nor let any one, who may in a
certain sense be styled a good angel, be regarded by myself as one in whom I
ought to put my trust \dots\ For angels also are called princes, even as we read
in Daniel, Michael, your prince.'' The second term is progressively strengthened
--- bad men, good men, good angels --- and still loses.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Prosper of Aquitaine, \work{Expositio Psalmorum}, PL 51:333B--C.}
\latin{Etiam si bonus homo, aut bonus
angelus adiuvet, non in eis, inquit, confidam; sed in Domino, qui me per illos
iuvat}; and at v.~9, \latin{Principes et homines et angeli possunt intelligi:
sed in neutris spes collocanda est; quia potestas non est nisi a Deo} (Rom.\
13:1). \textbf{His frame for the passage is Eph.\ 6:12, read three verses
earlier, and that is what licenses the angelic sense}, which v.~9 does not carry
by itself. \textbf{Three verses later he quotes the Offertory's own
words} --- \latin{ut gustemus et videamus quam suavis est Dominus} --- so a
Father joins the Gradual's psalm to the Offertory's verse in one sentence.
This \work{Expositio} is largely an epitome of Augustine's
\work{Enarrationes}, and how much of it is Prosper's own is unsettled.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Arnobius the Younger (\emph{attributed}), in Ps.~CXVII, PL
53:505A--C.} \textbf{The man not to be trusted is
oneself, and the confidence forbidden is confidence in free choice:}
\latin{Habes quidem liberum arbitrium, sed noli de eo praesumere; de Deo
praesume, quia vinci non potest, nam liberum arbitrium vinci potest}, with the
predestinarian objection raised and answered in the same passage.
\textbf{The anti-Pelagian books Cassiodorus names at this verse are a
cross-reference and no more}, and \textbf{the grace-and-free-will reading of the verse is
attested in a fifth-century Latin psalm commentary in its own right}, and not
only through the cross-reference Cassiodorus makes.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Godfrey of Admont, on the appointed verses, PL 174:579D--580A.}
\textbf{A fourth account of what the princes are, and the only one that keeps
them inside the man who prays:} the princes and the servants are the interior
and the exterior senses --- \latin{Interiores sensus nostri sunt principes,
exteriores sensus servi nostri} --- and the comparison itself he reads as
\latin{melius est, imo optimum confidere in virtute Dei, quam praesumere in
suis meritis}. \textbf{His Fourteenth Sunday is not the 1962 Fourteenth
Sunday}, as at the Introit.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Bruno the Carthusian (\emph{attributed}), \work{Expositio in Psalmos},
PL 152:1249D--1250B.} \textbf{He alone among
the Latin expositors asks whether the Gradual's \latin{quam} is
comparative at all, and he answers twice.} First he denies that the word is set
down as a comparative: on that reading the verse means that trusting the Lord by
enduring tribulation is useful and looking to the persecutor for bodily safety is
not, with Jer.\ 17:5 as proof. Then he grants the comparative,
\textbf{but only if \latin{principes} are heavenly spirits and spiritual men},
on the ground that God can help without them and they cannot help without God.
\textbf{This is a grammatical judgement about the appointed words themselves},
and the first proposal below stands or falls with his second answer.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{And one compilation carries the angelic reading forward without adding a
vote to it.} \textbf{Peter Lombard} (PL 191:1035B--C) makes v.~8's man
\latin{homo carnalis, cui salus vana est} and, under the siglum \latin{[Aug.]},
v.~9's princes \latin{angelis qui dicuntur principes. Unde in Daniele legitur
Michael princeps} --- \textbf{he is the \latin{Magna glosatura}, a sigla-marked
compilation.} \textbf{So the angelic reading is one position with
three carriers and not three independent witnesses:} Augustine's own
\work{Enarratio in Ps. CXVII} §4, quoted above, holds the
angelic reading with Daniel's Michael as its warrant, so Lombard's siglum
transmits him correctly; Prosper's exposition is largely an Augustine epitome,
and Prosper's reason is Lombard's reason. That \work{Enarratio} stands in
the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers} English, and Migne's Latin of it at
\work{Patrologia Latina} 36--37 does not stand beside it.

The Latin witness reads the accusative \latin{sperare in Dominum \dots\ in
principes} where the missal prints the ablative \latin{in Dómino \dots\ in
princípibus}.
\textbf{Whether that accusative is Augustine's psalter or a later hand's is not
established.}

\textbf{One cross-relation, and Augustine does not make it.} His
escalation to good angels makes the Gradual's exclusion absolute rather than
moral, which is the Gospel's own logic at \latin{Nemo potest duóbus dóminis
servíre}. \textbf{He does not cite Mt.\ 6:24 here}, and the join is made by
none of the expositors above.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{John Chrysostom, \work{Expositio in Psalmum CXVII}, \work{Patrologia
Graeca} 55:331, read in Greek; Migne prints no facing Latin at this passage.}
\textbf{He denies that the psalm's comparative form states a comparison at all}:
\emph{ou kata synkrisin tauta proagei, all' ethos te Graphe touto kechresthai to
tropo kai epi ton asynkriton dia ten astheneian ton tote akouonton} ---
Scripture uses this figure even of things that admit no comparison, on account
of the weakness of those then hearing --- \emph{ouk ara synkrinon touto phesin,
alla synkatabainon ekeinois}. \textbf{On his reading the Gradual's
twice-repeated \latin{Bonum est \dots\ quam} is divine condescension to weak
hearers and not a weighing of two goods.} He builds on the appointed verses a
catena: Jer.\ 17:5, that the man hoping in man is accursed; the spider's web, so
that hope in man is not only weak but perilous; Rom.\ 5:5, that hope does not
confound; Sir.\ 2:10 --- and he answers the objection \emph{All' ego, phesin,
elpisa, kai kateschynthen}, that the speaker hoped and was put to shame, by
locating the failure in the hoping. \textbf{This bears directly on
the one grammatical feature that distinguishes this Gradual}, and it cuts
against reading the chant as ranking two trusts on one scale. The
\work{Expositio in Psalmos} is generally received as authentic, unlike the
\work{In Psalmum XCIV} printed among his works; that attribution question
stands open, and Migne's text of the \work{Expositio} stands beside no critical
edition.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{Augustine supplies the principle that reconciles the Gradual's exclusion
with the Offertory's rescuing angel, and it is conditional.} At his own
exposition of this psalm, in the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers} English:
``Nor let any one, who may in a certain sense be styled a good angel, be
regarded by myself as one in whom I ought to put my trust: for no one is good,
save God alone; and when a man or an angel appear to aid us, when they do this
of sincere affection, He does it through them, who made them good after their
measure.'' \textbf{So the creature is excluded as an object of trust and
admitted as an instrument} --- \textbf{but for Augustine the tension never arises
at all, because for him the Offertory's \latin{Angelus} is Christ.} If that
angel is a creature, as Cassiodorus, Godfrey and Arnobius have it, the Mass does
trust at one chant a rescuer it excludes from trust at the other, and the
instrumental principle softens the tension without removing it.

\textbf{Chrysostom's exposition of this psalm is not in the Greek collection
that carries his \work{Expositiones in Psalmos}.} That
text is a fragment ending at PG~55:194
and covering Pss.~4--12 and 43--44 only, under a title that promises the whole;
the exposition read above stands in a different volume.
\textbf{And two Greek collections give the Gradual's own verses nothing at all}:
the \work{Expositiones} under Athanasius's name pass directly from Ps.~117:5 to
Ps.~117:10, and the \work{Selecta} under Origen's carry no fragment on
vv.~8--9. \textbf{Bellarmine expounds none of this psalm:} his exposition of Ps.~33 falls
at vv.~2--3, no appointed verse of any element, and it is
an abridged Victorian English translation rather than his Latin.

\subsection*{Alleluia \cue{All.}}

\elementid{Veníte, exsultémus Dómino}{no separate heading; reference inline --- Ps. 94, 1}{1576}

\textbf{The whole psalm, and where its reception went.} Psalm 94 calls the
assembly to praise, names God king above all gods and shepherd of his flock, and
then turns abruptly to warning: today if you shall hear his voice, harden not
your hearts. \textbf{The chant takes v.~1 alone} and drops the psalm title
\latin{Laus cántici ipsi David}, which the Clementine prints as part of that
verse, pointing the clause with a comma where the Clementine has a semicolon.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{Enarratio in Ps. XCIV} §2--3.} \textbf{He takes the imperative \latin{Venite} as the load-bearing
word and denies that it means motion}, in the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene
Fathers} English: ``It is not by place, but by being unlike
Him, that a man is afar from God \dots\ If therefore by unlikeness we recede
from God, by likeness we approach unto God.'' And \latin{exsultemus} is
specified as joy \emph{in the Lord} precisely to exclude a worldly joy the same
word would cover: ``if there were not in this life a wicked joy which is to be
distinguished from a righteous joy, it would be enough to say, Come, let us
rejoice; but he has briefly distinguished it.''
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{The Latin recovers something the translation loses entirely.} Augustine
closes the argument on a jingle built from the appointed verb itself ---
\latin{Pie debes Domino exsultare, si vis securus mundo insultare}: you must
piously \emph{exult} in the Lord if you would safely \emph{exult over} the world.
The English of the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, first series, renders
this ``piously joy in the Lord, if you dost wish safely to
trample upon the world,'' and the play vanishes. \textbf{The play depends on the
missal's own word \latin{exsultémus}.}

\textbf{This exposition's centre of gravity is vv.~7--11,
\latin{Hodie si vocem eius audieritis}, which the Alleluia does not
appoint}, and Augustine's argument there closes on the oath of exclusion from
rest. \textbf{That ending belongs to the psalm and not to the chant, which stops
at v.~1.} The same limit governs
the canonical relationship: Hebrews quotes Ps.\ 94:8--11 at 3:7--11, repeats the
opening at 3:15, and expounds it again through 4:1--11;
but \textbf{the formulary appoints v.~1 and no more, and the appointed text does
not say that its single verse carries the whole psalm's warning.} And the same
Clementine renders the quotation differently in the two places --- Ps.\ 94:9 has
\latin{in irritatione} where Heb.\ 3:8 quoting it has \latin{in exacerbatione} ---
\textbf{so the Vulgate's psalter and its Hebrews do not agree verbally.}

\textbf{The Alleluia is the least lexically integrated element of the
formulary.} Its only
substantive tie to any other element is \latin{salutári} --- \textbf{and that
tie is to three composed orations rather than to a lesson}; \latin{exsult-} and
\latin{iubil-} occur nowhere else in the ten elements, and it shares no other
content word beyond \latin{Dominus} and \latin{Deus}. \textbf{No lexical warrant joins the
Alleluia's joy to the Gospel's anxiety}: the Epistle's \latin{gaudium} and the
Alleluia's \latin{exsultémus}
are different roots in different elements with nothing joining them, and none
of the expositors above reads the one as the resolution of the other.

\textbf{Cassiodorus and Theodoret disagree about the
one word the chant contributes.} \textbf{Cassiodorus, in Latin at PL~70}, reads
\latin{iubilémus} as the psalm's
own invitation to the \textbf{jubilus} --- wordless praise beyond syllables ---
and takes \latin{Venite} through Is.~29:13, a people that draws near with the
mouth; he also glosses \latin{salutari} plainly of salvation, which quietly
underlines how little else the verse carries. \textbf{Theodoret, in Greek at PG~80}, hears in the same
verb \textbf{the shout of victors}, an epinician: the praise of those who have
already conquered, and he composes the psalm in the person of King Josiah
foreseeing his reform, from 4~Kgs 22--23 --- \textbf{his own reconstruction and
not what the psalm states}; he also reports the psalm \textbf{untitled among
the Hebrews}, against the Clementine's printed title. \textbf{They disagree
about what \latin{iubilémus} means.} \textbf{The \latin{Deo salutári nostro}
resonance:} the collocation is rare, and its only New Testament occurrence is
the Magnificat's \latin{Deus salutari meo}.

\textbf{Four further readers of this psalm stand.} \textbf{A homily of the
Chrysostomic corpus} (PG 55:615--618, \textbf{marked spurious in Migne's apparatus, so
the corpus and not Chrysostom carries it}) makes the psalm a
summons to repentance and confession rather than to festal praise ---
\latin{Metanoias gar ho paron bios, apeiles de kai kriseos he mellousa
parousia} --- proved from Hezekiah turning to the wall and from the Ninevites,
whose sackcloth prevailed where purple, diadem and throne did not.
\textbf{Arnobius the Younger} (PL 53:462D--463C): \latin{exsultemus non in
delectationibus vanis, sed in Domino; antequam praeoccupemur, nos praeoccupemus
faciem eius in confessione peccatorum nostrorum}, with a warning against
attending to Christ \latin{in forma servi} as though he were a mere man.
\textbf{Bruno the Carthusian} (\emph{attributed}, PL 152:1142A--1143A): the
title is \latin{non est David, sed Christi}; the \latin{Venite} addresses
unbelieving Jews of the sixth age; \latin{iubilo, id est nimia et intensa
interiori laetitia exsulto}; and the following half-verse is read twice, once
from court manners and once eschatologically, \latin{Secundus autem adventus
recte facies dicitur, eo quod tunc cunctis Christus manifestabitur}.
\textbf{Peter Lombard} (PL 191:875A--876A) allots the appointed verse's three
verbs to three faculties --- \latin{Venite, fide et exsultemus mente, Domino,
non in saeculo, Iubilemus voce, Deo salutari nostro Iesu} --- \textbf{which is
his own arrangement and is a reading of the chant's own words} --- while
carrying the classical jubilus formula at exactly the appointed verb,
\latin{Gaudium sit tantum, quod verbis dici non possit, quod tamen intus
conceptum, vox extra testetur}. \textbf{His sigla attribute the surrounding
material to Augustine, Cassiodorus, Haymo and Alcuin, so that formula is almost
certainly Cassiodorus's wordless jubilus carried forward and not a third
account.}

\textbf{All four read the psalm penitentially, and every one of them reaches
that reading through v.~2, \latin{Praeoccupemus faciem eius in confessione},
which the Alleluia does not appoint} --- Bruno's turning on the very word
\latin{faciem} the chant never sings. \textbf{The convergence is about the psalm
and not about the chant, which stops at v.~1}, the same limit that stands at the
Introit for Augustine and Cassiodorus.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{\work{Expositiones in Psalmos} under Athanasius's name, \work{Patrologia
Graeca} 27:413--414.} \textbf{It glosses the appointed verb technically, as the
\emph{alalagmos} --- the shout raised over fallen enemies}: \emph{ho gar
alalagmos hymnos estin epi peptokosin echthrois}, and the psalm therefore
signifies that \textbf{Christ has warred down the opposing powers on our
behalf and put them out of the way}, so that the singing is willed and not
merely commanded. \latin{Veníte} it reads as calling the disobedient to a feast.
\textbf{This is that collection's settled sense of the word and not an
improvisation at this psalm}, recurring at three further places in the same
volume; and it stands beside Theodoret's shout of victors rather than under it.
The authenticity of these \work{Expositiones} is contested, so the reading is
the collection's and not Athanasius's.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{And Augustine, on the appointed verse itself, draws a moral distinction
from it that the penitential readers do not.} In the \work{Nicene and
Post-Nicene Fathers} English: ``if there were not in this life a wicked joy
which is to be distinguished from a righteous joy, it would be enough to say,
Come, let us rejoice; but he has briefly distinguished it. What is it to rejoice
aright? To rejoice in the Lord.'' He is not one of the medieval and later
readers named above and he is not penitential here. \textbf{That is not joy
against anxiety but right joy against wrong joy} --- the same disjunctive shape
as the Gospel's two masters and the Gradual's two objects of trust.

\textbf{The Alleluia is the least stable element of this formulary in the older
chant books.} In the Roman Mass antiphonary the printed text gives
\latin{Hebdomada XIV} the 1962 Alleluia, while its footnotes report Compiègne
with \latin{Confitemini Domino} and the Theodericensian codices with
\latin{Quoniam Deus magnus} at that printed place --- which, under the
\emph{$\pm1$} disturbance in that book's own week-numbering, is
not established as Theodericus's own fourteenth week; across that stretch the
printed Alleluia series
agrees with 1962 at two weeks of twelve, and at both the collated manuscripts
disagree with the printed text. \textbf{So the agreement at this Sunday is
evidence about the editor's exemplar rather than about the ninth-century
state}, and those footnotes are an editor's reports of manuscript readings and
not transcriptions.

No later use of this chant's wording can be tied to Ps.~94:1 at all: the King
James wording of the verse stands at five psalms --- Pss.\ 66:1, 81:1, 95:1--2,
98:4, 98:6 and 100:1 --- and every located use is a music programme or a
service listing. \textbf{The single
appointed verse is also the opening of the daily Invitatory}, which puts any
candidate for this element beyond identification by wording.

\subsection*{Gospel \cue{Gosp.}}

\elementid{Nemo potest duóbus dóminis servíre}{\latin{Sequéntia sancti Evangélii secúndum Matthǽum} --- Mt. 6, 24-33}{1577}

\subsubsection*{Where the lesson stands in Matthew 6}

\textbf{The pericope is cut out of a continuous argument at both ends.} It
begins bare at v.~24, with no connective particle, immediately after the
treasure and eye sayings (\latin{Ubi enim est thesaurus tuus, ibi est et cor
tuum}, v.~21), and stops at v.~33 without v.~34, the passage's own conclusion.
\latin{Sollícitus} governs the discourse four times, at vv.~25, 28, 31 and 34,
and \textbf{the pericope carries three of them, ending on the promise of v.~33
rather than on the restriction to today.} That is also what lets the Communion take the
Gospel's last verse as its own antiphon.

\textbf{The pericope contains the second half of a repetition internal to
Matthew 6 and not the first.} The Father's foreknowledge of need at v.~32,
\latin{Scit enim Pater vester}, restates v.~8, which stands in the instruction on
prayer the pericope does not include --- and the Lord's Prayer stands between the
two members, asking for the kingdom's coming and for bread. Matthew supplies
that proximity; nothing in the appointed formulary points to it.

\textbf{Both sayings have Lucan parallels in different settings, and Luke orders
the material the other way round.} Luke places the two-masters saying at 16:13,
after the unjust steward, and adds \latin{servus}; Matthew has it inside the
Sermon without \latin{servus}. Luke's \latin{corvos} and \latin{cellarium, neque
horreum} differ from Matthew's \latin{volatília cæli} and \latin{hórrea}. And the
ordering is reversed: in Matthew treasure-and-heart precedes the anxiety
discourse and in Luke it follows --- \textbf{which bears directly on whether
Mt.\ 6:24 is heard as the close of the treasure unit or the head of the anxiety
unit.} The appointed pericope's boundary at v.~24 takes it as a head.

\subsubsection*{What the commentators actually argue}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{De sermone Domini in monte} II.47--58, continuous
direct commentary on the appointed pericope.} \textbf{He reads v.~24's asymmetry as deliberate:}
Christ says of the second master \latin{contemnet} and not \latin{odio habebit}
because, in the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers} English, ``almost no one's
conscience can hate God; but he despises, i.e.\ he
does not fear Him, as if feeling himself secure in consideration of His
goodness.'' \textbf{The move is a claim about which of two verbs the text uses,
and the appointed Latin preserves exactly that contrast --- but its premise is
the sentence Augustine afterwards withdrew}, at \work{Retractationes} I.19,
naming this very passage. \textbf{The observation about the two verbs stands on
the appointed text and the reason he gave for it does not stand on his own
later judgment}, and both are printed below. \textbf{Two things in the same
locus are his own:} the remark that
``mammon'' is Punic for gain, which no Semitic authority among the sources
above confirms;
and the importation of ``the prince of this world'' from Jn.\ 12:31 to identify
the second master, \textbf{which the appointed verse does not say.}
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Jerome, \work{Commentarii in Matthaeum}, on the appointed verses.}
\textbf{He draws the distinction on which any non-ascetical reading of the
Gospel depends:} \latin{non dixit, qui habet divitias, sed qui servit divitiis.
Qui enim divitiarum servus est, divitias custodit, ut servus; qui autem
servitutis excussit iugum, distribuit eas, ut dominus} --- \textbf{Aquinas
repeats the same distinction in almost the same words}, \latin{aliud est habere
divitias ut dominus, aliud ut servus}, and whether he drew on Jerome is not
established. \textbf{He states the limit against over-reading in one sentence
anchored inside the appointed bounds:} \latin{labor exercendus est,
sollicitudo tollenda} (ad v.~25), with \latin{De praesentibus ergo concessit
debere esse sollicitos qui futura prohibet cogitare} at vv.~31ff --- \textbf{the
second of the two standing inside the appointed bounds} --- and a third
independent Father setting the same limit. \textbf{He expressly rebuts
allegorising the birds}, naming those who read them as angels and ministering
powers, and refutes them from the \emph{a fortiori} itself: \latin{Si hoc ita
est, ut intelligi volunt, quomodo sequitur dictum ad homines: Nonne vos magis
pluris estis illis? Simpliciter ergo accipiendum} --- \textbf{a ground internal
to the appointed verse}.
\textbf{His comment on the lilies is aesthetic, not allegorical:}
\latin{quod sericum, quae regum purpura, quae pictura textricum potest floribus
comparari?} \textbf{He gives the other etymology of \latin{mammonae}:}
\latin{Mammona sermone Syriaco divitiae nuncupantur} --- \textbf{two Fathers,
two eastern languages, and no Semitic authority beside them; what stands is
their disagreement}. \textbf{And he records a variant the missal does not
print}, \latin{In nonnullis codicibus additum est neque quid bibatis} at
v.~25, the missal's shorter form being the one he treats as his own.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Hilary, \work{Commentarius in Matthaeum}, on the appointed verses.}
\textbf{He is the Latin tradition's allegorist at this pericope, and he argues
for it:} the literal sense does not cohere with what precedes ---
\latin{Quae consequuntur, non satis propositionibus congruunt. Numquid et
unius domini servus non possit circa vestem esse cibumque sollicitus?} --- and
he reads the whole pericope as doctrine about the resurrection of the body,
allegorising every appointed image: \textbf{birds the unclean spirits; the
cubit the risen body raised to the measure of the perfect man (Eph.~4:13);
lilies the brightness of the angels; the hay burnt tomorrow the gentiles.}
\textbf{His hay-as-gentiles reading ends in a bodily eternity of the damned no
other witness states}, and the birds-as-unclean-spirits reading is the one
place where his allegory works \emph{against} the pericope's consoling sense.
\textbf{At the verse the Mass lifts out he reads plainly}, grounding
\latin{Haec enim omnia gentes inquirunt} in unbelief rather than appetite ---
\latin{Gentium igitur est, infidelitatis istius cura angi} --- \textbf{the one
place where his resurrection reading and the plain sense converge}; his
exposition runs on to v.~34, which the lesson excludes. \textbf{His lemma lacks
the last step of Chrysostom's escalation}, reading \latin{cras in ignem
mittitur} where the missal prints \latin{cras in clíbanum} --- \textbf{the step
Chrysostom's rhetoric turns on is not in every ancient Latin witness}, and the
medieval Gloss knows the variant, \latin{Alii libri habent in ignem, vel in
acervum, qui habet speciem clibani}. \textbf{He is not refuted by Jerome:}
Jerome's target is a reading of the \emph{birds} as angels, Hilary's angels are
the \emph{lilies}, and that Jerome names Hilary among the Latin commentators he
had read is not evidence that he has him in view here.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Thomas Aquinas, \work{Super Matthaeum} cap.~6 lect.~3--5.}
\textbf{He fastens on the other
member of the same four-verb figure Augustine reads, and explains it from the
same asymmetry:} \latin{Et non dicit, diliget, quia Diabolus naturaliter diligi
non potest. Deus naturaliter diligitur; Diabolus vero sustinetur} ---
\textbf{complementary to Augustine's \latin{contemnet} observation, and both
checkable against the appointed Latin, which prints all four verbs.} He reads
the whole saying as a claim about ultimate ends, \latin{impossibile est quod
animus feratur ad duos fines simul et semel}, and \textbf{on the cubit he reads
an experimental proof of providence}: \latin{Hic probat experimento, quod sicut
providet avibus, ita et nobis \dots\ Unde augmentum non est nobis ex nobis, sed
ex Deo: unde de providentia Dei non debetis desperare.} The measure of a man's
stature is not in his own power, so growth is from God and not from ourselves,
and the anxiety the pericope forbids is on that ground demonstrably vain.
\textbf{He
independently names the sect Augustine's controversy leaves unnamed:}
\latin{destruit hic error Euticharum, qui dicebant quod viri apostolici non
debebant laborare}, answered from 2~Thess.~3:10 --- \textbf{which does not
settle the identification}: \work{De haeresibus} is the work that would name
them, and no text of it stands behind the name. \latin{Euticharum} is
the text's spelling and is reproduced as printed. \textbf{And \emph{Super
Matthaeum} ascribes the lilies-as-angels reading to Jerome}; the reading is
\textbf{Hilary's},
and the \work{Catena aurea} --- Thomas's own compilation --- attributes it
correctly to \latin{Hilarius in Matth.} \textbf{The slip is most likely the reporter's}
--- \work{Super Matthaeum} is a \emph{reportatio} --- \textbf{and belongs to
that text and not to Thomas.}
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, on the birds and the lilies.} \textbf{He explicitly forbids allegorising them}, in the \work{Nicene and
Post-Nicene Fathers} English: ``these examples are not to be
treated as allegories, so that we should inquire what the fowls of heaven or the
lilies of the field mean: for they stand here, in order that from smaller
matters we may be persuaded respecting greater ones,'' with the unjust judge as
his comparison case. \textbf{The Latin tradition is namedly divided at these
images} --- Hilary allegorises them, Jerome and Augustine forbid it --- and one
of the two principal Latin Fathers commenting on this very pericope says its
central images are not to be taken allegorically. His restraint is specific: he
bars
allegorising \emph{these examples}, not the pericope as a whole, and he is at
the same time willing to read ``seek first the kingdom'' as a doctrine of
ordered ends.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{John Chrysostom, \work{Homiliae in Matthaeum} 21--22.} \textbf{He reads the illustrations as rhetorically
engineered.} The birds are chosen \emph{because} they are irrational,
deliberately in place of Moses, Elijah or John --- in the \work{Nicene and
Post-Nicene Fathers} English, ``that He might touch them more
to the quick \dots\ He has cut off from them every excuse''; and the lilies are
progressively degraded --- lilies, then ``grass of the field,'' then ``which
today is,'' then ``cast into the oven'' --- so the \emph{a fortiori} grows at
every step. \textbf{The appointed Latin fits word for word:} \latin{fænum agri,
quod hódie est, et cras in clíbanum míttitur \dots\ quanto magis vos módicæ
fidei?}, and he calls the rebuke ``not want, but poverty, of faith.''
\textbf{Read now in the Greek at \work{Patrologia Graeca} 57:293, 299--300 and
303 --- homily XXI opens exactly at this Gospel's first verse --- he refuses a
different over-reading three times over}: what Christ abolished is not the
sowing and the spinning but the anxiety about them, \emph{ou toinyn to me
merimnan tauta ponos, alla to merimnan}, \emph{ou ton sporon anetrepsen, alla
ten phrontida}, \emph{ou to ergon aneilen, alla ten merimnan}. On
\latin{nec Sálomon in omni glória sua} he holds that Solomon was outdone not
once or twice but \emph{di' holes tes basileias autou}, through his whole reign.
\textbf{He cites Scripture loosely}, and Migne's apparatus records manuscript
disagreement at neighbouring words, so his short citations are evidence about
his citation habit and not a controlled witness to a Gospel text-form.
\textbf{And homily 22 carries the alternative number XXIII in its own running
head}, so the column is the surer address for it.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Thomas Aquinas, \work{Summa theologiae} II-II q.~55 a.~6 and q.~19
aa.~2--3, in the English of the Fathers of the English Dominican Province.}
\textbf{He expounds this pericope systematically.} Solicitude about temporal
things is unlawful in three ways, the third \textbf{``through over much fear,
when, to wit, a man fears to lack necessary things if he do what he ought to
do''}; and our Lord gives three motives for laying aside that fear ---
\textbf{the greater gifts already given, the care God takes of animals and
plants without man's assistance, and divine providence itself, ``through
ignorance of which the gentiles are solicitous in seeking temporal goods before
all others''} --- which read as ascending. \textbf{At q.~19 he divides fear by
its relation to God} into worldly, servile, filial and initial, holding that
``worldly love is, properly speaking, the love whereby a man trusts in the world
as his end \dots\ for which reason worldly fear is always evil,'' while allowing
regard for what is divine in the creature. \textbf{That is a reading of the
Gospel's \latin{gentes} and of its \latin{nolíte sollíciti esse} together.}
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{Where both Fathers comment they agree the images are arguments and not
figures, and they differ in what the argument is.} Augustine grounds the
inference in the \textbf{rank} of the creature --- in the English of the
\work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, first series, ``a rational being such as
man has a higher rank in the nature of things than irrational ones.'' Chrysostom
grounds it in the \textbf{vileness} of the creature and in the deliberate
rhetorical descent, and gives the anthropological ground in terms of \emph{gift}
rather than rank, in the English of the same series: ``you, to whom He gave a
soul, for whom He fashioned a body
\dots\ for whose sake He gave up His only begotten Son.'' \textbf{The difference
is not a contradiction, but the middle terms differ and the two yield different
homiletic conclusions from the same verses:} the argument from lesser to greater
is common to both Fathers, and the term that carries it is not.

\textbf{Both Fathers independently set a limit against over-reading the
pericope.} Augustine at II.57--58: Christ kept money bags, Paul worked with his
hands, the church at Antioch stored food against Agabus's famine, and the whole
precept reduces to the rule, in the English of the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene
Fathers}, first series, ``that even in looking after such things we should
think of the kingdom of God, but in the service of the kingdom of God we should
not think of such things.'' Chrysostom reaches the same limit by a different
route on v.~26, in the English of the same series: ``He said not, we must not
sow, but we must not take thought;
neither that one ought not to work, but not to be low-minded.'' \textbf{Where the
claim must rest inside the appointed bounds, Chrysostom's sentence is the better
one}, because Augustine's material there is anchored partly in v.~34, which this
lesson excludes, while Chrysostom's is anchored in v.~26, which it includes.

\subsubsection*{The Latin transmission of this Gospel, and the sentence its author withdrew}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{Retractationes} I.19, PL 32:617.} \textbf{He withdraws the sentence the Latin tradition repeats most often at this
Gospel's first verse:} \latin{Item quod dixi, Nullius enim fere conscientia Deum
potest odisse (Lib.~2, c.~14, n.~48), non video fuisse dicendum. Multi enim sunt
de quibus scriptum est: Superbia eorum qui oderunt te (Psal.\ LXXIII, 23).}
\textbf{The self-citation fixes the work as \work{De sermone Domini in monte}
II.14.48}, his exposition of \latin{aut unum sustinébit, et álterum contémnet}
--- \textbf{so the strongest single patristic move on this Gospel is one its
author afterwards refused.} The sentence stands as the \work{Retractationes} quotes
it and not as \work{De serm. Dom.} prints it.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{Five later Latin witnesses repeat the withdrawn sentence, and one denies
it outright.} Rabanus, the Glossa, Christian of Stavelot, Paschasius and the
exposition printed under Bede's name all carry it \textbf{without Augustine's
qualifier \latin{fere}}; \textbf{the Glossa alone flags the retraction};
\textbf{Rabanus prints \latin{ferre} for \latin{fere} and pseudo-Bede prints
\latin{Nullius enim scientia Deum odisse ferre potest}}, and \latin{scientia}
for \latin{conscientia} cannot be anyone's theology. \textbf{The pattern is
transmission}, and not five authors each hardening Augustine on his own account. And \textbf{Bruno of Segni contradicts the proposition
from Scripture}: \latin{Quis enim servit mammonae, nisi avarus? Avarus igitur
Deum contemnit et odio habet. Unde hoc? Quia idolis servit}, from Eph.\ 5:5.
\textbf{So ``no one can hate God'' is neither the tradition's settled reading
at Mt.\ 6:24 nor Augustine's own last word on it.}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Christian of Stavelot (Druthmarus), \work{Expositio in Matthaeum}
cap.~XIII, PL 106:1316C--1319A.} \textbf{The most
literal-minded of the Latin witnesses above.} He answers the objection that
Paul commands \emph{serve one another} by the Greek distinction of
\emph{latreusis} and \emph{douleusis}, quoting both words in Greek characters;
identifies the two masters as the devil and God; glosses mammon as Syriac for
riches, with \latin{Aliud est habere divitias, aliud servire divitiis};
\textbf{reads the lilies with no allegory at all}, on the bare ground that no
linen matches the lily's whiteness, no dye the rose, no purple the violet;
explains Solomon as the example because the Jews knew no richer king though
richer men existed; and supplies the elided
noun at Mt.\ 6:33 with its limit, \latin{haec, subauditur necessaria,
adiicientur \dots\ Ad necessitatem, non ad superfluitatem}.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Paschasius Radbertus, \work{Expositio in Matthaeum}, PL 120:306B--307A
and 312C--313A.} \textbf{He grounds the
impossibility of two masters in the baptismal renunciation} --- no one enters
the font until he renounces the devil, all his works and all his pomps --- and
calls the relapsed Christian a foolish soldier throwing away immortal arms.
\textbf{He excepts the Trinity from the rule}, \latin{Pater, et Filius, et
Spiritus sanctus, non tres Domini}, because service fails only where a
discordant will intervenes. \textbf{And at Mt.\ 6:33 he denies that
\latin{primum} marks order at all:} \latin{Primum autem, non ad ordinem
referendum puto \dots\ magis ad quantitatem referendum est, ut extra hoc nihil
quaerendum sit}, calling the sequence \latin{praeposterus} and explaining that
the kingdom was put first to soothe the hearing before justice was brought in as
the laborious thing, and reading the promise through use and enjoyment,
\latin{his utamur in via, quatenus illo fruamur in patria}. \textbf{So the word
the Communion antiphon promotes to its head is contested, and Paschasius stands
against the Augustine the compilations transmit.}
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Rabanus Maurus, on the appointed verses, PL 107:836B--839A.}
\textbf{At these verses he compiles rather than composes.} The material runs
\latin{(Hier.)} for the mammon gloss and the flowers and \latin{(Aug.)} for the
two masters and for \latin{Quaerite primum}, with \textbf{one} sentence of his
own marked \latin{(Maur.)}. The Augustine he transmits supplies
the sharpest surviving formula for the Communion's word --- \latin{Cum enim
dicit illud primum, significavit quod hoc posterius quaerendum est, non tempore,
sed dignitate} --- \textbf{but that sentence stands inside his compilation and
Migne's sigla are editorial.}
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Glossa ordinaria on Matthew, PL 114:105A--107A.} It reports mammon as also the name of a demon set over riches ---
\textbf{and reports it as hearsay, \latin{Dicitur hoc nomen esse daemonis}};
grounds the two masters in baptism exactly
as Paschasius does; \textbf{records a variant at Mt.\ 6:30}, \latin{Alii libri
habent: in ignem vel in acervum qui habet speciem clibani}; and \textbf{alone of
the compilations flags that Augustine withdrew the sentence about hating God.}
\textbf{The variant is a fact about the Gospel text, and no Latin witness here
settles whether any book actually prints \latin{in ignem}.}
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{The Matthew exposition printed under Bede's name, PL 92:34D--36A.}
It raises the question whether bodily food belongs
to the soul and answers it by taking \latin{anima} at Mt.\ 6:25 as this present
life, \latin{cuius retinaculum est alimentum istud corporeum}; carries the
anti-allegorical line \latin{Ista vero documenta de avibus et liliis non
allegorice ponuntur}; and \textbf{reads the Communion's own verb precisely},
\latin{Non dixit nobis dari, sed adiici, quia nobis in intentione aeternitas, in
usu vero debet esse temporalitas}.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Bruno of Segni, \work{Commentaria in Matthaeum}, PL 165:121B--123C.}
\textbf{The sharpest counter-witness
at this Gospel.} Besides denying that the covetous man can fail to hate God,
he raises a literal objection nobody else here raises --- a man plainly
\emph{can} serve many masters, \latin{non solum duobus dominis, sed et multis
dominis servire non est impossibile}, which is why the verse must be read of
these two in particular; defines serving riches as fearing to spend them; reads
the lilies literally; and \textbf{restricts the whole prohibition of anxiety to
apostles, their successors and those aiming at perfection.} \textbf{So his exposition is
addressed to a narrower audience than a Sunday congregation.} His own
\work{Expositio in Psalmos} reaches all four appointed psalms, and no reading of
them here is his.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Godfrey of Admont, \work{Homilia} LXXXIII, PL 174:580C--583D --- his
Fifteenth Sunday, and the far end of the Latin division.} He
takes the whole prohibition of anxiety as concerning \textbf{spiritual} food and
clothing, not visible food, drink or dress but the invisible nourishment and
clothing of soul and spirit, so that \latin{quid manducetis} asks what taste of
contemplation one may have; widens mammon past riches to whatever a man sets his
heart on and loves more than God; makes the kingdom to be
sought first the heart, which ought always to be God's kingdom;
makes \textbf{the birds the good angels}, who neither sow by reading nor reap by
taking the fruit of reading nor gather into barns by recollecting it into
memory; makes \textbf{the lilies the evil spirits}, who do not spin because a
spinner draws the thread from left to right and they draw everything they do the
other way; and makes \textbf{Solomon Christ}, Solomon's name being interpreted
the peaceable one. \textbf{He raises the objection
his own allegory creates and answers it, so he is a self-aware instance of the
allegorising side and not a naive one.} \textbf{His spiritual-food reading depends on
the pericope running past Mt.\ 6:33, where this lesson stops, and he is
preaching to monks about \emph{lectio} and not to a Sunday congregation.}
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{So the Latin division at the images is sharper than a two-way split.}
On one side the Augustine the compilations transmit, pseudo-Bede repeating him,
Christian of Stavelot refusing allegory on aesthetic grounds and Bruno of Segni
reading the lilies literally; on the other Godfrey, allegorising every image and
defending the allegory against his own objection. \textbf{That is not a
difference of emphasis.} Godfrey's proof text for the pride of the
fallen angels is Ps.~73:23, \textbf{the same verse Augustine gives in the
\work{Retractationes} as his reason for withdrawing the sentence about hating
God.}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{De opere monachorum} §27, §30, §31 --- a
\emph{controversy} over these verses.} \textbf{This runs against the obvious
devotional reading.} Certain monks quoted these very verses to argue that they
need not work, and Augustine wrote against them, in the \work{Nicene and
Post-Nicene Fathers} English: ``let these persons, who from
perverse understanding of the Gospel labor to pervert apostolic precepts, either
take no thought for the morrow, even as the birds of the air; or let them obey
the Apostle''; ``against the Apostle of Christ, they recite a Gospel of Christ.'' The
identification of those monks as the Euchitae rests on the
\latin{Augustinus de Haeres.} citation; \work{De haeresibus} is the work that
would settle it, and no text of it stands behind the name.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{Four of the \work{Catena aurea}'s attributions on this Gospel hold
against the underlying works and the rest do not.} The appointed Gospel occupies
cap.~6 lect.~16--21, drawing on Augustine, Jerome, Hilary, Chrysostom, Gregory,
Rabanus, Remigius and the Gloss. \textbf{The Catena cites \latin{Chrysostomus
in Matth.} and \latin{Chrysostomus super Matth.} as different things:} the
first is the genuine \work{Homiliae in Matthaeum}, sentence for
sentence Homily 22's own text; \textbf{the second is
the anonymous \work{Opus imperfectum in Matthaeum}, a fifth-century work with
Arian features whose attribution to Chrysostom is universally rejected.}
\textbf{So a citation of ``Chrysostom'' on Mt.\ 6:33 drawn from the
\latin{super Matth.} formula names a Doctor of the Church for a text that is not
his.}
\textbf{Four of its attributions hold against the underlying works} --- the
Chrysostom of lect.~19, the \work{De opere monachorum} of lect.~17, and
\textbf{every \latin{Hilarius} and \latin{Hieronymus} excerpt on the appointed
Gospel, sentence for sentence what those works themselves carry}, which the
Catena reproduces faithfully. Those excerpts stand against the same
Migne recensions the Catena drew on, so what agrees is the transmission and not
the Migne text's own correctness. \textbf{No work underlying its Gregory or its Remigius stands beside them}, so
each of those two attributions rests on the Catena alone. \textbf{Rabanus and the Gloss stand
directly at Migne above}, where what they are is visible --- compilations whose
sigla are editorial.

\subsection*{Offertory \cue{Off.}}

\elementid{Immíttet Angelus Dómini}{\latin{Ant. ad Offertorium} --- Ps. 33, 8-9}{1578}

\textbf{The psalm is anchored by its own superscription to a named deliverance
narrative --- and the narrative does not fit the name.} \latin{Davidi, cum
immutavit vultum suum coram Achimelech}. The Douay prints the
cross-reference in the text as ``[1 Kings 21.],'' which in Douay book-naming is
modern \textbf{1~Samuel~21}. \textbf{Read whole, that chapter shows Achimelech
the priest at Nobe in vv.~1--9 and the change of countenance at v.~13 before
Achis, king of Geth}; the CPDV reads \latin{Abimelech} at the
superscription. \textbf{No witness here explains the divergence}, and the standard scholarly
explanation of it lies outside them all. It bears materially, because Augustine's
Eucharistic reading below is anchored in David \emph{before Achis} while the
Latin superscription names Achimelech. So the angel who encamps stands in a
psalm whose superscription reads it off a concrete escape, and \latin{eripiet
eos} at v.~8 repeats the \latin{eripuit me} of v.~5. \textbf{The antiphon stops
one clause short of the psalm's beatitude.}

\textbf{Cassiodorus reports the psalm's alphabetic acrostic and locates the
appointed verses within it.} His \latin{Divisio psalmi}
states it: \latin{Per totum psalmum
verba prophetae sunt, alphabeti Hebraei litteras, excepta sexta, in capitibus
versuum per ordinem ponentis}, tagging every verse with its letter ---
\textbf{HETH at the Offertory's first verse and TETH at its second} --- and on
the skipped letter, \latin{Ego enim non inveni Patrum definitam de hac parte
sententiam}. He is a sixth-century Latin reader
reporting about a Hebrew text he does not print, so
what stands is his report and no statement about the Hebrew itself. Bellarmine
notes the same structure, but at vv.~2--3, reaching
no appointed verse, and in an abridged Victorian English translation.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{Enarratio in Ps. XXXIII} \textbf{Sermo II}.}
\textbf{The passage stands in Sermo~II and is absent from Sermo~I entirely.}
\textbf{He rejects a rival reading and defends the one the
missal prints}, in the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers} English: ``So it is
written, brethren, not as some bad copies have it,
\emph{The Lord shall send His Angel round about them that fear Him} \dots\ but
thus, \emph{The Angel of the Lord shall send round about them that fear Him}.''
He then identifies that Angel as Christ, ``the Angel of the great Counsel.'' The
1962 Offertory prints \latin{Immíttet Angelus Dómini} --- \latin{Angelus Dómini}
nominative subject of \latin{immíttet} --- \textbf{the very reading he defends.}
His Latin runs: \latin{mendosi codices: Immittet Angelum Dominus \dots\ sed
sic: Immitte[t] Angelus Domini \dots} \textbf{And at §11 he reads
\latin{gustáte et vidéte} of the Eucharist}, ``the same Sacrament, whereby He
was carried in His Own Hands,'' against Jn.\ 6:52--53.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{The printed books carry a three-way split at that verb and subject, and
it changes the sense.} The 1962 typical edition's own page
reads \latin{Immíttet Angelus Dómini in circúitu timéntium eum}; the Roman Mass
antiphonary at \work{Patrologia Latina} 78 reads \latin{Immittet angelum
Dominus}; and Legg's Sarum reads \latin{Inmittit angelus domini}, Dickinson's
\latin{Immittit angelus Domini}. \textbf{\latin{Immittet angelum Dominus} makes
the Lord send an angel; \latin{Immíttet Angelus Dómini} makes the angel of the
Lord encamp; and the Sarum present tense makes it a standing fact rather than a
promise.} \textbf{Which reading each reception witness had before him is not
established, and it matters for every reading that turns on the angel's
identity.}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Basil of Caesarea, \work{Homilia in Psalmum XXXIII} §§5--6,
\work{Patrologia Graeca} 29:363--366, read in Greek with Migne's facing Latin.}
\textbf{He expounds both halves of the appointed Offertory directly and
consecutively.} At v.~8 the single angel is worth a whole encamped army, and
\latin{in circuitu} is a city wall: \latin{Quemadmodum enim urbium muri in
circuitu omni ex parte circumducti, undecunque hostium assultus arcent: ita
etiam angelus et praemunit a fronte, et a tergo custodit} --- \textbf{and that
Latin is Migne's facing translation at this column and not Basil's own Greek}.
He warns that grave
sin drives the angel off as smoke drives bees and stench doves. At v.~9 he
grounds \latin{gustáte et vidéte} on the soul's senses being named
homonymously with the body's, and, \textbf{because the Lord is the true bread
and his flesh true food, insists the sweetness is reached by a spiritual
tasting}; \textbf{and the psalm says \emph{Taste} and not \emph{Be filled},
because the present grace is an \emph{arrhabo}} that will only later become full
fruition, with 1~Cor.\ 13:12 beside it. \textbf{Whether he means the eucharistic
species specifically or the spiritual apprehension of the Word is not settled by
the passage, and both halves of the sentence stand in it.} \textbf{Migne's
facing Latin renders his lemma at v.~8 \latin{Castra metabitur angelus Domini}},
for the Greek \emph{parembalei}, where the missal's Latin prints
\latin{Immíttet Angelus Dómini} --- \textbf{the two Latin verbs differ} ---
and \textbf{that Latin column carries \latin{angelus Domini} as its subject,
standing with the missal at the disputed noun}. \textbf{The remainder of v.~8
and the whole of v.~9a stand word for word as the missal prints them.}
Only §§5--6 reach the appointed
verses, and Migne's text of them stands beside no critical edition of his psalm
homilies.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{\work{Selecta in Psalmos} under Origen's name, \work{Patrologia Graeca}
12:1307--1308.} At v.~8 it carries \textbf{the earlier form of Basil's own
gloss} --- \emph{ho angelos tou Kyriou holes paremboles logon epechei heis on},
the one angel holding the place of a whole encampment. \textbf{At v.~9 it gives
a different account from Basil's}: \emph{ei geustos estin ho Kyrios, dia tes
pisteos esti geustos; kai ei chrestos, dia tes gnoseos esti chrestos} --- tasted
through faith, found good through knowledge. At v.~10 it raises the objection
the verse invites, from Heb.\ 11:37--38, and answers that those who lacked in
food and drink did not lack in knowledge. The wording is close enough and the
collection early enough that Basil is plainly developing an inherited gloss
rather than inventing one, but a catena's attributions cannot establish the
direction of dependence. The
faith-and-knowledge antithesis and the appeal to \emph{gnosis} are the Evagrian
idiom, and the attribution caution recorded at the Introit governs here too.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{Augustine's text reads \latin{et ERUET eos} where the missal prints
\latin{et ERIPIET eos}} --- \textbf{so the two agree exactly on the point in
dispute, which noun is the subject, and differ on the following verb}, and the
missal's text is therefore not simply Augustine's. What stands between them is a
convergence at the disputed word: the appointed text and a Father's defended
reading agree on which noun governs the verb. \textbf{The missal's capital
\latin{Angelus} is an orthographic fact}, and no textual history joins the two
readings by descent.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Cassiodorus, \work{Expositio in Psalterium}, at Ps.~33 vv.~8--9.}
\textbf{His lemma reads \latin{Immittet angelum Dominus} --- exactly the
reading Augustine denounced as belonging to faulty copies.} \textbf{So the
variant Augustine fought was still standard in the principal medieval Latin
psalm commentary, and the 1962 Offertory follows Augustine against
Cassiodorus} --- a convergence and not a descent, nothing tracing
transmission. \textbf{He does not identify the angel with Christ}: he reads
\latin{Immittet} as deliberately covert --- \latin{propter insolentiam humanae fragilitatis non
palam facit, sed occultis immissionibus operatur} --- and turns to the moral
sense, \latin{si vis te angelum fieri, fac quod praecipit \dots\ Tunc enim
spiritu angeli sumus, quando ministri supernae voluntatis efficimur}.
\textbf{He is positive evidence that a Latin reader could take the verse
otherwise.}
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Cassiodorus, on \latin{gustáte et vidéte} in the same exposition.}
\textbf{His eucharistic reading is anchored in the
appointed verbs themselves, where Augustine's is anchored in the superscription
the antiphon does not print:} \latin{Gustate non pertinet ad palatum, sed
animae suavissimum sensum, qui divina contemplatione saginatur. Nam ut ipsum
gustum intelligeres, sequitur, videte, quod utique non ad os pertinet} --- and
he reads it eucharistically from the verb, citing Jn.~6:53 and the life-giving
flesh taken from the Virgin. \textbf{So the reading is available to a hearer of
the antiphon alone}, lifting the limit Augustine's route imposes; and it
converges with Cyril of Jerusalem's ``trust not the judgment to your bodily
palate'' --- in the English of the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, second
series, vol.~7 --- reached in Latin and without dependence on the Jerusalem
catechesis.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, on \latin{gustáte et vidéte} in the same exposition.}
\textbf{He reads v.~9 as openly Eucharistic},
decoding the psalm's superscription: the David who, in the English of the
\work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, first series, ``was carried in his own
hands'' before Achish is Christ holding his own Body --- \latin{cum commendaret
ipsum corpus suum et sanguinem suum, accepit in manus suas quod norunt fideles}
--- so ``taste'' is set against the incomprehension of Jn.\ 6:52. \textbf{His
reading is anchored in the
psalm's superscription, and the Offertory antiphon prints no superscription}, so
his reasoning stands as reception of the psalm and not as
something a hearer of the antiphon alone could recover.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Arnobius the Younger (\emph{attributed}), in Ps.~XXXIII, PL
53:368C--369B.} \textbf{He makes two different claims at this verse.} The \textbf{Eucharistic} gloss:
\latin{Gustate corpus vitae, et videte quam suavis est Dominus: Vitam enim habet
in semetipso, qui manducaverit carnem eius, et biberit sanguinem eius} (Jn.~6)
--- \textbf{a third Latin witness at this verse, and unlike the other two a
psalm commentary reading the appointed verse in place rather than a
mystagogical reuse.} And the \textbf{paschal} identification of the angel:
\latin{misit angelum suum in circuitu timentium corpus eius, et revolvit lapidem
a monumento} (Mt.~28), with the poor man who cried read as Christ on the cross.
\textbf{The second depends on repointing \latin{timentium
eum} as those fearing \emph{for} his body, which is not what the missal's Latin
says}.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{And two further readers reach the angel and the verse.}
\textbf{Godfrey of Admont} (PL 174:580A--B) makes the angel the gift of grace
itself --- \latin{Angelus iste, quem Deus in circuitu timentium se immittit,
donum gratiae illius est, sine cuius munere homo nunquam videre et gustare valet
quoniam suavis est Dominus} --- his Fourteenth Sunday again not the 1962 one.
\textbf{And Prosper of Aquitaine quotes this Offertory's own words inside his
exposition of the Gradual's psalm}, \latin{ut gustemus et videamus quam suavis
est Dominus}: \textbf{a Father joining the two chants' psalms in one sentence,
which is a fact about his exposition and not about the formulary.} \textbf{So
the angel of this chant now carries four accounts --- Christ, the moral sense,
the angel of the sepulchre, and the gift of grace --- and the formulary
adjudicates none of them.}

\textbf{Two fourth-century mystagogical witnesses attest the verse as the
Church's own communion invitation, and the 1962 Mass does not use it that way.}
Cyril of Jerusalem, in the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, second
series vol.~7, reports it actually being chanted at the communion: ``ye hear
the chanter inviting you with a sacred melody to the communion of the Holy
Mysteries, and saying, O taste and see that the Lord is good. Trust not the
judgment to your bodily palate.'' Ambrose, in vol.~10 of the same series, cites it to identify what is eaten:
``Taste and see that the Lord is good, blessed is the man that hopes in Him. In
that sacrament is Christ.'' \textbf{In the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday the same verse
stands at the Offertory, before the consecration, and this Mass's Communion is a
different text entirely.} Note also
that \textbf{Ambrose quotes the verse \emph{with} the clause \latin{beatus vir
qui sperat in eo} which the Roman Offertory omits}, which sharpens rather than
weakens the observation that the antiphon cuts at \latin{Dóminus}.
\textbf{Cyril's authorship of the Mystagogical Catecheses is disputed in modern
scholarship}, and both witnesses speak at Jerusalem and Milan and not at Rome.

\textbf{And the displacement is attested as living practice in a surviving
Western rite.} The Mozarabic \work{Missale mixtum} appoints this psalm verse as
the \latin{ad accedentes} chant at communion time, cued by incipit thirty-four
times across the book. \textbf{What that book attests is the appointment
and not the text}: what it prints at those cues is not established.

\textbf{The Offertory's argument runs close to the Gospel's two verses past its
own cut.} Ps.\ 33:10--11 (\latin{Timete Dominum \dots\ Divites eguerunt, et
esurierunt; inquirentes autem Dominum non minuentur omni bono}) and
Mt.\ 6:32--33 both set seeking God against want of food and both promise that
those who seek will not lack. \textbf{The likeness is one of argument and not of
wording, and neither text quotes the other.}
The Latin verbs differ, and \textbf{the Gospel's own \latin{inquírunt} at v.~32
is used of the gentiles negatively where the psalm uses the participle
positively}, which cuts against reading it as a verbal link. \textbf{And the
appointed excerpt does not contain the psalm verses in question at all}, so the
resemblance is between the Gospel and the psalm and not between two appointed
texts.

\textbf{The chant's other Roman use.} Ps.\ 33:8--9 stands at \textbf{exactly two
Masses of the 1862 Missal}, the second appointed immediately after the
Canaanite-woman Gospel ending \latin{O mulier, magna est fides tua}.

\subsection*{Secret \cue{Sec.}}

\elementid{Concéde nobis, Dómine, quǽsumus}{\latin{Secreta}}{1579}

\textbf{One victim, two genitives.} The two \latin{et} clauses are parallel
predicates of the one subject \latin{hæc hóstia salutáris}, and the second
genitive is objective in a different way from the first: the victim is
\latin{purgátio} \emph{of our offences} and \latin{propitiátio} \emph{of thy
power} --- \textbf{the appeasing of God's power, not a power belonging to the
propitiation.} At the offering, the protection the Introit and Collect asked for
becomes propitiation. \textbf{Guéranger construes \latin{propitiátio potestátis}
the same way}, and \textbf{Schuster likewise reads \latin{tuæ propitiátio
potestátis} as the appeasing of the divine omnipotence.}

\textbf{The printed books do not agree on the word that carries it, and the
sense expounded here is read of this Sunday's own form and not of a settled
wording.} 1962 and Menard's penitential votive series at PL~78:208--209 have
\latin{potestátis}; his Lenten station at St~Chrysogonus, PL~78:74, has
\latin{Majestatis}; and Legg's Sarum has \latin{pietatis}. \latin{Hóstia}
against \latin{oblacio}, and \latin{delictórum} against \latin{peccatorum},
vary independently of that word and of each other. \textbf{The doublet the 1962 Missal
prints straddles the split}: its Passion Monday Secret is Menard's Lenten form
verbatim and this Sunday's is his votive form with \latin{delictórum} for
\latin{peccatorum}, so the two part at the address --- \latin{Domine Deus
noster} in the Lenten form against this Sunday's \latin{Dómine, quǽsumus} ---
and at the word expounded here, the Lenten form reading \latin{Majestatis}. \textbf{Four printed books --- three of
them nineteenth-century editions of medieval texts and one a modern typical
edition --- are not a critical apparatus}, and no manuscript stands behind any
of the three readings; two independently edited books agreeing at so unusual a
word is far past what a misreading of type explains. A modern registry of the
1962 calendar, and not a facsimile of that day's page, is what puts the Lenten
form at Passion Monday. \textbf{Which reading is prior, and whether the Lenten and the
Sunday forms are one prayer or two, is not established.}

\textbf{Transmission.} The Secret stands with the Collect in Gelasianum
Book~III sect.~X and has a second, penitential home at sect.~XLI --- with the
narrowing recorded above, that the second home is a feature of the Vatican
manuscript's arrangement rather than of the Gelasian tradition at large.

\textbf{No patristic or medieval exegesis of this prayer stands anywhere in the
\work{Patrologia Latina}}: \latin{propitiatio potestatis} and \latin{purificent
semper et muniant} return five occurrences between them across that whole
collection, and every one is a sacramentary printing one of the two prayers.
\textbf{Three of the five are the Secret's} --- the Gelasian at Book~III
sect.~X and at the penitential sect.~XLI, and the Gregorian at \latin{Dominica I
ante natalem Domini}, reading \latin{purgatio peccatorum} for
\latin{delictorum}. \textbf{The other two are the Postcommunion's} --- the
floating series of unassigned Postcommunions appended after the Canon at printed
p.~237, and the saint-adapted Gelasian recension for St~Andrew's vigil, reading
\latin{nos, Domine}. \textbf{What the Secret and the Postcommunion do have is
modern comment at paraphrase length:}
Schuster treats all three orations of this Mass and Guéranger gives the Secret
and the Postcommunion a sentence each. \textbf{The Collect, alone of the three,
has a medieval reception, and it is set out above.}

\subsection*{Communion \cue{Comm.}}

\elementid{Primum quǽrite regnum Dei}{\latin{Ant. ad Communionem} --- Mt. 6, 33}{1580}

\subsubsection*{The one substantive act this formulary performs on its Bible text}

\textbf{The Communion departs in exactly four ways from Mt.\ 6:33 as the
Clementine, the Lucan parallel and the Byzantine Greek carry it}: \latin{ergo}
is dropped and \latin{Primum} promoted to head the clause; \latin{et iustítiam
eius} is omitted; \latin{hæc} is dropped before \latin{ómnia}; and \latin{dicit
Dóminus} is added. \textbf{That tag stands in none of Clementine Mt.\ 6:33,
Clementine Lk.\ 12:31, or the Byzantine Greek at Mt.\ 6:33.} And \textbf{Luke,
unlike the antiphon, keeps \latin{iustitiam eius}, so the omission is not
explained by conflation with the Lucan form.}

Two of the four bear theologically. Adding \latin{dicit Dóminus} \textbf{turns a
saying inside a sermon into an address made to the communicant at the moment of
reception}; and dropping \latin{et iustítiam eius} leaves the kingdom to be
sought without the justice the Gospel names beside it.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine, \work{De sermone Domini in monte} II.53, II.55, II.56, on
\latin{primum}.} \textbf{He argues an ordering of importance and not of time}, in the
\work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers} English: ``in using that
word \emph{first}, He has indicated that this is to be sought later, not in
point of time, but in point of importance.'' \textbf{Rabanus Maurus's
compilation on these verses carries that same sentence in Latin} ---
\latin{non tempore, sed dignitate}, at \work{Patrologia Latina}
107:836B--839A under the siglum \latin{(Aug.)} --- so a compilation's Latin
answers the English, and Migne's sigla there are editorial. He makes the point by denying
the sequence the word invites, in the same English --- ``He did not say,
\emph{Then} seek such
things.'' He offers an inversion test at II.55 and ties the phrase back to
Mt.\ 6:24 at II.56.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{And the word the recasting promotes is contested.} \textbf{Paschasius
Radbertus denies that \latin{primum} marks order at all} --- \latin{non ad
ordinem referendum puto \dots\ magis ad quantitatem referendum est} --- against
the \latin{non tempore, sed dignitate} the compilations transmit under
Augustine's name. His is the earliest reading of that word recorded here.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Thomas Aquinas, \work{Super Matthaeum} cap.~6 lect.~5, on all three
members of the verse the antiphon recasts.} \textbf{He expounds the kingdom as
the end sought}, because the kingdom is beatitude and \latin{Regnum dicitur a
regendo: tunc enim homo regitur, quando voluntati regentis subditur}; the
justice as \emph{his} and not man's, \latin{quia per iustitiam propriam nullus
potest venire ad regnum}; and the rest as added over and above, \latin{quasi,
ultra forum}. He adds \latin{non debemus evangelizare ut comedamus, sed potius e
converso}. \textbf{This is a reading of Mt.\ 6:33 in its Gospel place and not of
the antiphon}, and his gloss on \latin{iustitiam eius} answers a question the
recasting itself raises, since the antiphon drops that clause; whether the
recaster meant anything by the omission is not established. \textbf{And Hilary,
who allegorises every other image in this pericope, reads this verse plainly},
making the seeking the wage of our life, \latin{regnumque Dei vitae nostrae
stipendiis quaeramus}.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{The verb the antiphon keeps when it drops everything else has readers
too.} \textbf{Chrysostom rests the whole promise on it}, in Greek at
\work{Patrologia Graeca} 57:303: \emph{kai ouk eipe, Dothesetai, alla,
Prostethesetai} --- he said not \emph{shall be given} but \emph{shall be added},
``that you may learn that the things present are nothing great, set beside the
greatness of the things to come,'' in the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}
English, first series. Pseudo-Bede: \latin{Non dixit nobis dari, sed
adiici, quia nobis in intentione aeternitas, in usu vero debet esse
temporalitas.} Christian of
Stavelot supplies the elided noun and its limit, \latin{subauditur necessaria
\dots\ Ad necessitatem, non ad superfluitatem.} Paschasius reads the promise
through use and enjoyment. \textbf{And Godfrey of Admont reads \latin{regnum
Dei} at this verse as the heart}, which ought always to be God's kingdom
--- a fourth thing done with the phrase the Communion sings.

\textbf{One further fact bears on how the antiphon's omission may be read.}
Chrysostom's own citation of Mt.\ 6:33, at the same column, \textbf{drops
\latin{et iustítiam eius} exactly as the antiphon does} --- while keeping the
kingdom \emph{of heaven} where the antiphon keeps \latin{regnum Dei}, and
fronting no \latin{primum} at all. \textbf{That is a convergence in the practice
of abbreviating and nothing more}: it shows how ordinary such a shortening is,
nothing supports dependence in either direction, and he cites Scripture loosely
enough that his short form is evidence about his citation habit rather than
about any Gospel text-form.
\textbf{Every one of them comments on Mt.\ 6:33 in its Gospel place, and none of
them comments on the antiphon as such}, and the one reader who
comes to it as an antiphon, Schuster, \textbf{reaches back for the
\latin{et iustítiam eius} the recasting removed} and glosses the justice from
the Gospel verse.

Augustine's exegesis of Mt.\ 6:33 is his own, and it makes word order carry the
doctrine. \textbf{The missal's Communion antiphon prints \latin{Primum} at its
head}, where the Gospel verse carries it third; Augustine expounds the verse and
not the antiphon, and his exposition runs on to v.~34, which the appointed
pericope excludes.

\subsubsection*{And the Communion is a later substitution at this Mass}

\textbf{Five independent witnesses converge on the antiphon being secondary in
this position.}
\textbf{\latin{Primum quaerite} is not a late composition:} it is an ancient
Roman antiphon standing at the \emph{Ninth} Sunday in four ninth-century
antiphonaries, while the Fourteenth Sunday's Communion in those books is
\latin{Panis quem ego dedero}. \textbf{So the antiphon is secondary in this slot
only, and not in the repertory.} \textbf{And the substitution broke a pair}:
\latin{Panis quem ego dedero} travels with the Offertory \latin{Immittet
Angelus} at \emph{both} its occurrences in those books, the Lenten Thursday and
this Sunday, so putting \latin{Primum quaerite} here
separated two chants the older books kept together.

The other four. First, the appointed text itself: this is the formulary's one
substantive departure from its Bible text. Second, Guéranger: ``The
Communion-anthem, taken from the Gospel which now is assigned to this Sunday,
was not the one primitively used; the ancient liturgists make no mention of it
in its present position, nor is it to be found there in any of the manuscripts
consulted by Blessed Thomasi.'' Third, the ninth-century chant apparatus:
Wilson's index shows \latin{Primum quaerite} occurring as a Communion at exactly
one locus, whose chant set is the 1962 \emph{Ninth} Sunday's, the
\latin{Protector noster} set there taking \latin{Panis quem ego}. Setting that
against the 1962 book shows \textbf{a rotation rather than a single
replacement}: in that apparatus the 1962-Ninth set takes \latin{Primum
quaerite}, the 1962-Fourteenth set \latin{Panis quem ego}, and the
1962-Fifteenth set \latin{Qui manducat}, while 1962 assigns \latin{Qui manducat}
to the Ninth, \latin{Primum quaerite} to the Fourteenth and \latin{Panis quem
ego} to the Fifteenth. Fourth, Honorius: the Communion of his Fourteenth-Sunday
chapter is \latin{Panis, quem ego dabo} and not \latin{Primum quaerite}, and it
stands there with the Gradual and Alleluia of the 1962 Fifteenth, so his whole
chant strand and not the Communion alone runs one Sunday later.

\textbf{A control from within the same Office, checkable in the same pages.}
Guéranger prints this
Sunday's Magnificat antiphon at Vespers as \latin{Quaerite primum regnum Dei, et
justitiam ejus, et haec omnia adjicientur vobis. Alleluia.} \textbf{That antiphon
retains both \latin{et iustítiam eius} and \latin{haec} --- exactly the two
clauses the Mass's Communion drops --- and leaves \latin{primum} in its Matthaean
position after the verb.} Both forms above are Guéranger's own printing and not the
\work{Breviarium Romanum}'s.

\textbf{Guéranger's judgment is his own reading of Tommasi's Antiphonary and of
``the ancient liturgists,'' and carries no more than that}. Antiphon texts are routinely shortened for chant and \latin{dicit
Dóminus} is a common formulaic tag, so both changes may be conventional rather
than expressive, and no count of either habit across the repertory stands behind
this. \textbf{The Fifteenth Sunday's Communion is the one assignment of the three
that no second witness here confirms.} What the older books
establish is \textbf{what the older pairing was, not when the substitution
happened.} \textbf{The exchange is later than those
antiphonaries, later than the Ottobonianus margin and later than Honorius, and
earlier than the Pustet Missal of 1862}; \textbf{when it was made, no witness
above says, and none explains why.} \textbf{And the
recasting is not proper to the 1962 edition:} the same form is printed in the
Pustet Missal of Ratisbon, 1862 --- which shows the recasting is old and says
nothing about why it was made. That the Communion selects the imperative rather than the pericope's three
prohibitions \textbf{may be an artefact of taking the final verse} rather than a
choice among the pericope's parts.

\textbf{No witness connects the omission of \latin{et iustítiam eius} to the
Epistle}, and Paul's ``not under law'' and Matthew's \latin{iustítia} are not
the same concept.

\subsection*{Postcommunion \cue{Postcomm.}}

\elementid{Puríficent semper et múniant}{\latin{Postcommunio}}{1581}

\textbf{The figure, and the noun held to the end.} \latin{Ad perpétuæ \dots\
ducant salvatiónis efféctum} splits the genitive around the verb, exactly as the
Thirteenth Sunday's Postcommunion split \latin{redemptiónis ætérnæ \dots\
augméntum} around its own --- the same figure, one Sunday apart, and in both
cases the noun the prayer is reaching for is held back to the end. This is also
\textbf{the formulary's only reach past this life}: \latin{perpétuæ
salvatiónis} is the one phrase in the ten elements that looks beyond the
present.

\textbf{\latin{Semper}, a second time.} The Collect asked \latin{tuis semper
auxíliis}; this prayer opens \latin{Puríficent semper}. Of the fifteen 1962
formularies collated for this collection, three carry \latin{semper} in an oration
and \textbf{this is the only one of those fifteen that carries it twice.}

\textbf{Transmission, and it is the sharpest structural point in the oration
dossier.} This prayer does not come from the same place as the other two, and
\textbf{its Gelasian base is materially narrower than theirs}: it is attested
for Rheinau and Gerbert but \textbf{not} for S.~Gallen, Pamelius or Menard,
since S.~Gallen omits the whole appended series. \latin{Purificent semper}
stands in the floating series of unassigned Postcommunions appended after the
Canon, in exactly the form the 1962 book prints, and separately in a
saint-adapted recension in the Vigil Mass of St~Andrew at Book~II
sect.~\textbf{LXVIII}. Which recension is prior is settled by no witness above.

\textbf{One measured fact about its vocabulary.} \latin{Salvatio} as a noun
occurs \textbf{once} in the forty-five orations of those same fifteen collated
formularies, and it is here.

\subsection*{The Secret and the Postcommunion: no exegetical reception in any period}

\textbf{No patristic or medieval exegesis of the Secret \latin{Concéde nobis,
Dómine} or of the Postcommunion \latin{Puríficent semper et múniant} stands
anywhere in the \work{Patrologia Latina}.} \latin{Propitiatio potestatis} and
\latin{purificent semper et muniant} occur five times between them across that
whole collection, and every one of the five is a sacramentary printing the
prayer.

\textbf{The Collect stands outside that negative}: Honorius cites it by
incipit, Godfrey glosses it word by word and Schuster comments on it
continuously, as the Collect's own treatment above sets out.

\textbf{These are anonymous composed Roman orations, and the patristic and
medieval genres that reach this formulary --- psalm expositions, scriptural
homilies, epistle commentaries --- do not take liturgical orations as their
object.} \textbf{Schuster comments on all three orations and supplies no
patristic or medieval witness for these two}; \textbf{Rupert of Deutz, Sicard of
Cremona and Durandus supply none either}, their treatments of this Sunday being
calendrical and typological rather than euchological.

\textbf{And Guéranger is a reception witness only and never a text witness.} His
printed Latin differs from the 1962 typical edition at four points: he prints
\latin{millia} for \latin{mília}; a \textbf{comma} where the typical edition
prints the \textbf{semicolon} the Collect's pointing turns on;
lower-case \latin{angelus}; and \latin{Concede nobis, Domine, quaesumus:} with a
colon and a capitalised \latin{Hostia}, plus the short conclusion \latin{Per
Dominum} at both Secret and Postcommunion where the typical edition prints the
longer form at the Postcommunion. \textbf{None of the appointed Latin above is
his.} The last of the four is the weakest as evidence, since hand-missal
presentations abbreviate conclusions as a matter of course.
