\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\label{triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

\movementthesis{The formulary orders desire by ordering allegiance. Longing
for God's courts becomes a walk under the Spirit, a refusal of rival mastery,
and the first seeking of the kingdom; divine guardianship, providence, angelic
ministry, and the sacraments sustain that movement without denying embodied
need.}

\subsection*{1. Desire receives its end}

The Introit begins with a soul that ``longeth and fainteth for the courts of
the Lord,'' while petitioning God to look upon his anointed. Its verse values
one day in those courts above thousands elsewhere. Bellarmine reads Psalm~83
as the pilgrim soul's longing for the heavenly dwelling; Augustine and
Cassiodorus read its one day as the everlasting day without yesterday or
tomorrow. Theodoret attends instead to the rescued body and Israel's
displacement. Desire is alive at the entrance, but no lesser good may become
its final home.

Galatians exposes desire's moral contest without opposing spirit to the created
body. Chrysostom and Theodoret deny that Paul condemns bodily nature, and
Jerome reads the language as the whole person's vices rather than the body's
substance. Opposed desires produce opposed courses of action: the plural works
of the flesh and the singular fruit of the Spirit. For Augustine, desire may
press upon grace-assisted freedom without completing itself in action; humility
receives the help by which the Spirit's fruit ripens.

The Gospel names the governing issue: no one can serve two masters; treasure,
sight, anxiety, and mammon disclose rival ultimate ends. Aquinas identifies two
contrary beatitudes, while Augustine reads ``first'' as priority of dignity,
not permission to postpone the kingdom. Food and clothing remain goods, but
not masters. The Communion repeats the command at reception: \latin{Primum
quǽrite regnum Dei}. Desire has moved through conflict into a governing object.

\subsection*{2. Grace enables the commanded walk}

The Collect confesses that human mortality slips without God and asks him to
guard his Church, withdraw her from harm, and direct her toward saving things.
Its petitions precede the Epistle's command, ``Walk in the spirit'': the
command names the form taken by the aid already sought. Augustine joins free
choice to grace, Chrysostom human diligence to God's kindness, and Theodoret
the Spirit's indwelling grace to the soul's cooperation.

Christ's appeal to bodily need confirms the same order: birds feed and lilies
are clothed. Jerome preserves the ordinary comparison, and Chrysostom the
creatures as evidence of providence. The Father's knowledge precedes the
disciple's asking, but his promise does not abolish labor. Augustine, Jerome,
Chrysostom, Benedict, and Aquinas preserve prudent action while refusing
anxious service.

The Postcommunion's \latin{puríficent}, \latin{múniant}, and
\latin{ducant} make the sacraments agents of purification, fortification,
and leading toward perpetual salvation. The Collect likewise joins
\latin{auxíliis} to \latin{dirigátur}. The Epistle's crucifixion of
passions and desires rests within this prior and continuing grace: neither
passivity nor an autonomous moral program.

\subsection*{3. Trust widens without losing its center}

\latin{Protéctor noster} calls upon God; the Collect asks his guardianship;
the Gradual sets confidence in him above confidence in princes. The Gospel
places ordinary provision under the Father's knowledge, the Offertory gives
protection an encamped angel, and the Postcommunion asks the sacraments to
fortify. One trust appears as sovereignty, providence, creaturely ministry,
and sacramental action.

Augustine and Cassiodorus include angelic powers among the Gradual's princes;
Theodoret reads earthly rulers. Bellarmine allows creaturely help while
requiring hope to pass through it to God. Augustine identifies the Offertory's
Angel with Christ; Cassiodorus and Bellarmine with a ministering angel;
Theodoret with ministering angels; Aquinas records created, Christological,
and ecclesial readings. The Mass does not decide the lexical dispute, but
created ministry still differs from ultimate mastery.

Providence is therefore not a promise of physical immunity. Psalm~33 keeps
affliction, truthful speech, the cry of the poor, and redemption in view;
Matthew keeps work, bodily need, and the day's own trouble in view. Trust
changes the governing allegiance under which those realities are endured.

\subsection*{4. Salvation becomes liturgical action}

The Alleluia acclaims ``God our saviour.'' Augustine and Cassiodorus hear joy
too full for speech; Theodoret a shout of victory; Bellarmine inward gladness
joined to praise. Yet the psalm passes from joy to hearing God's voice today.
Acclamation is already an invitation to obedience.

The prayers give salvation a sequence of actions. The Collect directs mortality
\latin{ad salutária}; the Secret calls the offering a \latin{hóstia
salutáris}, purifying offenses and propitiating divine power; the Postcommunion
asks the sacraments to effect perpetual salvation. Direction, victim,
purification, fortification, and final effect remain distinct moments of one
liturgical action.

The Offertory's ``O taste, and see'' stands at that threshold. Augustine and
Cassiodorus receive it in Christ and the Church; \work{Mystagogical
Catechesis} V.20, traditionally attributed to Cyril of Jerusalem though its
authorship remains disputed, and Ambrose's \work{De mysteriis} IX.58 apply it
to Eucharistic reception. Theodoret treats tasting as experiential knowledge;
Aquinas and Bellarmine as divine sweetness known by experience. The verse
remains an Offertory, so this reception illuminates without changing its ritual
place. The saving victim is offered and received as sacrament, then asked to
purify and strengthen unto salvation.

\subsection*{5. Priority reaches the altar}

The Gospel promises necessities after the kingdom and God's justice are sought
first. The Communion fronts \latin{primum}, omits \latin{ergo} and
\latin{et iustítiam eius}, removes \latin{hæc} before \latin{ómnia}, and adds
\latin{dicit Dóminus}. The Fathers expound the complete Gospel verse rather
than this recasting. In the rite, the Gospel's command reaches the communicant
at sacramental reception.

The contraction gathers the movement: the Introit's longing finds its first
object; Galatians' desires are judged by fruit; the Gradual fixes hope on the
Lord; the Gospel distinguishes care from servitude to mammon; the Offertory
places protection and tasting at the altar; the prayers ask God to guard,
purify, fortify, and lead. Material needs are received within allegiance to
the kingdom and perpetual salvation.
