\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\label{triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

\movementthesis{One phrase carries this Mass, and Matthew hardly ever uses it:
\latin{Regnum Dei} stands verbatim in the Epistle, which names who is excluded
from the kingdom, the Gospel, which commands that it be sought first, and the
Communion, which repeats that command as the Lord's own word at reception.
Around that spine the formulary states an absolute either/or and then refuses to
let it read as contempt for created goods --- \latin{Primum}, not
\latin{solum} --- while its own history keeps it honest: the number on this
Sunday is the least stable thing about it, and the one substantive change it
makes to its Bible text is older than this book.}

English is the Douay--Rheims (Challoner) for the
scriptural propers and the 1861 Cummiskey hand missal for the orations; the
project has translated nothing. \textbf{Every patristic string quoted here is the
\work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}' English, first series, uncollated against
Migne}; Guéranger is the Duffy 1900 edition, whose translator no lane
established.

\subsection*{1. The kingdom, said three times}

\latin{Regnum Dei} is rare in the Gospel that supplies it: four occurrences in
Matthew against thirty-four of \latin{regnum caelorum}, Mt.\ 6:33 the first, and
the only one in Galatians. Across the fifteen 1962 formularies collated here it
stands in \textbf{this formulary alone}, at three loci, independently reproduced
--- \textbf{a claim about fifteen formularies, not about the Missal}. Negative
and positive are two faces of one claim: \latin{qui tália agunt, regnum Dei non
consequéntur}; \latin{Quǽrite ergo primum regnum Dei}; then the same imperative
made an address over the communicant, \latin{dicit Dóminus}.

\textbf{Two defeaters travel with the spine.} A Communion drawn from the day's
own Gospel is ordinary practice, so a mechanical carry-over would reduce the
spine to two elements and an echo --- not touching the Epistle's occurrence, from
another book. And Anthony of Padua, the one medieval preacher on this Epistle the
library registers, preaches it beside a \emph{different} Gospel, his numbering a
Sunday ahead --- so \textbf{every join between this Epistle and this Gospel is a
claim about how the 1962 formulary reads, not how the tradition read them.}

\subsection*{2. The flesh that is crucified is not the body that is fed}

The two lessons are lexically disjoint: \latin{carn-} stands five times in the
Epistle and nowhere else, \latin{corp-} twice in the Gospel and nowhere else.
\textbf{That makes them one unit, not a contrast}, and a reading setting a
mortifying Epistle against a providing Gospel has inverted it.

The tradition reaches that twice, by routes differing in kind.
Chrysostom argues from the vice-list's contents --- ``by the flesh, he does not
mean the body,'' since hatred, strife, heresies and witchcraft ``arise merely
from a depraved moral choice'' --- pressing the missal's \latin{qui tália agunt}.
Aquinas answers the same objection by citing Augustine's \work{De civitate Dei}
XIV.2 --- whoever lives according to himself lives according to the flesh, so
\latin{caro} stands for the whole man --- \textbf{a work not opened here, so what
is reported is Aquinas's use of Augustine, not Augustine's wording.} \textbf{Preserve the difference: no single patristic-scholastic consensus.}
Augustine supplies a third leg: the ascetic misreading of the Gospel half
actually happened, and \work{De opere monachorum} was written against monks who
quoted these verses to prove they need not work.

\textbf{Limits.} Some read Gal.\ 5:24's \latin{carnem suam crucifixérunt} as
including the body proper, and the Vulgate elsewhere uses \latin{caro} of the
body simply: a fact about two pericopes, not a lexical law. The Introit's psalm
has \latin{caro} positively at Ps.\ 83:3b, outside the appointed cut. And the Latin lists run longer than the Greek Fathers' --- seventeen vices
against fifteen, twelve fruits against nine --- so \textbf{no Greek witness is
made to comment on \latin{homicídia} or \latin{cástitas}}; Aquinas closes that
gap, Chrysostom cannot.

\subsection*{3. The images are arguments, and the sentence the Mass lifts out of them is doctrine}

\textbf{Gospel and Communion.} Augustine expressly \emph{forbids} allegorising
the birds and the lilies:
``these examples are not to be treated as allegories \dots\ they stand here, in
order that from smaller matters we may be persuaded respecting greater ones.''
Chrysostom reads them as arguments too, but rhetorically engineered --- the
birds chosen \emph{because} irrational, ``that He might touch them more to the
quick,'' the lilies degraded step by step so the \emph{a fortiori} grows at
each. \textbf{Where
they agree they still differ, and that is the interesting half:} Augustine's
middle term is the creature's \textbf{rank}, Chrysostom's its \textbf{vileness},
grounded in gift rather than rank.

\textbf{The Mass appoints Mt.\ 6:33 twice} --- as the Gospel's last verse and,
recast, as the Communion antiphon --- \textbf{and the same witness reads the two
in different registers}: at the images he refuses figure outright; at the
imperative the missal lifts out for reception he reads doctrine, \latin{primum}
an ordering of importance and not of time, \latin{non témpore, sed dignitáte},
``He did not say, \emph{Then} seek such things'' --- corroborated in Latin,
uncollated against \work{Patrologia Latina}~34. \textbf{Limits:} this Communion
carries §1's carry-over defeater, and unlike §1 the claim has no third element
behind it. The promotion of \latin{Primum} is \textbf{this guide's editorial
observation and not a patristic one}; §5 puts the datum to another question.
\textbf{Page~1's allegorical row stands in real tension with this and is
preserved there}, not resolved by allegorising the lilies --- \textbf{a
consequence of the claim, not an element of it:} the four senses are this
guide's apparatus, not a ritual moment.

\subsection*{4. Three chants stop one clause short, and a fourth sings it}

\latin{Beatus \dots\ qui sperat in eo} closes both Ps.~33 and Ps.~83. It lies
past the Introit antiphon's cut at v.~11a \emph{and} past its psalm verse's cut
at v.~3a; one clause past the Offertory's cut at v.~9a; and the Gradual alone
sings it outright. \textbf{The Introit belongs to that observation because the
formula lies beyond both its boundaries, not because it uses hope vocabulary ---
it does not}: the \latin{sper-} and \latin{confid-} stems occur in the Gradual
block only. \textbf{Strongest limit: antiphon
extents are inherited chant tradition, the cuts have other explanations, intent
not established.}

Both chants carry documented divisions the Mass prints without adjudicating.
Augustine defends the Offertory's \latin{Immíttet Angelus Dómini} against ``bad
copies'' reading \latin{Immittet Angelum Dominus} and identifies that Angel as
Christ --- while his text continues
\latin{et éruet eos} where the missal prints \latin{et erípiet eos},
\textbf{evidence that the missal's text is not simply his}; the
\work{Mystagogical Catecheses} --- \textbf{of disputed attribution to Cyril of
Jerusalem, a question not swept} --- and Ambrose attest the same verse as the
Church's \emph{communion} invitation, which this Mass puts at the offertory. At the Gradual he escalates the excluded
second term to \textbf{good angels}. \textbf{So the angel who is the Mass's
rescuer at one chant is its excluded object of trust at the other} --- reached
separately, and he cites \latin{Nemo potest} at neither. The Alleluia stands
apart: its one substantive tie to any element is \latin{salutári},
\textbf{the least lexically integrated element of the formulary}; Augustine's
exposition of its psalm centres on vv.~7--11, which this chant does not
appoint.

\subsection*{5. What the book inherited, and the one thing it altered}

The Collect and Secret stand two Sundays later in the Frankish Gelasian books
and one later in the Gregorian, uniformly across eleven consecutive Masses, on a
published critical collation --- and no one has since opened any of the five
books at the locus, the assignment covering only \textbf{two of this formulary's
ten elements}. Ninth-century chant cues sit
two Sundays ahead of the orations beside them, and Honorius agrees with 1962 in
four elements and differs in four. \textbf{The earlier books do not carry this
formulary under another number: orations, chants and Gospel were numbered and
paired independently.}

The formulary's one substantive recasting is at the Communion. Mt.\ 6:33 is
altered in four ways, verified against the Clementine, the Lucan parallel and
the tracked Greek: \latin{ergo} dropped and \latin{Primum} promoted,
\latin{et iustítiam eius} omitted, \latin{hæc} dropped, \latin{dicit Dóminus}
added --- which stands nowhere in Matthew here or in Luke. The tag turns a
saying inside a sermon into an address at reception; the omission leaves the
kingdom sought without the justice beside it (on the promotion, §3). Guéranger
judges the antiphon not primitive --- \textbf{a checked report of an unchecked
claim, his and not established chant history} --- and his own pages control it:
the day's Magnificat antiphon keeps \emph{both} clauses this Communion drops. The
recast form is already in the Pustet Missal of 1862, so it is not this book's
doing. Against that inheritance the orations are the Mass's own register ---
\latin{propitiátio} twice, \latin{salut-} four times, \latin{semper} and
\latin{perpétu-} twice each. \textbf{Bounds:}
standard Roman collect vocabulary; \latin{propitiátio} stands in no other of the
fifteen collated formularies, though the wider \latin{propiti-} family reaches
eleven.
