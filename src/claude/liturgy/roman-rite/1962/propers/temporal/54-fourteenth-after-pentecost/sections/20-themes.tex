\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\label{triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

\movementthesis{This Mass was assembled from three strands counted on different
schedules, and exactly one of its ten elements was exchanged rather than
inherited: the Communion. \textbf{That one exchanged element is where the
formulary's single thrice-repeated substantive word, its single dropped word and
its single contested adverb all stand}, and around it the propers state an
absolute either/or without contempt for created goods.}

\subsection*{1. The kingdom, said three times}

\latin{Regnum Dei} is rare in the Gospel that supplies it --- four verses in
Matthew against twenty-four carrying \latin{regnum caelorum}. It stands
verbatim in three appointed elements, and \textbf{no other exact form reaching
three or more of the ten is anything but a divine name or a function word}.
Negative and positive are one claim: \latin{qui tália agunt, regnum
Dei non consequéntur}; \latin{Quǽrite ergo primum regnum Dei}; then the same
imperative made an address at reception, \latin{dicit Dóminus}.

\textbf{And the element that repeats the phrase is the element that cuts it.}
\latin{Iustítia} stands once in the whole appointed Latin, in the Gospel's last
verse --- and the Communion, quoting that verse, drops it. A Communion drawn
from the day's own Gospel is ordinary practice, so on that side the spine may be
two elements and an echo; the Epistle's occurrence comes from another book and
another pericope and stands on its own.

\subsection*{2. The flesh that is crucified is not the body that is fed}

\latin{Carn-} stands five times in the Epistle and nowhere else, \latin{corp-}
twice in the Gospel and nowhere else. \textbf{That makes the two lessons one
unit, not a contrast}, and a reading setting a mortifying Epistle against a
providing Gospel has inverted it. \textbf{The Introit's cut keeps the division
clean:} its psalm verse stops one clause before \latin{cor meum et caro mea
exsultaverunt in Deum vivum}, which would have given \latin{caro} a positive
sense.

\textbf{Four positions stand on what \latin{caro} is at the Epistle's v.~17.}
\textbf{Chrysostom's Greek denies desire to the flesh}: desire belongs
to the soul, so the flesh here is the earthbound reasoning.
\textbf{Marius Victorinus} grants the flesh motions of its own, arguing from
the soulless elements. \textbf{The Glossa}
makes it a manner of speaking about a joint act, \latin{ex utroque enim fit}.
\textbf{Ambrosiaster} shifts the ground to two laws, \latin{unam Dei, alteram
peccati}, so the crucified flesh is the world. \textbf{Chrysostom and Victorinus
comment on the same verse and cannot both be right.} Jerome defends the missal's
own \latin{vitia} against a reading of the body, \latin{ut non naturam corporis
videretur in spiritualibus viris negare, sed vitia}.

The disjunction holds of these two pericopes and is not a law of the two words.
Ambrosiaster expounds a Latin text that is not the missal's, so he speaks to the
sense of \latin{caro} and not to the count or the asymmetry of the two lists;
and \latin{luxúria}, \latin{modéstia} and \latin{cástitas} have no counterpart
in the Greek these Fathers read, so none of them comments on those three words.

\subsection*{3. Divided at the images, and the most-repeated sentence withdrawn by the man who wrote it}

\textbf{The Latin tradition is divided by name about whether the Gospel's images
may be figures.} Augustine bars allegorising these examples, \textbf{Christian of Stavelot} refuses allegory at the lilies on frankly
aesthetic grounds, Jerome forbids reading the birds as angels, and Bruno of
Segni reads them literally. At the far end
\textbf{Godfrey of Admont} makes the birds good angels, the lilies evil spirits
and Solomon Christ; \textbf{Hilary allegorises every image into doctrine of the
risen body.}

\textbf{And the sentence the tradition repeats most often at this Gospel's first
verse was withdrawn by its author.} Augustine's \latin{Nullius enim fere
conscientia Deum potest odisse} is retracted at \work{Retractationes} I.19,
naming his own exposition of this verse; five later witnesses repeat it \emph{without his qualifier} \latin{fere}, the
Glossa alone flagging the retraction; and \textbf{Bruno of Segni denies the
proposition outright}, the covetous man serving idols and therefore hating God.

\textbf{At Mt.\ 6:33, which this Mass appoints twice, the argument narrows to a
single adverb.} The Augustine the compilations transmit reads \latin{primum} as
an ordering of importance and not of time, \latin{non témpore, sed dignitáte};
\textbf{Paschasius denies that it marks order at all.} Jerome and Hilary do not
meet on this ground, Hilary's angels being the \emph{lilies} and Jerome's
prohibition falling on the birds; and Bruno of Segni restricts the whole
prohibition of anxiety to apostles and those aiming at perfection while Godfrey
preaches it to monks, so both address a narrower audience than a Sunday
congregation.

\subsection*{4. Three chants stop short of one formula, and two divide the Fathers}

\latin{Beatus \dots\ qui sperat} stands in exactly two verses of the Clementine,
and they are the two psalms this Mass takes its Offertory and its Introit from:
\latin{beatus vir qui sperat in eo} at Ps.\ 33:9b, \latin{beatus homo qui
sperat in te} at Ps.\ 83:13. It lies past both the Introit's cuts and one clause
past the Offertory's, while
\textbf{the Gradual alone is uncut and sings the hope vocabulary outright},
\latin{sper-} and \latin{confid-} twice each and in no other element.
\textbf{The Introit belongs to that because the formula lies past its
boundaries, not because it uses hope vocabulary --- it does not.}
These extents are the ones the chant tradition handed on.

Two chants carry documented divisions the Mass prints without adjudicating. \textbf{The Offertory's angel has four
accounts}: Augustine's Christ, Cassiodorus's moral sense, Arnobius's angel of the sepulchre --- \textbf{which turns on a
repointing the missal's Latin does not carry} --- and Godfrey's gift of grace.
\textbf{The Gradual's princes have four positions and a question}: Augustine's
good angels, Cassiodorus's devil and good angel, \textbf{Theodoret's earthly
rulers, whose authority is temporary}, Godfrey's interior and exterior senses ---
and \textbf{Bruno the Carthusian asks whether the verse is comparative at all},
answering both ways. \textbf{So the angel
who is the Mass's rescuer at one chant is its excluded object of trust at the
other.} Peter Lombard puts the angelic reading under Augustine's name and
Prosper's exposition is largely an Augustine epitome, so what looks like three
witnesses to that reading may be one position with three carriers. The Alleluia is still the
least integrated: Cassiodorus hears in \latin{iubilémus} a wordless jubilus and
\textbf{Theodoret the shout of victors} --- \textbf{but every later reader of the
psalm reads it penitentially, through a verse the Alleluia does not appoint.}

\subsection*{5. Three strands, one exchange, and the goods the Mass never asks for}

\textbf{The strands were counted from different feasts.} The Gregorian Hadrianum
heads this Mass \latin{Dominica XV post Pentecosten}, one later than 1962; the
Frankish Gelasian books stand two later.
\textbf{The chants did not move with them}: the ninth-century antiphonary
formulary carrying this Introit, Gradual and Offertory is numbered
\emph{Dominica XIV}, the number 1962 gives it. \textbf{And the readings never
moved at all}: the \work{Liber Comitis} pairs this Epistle with this Gospel, cut
at exactly the verses 1962 cuts. Two distinct mechanisms stand: Wilson's
numeration base, and \textbf{Schuster's Roman cycle built around the feast of
St~Lawrence, whose count ``was not everywhere the same.''} In
Honorius \textbf{three of five chants are the ones 1962 sings the following
Sunday} --- the displacement Godfrey meets independently.

\textbf{The one exchange is at the Communion, and it broke a pair.}
\latin{Primum quaerite} is an ancient Roman antiphon standing at the
\emph{Ninth} Sunday in four ninth-century antiphonaries, where this Sunday's
Communion is \latin{Panis quem ego dedero} --- which travels with the Offertory
\latin{Immittet Angelus} at \emph{both} its occurrences. \textbf{So the antiphon
is secondary in this slot only and not in the repertory}, and the substitution
separated two chants the older books kept together, confirming from a source he
did not have Guéranger's report that the Communion ``was not the one primitively
used.'' The recasting itself --- \latin{Primum} promoted, \latin{et iustítiam
eius} dropped, \latin{dicit Dóminus} added --- is older than this book.
\textbf{When it was made, no witness says.}

\textbf{What the Mass asks for and what it promises do not meet.}
\latin{Salut-} stands once each in the three orations and the Alleluia and in no
other element. \textbf{No oration of any of the fifteen collated 1962 identities
asks for food, drink or clothing}, so this Sunday's silence about the three goods
its Gospel names is a genre fact --- though the season's orations are not
uniformly non-temporal, four of the fifteen asking for peace, health or the
world's ordering. \textbf{The singularity lies on the reading side:} the
Fourteenth Sunday is the only one of the fifteen whose appointed texts name
eating, drinking \emph{and} clothing together. The three orations never command
the assembly; \textbf{the one command the Mass repeats at reception is the
promise's own \latin{Quǽrite}.} The four \latin{salut-} words are one root in
four senses --- of a victim, of God, of things that save, and of a state.
