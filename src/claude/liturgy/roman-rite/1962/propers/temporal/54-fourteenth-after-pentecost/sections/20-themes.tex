\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\label{triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

\movementthesis{One phrase carries this Mass, and Matthew hardly ever uses it:
\latin{Regnum Dei} stands verbatim in the Epistle, which names who is excluded
from the kingdom, the Gospel, which commands that it be sought first, and the
Communion, which repeats that command as the Lord's own word at reception.
Around that spine the formulary states an absolute either/or and refuses to let
it read as contempt for created goods --- \latin{Primum}, not \latin{solum} ---
while asking God for nothing it promises and promising nothing it asks. Its own
history keeps it honest: this Sunday's number is the least stable thing about it,
and its one substantive change to its Bible text is older than this book.}

English is the Douay--Rheims (Challoner) for the scriptural propers and the 1861
Cummiskey hand missal for the orations; the project has translated nothing.
\textbf{Witness states differ by witness}: Hilary, Jerome, Cassiodorus, Augustine
on Galatians and Aquinas are quoted from Latin transcriptions uncollated against
Migne; Theodoret and Chrysostom on Galatians were \textbf{read in Greek},
transliterated here because this document sets no Greek face; Chrysostom on
Matthew and Augustine on Matthew and the psalms are the \work{Nicene and
Post-Nicene Fathers}' English, first series. Guéranger is the Duffy 1900
edition.

\subsection*{1. The kingdom, said three times}

\latin{Regnum Dei} is rare in the Gospel that supplies it --- four occurrences in
Matthew against thirty-four of \latin{regnum caelorum}, Mt.\ 6:33 the first ---
and in Galatians it is the letter's only word of the \latin{regn-} stem at all.
Across the fifteen 1962 formularies collated here it stands in \textbf{this one
alone}, and \latin{regnum} is the only substantive content word standing in three
or more of the ten elements. Negative and positive are two faces of one claim:
\latin{qui tália agunt, regnum Dei non consequéntur}; \latin{Quǽrite ergo primum
regnum Dei}; then the same imperative made an address at reception, \latin{dicit
Dóminus}.

\textbf{Bounds.} Fifteen formularies is not the Missal. A Communion drawn from
the day's own Gospel is ordinary practice, which would leave the spine two
elements and an echo --- not touching the Epistle's occurrence, from another
book. And Anthony of Padua, the one registered medieval preacher on this
Epistle, preaches it beside a \emph{different} Gospel --- so \textbf{every join
between this Epistle and this Gospel is a claim about how the 1962 formulary
reads, not how the tradition read them.}

\subsection*{2. The flesh that is crucified is not the body that is fed}

\latin{Carn-} stands five times in the Epistle and nowhere else, \latin{corp-}
twice in the Gospel and nowhere else. \textbf{That makes the two lessons one
unit, not a contrast}, and a reading setting a mortifying Epistle against a
providing Gospel has inverted it. The tradition reaches it three times:
\textbf{Jerome}, on the missal's own word, that \latin{vitia} renders
\emph{pathemata} and \latin{desideria} was added \latin{ut non naturam corporis
videretur in spiritualibus viris negare, sed vitia}; \textbf{Chrysostom, read in
Greek}, that the word is \emph{sarx} against \emph{logismos}, an earthly cast of
mind, and not \emph{soma}, and that at the appointed v.~24 they did not destroy
the flesh; \textbf{Aquinas}, through Augustine's \work{De civitate Dei} XIV.2,
that \latin{caro} stands for the whole man --- a work not opened here.

\textbf{Limits.} The disjunction is a fact about two pericopes and not a lexical
law. The appointed lists run longer than the Greek --- seventeen vices and twelve
fruits here, seventeen and nine in the Byzantine text, fifteen and nine in the
critical text --- so \textbf{no Greek witness is made to
comment on \latin{luxúria}, \latin{modéstia} or \latin{cástitas}}, the three
appointed words with no Greek counterpart; and Jerome, who allowed \latin{non
plus quam quindecim} vices, is himself the witness that \latin{longanímitas} and
\latin{patiéntia} render one \emph{makrothymia}.

\subsection*{3. Divided at the images, agreed at the verse the Mass lifts out}

\textbf{Gospel and Communion, and the Latin tradition is namedly divided.}
Augustine forbids allegorising the birds and the lilies. Jerome forbids it on a
ground inside the appointed verse: if the birds were angels, the \emph{a
fortiori} drawn to men would not follow, \latin{Simpliciter ergo accipiendum}.
\textbf{Hilary of Poitiers does the opposite}, holding that the literal reading
fails, \latin{non satis propositionibus congruunt}, and allegorising every
appointed image into doctrine of the risen body.
Chrysostom reads them as arguments too, but rhetorically engineered, the
illustrations degraded step by step --- and \textbf{where the Fathers agree they
still differ}, Augustine's middle term being the creature's \textbf{rank} and
Chrysostom's its \textbf{vileness}.

\textbf{At Mt.\ 6:33, which this Mass appoints twice, nobody argues from a lesser
example.} Augustine reads \latin{primum} as an ordering of importance and not of
time, \latin{non témpore, sed dignitáte}; Aquinas glosses the justice as
\emph{his} because \latin{per iustitiam propriam nullus potest venire ad
regnum}; and \textbf{Hilary, who allegorises everything else here, reads this
verse plainly}, making the seeking the wage of our life. \textbf{So figure is
contested at the images and doctrine read at the imperative --- and the sentence
the missal lifted out for reception is the one on which even the allegorist reads
doctrine.} \textbf{Limits:} Jerome is not refuting Hilary, his target being the
\emph{birds} as angels where Hilary's angels are the \emph{lilies}; this
Communion carries §1's carry-over bound and has no third element behind it; the
promotion of \latin{Primum} is \textbf{this guide's observation and not a
patristic one}. \textbf{Page~1's allegorical row is settled by this division and
not strained by it.}

\subsection*{4. Three chants stop one clause short, and two divide the Fathers}

\latin{Beatus \dots\ qui sperat in eo} closes both Ps.~33 and Ps.~83: it lies
past the Introit antiphon's cut at v.~11a \emph{and} past its psalm verse's cut
at v.~3a, one clause past the Offertory's cut at v.~9a, and the Gradual alone
sings it outright. \textbf{The Introit belongs to that because the formula lies
past both its boundaries, not because it uses hope vocabulary --- it does not.}
\textbf{Limit: antiphon extents are inherited chant tradition; intent is not
established.}

Two chants carry documented divisions the Mass prints without adjudicating. At
the Offertory Augustine defends \latin{Immíttet Angelus Dómini} against ``bad
copies'' reading \latin{Immittet Angelum Dominus} and identifies that Angel as
Christ --- and \textbf{Cassiodorus's lemma is the reading he denounced}, taken
morally without the identification. At
the Gradual the excluded second term is escalated three ways: Augustine's good
angels, Cassiodorus's devil and good angel, \textbf{Theodoret's earthly rulers,
whose authority is temporary}. \textbf{So the angel who is the Mass's rescuer at
one chant is its excluded object of trust at the other}, the two positions
reached separately. The Alleluia stays the least integrated --- its one
substantive tie is \latin{salutári} --- but no longer thin: Cassiodorus hears in
\latin{iubilémus} a wordless jubilus, \textbf{Theodoret, in Greek, the shout of
victors}.

\subsection*{5. What the Mass asks, what it inherited, and the one thing it altered}

\textbf{The three orations ask for no temporal good whatever}, and \textbf{the
only goods the Mass promises are exactly those}: the Gospel states the antecedent
twice and promises \latin{et hæc ómnia adiciéntur vobis}, and the Communion
repeats the promise alone. The imperatives addressed to God are \latin{áspice},
\latin{réspice} and
\latin{Custódi}; \textbf{the only imperative addressed to the assembly is
\latin{Quǽrite}}. \textbf{That resolves the tension this formulary most obviously
raises --- a Gospel forbidding anxiety about food inside a rite whose business is
petition --- because the objects differ.} \textbf{Limit, unrun rather than
answered: season orations are conventionally non-temporal, the counter-test
across the other fourteen collated identities was not run, so this is not stated
as distinctive.}

The three orations stand two Sundays later in the Frankish Gelasian books and one
later in the Gregorian, uniformly across sixteen consecutive sections, and
\textbf{the Gregorian Hadrianum prints all three together} at its \latin{Dominica
XV}. \textbf{One of the five books is now open} --- Gerbert's \latin{Dominica XVI.
post Pentecosten} at printed p.~173, the same three orations --- and \textbf{he
is the least independent of the five}; four remain unopened, and \textbf{the
offset is a numeration base, not an interpolation}. The chants run
\emph{later} on two witnesses disagreeing in magnitude --- Honorius by one Sunday,
the Ottobonianus cues by two, \textbf{a live disagreement the page image
controls} --- while Honorius's Gospel here is the Thirteenth Sunday's.
\textbf{Orations, chants and Gospel were numbered and paired independently.}

The one substantive recasting is at the Communion, and a mechanical comparison
found no second: \latin{Primum} promoted, \latin{et iustítiam eius} and
\latin{hæc} dropped, \latin{dicit Dóminus} added.
\textbf{Four independent witnesses make the antiphon secondary}: the appointed
text; Guéranger's ``was not the one primitively used'' (\textbf{his report of a
claim he did not check}); the Ottobonianus cue block whose Introit is this
Mass's own but whose Communion is \latin{Panis quem ego}; and Honorius, whose
Communion here is \latin{Panis, quem ego dabo}. Guéranger's own Magnificat
antiphon for the day keeps \emph{both} clauses this Communion drops, and the
form is already in the Pustet Missal of 1862.
