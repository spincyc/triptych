\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\label{triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

\movementthesis{The formulary orders desire by ordering allegiance. Longing
for God's courts becomes a walk under the Spirit, a refusal of rival mastery,
and the first seeking of the kingdom; divine guardianship, providence, angelic
ministry, and the sacraments sustain that movement without denying embodied
need.}

\subsection*{1. Desire receives its end}

The Introit does not begin by extinguishing desire. It begins with a soul that
``longeth and fainteth for the courts of the Lord,'' and places that longing
beside the petition that God look upon the face of his anointed. The verse that
follows values one day in those courts above thousands elsewhere. Bellarmine
therefore reads Psalm~83 as the pilgrim soul's longing for the heavenly
dwelling, while Augustine and Cassiodorus read its one day as the everlasting
day that knows no yesterday or tomorrow. Theodoret's concern for the rescued
body and Israel's displacement gives the same psalm a more historical weight.
The Church enters the Mass with desire already alive, but not yet allowed to
name any lesser good as its final home.

Galatians then exposes desire's moral contest. Paul does not oppose spirit to
the created body as though holiness required contempt for flesh. Chrysostom
and Theodoret explicitly deny that the Apostle condemns bodily nature, and
Jerome reads the language as the vices of the whole person rather than the
substance of the body. The opposed desires produce opposed courses of action:
the plural works of the flesh and the singular fruit of the Spirit. Augustine
locates the struggle within grace-assisted freedom: desire may press upon the
will without completing itself in action, and humility receives the help by
which the Spirit's fruit ripens.

The Gospel names the governing issue with final clarity. No one can serve two
masters; treasure, sight, bodily anxiety, and mammon disclose rival ultimate
ends. Aquinas identifies that rivalry with two contrary beatitudes, while
Augustine reads ``first'' as priority of dignity rather than a timetable that
permits the kingdom to be postponed. Food and clothing remain goods, but they
cannot become masters. The Communion repeats the command at reception:
\latin{Primum quǽrite regnum Dei}. Desire has moved from longing, through
conflict, into a first and governing object.

\subsection*{2. Grace enables the commanded walk}

The Collect confesses that human mortality slips without God and therefore
asks the Lord to guard his Church perpetually, withdraw her from harm, and
direct her toward saving things. Its verbs are petitions before the Epistle's
imperative is heard. When Paul commands, ``Walk in the Spirit,'' he is not
replacing prayer with self-direction; the command names the form taken by the
aid for which the Church has prayed. Augustine joins free choice to grace,
Chrysostom joins human diligence to God's kindness, and Theodoret describes
the Spirit's indwelling grace as cooperating with the soul.

Christ's appeal to the body confirms the same order. Life needs food; the body
needs clothing; birds feed and lilies are clothed. Jerome resists an allegory
that would destroy this ordinary comparison, and Chrysostom preserves the
creatures as evidence of providence. Hilary reads the same images through the
resurrection and angelic glory, but his spiritual senses do not turn need into
evil. The Father's knowledge precedes the disciple's asking, and his promise
that necessities will be added does not abolish labor. Augustine, Jerome,
Chrysostom, and Aquinas all preserve prudent action while refusing anxious
service.

The Postcommunion completes the grammar of grace. The sacraments are asked to
purify, strengthen, and lead the faithful to the effect of perpetual
salvation. The Epistle's active crucifixion of passions and desires rests
inside this sacramental passivity: the Christian acts because God first guards,
directs, purifies, and fortifies. The formulary neither dissolves moral effort
into passivity nor imagines an autonomous moral program.

\subsection*{3. Trust widens without losing its center}

\latin{Protéctor noster} calls upon God at the entrance; the Collect asks his
perpetual guardianship; the Gradual sets confidence in him above confidence in
man and princes. The Gospel then places ordinary provision under the Father's
knowledge, and the Offertory gives protection the image of an encamped angel.
At the end, the Postcommunion asks the sacraments to fortify. One act of trust
is expressed through divine sovereignty, providence, creaturely ministry, and
sacramental action.

The witnesses preserve necessary distinctions within that unity. Augustine
and Cassiodorus include angelic powers among the Gradual's princes; Theodoret
reads mutable earthly rulers. Bellarmine does not forbid creaturely help, but
requires hope to pass through the creature to God. The Offertory's Angel is
likewise received in several senses: Augustine identifies the protecting
Angel with Christ, Cassiodorus and Bellarmine with a ministering angel,
Theodoret with the reality of ministering angels, and Aquinas records created,
Christological, and ecclesial readings. The Mass does not decide the lexical
dispute. It does show that created ministry differs from ultimate mastery:
help may be received without placing the creature where only God belongs.

Providence is therefore not a promise of physical immunity. Psalm~33 keeps
affliction, truthful speech, the cry of the poor, and redemption in view;
Matthew keeps work, bodily need, and the day's own trouble in view. Trust
changes the governing allegiance under which those realities are endured.

\subsection*{4. Salvation becomes liturgical action}

The Alleluia acclaims ``God our saviour.'' Augustine and Cassiodorus hear its
jubilation as a joy too full for articulate speech; Theodoret hears the shout
of victory; Bellarmine joins inward gladness to vocal praise. Yet the whole
psalm passes from that opening joy to the urgency of hearing God's voice
today. Acclamation is already an invitation to obedient passage.

The prayers then give salvation a sequence of actions. The Collect asks that
mortality be directed \latin{ad salutária}; the Secret names the offering a
\latin{hóstia salutáris}, purification of offenses and propitiation of divine
power; the Postcommunion asks that the sacraments produce the effect of
perpetual salvation. The related words do not make the grammatical subjects
interchangeable. Direction, victim, purification, fortification, and final
effect name distinct moments of one liturgical action.

The Offertory's ``taste and see'' stands at the threshold of that action.
Augustine, Cassiodorus, Theodoret, Cyril of Jerusalem, and Ambrose receive the
verse sacramentally in different contexts; Aquinas and Bellarmine join tasting
to experiential knowledge of divine sweetness. In this Mass the verse remains
an Offertory rather than a Communion antiphon, so the reception illuminates
the words without rewriting their ritual placement. What is offered as the
saving victim is received as sacrament, and what is received is asked to
purify and strengthen unto salvation.

\subsection*{5. Priority reaches the altar}

The Gospel promises that needed things will be added after the kingdom and
God's justice are sought first. The Communion contracts the sentence: it
fronts \latin{primum}, omits the Gospel's \latin{ergo} and
\latin{et iustítiam eius}, removes \latin{hæc} before \latin{ómnia}, and adds
\latin{dicit Dóminus}. The antiphon is not a verbatim quotation, and the
Fathers' exposition of the complete Gospel verse is not direct reception of
this liturgical recasting. Its effect within the rite is nevertheless plain:
the command heard in the Gospel is placed on the communicant's lips and ears
at the moment of sacramental reception.

That contraction gathers the whole movement. The Introit's longing has found
its first object; Galatians' rival desires have been judged by their fruit and
their relation to the kingdom; the Gradual has fixed hope on the Lord; the
Gospel has distinguished provident care from servitude to mammon; the
Offertory has placed protection and tasting at the altar; and the prayers have
asked God to guard, purify, fortify, and lead. Material needs are neither
denied nor enthroned. They are received within an allegiance whose end is the
kingdom and whose final horizon is perpetual salvation.
