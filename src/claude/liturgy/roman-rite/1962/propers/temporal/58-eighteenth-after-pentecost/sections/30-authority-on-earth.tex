\runninghead{Power on Earth to Forgive}
\properlane{authority-on-earth}{Power on Earth to Forgive Sins: The Paralytic in His Own City and the Altar of the Covenant}
\label{sec:authority-on-earth}

Heard from its Gospel, the Mass turns on a sentence about power and place:
\latin{Ut autem sciatis, quia Filius hominis habet potestatem in terra
dimittendi peccata}, ``But that you may know that the Son of man hath power on
earth to forgive sins''. The Gospel proves an unseen act by a seen one. The
forgiveness of the paralytic's sins cannot be verified; his rising from his bed
can, and the second is done so that the first may be believed. The rest of the
formulary moves in the opposite direction. It takes visible things, an altar,
victims, an evening sacrifice, bread received, and asks that an invisible work
be done through them. The Offertory recalls Moses at the altar of the covenant,
and the Secret names the holy exchange by which God makes his people sharers in
his Godhead. Read from the Gospel, the Mass sets beside the power shown in
Christ's own city the altar at which the Church offers the sacrifice of the covenant
whose blood, in the words of institution, is shed ``for the remission of sins''.
The principal witnesses are St John Chrysostom and St Augustine on the Gospel,
St Jerome beside them, St Ambrose on Luke's telling of the same healing, and St
Thomas Aquinas, who in his lectures on Matthew
asks how the Son of man's power stands to the power his apostles would
exercise. Bl.\ Ildefonso Schuster, commenting on this Mass, reads the Gospel's
last verse of that same communicated power.

\subsection{The unseen healing proved by the seen}

The healing happens at a place. Jesus crosses the lake ``and came into his own
city'', and the witnesses name the town. St John Chrysostom says: ``By His own
city here he means Capernaum. For that which gave Him birth was Bethlehem; that
which brought Him up, Nazareth; that which had Him continually inhabiting it,
Capernaum.'' St Jerome says as flatly that the city is Nazareth,
\latin{unde et Nazaræus appellatus est} (PL 26, col.~54). St Augustine,
reconciling Matthew with Mark, places the healing at Capernaum, either because
Galilee may be called Christ's city or because Matthew passes over what
happened between Christ's coming into his own city and his reaching Capernaum
(\work{De consensu evangelistarum} II.25.58). Chrysostom and Jerome disagree
about the town, and nothing in the text settles it; all three agree that there
was one. The power to forgive is shown in a town, before scribes who can see
the man walk.

Chrysostom's reading turns on the order of the two healings. Christ,
``to signify His indifference to honor'', ``did not straightway hasten to heal
the visible body, but He takes His occasion from them; and He healed first that
which is invisible, the soul, by forgiving his sins; which indeed saved the
other, but brought no great glory to Himself''. The visible miracle is then
offered as evidence. Christ's question, which is easier to say, means: ``by how
much a soul is better than a body, by so much is the doing away sins a greater
work than this; but because the one is unseen, the other in sight, I throw in
that, which although an inferior thing, is yet more open to sense; that the
greater also and the unseen may thereby receive its proof''
(\work{Homilies on Matthew} 29, 1--2). The scribes supply a second proof by their
silence. They say nothing aloud, and Christ answers what they think: ``But that
it belongs to God only to know men's secrets, hear what saith the prophet''.
And Chrysostom notices the grammar of the two sayings. At first ``He said not,
`I forgive thee thy sins,' but, `thy sins be forgiven thee:' upon their
constraining, He discloses His authority more clearly''. He does not say that
the Son of man needs another, or that authority has been given him, ``but,
`He hath authority'\,''.

St Jerome gives the same argument in fewer words. \latin{Inter dicere, et
facere, multa distantia est}: between saying and doing there is a great
distance. Whether the paralytic's sins were forgiven, only the one who forgave
them knew, and so \latin{Fit igitur carnale signum, ut probetur spirituale}: a
sign is given in the flesh so that the spiritual may be proved. Of Christ's
reading of the scribes' thoughts he says that Christ shows himself God, able to
know the secrets of the heart, and speaks in silence: \latin{Eadem majestate et
potentia qua cogitationes vestras intueor, possum et hominibus peccata
dimittere}, by the same majesty and power with which I see your thoughts I can
also forgive sins to men (\work{Commentarii in Matthaeum} I, on 9:4--6; PL 26,
col.~55). He adds a further consideration of his own, and offers it as a
possibility: \latin{datur nobis intelligentia, propter peccata plerasque
evenire corporum debilitates. Et idcirco forsan dimittuntur prius peccata tua,
ut causis debilitatis ablatis, sanitas restituatur}. Many bodily weaknesses come
of sin, and perhaps the sins are forgiven first so that, the cause of the
weakness taken away, health may be restored. St Thomas Aquinas makes the same
point without Jerome's hesitation: the sins are forgiven first \latin{quia
peccatum erat causa aegritudinis}, and God did as a good physician does,
\latin{qui causam curat} (\work{Super Matthaeum} c.~9).

\subsection{Son and man: who forgives, and where}

St Augustine, harmonising the Gospels, finds in one word of address the whole
point of the episode. Matthew's Christ says ``Son'', Luke's ``Man''; the
difference, far from a discrepancy, brings out the meaning more fully: ``For
these sins were [thus said to be] forgiven to the `man,' inasmuch as the very
fact that he was a man would make it impossible for him to say, `I have not
sinned;' and at the same time, that mode of address served to indicate that He
who forgave sins to man was Himself God'' (\work{De consensu evangelistarum}
II.25.57). One word establishes that the patient is every man and that the
physician is not a man only. Chrysostom and Augustine reach the same conclusion
by different roads, Chrysostom from the order of the healings and the reading
of hearts, Augustine from Luke's \latin{homo} beside Matthew's \latin{fili}:
the episode is a demonstration
that the one who forgives is God.

St Thomas Aquinas presses the words of verse 6 one by one. Christ confutes the
scribes \latin{sua cognitione, verbo, et facto}, by his knowledge, his word and
his deed, for \latin{sicut soli Deo pertinet dimittere peccata, sic cognoscere
secreta cordis}. The phrase \latin{Filius hominis} excludes Nestorius, who
divided the Son of man from the Son of God, since it says of the one what
belongs to the other. The phrase \latin{in terra} excludes Photinus, who held
that Christ began from the Virgin and earned his divinity, and who appealed to
``All power is given to me in heaven and in earth'' (Mt 28:18): the power to
forgive belongs to Christ already on earth. Then Aquinas raises the objection
on which this reading turns. The verse seems not to prove Christ's divinity,
\latin{quia etiam ipsi Apostoli habebant potestatem}, since the Apostles too
had the power to forgive sins; and he answers, \latin{Sed dicendum, quod ipsi
habebant per viam administrationis, non auctoritatis}: they had it by way of
ministry, not of authority (\work{Super Matthaeum} c.~9). The distinction
between Christ's authority and the Apostles' ministry is drawn at the very
verse that says \latin{in terra}, and it is drawn to keep the authority
Christ's.

\subsection{What the crowd said: ``such power to men''}

The Gospel ends with the crowds, who ``feared, and glorified God that gave such
power to men''. Chrysostom hears in the words a confession that falls short.
Having shown himself Creator of souls and bodies, Christ ``makes the invisible
evident by that which is in sight. But nevertheless they still creep upon the
earth''; ``the flesh was an offense unto them''. He does not rebuke them but
raises their thoughts by his works, ``Since for the time it was no small thing
for Him to be thought greater than all men, as having come from God. For had
they well established these things in their own minds, going on orderly they
would have known, that He was even the Son of God. But they did not retain
these things clearly'' (\work{Homilies on Matthew} 29, 2). St Ambrose, at
Luke's parallel, judges the fear more severely. Those who watch the man rise
are unbelievers, \latin{Spectant surgentem increduli}, and \latin{divini
operis miracula malunt timere quam credere}, they would rather fear the
miracles of the divine work than believe them. Had they believed, they would
not have feared but loved, and so \latin{quia non diligebant,
calumniabantur}: because they did not love, they calumniated
(\work{Expositio in Lucam} V.15). Chrysostom's crowd glorify God and fall
short; Ambrose's fearers are the calumniators. St Hilary judges otherwise. For him all things have been concluded in their
order, \latin{conclusa sunt omnia suo ordine}, and the honour the crowd pays is
fitting; yet the power was owed to Christ alone, \latin{soli de communione
paternae substantiae}, and the men for whom God is glorified are those who
receive the forgiveness, not those who give it (\work{Commentarius in
Matthaeum} VIII.8; PL 9, cols.~961--962). St Jerome passes from the risen man carrying his
bed straight to the call of Matthew. Aquinas notes that it is the crowds and
not the scribes who fear and give glory, \latin{quia omnia in Deum retulerunt}.

Schuster, commenting on this Mass, gives the verse three senses in order. The
crowd's words ``may be understood as a subjective judgement on the part of the
people who had not yet grasped the divine nature of Christ'', which is
Chrysostom's reading. ``The words, however, have a still deeper meaning'': the
divine nature worked the miracle through the human nature united to it. And
``Further, the Gospel narrative \dots\ is both symbolical and prophetic. This
power of remitting sins had to be communicated to men---that is, to the
apostles and to their successors in the priesthood; consequently, the Holy
Ghost widens the field of this evangelizing energy, and the crowd at once
rejoices that men also have received from heaven such truly divine authority''
(\work{The Sacramentary} III, p.~169). The continuation of \work{The Liturgical
Year}, also commenting on this Mass, invites the faithful ``to meditate upon the
prerogative which these same men'', the Church's pastors, ``have of forgiving
sins and healing souls'', and it reads the paralytic as the human race, brought
to the Church by the apostles' faith, to which the Church, speaking as a
mother, says ``Be of good heart, son! thy sins are forgiven thee!'': ``And the
angels, beholding such a spectacle of conversion and holiness, are in
amazement, and sing glory to God, who gave such power to men'' (vol.~XI,
pp.~403, 405).

\subsection{Moses at the altar, and the blood of the covenant}

The Offertory takes the Mass from the house in Christ's own city to the foot of Sinai.
Its Moses hallows an altar, offers holocausts, immolates victims and makes the
evening sacrifice, and the words that describe him are not those of Exodus
24:4--5 but the language of consecration and of the daily evening offering.
The chapter it cites goes on to the blood. Moses pours half upon the altar and
sprinkles the people with the rest, saying ``This is the blood of the
covenant, which the Lord hath made with you'' (Ex 24:6--8).

St John Chrysostom expounds that sacrifice as the Letter to the Hebrews tells
it (Heb 9:18--22), and joins it to the remission of sins. ``Tell me then why is
the book of the testament sprinkled, and also the people, except on account of
the precious blood, figured from the first?'' The old rites purged ``almost''
all things, ``Because those [ordinances] were not a perfect purification, nor a
perfect remission, but half-complete and in a very small degree. But in this
case He says, This is the blood of the New Testament, which is shed for you,
for the remission of sins.'' And again: ``With this blood not Moses but Christ
sprinkled us, through the word which was spoken''. There the body was cleansed
outwardly; ``but here, since the purifying is spiritual, it enters into the
soul, and cleanses it, not being simply sprinkled over, but gushing forth in our
souls'' (\work{Homilies on Hebrews} 16, 3--5). Chrysostom is expounding Hebrews
and not the Offertory's verses, but his reading joins Moses' covenant sacrifice
to the forgiveness of sins that the Gospel proclaims. Cornelius a Lapide,
commenting on Exodus~24 itself, reads the twelve \latin{tituli} as the twelve
tribes' share in the covenant sacrifice, the young men who offered as the
firstborn who were priests before Aaron, and the covenant blood as the type of
the blood by which Christ sealed the new covenant and which he named in
instituting the Eucharist (\work{Commentaria in Pentateuchum}, on Ex
24:4--8).

The antiphon once had verses that made Moses an intercessor for sinners.
Schuster regrets that the Roman Missal has cut the Offertory down to its
antiphon, and describes the verses in which the lawgiver ``intercedes for the
apostate people, imploring mercy for them''. The continuation prints the
verses in full and calls Moses ``the model of those true leaders of God's
people, who devote themselves in order to procure mercy and peace for those
whom they guide'' (vol.~XI, pp.~406--408). The 1962 Missal sings only the
antiphon, and in the antiphon Moses sanctifies and offers.

\subsection{The exchange and the courts}

The Secret says what the altar is for. \latin{Per huius sacrificii veneranda
commercia}, through the venerable exchanges of this sacrifice, God makes us
sharers in his one supreme Godhead, and the verb is present, \latin{efficis}.
Schuster explains the exchange: ``whilst we offer him our gifts he, in his
turn, bestows on us the gift of himself''. The prayer claims for a visible rite
an invisible effect, as the Gospel's sign claimed an invisible forgiveness.

At the Communion three witnesses read \latin{Tollite hostias} of the Church's
sacrifice offered by its priests. Theodoret reads the sacrifices as the
rational sacrifices that ``we see continually offered and sacrificed by the
priests'', and the plural courts as the churches, with the prophecy of
Malachias of a clean oblation among the nations (\work{Interpretatio in
Psalmos}, in Ps.~95; PG 80, cols.~1647--1648). The expositions printed under
St Athanasius's name call the ``families'' of the psalm the leaders of the
churches, ``priests, I mean, and presidents'', who offer spiritual sacrifices in
God's courts (PG 27, cols.~415--416). St Robert Bellarmine gives the contrite
heart first and then adds: ``This may also apply to the Eucharistic sacrifice,
that took the place of all the Jewish sacrifices, according to the prophecy of
Malachy, and which is offered \dots\ to God, by the converted gentiles, through
the hands of the priests of the New Testament''. Augustine and Cassiodorus read
the same \latin{hostiae} as the contrite heart and name no altar; their reading
belongs to the third reading of this Mass. The continuation holds both
together: the Communion ``is addressed to the priests, and, at the same time,
to us all: for if the priest offers the Victim, which is the holiest that can
be, we should not think of accompanying him into the court of our God, without
bringing up, that they may be united to the divine Host, other victims, that is
ourselves'' (vol.~XI, pp.~408--409).

\subsection{The other elements in this reading}

The Introit asks as a servant asks, \latin{exaudi preces servi tui}, and claims
nothing. The Collect asks that God's mercy do the directing; the authority at
work in the Mass is not ours. The Epistle gives thanks for gifts given ``in
Christ Jesus'' and for the testimony of Christ confirmed among the Corinthians,
and the continuation of \work{The Liturgical Year}, reading it after the
September Ember ordinations, hears in those gifts ``the powers conferred by the
imposition of the bishop's hands on the ministers of the Church'' (vol.~XI,
p.~394). The Gradual's \latin{in domum Domini ibimus} stands beside the
Gospel's \latin{vade in domum tuam}: the forgiven man is sent to his house, and
the Church goes up to God's. Aquinas, reading the command morally, sends the
forgiven sinner \latin{in domum aeternitatis, vel in conscientiam propriam},
into the house of eternity, or into his own conscience. Ambrose, at the same
command in Luke, reads the house as paradise: \latin{domum suam repetere, hoc
est, ad paradisum redire \dots\ ea est enim vera domus, quae hominem prima
suscepit: non jure amissa, sed fraude}, the true home that first received man,
lost not by right but by fraud (\work{Expositio in Lucam} V.14). Hilary makes
the return home the last step of an order: Christ first grants the remission
of sin, then shows the power of resurrection, then in the lifted bed teaches
that weakness and pain will leave the body, and last, in the man's return to
his own house, shows that the way into paradise, which Adam left, is to be
given back to believers (\work{Commentarius in Matthaeum} VIII.7; PL 9,
col.~961). The Alleluia's \latin{Timebunt gentes} widens to the nations the
fear of the crowds who saw the paralytic walk, \latin{timuerunt}. The
Postcommunion gives thanks for a gift by which the communicants have been
quickened, \latin{sacro munere vegetati}, a present effect claimed for what
they have received, and still asks that they be made worthy of it.

\subsection{Where the ministerial sense stands, and where it does not}

The weightiest evidence against this reading's most obvious form comes from
the Fathers. Read quickly, the crowd's ``such power to men''
names the Church's power to absolve, and the Offertory's altar and the Secret's
exchange are where that power acts. Of the Fathers read here, none reads verse
8 in that way. Chrysostom hears the crowd's words as a confession that fell
short of Christ's divinity; Hilary hears in them the many who receive
forgiveness, not those who minister it; Jerome does not comment on the words at
all; Augustine's harmony does not reach them; and Luke's parallel, on which
Ambrose comments, does not contain them.

One Father does connect Christ's forgiving with men to whom the power is given,
and at this healing. Ambrose, commenting on Luke's ``Which is easier to say'',
recalls the scribes' question and answers it in a parenthesis: \latin{quis
enim potest peccata dimittere nisi solus Deus, qui per eos quoque dimittit,
quibus dimittendi tribuit potestatem}, who can forgive sins but God alone, who
forgives also through those to whom he has given the power to forgive
(\work{Expositio in Lucam} V.13). He says it in order to rank the forgiving of
sins below the raising of bodies, and he says it of the scribes' question, the
parallel of Matthew's verse 3, not of the crowd's words; St Bede takes the
clause into his own commentary on Luke. Below the Fathers the sense stands in
several places. Aquinas states it at verse 6, where the Apostles forgive
\latin{per viam administrationis, non auctoritatis}. Schuster, a Blessed, reads
verse 8 itself of the power communicated ``to the apostles and to their
successors in the priesthood'', as the third of his senses and after the
crowd's inadequate confession. The continuation reads the present Gospel of the
pastors' prerogative of forgiving sins, which it names as the power of the keys
exercised in the sacrament of Penance, and applies the verse's closing words to
the Church's power allegorically. At the Communion's psalm Theodoret, the Greek
expositions and Bellarmine read the sacrifices of the Church's priests.

Not every commentator on these chants reads them of the ministry. Honorius and
Sicard read them of the people returning from the Babylonian captivity, who must
build an altar as Moses built one; St Anthony of
Padua, preaching this Gospel as a Sunday Gospel, gives its last verse no
ministerial sense.

What the witnesses support together is this. The Gospel establishes that the
authority to forgive is exercised on earth, visibly, by one who is also man.
Ambrose, glossing the scribes' question, says that God forgives also through
those to whom he has given the power. Aquinas says at verse 6 that the Apostles
exercise it by ministry and not by authority. Schuster reads the crowd's
praise, in its prophetic sense, of that power communicated to men. And the
Mass's own prayers claim a present effect for a visible rite: \latin{efficis}
in the Secret, \latin{vegetati} in the Postcommunion. The altar is not where
that power absolves: the continuation places absolution in the keys and in
Penance. What the Offertory and the Secret concern is the sacrifice whose
blood, in the words of institution, is shed ``for the remission of sins'', and
through that blood alone the altar meets the Gospel's power.

A simpler reading of the same texts is possible. The Offertory's Moses may be
heard as pure figure, recalled at the offering because the Church's sacrifice
fulfils his, with no claim about authority at all, and the Gospel's power as
Christ's alone, remembered in the Mass. That reading and this one differ over
the two words \latin{in terra}. This reading takes them as a statement about
where the power is exercised, and hears the Mass's altar as a place on earth.

The Pustet Missal of 1862 already sets this Gospel beside this Offertory, as
the 1962 book does, and Schuster and the continuation expound them together.

\subsection{The four senses of this reading}

\begin{fourSenses}
\item[Literal.] Jesus, come to his own city, is brought a paralytic; he forgives
the man's sins and then, so that the scribes who think him a blasphemer may know
that the Son of man has power on earth to forgive, tells him to rise, take up
his bed and go home. The visible command proves the invisible word (Chrysostom,
Jerome). At Sinai Moses builds an altar, offers holocausts and victims, and
seals the covenant with blood sprinkled on the altar and on the people.

\item[Allegorical.] The one who forgives the man is God, as the word ``Son''
beside Luke's ``Man'' shows (Augustine), and he has the authority as his own
(Chrysostom). The altar of the covenant is a figure of the one where the blood
of the New Testament is poured out ``for the remission of sins'', with which
``not Moses but Christ sprinkled us'' (Chrysostom on Hebrews); its blood is the
type of the blood Christ named at the institution of the Eucharist (a Lapide).
The power shown in his own city is exercised in the Church by way of ministry and
not of authority (Aquinas), and the crowd's praise foretells its communication
to the apostles and their successors (Schuster).

\item[Moral.] The sinner is brought, laid down, and given one command,
\latin{Surge, tolle lectum tuum}. Aquinas makes it penitential: \latin{Surge, a
peccato per contritionem; tolle lectum, per satisfactionem}, and go home into
your own conscience. St Bede, on Luke's telling,
reads the taking up of the bed as the flesh itself curbed by the reins of
continence and, in hope of heavenly rewards, deprived of earthly delights
(\work{In Lucam} II, on 5:24). The Communion bids those who enter the courts bring
with the priest's Victim ``other victims, that is ourselves'' (the continuation
of \work{The Liturgical Year}).

\item[Anagogical.] The power is exercised \latin{in terra}, and the forgiven
man is sent home. Hilary reads the command as the last step of the cure's
order: the remission of sins, the power of resurrection, the end of the body's
weakness and pain, and last the way into paradise, which Adam left, given back
to believers. Ambrose calls that home the true one, which first received man
and was lost not by right but by fraud; Aquinas calls it the house of
eternity. Forgiveness given at a place on earth is the first step of the way
home.
\end{fourSenses}
