\section{Peace Asked For, and Power Given to Men}
\label{sec:opening}

The Eighteenth Sunday after Pentecost, \latin{Dominica decima octava post
Pentecosten}, is a Sunday of the second class in the Proper of Time of the 1962
\work{Missale Romanum}. Its Mass stands on pp.~410--411 of the typical
edition, under the marginal numbers 1669--1678, immediately after the Mass of
the Ember Saturday of September. In 2026 it falls on 27~September, the Sunday
after the September Ember Days of the 23rd, 25th and 26th. The Mass is said in
green, with Gloria and Credo, and with the Preface of the Most Holy Trinity,
which the Missal appoints for the Sundays of the second class outside the
Christmas and Easter seasons. The calendar's feast of Saints Cosmas and Damian,
a feast of the third class, gives way entirely: a Sunday of the second class
admits no commemoration but that of a feast of the second class
(\work{Rubricae generales} 111~b; \work{Rubricae generales Missalis} 434~b),
and the Mass has a single oration of each kind.

The Sunday's place after the Ember Days has shaped how its Mass was read.
Bl.\ Ildefonso Schuster heads it \latin{Tertia post natale Sancti Cypriani},
the third Sunday after St Cyprian's feast, and explains that at Rome it
``should be styled Dominica vacat'': the Ember Saturday's night vigil ended
with the Mass that served as the Sunday's own, and outside Rome, where that
vigil was not kept in the Roman way, ``the Dominica vacat of the Roman
Sacramentaries soon received \dots\ its own liturgical formulary''. The
continuation of \work{The Liturgical Year} (Dom Lucien Fromage) reads the same
Mass in the light of the ordinations that the Ember Saturday brought: the gifts
for which the Epistle gives thanks, it writes, are above all ``the powers
conferred by the imposition of the bishop's hands on the ministers of the
Church''.

The Mass opens with a request for something it does not define. \latin{Da
pacem, Domine, sustinentibus te}: the Introit asks peace for those who wait on
the Lord. Its psalm verse is a pilgrim's joy, ``I rejoiced at the things that were said to me: We
shall go into the house of the Lord'', and the same verse returns as the
Gradual, now joined to a prayer for peace in the city's strength and
abundance in its towers. The Alleluia sings of nations and kings who will fear
the Lord's name. The Communion bids the worshippers bring up sacrifices and
come into his courts. Between these chants stand two lessons. The Epistle is
St Paul's thanksgiving for the grace given to a divided church at Corinth,
rich in utterance and knowledge and waiting for the day of the Lord. The Gospel
is the healing of the paralytic whom others brought to Jesus. The Lord forgives
his sins before he heals his body, so that those who doubt may know ``that the
Son of man hath power on earth to forgive sins''. The Offertory recalls Moses
hallowing an altar and offering sacrifice before the children of Israel; the
Secret names the \latin{veneranda commercia} of the sacrifice, the holy
exchange by which God makes us sharers in his one supreme Godhead; and the
Postcommunion gives thanks for the gift received and asks to be made worthy of
it. The Mass has one Collect, and it gives as its reason what the whole
formulary assumes: \latin{quia tibi sine te placere non possumus}. In the
English of the 1861 Philadelphia missal, ``for without thy help we can do
nothing well pleasing to thee''; in the Latin, the one without whom we cannot
please God is God himself, \latin{sine te}.

The day's texts thus put three questions to those who pray them. What is the
peace for which the Introit asks, and where is it found? What is the power
that the Gospel shows, and where is it exercised now? And what, in all this,
is the part of the one who prays, if without God he cannot please God? The
three readings of the formulary that follow take up these questions in turn.
The first hears the Mass from its chants, as a single ascent from the prayer
for peace to the entry into the Lord's courts, and finds the peace of the
city in the charity of which it is built. The second hears it from the
Gospel, as the Son of man's power on earth to forgive, proved in his own city and
brought to bear at the altar that the Offertory's Moses prefigures. The third
hears it from the Collect, as a Mass that gives thanks at both ends for what
no one in it earned, with a man at its centre who was carried. Each is built
from what the Fathers and saints taught about the particular texts; each is
carried through every element of the formulary; and each closes with its own
literal, allegorical, moral and anagogical senses. A short comparison then
shows what the three hold in common and where their answers part.

\subsection{The appointed elements at a glance}

\begin{maptable}
Introit & \latin{Da pacem, Domine}; cited Ecclus 36:18; verse Ps 121:1 &
Peace for those who wait on the Lord, that his prophets may be found
faithful, and a hearing for the prayers of his servant and of his people
Israel. The verse: the pilgrim's joy at going into the house of the Lord.\\
Collect & \latin{Dirigat corda nostra} &
That the working of God's mercy may direct our hearts, because without him
we cannot please him.\\
Epistle & 1 Cor 1:4--8 &
Paul thanks God for the grace given to the Corinthians in Christ, their riches
in utterance and knowledge, and the testimony confirmed among them; nothing is
wanting to them as they wait for the Lord's revelation, and he will confirm
them to the end, without crime, in the day of his coming.\\
Gradual & \latin{Laetatus sum}; Ps 121:1, 7 &
The Introit's verse again: joy at going into the house of the Lord. The
versicle: peace in the city's strength, abundance in its towers.\\
Alleluia & \latin{Timebunt gentes}; Ps 101:16 &
``The Gentiles shall fear thy name, O Lord, and all the kings of the earth
thy glory.''\\
Gospel & Mt 9:1--8 &
Jesus crosses to his own city; a paralytic is brought on a bed; seeing their
faith, Jesus forgives the man's sins; to the scribes who think ``He
blasphemeth'' he shows that the Son of man has power on earth to forgive, and
tells the man to rise, take up his bed and go home; the crowds fear and
glorify God, ``that gave such power to men''.\\
Offertory & \latin{Sanctificavit Moyses}; cited Ex 24:4--5 &
Moses hallows an altar to the Lord and offers holocausts, victims and an
evening sacrifice upon it, in the sight of the children of Israel. A compiled
antiphon, not a quotation of the cited verses.\\
Secret & \latin{Deus, qui nos, per huius sacrificii} &
God makes us partakers of his one supreme Godhead through the venerable
exchanges of this sacrifice; may we pursue by worthy conduct the truth we
know.\\
Communion & \latin{Tollite hostias}; Ps 95:8--9 &
``Bring up sacrifices, and come into his courts: Adore ye the Lord in his holy
court.''\\
Postcommunion & \latin{Gratias tibi referimus} &
Thanks for the sacred gift that has quickened us, and a plea that God's mercy
make us worthy of our share in it.\\
\end{maptable}
