\section{Each Element in Its Own Setting}
\label{sec:each-element}

The ten elements of this Mass come from several books. The Introit's antiphon
is cited to Ecclesiasticus and the Offertory to Exodus; the Introit's verse,
the Gradual, the Alleluia and the Communion are taken from three psalms; the
lessons are St Paul's first letter to Corinth and St Matthew's Gospel; and the
three orations stand together in the Gelasian and Gregorian sacramentaries.

\subsection{Introit: peace for those who wait}

The antiphon is cited to Ecclus 36:18, the close of a prayer that fills the
whole first part of the chapter. The son of Sirach asks God to have mercy on
``thy people, upon whom thy name is invoked'', to ``have mercy on Jerusalem,
the city which thou hast sanctified, the city of thy rest'', to ``fill Sion''
with his words and his people with his glory, and to ``raise up the
prophecies which the former prophets spoke in thy name'' (36:14--17). The
verse the Missal cites follows, and it asks God to reward those who wait for
him, ``that thy prophets may be found faithful''. The chant keeps that clause
exactly and changes its frame. It asks \latin{pacem} where the Vulgate asks
\latin{mercedem} and the Greek a reward; it adds \latin{Domine}; and it prays
for \latin{servi tui, et plebis tuae Israel}, one servant and the people, where
the Vulgate has \latin{servorum tuorum}. The words \latin{Da pacem} stand in no
verse of the Clementine Vulgate, and \latin{plebis tuae Israel} only in
Simeon's canticle (Lk 2:32). The nearest scriptural shape for the closing
petition is Solomon's prayer at the dedication of the Temple, that God
``hearken \dots\ to the prayers of thy servant, and of thy people Israel'' (2 Par
6:21; compare 3 Kings 8:30), though neither verse is the chant's wording.

The psalm verse is the opening of Ps~121, a Song of Ascents, the pilgrim's
joy at the call to go up to the house of the Lord. The same verse returns as
the Gradual's respond, so that it is sung twice in the Mass.

The continuation of \work{The Liturgical Year} notes that from this Sunday the
Introits of the season cease to follow the Psalter and are taken from other
books of the Old Testament, with one exception. The liturgical commentators on
the Mass do not read the antiphon alike. Rupert of Deutz hears
in it the pastor's plea for the forgiveness of his people's sins: \latin{pax
enim Dei, peccatorum remissio est}. William Durandus and the continuation
identify the prophets as the Church's pastors (\latin{Prophetae tui, id est,
Praelati}; ``the pastors, whom the Church sends''). Honorius and Sicard hear
the voice of Daniel praying for the people's return from captivity, and
Honorius, in a second reading, the prayer of St Gregory the Great for peace
between the Romans and the Lombards. Schuster reads the prophets' promises
spiritually, of the salvation that grace brings, against any expectation
``after the manner of the Jews and the ancient Millenarians''.

\subsection{Collect: the working of mercy that directs the heart}

The Collect asks that \latin{tuae miserationis operatio}, the working of God's
mercy, may direct our hearts, and it gives the reason in a clause as short as
any in the Missal: \latin{quia tibi sine te placere non possumus}. The one we
are to please is God, and the one without whom we cannot please him is God
himself: the clause names no other help. The 1861 English and the
continuation's English both render \latin{sine te} ``without thy help''.

The prayer is older than any Sunday number the Missal gives it. In the Old
Gelasian Sacramentary it opens a Mass headed only \latin{Item alia missa}
(Book~III, sect.~XIV, in H.~A. Wilson's edition), with a second collect beside
it and with this Sunday's Secret and Postcommunion. Wilson's apparatus
reports that the Rheinau and St Gall books and Gerbert's edition assign that
Mass to the twenty-second Sunday after Pentecost, and Pamelius to the
nineteenth; the Gregorian sacramentary in Wilson's edition sets the whole
oration-set at the Nineteenth Sunday (sect.~CXXXVII); the 1962 Missal sets it
at the Eighteenth. Berno of Reichenau, in the eleventh
century, explains the Collect from the Communion that follows it: because we
can please God neither in sacrifice nor in the offering of victims without his
help, \latin{nec in atria ejus introire, nec in aula ejus adorare eum}, the
priest rightly prays \latin{Dirigat corda nostra}. Schuster reads it as the
confession that ``All the good which we do is the work of grace'', and adds
St Paul's question, \latin{quid gloriaris, quasi non acceperis?} (1 Cor 4:7).

\subsection{Epistle: thanksgiving for a grace already given}

The lesson is the thanksgiving with which St Paul opens his first letter to
Corinth, written from Ephesus (1 Cor 16:8) to a church he had founded. He
thanks God ``always for you, for the grace of God that is given you in Christ
Jesus'': they are ``made rich in him, in all utterance and in all knowledge'',
the testimony of Christ was confirmed among them, and nothing is wanting to
them in any grace as they wait for the revelation of the Lord, who ``will
confirm you unto the end without crime, in the day of the coming of our Lord
Jesus Christ''. The lesson stops one verse before \latin{Fidelis Deus} and two
before the appeal that follows it: ``that there be no schisms among you''
(1:10), for ``every one of you saith: I indeed am of Paul; and I am of Apollo;
and I of Cephas; and I of Christ'' (1:12). The name of Christ stands in four of
the lesson's five verses.

Its readers divide twice. St John Chrysostom hears the thanksgiving as a
physician's approach to a divided church, praise under which he ``touches them
sharply'', and he hears in verse 8 a covert accusation; Theodoret and
Theophylact agree that the last verse shows the Corinthians still liable to
charges. Ambrosiaster, St Thomas Aquinas and Cornelius a Lapide read verse 8 as
a promise to a body most of whose members were sound, and they divide the
praise between the sound and the unsound where Chrysostom addresses it to the
whole church. On one point all agree: the gifts for which Paul gives thanks
were given, and given by God.

The lesson breaks into the series of Sunday lessons from the Letter to the
Ephesians, which resumes on the Nineteenth Sunday. Schuster and the
continuation both note the interruption, and the continuation reads the
Epistle's gifts, after the Ember ordinations, as the powers given to the
Church's ministers. In the Würzburg epistle list, which Germain Morin published
as the oldest lectionary of the Roman Church, the lesson already stands with
the 1962's exact extent, as the second lesson under \latin{In nat. angeli},
immediately after the lessons of the September Ember Saturday. Morin notes that
the pericope does not seem to belong specially to the dedication of St
Michael, and that Alcuin's lectionary, like the Roman Missal of his own day,
assigns it to the Sunday after the Ember Days.

\subsection{Gradual: the house of the Lord and the peace of the city}

The Gradual sings verses 1 and 7 of Ps~121, the third of the fifteen Songs of
Ascents (Pss~119--133 in the Missal's numbering). Between the two verses the
psalm sets the pilgrims' feet in the courts of Jerusalem, describes the city
``built as a city, which is compact together'', and names the tribes that go
up ``to praise the name of the Lord'' and the seats of judgment ``upon the
house of David'' (vv.~2--5). It bids them ``pray ye for the things that are
for the peace of Jerusalem'' (v.~6); verse 7 is that prayer; and the psalm
ends ``Because of the house of the Lord our God, I have sought good things for
thee'' (v.~9).

The witnesses divide over what city the psalm is about, and this division runs
through the first reading. St Augustine, St Hilary of Poitiers and Cassiodorus
read the heavenly Jerusalem built of living stones, and Hilary refuses the
earthly city in terms. St John Chrysostom and Theodoret read the earthly
Jerusalem rebuilt after the Babylonian captivity, and nothing further. St
Robert Bellarmine gives both, the return from exile first and the heavenly
city as the higher sense, and Schuster, commenting on this Mass, reads the
Gradual in the same order. Augustine's own psalter read the first verse
differently from the Missal: \latin{Jucundatus sum in his qui dixerunt mihi},
I rejoiced in those who said to me, where the Missal sings \latin{Laetatus sum
in his, quae dicta sunt mihi}, in the things that were said.

On the second clause of verse 7 the readings multiply. For Augustine and
Cassiodorus the city's \latin{virtus} is charity; for Hilary it is the
firmness that the Church's peace and unity confer. The towers are the martyrs
for Cassiodorus, the steadfast among the faithful for Hilary, and for Augustine
the city's \latin{excelsi}, the few who will sit in judgment and receive the
many into the eternal tabernacles. Chrysostom and Theodoret take the walls,
towers and palaces as the ruined city's own.

The gregorien.info database, reporting the six manuscripts of the
\work{Antiphonale Missarum Sextuplex}, gives \latin{Laetatus sum} as the
Gradual of the Eighteenth Sunday after Pentecost in four of them, the books of
Rheinau, Mont-Blandin, Corbie and Senlis. The same four give this Sunday's
Offertory and Communion, and three of them its Introit.

\subsection{Alleluia: the nations and the building of Sion}

The Alleluia verse is Ps~101:16. The psalm is headed ``The prayer of the poor
man, when he was anxious, and poured out his supplication before the Lord'',
and its lament turns to hope at verse 14: ``Thou shalt arise and have mercy on
Sion: for it is time to have mercy on it''. Verse 15 speaks of the servants who
love Sion's stones; verse 16, the Alleluia, of the Gentiles and kings who will
fear the Lord's name; verse 17 gives the reason, ``For the Lord hath built up
Sion: and he shall be seen in his glory''. The Missal drops the connective
\latin{Et} and ends the verse before that reason.

The direct witnesses agree on the structure of the verse: the fear of the
nations follows from the building of Sion, and Sion is the Church. Augustine
hears ``another wall'' approaching from the heathen to meet the first at the
Corner Stone. Cassiodorus hears the nations turning from their idols
\latin{per timorem}, and names the building \latin{mater Ecclesia, de vivis
lapidibus fabricata}. The Greek expositions printed under St Athanasius's name
in Migne make Sion the Church. Theodoret gives the verse a first sense in the
return from Babylon and then says that it was fulfilled ``properly and truly''
only after the Incarnation. Bellarmine refuses the historical reading
altogether.

In the Sextuplex graduals, as gregorien.info reports them, this Sunday's
alleluias are other verses, and \latin{Timebunt gentes} stands in all six
books as a Gradual at other Sundays.

\subsection{Gospel: the paralytic forgiven and healed}

The Gospel is Mt 9:1--8. It follows the healing of the two possessed men ``in
the country of the Gerasens'' (8:28), whose townsmen ``besought him that he
would depart from their coasts'' (8:34), and it is followed at once by the call of Matthew
at the custom house (9:9). Jesus crosses the lake ``and came into his own
city''. Men bring a paralytic lying on a bed; seeing their faith, Jesus says to
the sick man, ``Be of good heart, son, thy sins are forgiven thee''. Some of
the scribes say within themselves, ``He blasphemeth''; Jesus, seeing their
thoughts, asks which is easier to say, ``Thy sins are forgiven thee'' or
``Arise, and walk'', and to show ``that the Son of man hath power on earth to
forgive sins'' he bids the man rise, take up his bed and go home. The man
does so, and the crowds fear and glorify God ``that gave such power to men''.
St Mark and St Luke tell the same story more fully (Mk 2:1--12; Lk 5:17--26).

Three questions in the text divide its interpreters, and each returns in the
readings below. Which city is ``his own''? Chrysostom says Capernaum; St Jerome
says Nazareth. Augustine, reconciling Matthew with Mark, takes ``his own city''
first as Nazareth and places the healing at Capernaum, with two explanations:
Galilee may be called Christ's city, Capernaum being its chief town, or Matthew
has passed over what happened between Christ's coming into his own city and his
reaching Capernaum (\work{De consensu evangelistarum} II.25.58). Aquinas
harmonises with three cities, Bethlehem by birth, Nazareth by upbringing and
Capernaum by dwelling, and St Hilary, who names no town, reads the city
allegorically as the people of the faithful. Whose faith did Jesus see? Jerome
says the bearers' and not the sick man's, and St Ambrose, on Luke's telling,
agrees; Chrysostom says that the sick man shared it. And what did the crowd mean by ``such
power to men''? Chrysostom hears a confession that fell short of the truth;
Hilary hears fitting praise for what men had received; and later Latin readers
give the words further senses.

The Gospel has also travelled. The seventh-century Roman gospel list of
Würzburg sets it on the third Sunday after St Cyprian's feast, the name
Schuster gives this Sunday. The Frankish comes of Murbach and the
\work{Liber Comitis} set it with a lesson from Ephesians, and set this Sunday's
Epistle with a Gospel from Mt~22 at a different Sunday. The Pustet Missal of
1862 prints this Gospel with this Offertory, as the 1962 book does, and Schuster and the continuation expound the Mass with it.

\subsection{Offertory: Moses at the altar}

The antiphon is cited to Ex 24:4--5 and is a compilation. The chapter narrates
the ratifying of the covenant at Sinai: Moses writes the words of the Lord,
rises in the morning, builds an altar at the foot of the mountain with
``twelve titles according to the twelve tribes of Israel'', and sends young
men to offer holocausts and pacific victims; he pours half the blood upon the
altar, reads the book of the covenant, and sprinkles the people with the rest: ``This is the blood of
the covenant, which the Lord hath made with you'' (24:4--8). The antiphon keeps
the altar, the holocausts and the victims. It says \latin{sanctificavit} where
Exodus says \latin{aedificavit}, a verb the Vulgate uses of Moses' altar at its
consecration (Lev 8:11; Num 7:1); it adds \latin{fecit sacrificium vespertinum
in odorem suavitatis}, the language of the evening lamb of the daily sacrifice
(Ex 29:41) and of the evening sacrifice of Ps 140:2; and its closing words,
\latin{in conspectu filiorum Israel}, stand at 24:17, where the glory of the
Lord burns on the mountain before the people. Neither the Vulgate nor the Greek
of verses 4--5 contains those clauses.

The antiphon once had verses. Rupert of Deutz gives the first, in which Moses
intercedes for his people after the golden calf, \latin{Obsecro, Domine,
dimitte peccata populi tui}; Berno names two; the continuation prints both,
the second being Moses' prayer to see God's glory. The 1962 Missal sings the
antiphon alone, and the covenant blood of the chapter it cites is taken up in
the second reading.

\subsection{Secret: the holy exchange}

The Secret says that God, \latin{per huius sacrificii veneranda commercia},
through the venerable exchanges of this sacrifice, makes us \latin{unius summae
divinitatis participes}, sharers in the one supreme Godhead, and asks that as
we know his truth, so we may attain it \latin{dignis moribus}, by a worthy
life. The verb \latin{efficis} is present: the prayer speaks of what the
sacrifice does now. The Old Gelasian carries the prayer in the same Mass as the
Collect, and again in a Mass of its first book; Wilson's apparatus shows that
the 1962's \latin{efficis}, \latin{cognoscimus} and \latin{dignis moribus}
without \latin{et mentibus} each stand in early witnesses of the prayer. On the
printed page the Secret is followed at once by the direction to say the
Preface of the Most Holy Trinity. What the exchange is, the second reading
takes up.

\subsection{Communion: the sacrifices and the courts}

The Communion takes the middle of Ps 95:8--9. The psalm is headed in the
Vulgate \latin{Canticum ipsi David, quando domus aedificabatur post
captivitatem}, ``when the house was built after the captivity'', and it calls
the whole earth and the kindreds of the Gentiles to sing a new song and bring
glory to the Lord. The antiphon begins after ``Bring to the Lord glory unto his
name'' and ends before ``Let all the earth be moved at his presence''. Where the
Vulgate reads \latin{in atrio sancto eius}, the Missal sings \latin{in aula
sancta eius}. Augustine and Cassiodorus both read \latin{atrio} in their
psalters. Berno of Reichenau, paraphrasing this Sunday's Communion in the
eleventh century, already writes \latin{in aula sancta ejus}; the 1862 Missal
prints \latin{aula}.

Augustine takes the psalm's title as the key to the whole: the house built
after the captivity is the Church, built of those redeemed from the captivity
of the devil. On the Communion's two clauses the witnesses agree that the
courts are the Church's, and divide over the sacrifices. For Augustine and
Cassiodorus they are the contrite heart and the pure offerings of conscience;
for Theodoret and the expositions under Athanasius's name they are the
spiritual sacrifices that the Church's priests offer in the churches; St
Robert Bellarmine gives both, and names the Eucharistic sacrifice in the
second place. Schuster sets the two covenants side by side: ``In the Old
Covenant it was the people who brought gifts to God in his temple, in the New
it is God who gives himself to his people.''

\subsection{Postcommunion: thanks, and a plea to be made worthy}

The Postcommunion begins \latin{Gratias tibi referimus}, thanks for the sacred
gift, \latin{sacro munere}, by which the communicants have been
\latin{vegetati}, quickened or refreshed, and it asks that God's mercy make
them worthy of their share in it: \latin{ut dignos nos eius participatione
perficias}. The Old Gelasian carries it twice: in the Mass it shares with the
Collect and Secret, and again as the Postcommunion of a Mass for the dedication
of a place that had been a synagogue (Book~I, sect.~XCIII). Schuster draws the
practical consequence of its plea: ``a fervent Communion \dots\ is the best
preparation for the next''. The Mass whose Epistle begins \latin{Gratias ago}
thus ends its prayers with \latin{Gratias tibi referimus}.
