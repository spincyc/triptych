\section{The Three Readings Compared}
\label{sec:comparison}

The three readings share more than they divide. All three need the Collect,
though only the third is governed by it. \latin{Quia tibi sine te placere non
possumus} is the Mass's single oration, and each reading has to find room for
it: the first because a city that no one can build for himself is still the
city to which the people go up; the second because the power at work in the
Mass is expressly not ours; the third because the Collect is its controlling
claim. All three hear the Communion as an entry rather than a conclusion, and
all three hear the Postcommunion asking for something still to come. All three
take the Offertory as it is sung, a compiled antiphon in which Moses sanctifies
and offers and the people are present, rather than as a quotation of Exodus.
And none of the three sets the crowd's praise at the end of the Gospel against
St John Chrysostom's reading of it, a confession that fell short of who Christ
is. The first and third readings give the verse no weight; the second takes
Chrysostom's reading first and the ministerial sense only where Aquinas gives it
at the verse before and Schuster in the third of his senses of this one, and
even Schuster gives the crowd's inadequate confession first.

They part on the questions below.

\begin{comparisontable}{Question}{The peace of a city}{Power on earth to forgive}{Thanks at both ends}
What the Mass is about & A city being built & A power being exercised & A gift
being acknowledged\\
Where it is anchored & The chants, above all Ps 121:1 sung twice & The Gospel,
Mt 9:6, with the Offertory and Secret & The Collect, with the Epistle's and
Postcommunion's thanks\\
What the Gospel gives & The boat and the city: Christ enters the people of the
faithful (Hilary, as Aquinas reports him) & The centre: the unseen forgiveness
proved by the seen healing (Chrysostom, Jerome) & The man who was carried, and
whose faith brought him (Jerome, Chrysostom)\\
What the Offertory gives & The people present at their own sacrifice, \latin{in
conspectu filiorum Israel} & The altar of the covenant, whose blood is joined to
the remission of sins (Chrysostom on Hebrews) & An antiphon in which every verb
is Moses'\\
What \latin{pax} is & The city's strength: charity (Augustine, Cassiodorus), or
the firmness its peace confers (Hilary) & Forgiveness, in Rupert's reading of
the chant & Not addressed directly\\
Principal witnesses & Augustine on three psalms; Hilary and Cassiodorus on
Ps~121 & Chrysostom and Augustine on the Gospel; Aquinas at Mt 9:6; Schuster at
Mt 9:8 & Chrysostom on 1 Corinthians; Augustine on Ps~95; Jerome and
Chrysostom on Mt 9:2\\
Where it is weakest & Chrysostom and Theodoret read its governing psalm of the
earthly city rebuilt, and nothing more & No Father reads Mt 9:8 of the
Church's ministry, and some commentators on the chants read them of the return
from exile & Pressed too far it becomes quietism\\
\end{comparisontable}

The readings are complementary emphases, not rival doctrines. They are three
governing questions put to one set of texts, and each accounts for something
the others leave aside. The first alone gives weight to the return of the
Introit's psalm verse as the Gradual, so that the house named at the entrance is named again
after the Epistle. The second alone gives a meaning to the placing, at the
offering of this Gospel, of a Mosaic altar and the covenant sacrifice. The
third alone gives weight to the thanksgiving at both ends of the Mass, the
Epistle's \latin{Gratias ago} and the Postcommunion's \latin{Gratias tibi
referimus}.

Where the readings genuinely conflict, the conflict is the Fathers' own, and it
concerns the Gospel's first verse. Christ ``came into his own city''. The first
reading needs that city left open, because what it takes from the Gospel is
Hilary's \latin{Dei civitas fidelium plebs est}: the boat is the Church and the
city Christ enters is the people of God. St Augustine's refusal to settle which
town is meant, Galilee as a whole with Capernaum its chief city, or a town
reached after events that Matthew passed over, leaves room for it. The second reading needs the city located, because its whole claim
is about where: the Son of man has this power \latin{in terra}, at a place,
before witnesses who can check the visible half of it. Chrysostom and
Jerome, on whom its reading of the healing rests, do locate the town, and
disagree about which one.
Chrysostom names Capernaum, ``that which had Him continually inhabiting it'';
St Jerome says flatly \latin{Civitatem ejus non aliam intelligimus quam
Nazareth} (PL 26, col.~54). St Thomas Aquinas holds both sides: he harmonises
the Gospels with three cities, Bethlehem by birth, Nazareth by upbringing and
Capernaum by dwelling and miracles, and he also reads the words allegorically,
\latin{in civitatem gentium, quae sibi datae sunt}. So the two readings want
incompatible things from the same clause, one wanting it unlocated so that it
can be the Church and the other wanting it located so that the power is
exercised somewhere. Neither is refuted by the evidence. The third reading takes no position on
the city. The dispute it carries is internal to it, Jerome against Chrysostom
on whose faith brought the paralytic, and it stays inside that reading rather
than between readings.

Each reading ends where the Mass sends its people. The first leaves them at the
courts, with the city still ahead. The second leaves them forgiven and sent
home, with a perfecting still to be asked. The third leaves them giving thanks
for a gift and asking to be made worthy of it. In all three the last word of the
formulary is the one the Postcommunion gives: \latin{ut dignos nos eius
participatione perficias}.
