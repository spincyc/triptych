\section{The Three Readings Compared}
\label{sec:comparison}

The three readings share more than they divide. All three need the Collect,
though only the third is governed by it. \latin{Quia tibi sine te placere non
possumus} is the Mass's single oration, and it has a place in each reading:
in the first because a city that no one can build for himself is still the
city to which the people go up; in the second because the power at work in the
Mass is expressly not ours; in the third because the Collect is its controlling
claim. All three hear the Communion as an entry rather than a conclusion. All
three hear the Postcommunion's \latin{ut dignos nos eius participatione
perficias} asking for something still to come: for the first, the courts have
been entered and the city is still ahead; for the second, the communicants who
have been quickened are still to be made worthy; for the third, even fitness for the
gift is asked of God. All three take the Offertory as it is sung, a compiled
antiphon in which Moses sanctifies and offers and the people are present,
rather than as a quotation of Exodus. And no Father read here takes a sense of
the Church's ministry from the crowd's praise at the end of the Gospel. The second reading, the only one that develops that sense, takes it
from four places: St Ambrose's gloss on the
scribes' question in Luke's telling (\work{Expositio in Lucam} V.13), Aquinas
at verse 6, the third of Schuster's senses of verse 8, and the continuation of
\work{The Liturgical Year}, which names the pastors' power of forgiving as the
keys exercised in Penance. The readings do not hear the crowd's praise itself
alike. The first does not use the verse; the second reports St John
Chrysostom's reading of it first; the third draws on St Hilary's.

They part on the questions below.

proved by the seen healing (Chrysostom, Jerome) & The man who was carried, and

No doctrine of one reading is denied by another; they differ in emphasis. They are three
governing questions put to one set of texts, and each accounts for something
the others leave aside. The first alone gives weight to the return of the
Introit's psalm verse as the Gradual, so that the house named at the entrance is named again
after the Epistle. The second alone gives a meaning to the placing, at the
offering of this Gospel, of a Mosaic altar and the covenant sacrifice. The
third alone gives weight to the thanksgiving at both ends of the Mass, the
Epistle's \latin{Gratias ago} and the Postcommunion's \latin{Gratias tibi
referimus}.

The Gospel's first verse shows how the readings relate. Christ ``came into his
own city'', and the three readings use the clause in ways that do not compete.
The first reads it allegorically, with St Hilary: rejected by the Gerasene town,
which figures the Jewish people and the law, Christ goes back to his own city,
and \latin{Deo civitas fidelium plebs est}, to God the city is the people of
the faithful (\work{Commentarius in Matthaeum} VIII.4; PL 9, col.~960). Hilary names no town, and his
allegory holds whichever town the literal sense names. The second reading takes
the clause as the literal place of a verifiable sign: the Son of man has this
power \latin{in terra}, before scribes who can watch the man walk. The third
does not use the clause at all. St Thomas Aquinas shows that the literal and
the allegorical uses stand together, for in one lecture he gives both the
three-city harmony, Bethlehem by birth, Nazareth by upbringing and Capernaum by
dwelling and miracles, and the allegory \latin{in civitatem gentium, quae sibi
datae sunt}. St Augustine, reconciling Matthew with Mark, places the healing at
Capernaum and offers two explanations of ``his own city'': Galilee may be
called Christ's city, with Capernaum its chief town, or Matthew has passed over
what happened between Christ's arrival in his own city and his coming to
Capernaum.

Most of the real disagreements among the witnesses stand inside single
readings. Which
town is meant divides St John Chrysostom, who names Capernaum, ``that which had
Him continually inhabiting it'', from St Jerome, who says \latin{Civitatem ejus
non aliam intelligimus quam Nazareth} (PL 26, col.~54); that dispute belongs to
the second reading, whose literal sense needs a place and is served by either
town. Whose faith Christ saw divides Jerome, and St Ambrose beside him on
Luke's telling, who say the bearers', from Chrysostom, who says that the sick
man shared it; that dispute belongs to the third reading, which rests on what
both sides grant, that the man did not come by himself. What Ps~121 is about
divides Chrysostom and Theodoret, who read the earthly Jerusalem rebuilt after
the exile, from Augustine, Hilary and Cassiodorus, who read the heavenly city;
that dispute belongs to the first reading, which takes the one as the psalm's
literal sense and the other as its allegory.

One disagreement runs between two readings. What the crowd's praise at the end
of the Gospel amounts to divides Chrysostom from St Hilary. Chrysostom hears a
confession that fell short of who Christ is: the crowd ``still creep upon the
earth'', and had not grasped that he was the Son of God. Ambrose, at Luke's
parallel, judges the fear more severely: those who fear are the unbelieving,
who would rather fear than believe, and he makes them one with those who
calumniated (\work{Expositio in Lucam} V.15). His judgement is negative, as
Chrysostom's is, but it is his own. Hilary hears fitting
honour, paid to God because a power and a way, of the remission of sins, of the
resurrection of bodies and of the return to heaven, have been given through
Christ's word to the many who receive them. In the second reading
Chrysostom's judgement comes first and Hilary's after it, and the reading's
own sense of the verse is neither Father's but Schuster's; Hilary shows there
that the men of the verse are those who receive forgiveness and not those
who minister it. The third reading takes Hilary's side, and the choice changes
where its Gospel ends. On Chrysostom's reading the Gospel closes on praise that
still falls short and must be raised. On Hilary's it closes as the Epistle
opens, with God glorified for what men have been given, and the third reading
hears in it the thanksgiving that stands at both ends of the Mass.

The readings' own answers part, in the table above, on what the Mass is
about, where it is anchored, and what the Gospel and the Offertory give.
Those are differences of governing emphasis.

Each reading ends where the Mass sends its people. The first leaves them at the
courts, with the city still ahead. The second leaves them forgiven and sent
home, with a perfecting still to be asked. The third leaves them giving thanks
for a gift and asking to be made worthy of it. In all three the last word of the
formulary is the one the Postcommunion gives: \latin{ut dignos nos eius
participatione perficias}.
