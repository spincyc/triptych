# Interpretations — Eighteenth Sunday after Pentecost (1962 Roman Rite)

Three proposed readings of the whole formulary, with the reasoning of the checked
witnesses behind each, the contribution of every appointed element, the four
senses, the strongest difficulty, and the concrete comparison between them. This
record is the interpretive audit for the canonical leaf
`liturgy/roman-rite/1962/propers/temporal/58-eighteenth-after-pentecost`. The
evidence is in `research/scope.md`; the text is in `propers/verified.md`.

## The one rule this record keeps

**A reading of the whole formulary is the editor's synthesis.** Every Father
named below commented on one text — a psalm, a Gospel pericope, an epistle, a
chapter of Hebrews — and none of them commented on this Mass. Where a paragraph
joins two elements, it says so, and the join is the editor's. Where a paragraph
reports what a Father said of his own passage, it gives the locus. The two are
never blended, and no Father is made to approve an arrangement he never saw.

Two further boundaries apply throughout and are not repeated at each occurrence:

- **Jerome is read at his own locus; Hilary on Matthew is still the Catena's.**
  Jerome's Gospel readings below come from *Comm. in Matth.* I at PL 26,
  cols. 53–56, read on the page images (`research/scope.md` § 3.5). Where the
  *Catena in Matthaeum* differs from PL 26 — and at this pericope it splices two
  lemmata and carries one sentence the commentary does not have there — the
  commentary governs and the difference is marked. Hilary's Matthew commentary has
  no registered route and was not reached; every reading attributed to him **on
  the Gospel** is Aquinas's report of him — in the *Catena* on vv. 1–2, in the
  lectura on v. 8 — and is labelled so.
- **Each psalm has five or six checked witnesses, in Latin and Greek, and they are
  named, not totalled.** Ps 121: Augustine, Cassiodorus, Hilary, Chrysostom,
  Theodoret, Bellarmine. Pss 95 and 101: Augustine, Cassiodorus, Theodoret, the
  PG 27 expositions under Athanasius's name, Bellarmine. **On Ps 121 they divide
  about what the psalm is about**: the Latins read the heavenly city, the Greeks
  the earthly Jerusalem rebuilt after the exile and nothing else, Bellarmine both
  (`research/scope.md` § 4.6). No reading below may say "the Fathers" about a
  psalm: it says which witnesses, and where they diverge it says that too
  (`research/scope.md` §§ 3.3, 3.8, 4.6, 10.2).
- **Two kinds of later witness are kept apart from the Fathers.** Aquinas,
  Bellarmine, Cornelius a Lapide and Theophylact are later exegetes read at their
  own loci, and St Anthony of Padua preaches this Gospel as a Sunday Gospel. The
  liturgical commentators — Bl. Ildefonso Schuster and the continuation of
  Guéranger's *Liturgical Year* on the 1962 Mass itself; Rupert of Deutz, Durandus,
  Honorius, Sicard and Berno on a Mass with this Epistle and these chants but
  another Gospel — are documented reception of the compilation, not exegesis of its
  passages (`research/scope.md` §§ 2.7, 3.11). Each is named as what he is wherever
  he is used, and a commentator whose Mass had another Gospel is cited only for the
  elements his Mass shares with this one. **The *Liturgical Year* volume read here is
  the posthumous continuation (1909), not Guéranger**: its words are cited as the
  continuation's — "the continuation of *The Liturgical Year* (Dom Lucien Fromage)",
  as the library's standing registry names its writer — and never as Guéranger's.
  Under the profile's *Liturgical commentators* rule, Schuster, a Blessed, may be one
  of a reading's two authors provided the other is a Father or canonized saint; no
  other commentator here may be.

## Element keys

`introit` · `collect` · `epistle` · `gradual` · `alleluia` · `gospel` ·
`offertory` · `secret` · `communion` · `postcommunion`

## The three readings in brief

1. **`peace-of-the-house`** — *The peace that is asked for is a built city.* The
   Mass opens by asking for peace and ends by entering a court; between them the
   nations come in and one body is made out of what was divided. Augustine's
   three psalm expositions supply the movement, with Cassiodorus on all three
   psalms and Hilary's *Tractatus* on Ps 121 beside him; Chrysostom on
   1 Corinthians supplies what threatens it. Chrysostom and Theodoret read the
   Gradual's psalm of the earthly city rebuilt, and the reading says so.
2. **`authority-on-earth`** — *A man is forgiven, and the power that forgave him
   is exercised at an altar.* The Gospel's claim — the Son of Man has power **on
   earth** to forgive sins — is the formulary's centre; the Offertory's Mosaic
   altar and the Secret's *veneranda commercia* are where the Mass puts that
   power to work. Chrysostom on Matthew and on Hebrews, and Augustine on the
   Gospel harmony, with Aquinas's lectura at Mt 9:6 on the Apostles' exercise of
   that power *per viam administrationis, non auctoritatis*.
3. **`nothing-of-our-own`** — *The Mass thanks God twice for what no one in it
   did.* The lection begins with thanksgiving for a grace already given; the
   Postcommunion ends with thanksgiving for a gift just received; the Collect
   says outright that we cannot please God without God; and the man in the middle
   of the Gospel does nothing but get carried. Chrysostom on 1 Corinthians and
   Augustine on Ps 95, with Jerome and Chrysostom in open disagreement about
   whose faith healed the paralytic.

They are complementary, not rival: the first reads the formulary's chants, the
second its sacrificial centre, the third its grammar of grace. § 4 says exactly
where they diverge.

## 1. `peace-of-the-house` — Give peace: the city that is being built, and the courts we are asked into

### 1.1 Controlling claim

The formulary asks in its first words for something it does not define — *Da
pacem* — and then spends its chants defining it. The peace asked for is not the
absence of trouble but the completion of a building: the house of the Lord we go
up to (Introit verse and Gradual respond), the walls and towers whose strength
that peace is (Gradual verse), the nations and kings who come to fear the Name as
that house rises (Alleluia), and the courts we are finally told to enter
(Communion). The Mass is, in its chant-frame, one ascent from the request to the
entry.

### 1.2 Principal witnesses and their reasoning

**Augustine, *Enarrationes in Psalmos* 121 § 2.** (His lemma, in Migne's print, is
*Jucundatus sum in his qui dixerunt mihi* — joy "in those who said", persons —
not the Missal's *Laetatus sum in his, quae dicta sunt mihi*; the reading below is
built on his wording, and a study that sets it under the Missal's must say so;
`research/scope.md` § 3.2.) He begins from the title. *Canticum
graduum*: "for these degrees are not of descent, but of ascent." What is ascended to
is not the sky but a city — "there is in heaven the eternal Jerusalem, where are
our fellow citizens, the Angels: we are wanderers on earth from these our fellow
citizens." And then the point that makes v. 1 a *chant* and not a sentiment: the
joy is joy at *company*. "We find companions in this pilgrimage, who have already
seen this city herself; who summon us to run towards her. At these he also
rejoices, who says, I rejoiced in them who said to me, We will go into the house
of the Lord." The psalm's first person plural — *ibimus* — is doing the work.

**Augustine, *Enarr. in Ps.* 121 § 12.** *Fiat pax in virtute tua.* He asks what
a city's *strength* is and answers with charity: "O Jerusalem, O city, who art
being built as a city … Peace be in your strength: peace be in your love; for
your strength is your love." He proves it from *Canticles* 8:6, "Love is strong
as death," and draws the consequence that this strength is lethal to the old
self: "since this love slays what we have been, that we may be what we were not;
love creates a sort of death in us." So the Gradual's verse is not a second
petition beside the respond; it names the material the house is built of.

**Augustine, *Enarr. in Ps.* 101 § 16.** *Timebunt gentes nomen tuum.* He reads
the verse strictly in sequence: the Lord has had mercy on Sion, his servants have
loved her stones — "by acknowledging the foundation of the Apostles and Prophets"
— and only then, "hence preaching has increased among the heathen: let the heathen
fear Your Name, let another wall approach also from the heathen, let the Corner
Stone be recognised, let the two who come from different regions, but who no
longer differ in belief, meet in close union." The Alleluia is the moment the
second wall arrives.

**Augustine, *Enarr. in Ps.* 95 §§ 9–10.** He reads the whole psalm under its own
title, *when the house was being built after the captivity*, and makes the
building the subject: "Rejoice, because you have entered into the courts;
rejoice, because you are being built into the temple of God. For those who enter
are themselves built up, they themselves are the house of God." Of v. 9 he says
flatly what court is meant: "O worship the Lord in His holy court: in the
Catholic Church; this is His holy court."

**John Chrysostom, *Hom. in 1 Cor.* 2.** He supplies what threatens the city, and
he supplies it from the very lection this Mass appoints. The Corinthians are rich
"in all utterance and all knowledge" and are coming apart; Paul's thanksgiving is
strategy — "under the color of praises and thanksgiving he touches them sharply"
— and the cure is a name repeated until it binds: "consider how he always fastens
them as with nails to the Name of Christ … in order that by incessant application
of that glorious Name he may foment their inflammation, and purge out the
corruption of the disease." A city is not built of gifts. It is built of one Name
held in common.

**Hilary of Poitiers, *Tractatus super Psalmos*, in Ps. CXXI §§ 1–5.** He reaches
Augustine's city by a different road and arrives with the same stones. The psalm
is transparent, he says, to whoever is held by the hope of it — *Qui spe caelestis
desiderii detinetur, nihil obscuritatis in psalmo habebit* — because a man who
remembers that he is coheir, and *futurumque incolam ciuitatis caelestis uiuis
lapidibus extructae*, cannot help crying *Laetatus sum in his, quae dicta sunt
mihi*. The house is then identified out of another psalm (*quoniam elegit dominus
Sion … haec requies mea in saecula saeculorum*) and the earthly city refused
outright: *non utique … hanc terrenam et caducam … sed illam liberam et caelestem
Hierusalem, quia eadem et Sion est.* § 4 explains the strange *aedificatur ut
ciuitas*: not a city but *as* a city, because the earthly building only
prefigured. **What Hilary adds to Augustine here is the reason for the plural
*ibimus*** — the city is one body, *non quadam corporum confusione permixtum …
sed per fidei unitatem, per caritatis societatem* (§ 5). All five sections are
reported in `research/scope.md` § 3.2, with the physical lines of the registered
CSEL 22 layer at which each stands, so that every Hilary claim this reading makes
has a matrix row and a stated locus behind it; the binding's `loci` array reads
`121.1-5`.

**Cassiodorus, *Expositio psalmorum*, in Ps. CXXI and in Ps. XCV.** He is the
witness who makes the chant-frame's two ends one image. On Ps 121:1 the house is
*domus desiderabilis, domus de vivis lapidibus fabricata*; on Ps 95, reading the
title exactly as Augustine does, the building is identified without hedging —
*Domus enim ista, id est universalis Ecclesia, in qua Christus inhabitat, vivis
lapidibus semper exstruitur: quia quotidie de confitentibus aedificationis sumit
augmentum; nec aedificari desinit donec usque ad finem saeculi praedestinatorum
numerus impleatur*; and on Ps 101:17 the same phrase again, *aedificata est Sion,
hoc est mater Ecclesia, de vivis lapidibus fabricata*. One image carries the
Gradual, the Communion and the Alleluia.

**Where these three do not agree, and the reading must not hide it.** On the
Gradual verse the *virtus* of the city is charity for Augustine and for
Cassiodorus (*Virtus quippe ipsius pax sine dubitatione sanctorum est, quae
vocatur et charitas*) but for Hilary it is the firmness that the Church's peace
and unity confer — *pax enim ecclesiae et unitas uirtutem nobis firmitatemque
praestat* — so that peace and strength condition each other instead of being one
thing. On the towers of the same verse Cassiodorus reads the martyrs, Hilary the
steadfast among the faithful, and Augustine — in the Latin of PL 37, which the
NPNF English abridges — the *excelsi*, the few who will sit in judgment, to whom
the many at the right hand belong. This reading may use any sense of *virtus* and
of the towers; it may not report them as one (`research/scope.md` § 3.3).

**The witnesses this reading cannot claim.** Chrysostom (PG 55, cols. 347–351) and
Theodoret (PG 80, cols. 1879–1882) read Ps 121 wholly of the earthly Jerusalem
rebuilt after the captivity: the exiles' joy at going up again to the house of God,
the ruined towers and walls to be rebuilt, peace prayed for walls, palaces and
houses after the war that destroyed them. Neither reads the city of the Church or
of heaven at this psalm, and Hilary refuses in terms the earthly city they read.
Bellarmine gives both senses, the return from Babylon first and the heavenly
Jerusalem as the higher. Chrysostom's own turn to his hearers is moral, and it
serves this reading's literal sense well: Christians are slower to go up to the
house of prayer than the returning exiles were (`research/scope.md` §§ 3.2, 4.6).
The liturgical commentators on the Mass carry the same division into the chant
(`research/scope.md` §§ 3.11, 4.6): Bl. Schuster reads the Gradual first of the
psalmist rejoicing after the Babylonian exile and then of the peace of *the heavenly
Jerusalem*, which is this reading's order of senses; Honorius and Sicard read the
whole Mass's chants of the people returned from captivity and give it no heavenly
city; and Honorius's *sub gratia* reads *Da pacem* as Gregory the Great's prayer for
peace between the Romans and the Lombards and *Fiat pax in virtute tua* as peace *in
turribus Ecclesiae*. These are readings of the Mass, not of the psalm, and the
reading may use them only under those names.

**Where the Alleluia and the Communion stand.** At Ps 101:16 Theodoret, the Greek
who reads Ps 121 of the rebuilt city alone, says the fear of the nations was
fulfilled "properly and truly" only after the Incarnation, and the PG 27
expositions make Sion *τὴν Ἐκκλησίαν* (`research/scope.md` § 3.4). At Ps 95:8–9
Theodoret reads the courts as the churches (§ 3.8). So the Latin and Greek
witnesses agree on the Alleluia's and the Communion's psalms, and part only on the
Gradual's.

**Hilary on the Gospel, through the Catena.** *Dei civitas fidelium plebs est: in
hanc ergo introivit per navim, idest Ecclesiam, vectus* — the city Christ enters
is the people of the faithful, and the boat he crosses in is the Church. This is
the one checked witness that reads the Gospel's *civitátem suam* as a city in the
same sense the chants mean, and it is **Aquinas's report of Hilary**, not Hilary's
commentary, which has no registered route and was not reached.

### 1.3 How every element contributes

- `introit` — the request, and the first naming of the destination: *in domum
  Dómini íbimus*. The antiphon's own closing words, *plebis tuæ Israël*, name the
  people, not the individual.
- `collect` — what the pilgrims cannot do for themselves: *tibi sine te placére
  non póssumus*. The ascent is not self-propelled.
- `epistle` — the gifts held by a congregation that is tearing itself apart, and
  the Name that holds them (Chrysostom).
- `gradual` — respond: the destination again; verse: the material of the wall,
  which Augustine identifies as charity.
- `alleluia` — the second wall arriving from the nations.
- `gospel` — the city entered by boat; the paralytic's healing as the crowd's
  first, inadequate glorification of God (Chrysostom). *Editorial synthesis:*
  the movement from *civitátem suam* to the chants' *domus Domini* is the editor's
  join, resting on Hilary's allegorical identification and on nothing Chrysostom
  or Augustine says.
- `offertory` — Moses sanctifying an altar *in conspéctu filiórum Israël*: the
  people are present to their own sacrifice. *Textual observation:* that closing
  phrase is the one clause of the antiphon that stands verbatim in Exodus 24, at
  v. 17.
- `secret` — *uníus summæ divinitátis partícipes*: the unity the city is built
  toward is first the unity of God.
- `communion` — the entry: *introíte in átria eius … in aula sancta eius*.
  Augustine's "holy court" is the Church.
- `postcommunion` — the thanksgiving of those who have entered.

### 1.4 The four senses

- **Literal.** Israel prays for peace and for its prophets to be vindicated, and
  sings a pilgrim song on the way up to a Temple whose walls and towers it names;
  a psalm written when the house was rebuilt after the captivity summons the
  nations to bring their gifts into its courts.
- **Allegorical.** The city is Christ's body. It is built of two walls, one from
  Israel and one from the nations, meeting in the Corner Stone (Augustine on
  Ps 101:16); its strength is charity (Ps 121:7); and its court is the Church
  (Ps 95:9).
- **Moral.** Peace is not asked for as relief but received as charity, and charity
  kills the self that could not be at peace: "love creates a sort of death in us."
  The Collect is the honest form of the request.
- **Anagogical.** The house we say we will go into is entered, in this Mass, in
  sign; the psalm's *ibimus* stays future. What the Communion gives is the court,
  not yet the city — "when the building is finished, and the house dedicated"
  (Augustine on Ps 101 § 18) the running is over.

### 1.5 Strongest difficulty and alternative

**A first difficulty, from the evidence.** The Gradual's psalm, on which this
reading rests most, is read by two Greek Fathers of the rebuilt earthly city and
of nothing else (§ 1.2). The reading must present its city as the Latin
allegorical sense — Augustine's, Hilary's, Cassiodorus's, and Bellarmine's
higher sense — and give the return from exile as the literal sense, not ignore it.

**A second difficulty.** Augustine's expositions of Pss 121 and 95 are anti-Donatist
in their sharpest paragraphs: the heretics who say *Peace be with you* and have
not peace; "the frogs cry from the marsh, We alone are Christians"; the price
that some say "bought the Africans only." A reading that takes the city as its
theme inherits a polemic that is Augustine's fourth-century situation and not
this Sunday's, and a study that reproduces the register will have written about
the Donatists.

**The answer this reading gives.** Take the exegesis and leave the controversy:
Augustine's *argument* — that the peace of the city is its charity, and that the
court is one — does not depend on who his opponents were, and he states it in
propositions that stand without them.

**The alternative reading of the same evidence.** The chants can be read as three
independent psalm-verses chosen for their words rather than as a movement: the
Introit for *pacem*, the Gradual for *Laetatus sum*, the Alleluia for *gentes*,
the Communion for *hostias*. On that reading there is no ascent, only a
concordance. The formulary itself supplies the only answer available: Ps 121:1 is
appointed **twice**, as Introit verse and as Gradual respond, which is the one
structural fact a word-concordance does not explain.

### 1.6 Agreements and differences within the reading

Augustine and Chrysostom agree that the unity is not the congregation's
achievement — for Augustine it is the Corner Stone that joins the walls, for
Chrysostom the Name that nails the parties together. They differ in what they are
looking at: Augustine at a building rising, Chrysostom at one coming apart. The
reading needs both and should not smooth the second into the first; Chrysostom's
Corinth is what Augustine's Jerusalem is not yet.

Cassiodorus and Hilary join Augustine on the building and part from him, and from
each other, on two details of the Gradual verse (§ 1.2, and `research/scope.md`
§ 3.3). The reading is not weakened by that: the thing it needs — that the peace
asked for is the completion of a city, and that the city is made of people — is
what all three assert, in three vocabularies, at three of the four chant loci.
What the reading may not do is convert three named witnesses into "the Fathers" —
the Greek witnesses at Ps 121 read another city — or take the martyrs of
Cassiodorus's towers and the *virtus* of Augustine's charity as one author's single
picture.

## 2. `authority-on-earth` — The Son of Man's power on earth, and the altar where it is used

### 2.1 Controlling claim

The formulary's centre is a sentence about jurisdiction: *ut autem sciátis, quia
Fílius hóminis habet potestátem **in terra** dimitténdi peccáta*. The Gospel
proves an invisible act by a visible one. The rest of the Mass does the same
thing in the other direction: it takes a visible act — an altar, victims, an
evening sacrifice, bread received — and asks that the invisible one be worked
through it. The Offertory's Moses and the Secret's *veneránda commércia* are the
formulary's answer to the question the scribes asked.

### 2.2 Principal witnesses and their reasoning

**John Chrysostom, *Hom. in Matth.* 29.** His whole reading turns on the order of
the two healings. Christ "did not straightway hasten to heal the visible body,
but He takes His occasion from them; and He healed first that which is invisible,
the soul, by forgiving his sins; which indeed saved the other, but brought no
great glory to Himself." The visible
miracle is then produced as evidence: "by how much a soul is better than a body,
by so much is the doing away sins a greater work than this; but because the one
is unseen, the other in sight, I throw in that, which although an inferior thing,
is yet more open to sense; that the greater also and the unseen may thereby
receive its proof." He adds a second proof the scribes forced: reading their
thoughts: "But that it belongs to God only to know men's secrets, hear what says
the prophet …". And he notices the
grammar: "He said not, I forgive you your sins, but, your sins be forgiven you:
upon their constraining, He discloses His authority more clearly."

**Augustine, *De consensu evangelistarum* II. xxv. 57.** On the address: Matthew's
*fili* and Luke's *homo* are not a discrepancy but a doubling of the point. "For
these sins were [thus said to be] forgiven to the 'man,' inasmuch as the very fact
that he was a man would make it impossible for him to say, 'I have not sinned;'
and at the same time, that mode of address served to indicate that He who forgave
sins to man was Himself God." (The bracketed words are the NPNF translator's; the
inner quotation marks are NPNF's double marks.) The same sentence establishes
that the patient is everyone and that the physician is not.

**John Chrysostom, *Hom. in Heb.* 16.** On Heb 9:18–22, which narrates the same
Sinai sacrifice the Offertory names: "Tell me then why is the book of the
testament sprinkled, and also the people, except on account of the precious
blood, figured from the first?" And the transposition: "With this blood not Moses
but Christ sprinkled us, through the word which was spoken; This is the blood of
the New Testament, for the remission of sins." He insists on the difference of
degree — "those [ordinances] were not a perfect purification … but half-complete
and in a very small degree" (the bracket is the translator's) — and of depth: "there indeed the body was cleansed
outwardly … but here, since the purifying is spiritual, it enters into the soul,
and cleanses it, not being simply sprinkled over, but gushing forth in our souls."
*This is exegesis of Hebrews, not of Ex 24:4–5.* It is nonetheless the one checked
Father who links Moses' covenant sacrifice to the remission of sins, which is what
this reading needs.

**Jerome, *Comm. in Matth.* I, PL 26, col. 55**, read at his own locus, supplies
the aetiology the Gospel implies, and he attaches it to vv. 5–6 rather than to the
address: *Et datur nobis intelligentia, propter peccata plerasque [Al. plerisque]
evenire corporum debilitates. Et idcirco forsan dimittuntur prius peccata tua [Al.
tacet tua], ut causis debilitatis ablatis, sanitas restituatur* — perhaps sins are
forgiven first so that, the cause of the weakness being taken away, health may be
restored. He has already stated, on the same verses, the principle the whole
reading turns on: *Fit igitur carnale signum, ut probetur spirituale, quamquam
ejusdem virtutis sit, et corporis et animæ vitia dimittere* — the fleshly sign is
made so that the spiritual may be proved. (*The Catena's* Jerome runs this
sentence into the lemma on vv. 1–2 and reads *forsitan prius dimittuntur peccata*;
the commentary is what is quoted here.)

**Thomas Aquinas, *Super Evangelium S. Matthaei lectura*, cap. IX**, read on the
page images of the Venice 1745 *Opera*, pp. 121–122 (`research/scope.md` § 3.5).
He takes up this reading's hinge, *in terra*, and then the question the reading
has to face. Christ proves himself God *sua cognitione, verbo, et facto*; *in terra*
refutes Photinus, who argued from *data est mihi omnis potestas in caelo et in
terra* that Christ earned divinity; and then: *Videtur quod per hoc non
ostendatur, quia etiam ipsi Apostoli habebant potestatem. Sed dicendum, quod ipsi
habebant per viam administrationis, non auctoritatis.* The Apostles too have the
power to forgive sins — by way of ministry, not of authority. **This is the one
checked witness at the Gospel's own locus who connects Mt 9:6 with the Church's
ministers**, and he does it to keep the authority Christ's. His tropology is
penitential: *Surge, a peccato per contritionem; tolle lectum, per
satisfactionem … et vade in domum tuam, in domum aeternitatis, vel in
conscientiam propriam.*

**On the Communion's verse, three witnesses read *Tollite hostias* of the
Church's sacrifice offered by its priests** (`research/scope.md` § 3.8):
Theodoret, *θυσίας λέγει τὰς λογικάς, ἃς ὁρῶμεν διηνεκῶς ὑπὸ τῶν ἱερέων
προσφερομένας καὶ ἱερουργουμένας*, with Mal 1:11; the PG 27 expositions under
Athanasius's name, the "families" as *ἱερέας … καὶ προέδρους* who offer spiritual
sacrifices in the churches; and Bellarmine, the contrite heart first and then
"the Eucharistic sacrifice … offered … through the hands of the priests of the New
Testament." Augustine and Cassiodorus read the same *hostiae* as the contrite
heart and name no altar, and the reading must say so.

**On the Offertory's cited verses, one later exegete** (`research/scope.md`
§ 3.7): Cornelius a Lapide on Ex 24:4–8 reads the twelve *tituli* as the twelve
tribes' share in the covenant sacrifice, the young men as the firstborn who were
priests before Aaron, and the covenant blood as the type of the blood by which
Christ sanctioned the new covenant and which he named in instituting the
Eucharist. A seventeenth-century Jesuit, read in an optical layer only.

**Documented reception of the compilation, not exegesis** (`research/scope.md`
§§ 2.7, 3.11, 4.3). The commentators on this Mass divide, and the reading must say
which of them it uses.

- **Bl. Ildefonso Schuster, on the 1962 Mass itself** (*The Sacramentary* III,
  pp. 167–170). Sin *can only be remitted by God himself, hence the power to forgive
  sins granted by Jesus to the Church is a proof of his divinity*; the crowd's words
  at v. 8 are first *a subjective judgement on the part of the people who had not yet
  grasped the divine nature of Christ* — Chrysostom's reading — and then, in a deeper
  and *prophetic* sense: *This power of remitting sins had to be communicated to
  men—that is, to the apostles and to their successors in the priesthood*. He regrets
  that the Offertory has lost the verses in which Moses intercedes for the people,
  and he reads the Communion: *In the Old Covenant it was the people who brought gifts
  to God in his temple, in the New it is God who gives himself to his people.* **He is
  a Blessed, and the one registered witness who reads Mt 9:8 itself of the ministry,
  on the Mass this study is about.** Under the profile he may be one of this
  reading's two authors beside Aquinas, a canonized Doctor — Aquinas at v. 6,
  Schuster at v. 8, and the connexion between the two verses the editor's; the
  reading's patristic authors remain Chrysostom and Augustine.
- **The continuation of *The Liturgical Year*, on the 1962 Mass** (vol. XI,
  pp. 393–409): after the Ember ordinations the Epistle's gifts are *the powers
  conferred by the imposition of the bishop's hands*, and the present Gospel invites
  the faithful *to meditate upon the prerogative which these same men* — the pastors
  — *have of forgiving sins and healing souls*. The continuator's words, not
  Guéranger's.
- **Rupert of Deutz and William Durandus, on a Mass with another Gospel.** Rupert
  reads the chants of the rectors of souls: *Da pacem* as *peccata dimitte, pax enim
  Dei peccatorum remissio est*; *Fiat pax in virtute tua* as *remissio peccatorum in
  Spiritu sancto*; the Offertory's Moses — with the verse *Obsecro, Domine, dimitte
  peccata populi tui* — as the pastor's model of intercession. Durandus reads the Mass
  *de Praelatis*: *Prophetae tui, id est, Praelati*, and *Tollite hostias, loquitur
  enim de Praelatis*. **Their Mass paired that Offertory with the Gospel of Mt 23 or
  Mt 22; the paralytic stood at their Nineteenth Sunday**, where Rupert reads the
  bearers as prelates who offer the sinner by prayer and Durandus has the paralytics
  healed *merito fide Praelatorum*. They may be cited for the chants and for Mt 9 at
  that locus, never as readers of the 1962 pairing.
- **Honorius and Sicard read the same chants otherwise**: of the people returned
  from Babylon, Daniel's prayer for peace in the Introit and an altar to be built as
  Moses built one in the Offertory; Honorius, at his Nineteenth Sunday, reads the
  Gospel's *civitatem suam* as the Jerusalem the people regained *cum Jesu
  sacerdote*. The reading must not present the ministerial sense as the
  commentators' common one.

### 2.3 How every element contributes

- `introit` — *exáudi preces servi tui*, the singular servant who cannot speak for
  himself; the antiphon asks and does not claim.
- `collect` — the one oration of a second-class Sunday, and it is a petition that
  God's mercy do the directing: the authority in question is not ours.
- `epistle` — the forgiveness is not the end: *qui et confirmábit vos usque in
  finem sine crímine, in die advéntus*. Chrysostom: "not only the beginnings must
  be good, but the end also."
- `gradual` — *in domum Dómini íbimus*: the man is sent home (*vade in domum
  tuam*), and the Church goes to God's house. *Editorial synthesis:* the verbal
  echo between *domum tuam* and *domum Dómini* is real in the Latin and is the
  editor's to use; no checked witness draws it, though Aquinas reads *vade in domum
  tuam* as *in domum aeternitatis, vel in conscientiam propriam* (*Super Matth.*
  IX), which the editor may set beside the chant under Aquinas's name.
- `alleluia` — *Timébunt gentes*: the crowd feared and glorified God, and
  Chrysostom says their fear was not yet faith. The Alleluia widens the same
  reaction to the nations.
- `gospel` — the centre, treated above.
- `offertory` — Moses sanctifies an altar, offers holocausts, immolates victims,
  and makes an evening sacrifice; the Mass says this over its own gifts. *Historical
  note:* the pairing of this Offertory with this Gospel is attested in the 1862
  Pustet and the 1962 book and read by Schuster and the continuation; five
  registered medieval commentators — Rupert, Honorius, Sicard, Durandus, Berno —
  set the same Offertory, in Rupert's and Berno's books with verses on Moses'
  intercession, beside a Gospel from Mt 23 or Mt 22, Sicard and Durandus naming both
  as the use of different churches; the Frankish *comites* of Murbach and the *Liber
  Comitis* set this Gospel with Eph 4:23, and the seventh-century Roman gospel list
  of Würzburg sets it at the third Sunday after St Cyprian (`research/scope.md`
  § 2.7). None of these shows when this Gospel first stood beside this Offertory,
  and the reading asserts the pairing's age in neither direction.
  *Textual observation:* the antiphon is a compilation, and its verbs
  (*sanctificávit*, *fecit sacrifícium vespertínum in odórem suavitátis*) are not
  in Ex 24:4–5 in either Latin or Greek. The Church's chant has already reworked
  Moses' act into the language of consecration and of the daily evening oblation.
- `secret` — *per huius sacrifícii veneránda commércia, uníus summæ divinitátis
  partícipes éfficis*: the exchange is what the Offertory's altar is for, and it
  is named as a present-tense effect (*éfficis*), not a memory.
- `communion` — *Tóllite hóstias, et introíte*: the victims are brought in, not
  left outside.
- `postcommunion` — *dignos nos eius participatióne perfícias*: the forgiven man
  is not yet finished; perfection is asked for after communion, not claimed.

### 2.4 The four senses

- **Literal.** At Capernaum a paralysed man is carried to Jesus, told his sins are
  forgiven, and then told to rise, so that the second, verifiable command may
  prove the first, unverifiable one; and at Sinai Moses builds and consecrates an
  altar and offers the sacrifice that seals a covenant.
- **Allegorical.** The altar Moses sanctified is a figure of the one where the
  blood of the New Testament is poured "for the remission of sins" (Chrysostom on
  Hebrews 9); the authority claimed at Capernaum is exercised there.
- **Moral.** The sinner's part is to be brought, to be laid down, and then to obey
  the one command he is given — *surge, tolle lectum tuum* — carrying what once
  carried him. Rabanus, through the Catena: *lectum tollere est carnem a terrenis
  desideriis ad voluptatem spiritus attollere.* Aquinas, at his own locus, makes the
  command penitential: *Surge, a peccato per contritionem; tolle lectum, per
  satisfactionem.*
- **Anagogical.** The forgiveness given now is confirmation *usque in finem sine
  crímine*, and its term is *dies advéntus Dómini nostri Iesu Christi*. The Mass
  does not end the matter; it opens it toward the day.

### 2.5 Strongest difficulty and alternative

**The difficulty, and it is serious.** The obvious way to run this reading is to
take *qui dedit potestátem talem homínibus* as the Church's ministerial power to
absolve, and then to let the Offertory's altar and the Secret's *commércia* be
where that power acts. **No Father checked here reads v. 8 that way.** Chrysostom
reads the crowd's words as a *deficient* confession — "But nevertheless they
still creep upon the earth. … But they did not retain these things clearly" —
Augustine does not reach the verse at *De cons. evang.* II. xxv, Jerome at his own
locus says nothing about v. 8 beyond a single tropological sentence about the
risen soul carrying its bed, and Hilary, as Aquinas reports him, reads *hominibus*
of the power *ut fiant filii Dei*.

**What the later witnesses do support, and where.** The ministerial sense is not
the editor's invention. It stands below the Fathers in these places, and in none of
them is it a Father's reading of v. 8 (`research/scope.md` §§ 3.11, 4.3):

- **Aquinas at v. 6**, at his own locus: the Apostles have the power to forgive
  *per viam administrationis, non auctoritatis*. A Doctor, a different verse — the
  verse this reading already turns on — and a statement made to keep the
  authority Christ's.
- **Bl. Ildefonso Schuster at v. 8, on the 1962 Mass**: the power of remitting sins
  *communicated to men—that is, to the apostles and to their successors in the
  priesthood*, given as a deeper and prophetic sense beside the crowd's inadequate
  confession. A Blessed and a liturgical commentator, reading this pairing.
- **The continuation of *The Liturgical Year*, on the 1962 Mass**: the pastors'
  *prerogative … of forgiving sins*, after the Ember ordinations. The continuator's
  words.
- **Theodoret, the PG 27 expositions and Bellarmine at Ps 95:8**: the sacrifices
  brought into the courts are those the Church's priests offer. That is the
  Communion's verse, not the Gospel's.
- **Rupert and Durandus, on a Mass with another Gospel**: peace as remission of sins
  and the Offertory's Moses as the interceding pastor (Rupert); the Mass *de
  Praelatis* (Durandus); and at their Nineteenth Sunday, on Mt 9, the prelates who
  offer the paralytic by prayer (Rupert) and the paralytics healed *merito fide
  Praelatorum* (Durandus). Intercession and the prelates' faith, not absolution,
  and not readings of the 1962 pairing.

**And where it does not stand.** Honorius and Sicard read the same chants of the
people returned from Babylon, and St Anthony's Sunday sermon gives v. 8 no
ministerial sense (`research/scope.md` §§ 3.5, 3.11). The ministerial reading is one
reception of this Mass among others, not the tradition's single reading of it.

**The answer this reading gives.** Take the step only as far as the witnesses take
it, and name each. The Gospel establishes that the authority is exercised **on
earth**, visibly, by one who is also man; Aquinas says at that verse that the
Apostles exercise it by ministry and not by authority; Schuster, commenting on this
Mass, reads v. 8 of that power communicated to the apostles and their successors;
the Mass's own prayers claim a present effect for a visible rite (*éfficis*,
*vegetáti*); and the continuation reads the Mass of the pastors who forgive. That is
enough for the whole formulary to hold together without putting a doctrine in the
Fathers' mouths. What the reading may not do is attribute the ministerial reading
of v. 8 to Chrysostom, Jerome, Augustine or Hilary; present Rupert's or Durandus's
reading as a reading of the 1962 pairing; put the continuation's words in
Guéranger's mouth; or present the ministerial reading as the commentators' only
one.

**The alternative reading of the same evidence.** The Offertory can be heard as
pure figure and nothing more: Moses' sacrifice is recalled at the moment of
offering because the Church's offering is its fulfilment, with no claim at all
about authority. On that reading the Gospel's *potestas* is Christ's alone and the
Mass is a memorial of it. The reading above differs by taking *in terra* seriously
as a statement about where, and the difference is real and should be stated.

### 2.6 Agreements and differences within the reading

Chrysostom and Augustine agree that the episode's whole point is a demonstration
of divinity, and they reach it by different routes: Chrysostom from the order of
the two healings and the reading of hearts, Augustine from a single word of
address. They do not disagree. Jerome's aetiology — that bodily weakness often
comes of sin, so sins are loosed first — is a third move that neither of them
makes, and it is Jerome's own, at PL 26, col. 55; the study should mark it as a
further consideration of one Father, not as the common teaching, and should note
that he offers it tentatively (*forsan*).

## 3. `nothing-of-our-own` — Thanks at both ends, and a man who did nothing in the middle

### 3.1 Controlling claim

This formulary is unusually explicit about the grammar of grace, and it is
explicit in its structure as much as in its words. The lection's first clause is a
thanksgiving for a grace already given (*Grátias ago Deo meo semper pro vobis in
grátia Dei, quæ data est vobis*); the last prayer of the Mass is a thanksgiving
for a gift just received (*Grátias tibi reférimus, Dómine, sacro múnere
vegetáti*); the single oration between them says the thing outright — *quia tibi
sine te placére non póssumus*; and the man at the centre of the Gospel is carried
in, spoken to, and healed without contributing a word. The Mass's petitions are
therefore all of one kind: that God do in us what we cannot begin.

### 3.2 Principal witnesses and their reasoning

**John Chrysostom, *Hom. in 1 Cor.* 2**, reading the appointed lection clause by
clause, keeps taking the gifts out of the Corinthians' hands:

- On *for the grace of God*: "Do you see how from every quarter he draws topics
  for correcting them? For where grace is, works are not; where works, it is no
  more grace. If therefore it be grace, why are you high-minded? Whence is it
  that you are puffed up?"
- On *which is given you*: "And by whom was it given? By me, or by another
  Apostle? Not at all, but by Jesus Christ."
- On the praises: "not even did this praise belong to them, but to the grace of
  God. For that they had remission of sins, and were justified, this was of the
  Gift from above."
- And the sting in the last verse: *He shall confirm you … unreprovable* is "also
  covertly accusing them: for, to say, He shall confirm, and the word unreprovable
  marks them out as still wavering."

**Augustine, *Enarr. in Ps.* 95 § 9**, on the Communion's own first clause, gives
the one thing a creature can bring: "O heathen, if you will enter into His courts,
enter not empty. Bring presents. What presents shall we bring with us? The
sacrifice of God is a troubled spirit: a broken and a contrite heart … Enter with
an humble heart into the house of God, and you have entered with a present. But if
you are proud, you enter empty. For whence would you be proud, if you were not
empty?" *Tóllite hóstias* is therefore not a demand for something we own; it is a
demand for the one thing that costs nothing but pride.

**Jerome, *Comm. in Matth.* I, PL 26, cols. 54–55**, read at his own locus, on
*videns Iesus fidem illorum*, takes the strongest form of the claim: *Videns autem
Jesus non ejus fidem qui offerebatur, sed eorum qui offerebant* — the faith was not
the sick man's at all — and gives the reason in the previous sentence, which is
about incapacity and not about merit: *quia ipse ingredi non valebat*. On *filium
vocat*: *O mira humilitas, despectum et debilem, totisque membrorum compagibus
dissolutum, filium vocat, quem sacerdotes non dignabantur attingere.* And the
tropology, in his own words: *Juxta tropologiam interdum anima jacens in corpore
suo, totis membrorum virtutibus dissolutis, a perfecto doctore offertur curanda
Domino, quæ si misericordia ejus sanata fuerit, tantum roboris accipit, ut portet
statim lectulum suum* — the soul lying paralysed in its own body is *carried to
the Lord by someone else*, and receives strength only afterwards.

**A correction this record owes its own earlier draft.** The sentence
*unusquisque enim aeger petendae salutis precatores debet adhibere* — "every sick
man must employ intercessors" — which an earlier pass attributed to Jerome and
which this reading leaned on, is the **Catena's** wording and is not in PL 26 at
this pericope. Jerome's own tropological sentence, quoted above, carries the same
weight for this reading and is what it now uses.

**John Chrysostom, *Hom. in Matth.* 29**, refuses exactly that: "Seeing, it is
said, their faith; that is, the faith of them that had let the man down … Or
rather, in this case the sick man too had part in the faith; for he would not have
suffered himself to be let down, unless he had believed." This disagreement is
not incidental to the reading; it is the reading's central question, and § 3.5
states what the formulary does with it. Aquinas, at the same verse, will not
choose: *Curat aliquando Dominus aliquem propter fidem suam: aliquando propter
preces suas, et aliorum* (Venice 1745, p. 121).

**Theodoret and Aquinas on 1 Cor 1:4, and they sharpen the reading.** Theodoret
says Paul, about to accuse, first tends their hearing, *and* that what he says is
not false, *ἐπὶ γὰρ ταῖς δοθείσαις αὐτοῖς εὐχαρίστησε τῷ Θεῷ δωρεαῖς* — he gave
thanks to God for gifts they had been *given* (PG 82, col. 229). Aquinas says the
thanks are given *ut correctionem suorum defectuum tolerabilius ferant*, and that
the riches belong to the more perfect and are possessed by the rest through the
charity that joins them (*Super I Cor.* 1 lect. 1). Both keep what Chrysostom
keeps — the gifts are God's — and both say, as Chrysostom does not, that the
praise is true (`research/scope.md` §§ 3.6, 4.7).

**Theophylact, and Ambrosiaster beside him, on the same verses.** Theophylact (PG 124,
cols. 565–567, on the page images) puts this reading's thesis in one line: the
thanksgiving *Corinthiorum fastum deprimit*, because *gratia autem non est debitum,
neque compensatio* — grace is neither a debt nor a repayment — and Paul teaches them
to give thanks always, *et non propriis operibus*. Ambrosiaster (PL 17, read on a
later delivery of the registered transcription) says of the grace given in Christ
Jesus that *qui credit in Christum, salvus sit sine opere: sola fide gratis accipit
remissionem peccatorum*. They divide, as the six readers of the lection do, on v. 8
(`research/scope.md` § 4.7): Theophylact hears accusation there, with Chrysostom;
Ambrosiaster a promise, with Aquinas. On the gifts being given and not earned they
agree with each other and with Chrysostom.

**Documented reception of the compilation, not exegesis.** Two liturgical
commentators read the Collect exactly as this reading does. Berno of Reichenau
(PL 142, col. 1070) explains it from the Communion: *quia Deo nec in sacrificio, nec
in oblatione hostiarum sine ejus adjutorio placere possumus, nec in atria ejus
introire … merito sacerdos ex sua et nostra voce Deum deprecatur, dicens: Dirigat
corda nostra*. Bl. Ildefonso Schuster, on the 1962 Mass (*The Sacramentary* III,
p. 168): *All the good which we do is the work of grace, it is a gift received from
God*, with *quid gloriaris, quasi non acceperis?* Berno's Mass had another Gospel,
and he is cited for the Collect and the Communion only; Schuster, a Blessed, reads
this Mass whole (`research/scope.md` § 3.11).

### 3.3 How every element contributes

- `introit` — *sustinéntibus te*: those who wait. The verb is passive endurance,
  and the antiphon asks God to give what waiting cannot produce.
- `collect` — the thesis in one clause, and the only oration the Mass has.
- `epistle` — the lection's whole argument, on Chrysostom's reading.
- `gradual` — *Lætátus sum in his, quæ dicta sunt mihi*: the joy is at what was
  said *to* him, by others (Augustine, Enarr. in Ps. 121 § 2). Even the gladness
  is received.
- `alleluia` — the nations fear the Name because God has built Sion, not because
  they found him (Augustine, Enarr. in Ps. 101 § 16).
- `gospel` — the paralytic, and the dispute about his faith.
- `offertory` — Moses does the sanctifying, the offering and the immolating; the
  antiphon's every finite verb has Moses as its subject, and the people only
  stand by, *in conspéctu filiórum Israël*. *Textual observation:* that is a fact
  about the antiphon's syntax, which the Church nevertheless sings over gifts the
  people have brought.
- `secret` — *sicut tuam cognóscimus veritátem, sic eam dignis móribus
  assequámur*: the knowing is indicative and already given; the attaining is
  subjunctive and asked for.
- `communion` — Augustine's contrite heart, the one present that is not empty.
- `postcommunion` — thanks, then a petition to be made worthy of what has already
  been received: *ut dignos nos eius participatióne perfícias*. The order is the
  reading in miniature.

### 3.4 The four senses

- **Literal.** Paul thanks God for gifts the Corinthians did not earn and are
  misusing; a paralysed man is carried to Jesus and forgiven before he is healed;
  a psalmist tells the nations to come into the courts with a sacrifice.
- **Allegorical.** The Church is the paralytic: carried by the faith of others
  into the presence of the one who forgives first and heals after. Hilary, through
  the Catena, takes this furthest — *in paralytico gentium universitas offertur
  medenda* — and adds *fides enim sola iustificat*, meaning that the law could not
  loose what faith looses.
- **Moral.** The gifts that most tempt us to pride — utterance, knowledge,
  strength of mind — are exactly the ones Paul lists as given. The practical form
  of the Collect is to let oneself be carried: the paralysed soul, in Jerome's own
  tropology, is *a perfecto doctore offertur curanda Domino* — offered by someone
  else — rather than arriving under its own power.
- **Anagogical.** What is asked for at the end is perfection, not completion:
  *dignos nos eius participatióne perfícias*, and *confirmábit vos usque in finem
  sine crímine, in die advéntus*. Chrysostom: "not only the beginnings must be
  good, but the end also … therefore there is need of patience."

### 3.5 Strongest difficulty and alternative

**The difficulty.** Pressed, this reading becomes quietism, and the formulary
itself refuses that. Three of its ten elements ask something of the hearer:
*dignis móribus assequámur* (Secret), *Tóllite hóstias, et introíte* (Communion),
and the Gospel's own *surge, tolle lectum tuum, et vade*. Chrysostom, the
reading's principal witness, says in the same homily that "there is need of many
labors to be able to come unto the end."

**The answer this reading gives.** The Collect does not say we cannot please God;
it says we cannot please him *without him* — *sine te*. The distinction is in the
Latin and is the whole reading. The man rises and carries his bed; what he could
not do was be forgiven, or get himself to the house where forgiveness was.

**The alternative, and the live disagreement.** Jerome and Chrysostom differ about
whether the paralytic believed, and Aquinas declines to decide it. Jerome's reading makes the case at its strongest —
a man saved by other people's faith — and is the more useful for the allegory of
the Gentiles. Chrysostom's is the more careful about the text, since the man did
consent to be let down through a roof. **The formulary does not settle it**, and
the study must not: Mt 9:2 says *fidem illorum* and no more. What both readings
share, and what this interpretation actually rests on, is that the faith which
moved the healing was in no case the sick man's *achievement*.

### 3.6 Agreements and differences within the reading

Chrysostom and Augustine agree on the central proposition — that what is boasted
of was given — and they arrive by opposite temperaments: Chrysostom by taking
praises away from a proud congregation, Augustine by telling a poor one what it
may bring. Jerome agrees with both on the proposition and disagrees with
Chrysostom on the case. The reading is therefore built on an agreement that
survives a real dispute inside it, which is why the dispute is kept in the open
rather than resolved.

## 4. Comparison

### 4.1 What the three share

- **All three need the Collect; only one is governed by it.** *Quia tibi sine te
  placére non póssumus* is the Mass's single oration and the sentence each reading
  has to accommodate: `peace-of-the-house` because a city one cannot build is
  still the city one is going to, `authority-on-earth` because the power in
  question is explicitly not ours, `nothing-of-our-own` because the Collect *is*
  its controlling claim (§ 3.1). What governs each reading is stated in its own
  § .1 and tabulated in § 4.2 below: the chant-frame, the Gospel, the Collect.
  Shared dependence and shared government are different things and this record
  keeps them apart.
- All three read the Communion as an entry rather than a conclusion, and all three
  have the Postcommunion asking for something still to come.
- All three treat the Offertory as a compiled text whose verbs are Moses' and
  whose last clause is the people's presence; none treats it as a quotation of
  Exodus.
- None of them asserts a compiler's intention, and each marks its cross-element
  joins as the editor's.

### 4.3 How they relate

They are not rivals about the same question, and the study should say so rather
than stage a contest. They are three different governing emphases over one set of
texts, and each explains something the others leave alone: the first is the only
one that accounts for why Ps 121:1 is appointed twice; the second is the only one
that gives a meaning to the placing, in the 1962 book as in the 1862, of a Mosaic
altar at the offering of *this* Gospel — a placing whose age this record does not
establish in either direction, since the medieval commentators' books set the same
Offertory beside a Gospel from Mt 23 or Mt 22 and the earliest lists put this Gospel
at other places (`research/scope.md` §§ 2.7, 10.16); the third is the only one that
accounts for the thanksgiving at both ends.

**Where they agree, and it is worth saying because it looks like a disagreement.**
All three treat *qui dedit potestátem talem homínibus* the same way. § 2.3's
alleluia row and § 2.5 both follow Chrysostom in reading the crowd's words as an
inadequate confession, and § 2.5 says in terms that no checked Father takes the
verse as the ministerial power and that the reading may take the ministerial sense
only where the later witnesses put it — Aquinas at v. 6; Bl. Schuster at v. 8 and
the continuation of *The Liturgical Year*, both on this Mass; Rupert and Durandus on
a Mass with another Gospel — and under their names; § 1 gives the verse no weight
either. The one registered witness who reads v. 8 itself of the ministry is
Schuster, and he gives the crowd's inadequate confession first, as Chrysostom does,
so even he does not set the two readings against each other. `authority-on-earth`'s hinge is **Mt 9:6**,
*potestátem in terra dimitténdi peccáta* — the sentence § 2.1 quotes — and not
Mt 9:8. Nothing in this record sets one reading against another about the Gospel's
last verse, and a study that staged that contest would be inventing it.

**Where they genuinely conflict, and it survives compression.** They inherit, in
opposite directions, the disagreement the witnesses actually have about
*civitátem suam* (`research/scope.md` § 4.1).

- `peace-of-the-house` needs the city left open, because the sense it takes from
  the Gospel is Hilary's *Dei civitas fidelium plebs est* — the boat is the
  Church and the city entered is the people of God. That reading only works while
  the town is not pinned down, which is why Augustine's refusal to settle the
  question matters to it.
- `authority-on-earth` needs the opposite. Its whole claim is about *where*: the
  Son of Man has this power **on earth**, at a place, in front of witnesses who
  can check the visible half of it. Its two principal witnesses pin the town and
  disagree about which one — Chrysostom names Capernaum, "that which had Him
  continually inhabiting it"; Jerome at PL 26, col. 54 says flatly *Civitatem ejus
  non aliam intelligimus quam Nazareth* — and neither leaves it open. Aquinas, who
  harmonises the two (Bethlehem by birth, Nazareth by upbringing, Capernaum by
  dwelling and miracles), also gives the allegory `peace-of-the-house` wants —
  *in civitatem gentium, quae sibi datae sunt* — so the later Doctor holds both
  sides of this conflict and the Fathers do not.

So the two readings want incompatible things from the same clause: one wants it
unlocated so it can be the Church, the other wants it located so the power can be
exercised somewhere. Neither is refuted by the evidence, the disagreement is the
Fathers' own and not the editor's, and it does not dissolve when the study is
compressed. `nothing-of-our-own` takes no position on the city; the dispute it
carries is internal to itself — Jerome against Chrysostom on whose faith healed
the man (§ 3.6) — and the study keeps that one inside the third reading rather
than between readings.

### 4.4 Notes for the concise study's page-1 overview

The four-row overview on physical page 1 is a brief orientation for the reader and
does not replace any lane's own four senses. Drawn from what all three readings
share:

- **Literal** — Israel asks for peace and sings its way up to the house of the
  Lord; Paul thanks God for gifts a divided church did not earn; a paralysed man
  is carried to Jesus, forgiven, and told to rise; Moses consecrates an altar and
  offers the covenant sacrifice.
- **Allegorical** — the house is Christ's body, built of two walls; the altar is
  the one where the blood of the New Testament is poured for the remission of
  sins; the paralytic is the people who could not come on their own.
- **Moral** — what we have was given, and the one present we may bring is a
  contrite heart; peace is charity, and charity kills the old self; the forgiven
  man carries what carried him.
- **Anagogical** — the entry given here is into the courts; the city, the day of
  the Lord's coming, and the perfection asked for after communion are all still
  ahead.

### 4.5 Notes for the homily

One coherent argument, for an adult parish assembly. The strongest single line is
`nothing-of-our-own`, because it is the one a congregation can act on and because
its two anchors — the Collect's *without thee we cannot please thee* and the man
who is carried — are the two things in this Mass a hearer will remember. It can
take one element from each of the others without incoherence: the Gospel's *power
on earth* (why the forgiveness happens here and not in private), and the
Communion's courts (where the Mass is going). It must not attribute a ministerial
reading of Mt 9:8 to any Father, since none checked gives one; if it speaks of the
Church's ministry of forgiveness it must do so as Aquinas does at v. 6 — ministry
beneath Christ's authority — or as Bl. Ildefonso Schuster reads v. 8 on this Mass,
or as the continuation of *The Liturgical Year* reads it (never "Guéranger"), and
say whose the reading is; it must not present Rupert's or Durandus's reading of
their Mass as a reading of this Gospel; it must not manufacture the Fathers'
approval of the editor's arrangement; and it must assert the age of the Offertory's
pairing with this Gospel in neither direction — neither "ancient" nor "the 1962
book's" nor "not ancient" — since no checked witness shows when the two first stood
together (`research/scope.md` § 10.16). Exact loci, the
delivery estimate and the word count belong in the homily's terminal note, not in
the speech.
