% The spoken body of the homily for the Eighteenth Sunday after Pentecost.
% Continuous preaching, ready to read aloud: no heading, no direction to the
% preacher, no citation inside the speech. The loci, the relation to the
% reviewed readings, the audience, the word count and the pace estimate stand
% in the terminal note, which no one reads out. The blank lines are the
% paragraph breaks a speaker recovers his place by, and the five vertical
% spaces divide the speech into its six movements: the man who says nothing;
% the Collect and the Epistle's thanks for what was given; whose faith brought
% him; power on earth to forgive, and the crowd's praise; the Communion and the
% Postcommunion; and the command to rise and carry, with the week's response
% and the hope of the end.

There is a man in today's Gospel who never says a word. Our Lord speaks to him
twice. The scribes speak, though only inside themselves: ``He blasphemeth.''
At the end the whole multitude glorifies God. The men who bring him are silent
too, but at least they do something: they carry him. He does nothing. He is
carried in and laid down. He is forgiven before he has asked for anything. He
is told to rise and go home, and he goes.

Keep that man in view, because the rest of this Mass can be heard around him.

\bigskip

Listen again to the Collect, the prayer said just before the Epistle. An old
English missal renders it: ``May the influence of thy mercy, O Lord, direct
our souls: for without thy help we can do nothing well pleasing to thee.'' The
Latin is plainer still: \latin{sine te}, without thee. Not without thy help,
as if God lent us a tool and stepped back. Without God himself, we cannot
please God. That is not a sentence of despair. It is a sentence about where to
stand.

Saint Paul stands there. Today's Epistle opens his first letter to Corinth,
and it opens with thanks: ``I give thanks to my God always for you, for the
grace of God that is given you in Christ Jesus.'' They are ``made rich in him,
in all utterance and in all knowledge.'' A warm compliment, until you read on.
Two verses after the lesson ends, Paul begs them ``that there be no schisms
among you''. The church he thanks God for is breaking into parties.

Saint John Chrysostom saw what Paul was doing. He is not congratulating the
Corinthians; he is thanking God for them. Thanks is owed for a favour, and ``a
favor is not a debt nor a requital nor a payment.'' Then Chrysostom presses it
home: ``If therefore it be grace, why are you high-minded? Whence is it that
you are puffed up?'' The very things the Corinthians most admired in
themselves, their gift of speech and their knowledge, stand on the list of
things they had been given.

What am I proudest of? My mind, my competence, my good name; perhaps even my
faithfulness in coming here week after week. Paul's list would include every
one of them. Not one of them is a wage.

\bigskip

Now back to the man on the bed. Jesus, ``seeing their faith'', spoke to him.
Whose faith? Here the saints do not agree. Saint Jerome says plainly: not the
faith of the man who was brought, but of those who brought him, because he
could not come in by himself. Saint Ambrose, on Saint Luke's telling, says the
same: great is the Lord, who pardons some for the merit of others. Saint John
Chrysostom goes further. Saint Mark and Saint Luke tell us that the bearers
let the man down through the roof, and Chrysostom says the sick man too had
part in the faith, ``for he would not have suffered himself to be let down,
unless he had believed.''

We need not settle their argument; the Gospel says only ``their faith''. But
both sides grant this much: the man did not come by himself. Whatever faith he
had brought him exactly as far as he was carried, and if he had any, that too
was given him. Once laid down, says Chrysostom, he ``gives himself up to the
power of the healer.''

Then the first word: ``Be of good heart, son, thy sins are forgiven thee.''
Son. Saint Jerome marvels at it. Here is a man helpless and despised, whom, he
says, the priests would not deign to touch, and the Lord calls him son.

\bigskip

The scribes are scandalised: ``He blasphemeth.'' And they are right about one
thing: only God can forgive sins. So our Lord asks them: ``Whether is easier,
to say, Thy sins are forgiven thee: or to say, Arise, and walk?'' The first
costs nothing to say; nobody can check it. The second anyone can check at
once. Forgiving sins, Chrysostom explains, is the greater work, but it cannot
be seen; so the Lord adds the lesser work that can be seen, ``that the greater
also and the unseen may thereby receive its proof.'' ``But that you may know
that the Son of man hath power on earth to forgive sins'': rise, take up your
bed, go home.

Mark those two words: on earth. Not in some private corner of the soul where I
tell myself that I am forgiven, but on earth, in a voice that can be heard.
Saint Thomas Aquinas raises the obvious objection: the Apostles also forgave
sins, so how does this show that Christ is God? He answers that the Apostles
had the power by way of ministry, not of authority. The authority stays
Christ's; the ministry is exercised by men. So in the sacrament of Penance a
man says aloud, on earth, a sentence that only God can make true; and the
penitent, like the man on the bed, brings nothing to pay with: only his sins
and his sorrow for them, and the sorrow too is God's gift.

And the crowd? ``The multitude seeing it, feared, and glorified God that gave
such power to men.'' Two Fathers hear this differently. Chrysostom thinks they
understood only half: they took Jesus for one come from God, greater than all
men, but not yet for the Son of God. Saint Hilary hears fitting praise: God is
glorified because a power and a way have been given to men, to all of us who
receive them, through his word, for the forgiveness of sins, the resurrection
of the body, and the return to heaven. Heard with Hilary, the Gospel that began
with a man who contributed nothing ends with thanks for a gift, which is
exactly how the Epistle began.

\bigskip

The Mass itself has that shape. Its lessons open with Paul's ``I give
thanks'', and its last prayer, in that same old English, says: ``Being fed, O
Lord, with this holy sacrifice: we give thee thanks, humbly beseeching thy
mercy: that thou wouldst make us worthy of what we have received.'' Thanks at
the beginning, thanks at the end, and in the middle a man who was carried.

Before that prayer the Communion verse gives a command: ``Bring up sacrifices,
and come into his courts.'' But if without God we can do nothing pleasing to
God, what are we to bring? Saint Augustine answers from that very psalm:
``Confession is a present unto God.'' And: ``Enter with an humble heart into
the house of God, and thou hast entered with a present. But if thou art proud,
thou enterest empty.''

So the sacrifice this verse asks for is the one present that claims nothing as
its own and costs nothing but pride. ``What hast thou,'' Paul asks those same
Corinthians, ``that thou hast not received?'' Our confession and our humble
heart are his gifts too. We carry them into his courts when we come up to this
altar, and he is pleased to call them a present. What he gives us there is the
holy sacrifice that last prayer thanks him for, ``what we have received'':
Christ himself, his Body given up on the Cross and his Blood poured out for the
forgiveness of sins, the Lord who died and rose again.

\bigskip

Does all this make us passive, lying on our beds waiting to be carried? No.
Christ's last words to the man are commands: arise, take up thy bed, go into
thy house. Saint Thomas noticed their exact shape. The man, he says, had three
troubles: he lay in bed, he was carried by others, and he could not walk. So
Christ says ``Arise'' to the man who lay there; because he was carried, he
commanded him to carry; and because he could not walk, he bade him walk. The
bed that carried him, he now carries. Grace is not taken back when we stand up.
It is what we stand in.

So, three things for this week. First, bring what you cannot mend to the place
on earth where the power to forgive has been given. Do not leave your sins to
your feelings: go to confession, with your sins and your sorrow for them, and
nothing to pay with. Second, be one of the bearers. Someone you know cannot
come in by himself: too ashamed, too tired, too far off. You cannot forgive
him, but you can carry him. Saint Ambrose tells the sinner who despairs of
pardon to call in the Church to pray for him. Be that Church for someone: pray
for him by name, and if you can, bring him. Third, take the thing you are
proudest of and thank God for it, as Paul did for the Corinthians: as a gift,
not a wage.

And do not be afraid of the end. Paul tells those quarrelsome Corinthians that
Christ ``will confirm you unto the end without crime.'' Chrysostom hears a
warning in it; Saint Thomas hears a promise. Either way, the confirming is
Christ's. The end is given as the beginning was. The man on the bed went home
carrying what had carried him. Without God we cannot please God; with him, the
most helpless of us is called son, forgiven on earth, and sent home.
