# Proposed interpretations: resumed Fifth Sunday after the Epiphany (1962)

Research stage, iterations 0 and 1, `proper-study` v17 run `eb621aedbeee828e`,
5 November 2026. Three readings of the whole formulary are proposed. Each is
the editor's synthesis over a compilation (three-document profile, *The
formulary is a compilation*): the Fathers and Doctors named below commented on
single passages, and each is credited only with what his checked locus says.
Every locus cited is registered in `research/scope.md` section 5, with the
bytes it was read in. Element keys are those of `propers/verified.md`.

Iteration 1 re-tested each reading against the witnesses the research review
found held and unread: Augustine's *Sermo* 73 and *Ep.* 93, Hilary and
Chrysostom on Ps 129, Chrysostom, Ambrose, Aquinas and Cassiodorus on Ps 43,
Athanasius and Cassiodorus on Ps 84, Aquinas on Col 3:12-15a, Theodoret and
Theophylact on Col 3, Rabanus on Mt 13, and the liturgical commentators Rupert,
Durandus and Fromage. Where that evidence changes a claim, a contribution or a
limit, the change is made in place and marked *(iteration 1)*.

Throughout, **[A]** marks what an author states at his locus; **[E]** marks
the editor's connection between elements or between an author and an element
he did not comment on.

## The question each reading answers

| Key | The exegetical question | The answer, in a sentence | The difficulty it must pass |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `patience-of-the-field` | Why does the householder forbid the uprooting of the tares, and what does the command ask of those who hear it now? | Because the servants may be deceived and the tare may become wheat, judgment is reserved to the harvest and to angels who cannot err, and the Church's present task is the forbearance and forgiveness the Epistle commands. | *Sínite útraque créscere* against *Auferte malum* (scope 7.1), and the disagreement over coercion (scope 8.1). |
| `cry-from-captivity` | What do the chants make of the people who sing them, and why does a God who has already promised still command them to pray? | The people are exiles, martyrs and sinners in the deep, to whom God's thoughts of peace are already spoken; he commands their prayer so that they may receive what he promised, and the Communion assures them that such prayer receives. | The Introit's omissions and Jerome's *poterat … sed hortatur* (scope 7.4); the tense of *Liberásti* (scope 7.5); the Communion's condition (scope 7.7). |
| `word-in-the-heart` | If the enemy sows *while men slept*, how is the field kept? | By a watchfulness that is possible because the word of Christ dwells richly in the heart and the peace of Christ rules there; the Mass's own singing and its cry from the depths are meant to come from the heart and to rouse it. | Who the sleepers are (scope 7.2); the Greek umpire against the Latin *exsúltet* (scope 7.6). |

The three answers are not three titles over one application. The first is
about how to treat others whom one judges to be tares; the second is about
how a people under affliction stands before a promise; the third is about the
interior field of each hearer. They share the Gospel and the Epistle and use
different words of each.

## 1. `patience-of-the-field` — Let both grow until the harvest

### Controlling claim

The Gospel's refusal to let the servants weed the field is the centre of the
Mass. The householder gives his reason in the parable itself: *ne forte
colligéntes zizánia, eradicétis simul cum eis et tríticum*. The Fathers who
expound it find in that reason two facts about the present age: those who
would judge may be deceived, the wheat and the tares being not yet
distinguishable with certainty; and a tare may become wheat. Judgment of
persons therefore belongs to the harvest and to the Son of Man's angels. What
remains for the Church meanwhile is what the Epistle names: *patiéntiam:
supportántes ínvicem, et donántes vobismetípsis … sicut et Dóminus donávit
vobis*.

### Carrying authors and their reasoning

- **Augustine** (father), *Sermo* 73 on this Gospel *(iteration 1)*, with
  *En. in Ps.* 42.2-3 and 64.15 and *Quaest. Ev.* I.10. [A] In his own sermon
  on the parable he states the whole controlling claim. To the zealous
  servants: *"be quiet, it is not the time of harvest. That time will come,
  may it only find you wheat!"*; *Let both grow* because *"ye are such as may
  be deceived"*; *"We are but men, the reapers are the angels"*, who *"can make
  the separation, and who cannot make mistakes"*. To the tares, who are evil
  Christians: *"they who to-day are tares, may to-morrow be wheat"*, so
  *"amend yourselves before the harvest come"*. To all: wheat and tares stand
  *"even in these high seats … and among the laity"*; *"Let the good tolerate
  the bad; let the bad change themselves, and imitate the good."* In the
  Psalm expositions: *"a certain distinction without separation"*, and
  *"through endurance of the tares the fruit of the wheat is increased"*; in
  *Quaest. Ev.*, the separation is the good angels' work at the end.
  Augustine's contribution is the double ground (the judges' fallibility and
  the tares' convertibility), the charge to both wheat and tares, and the state
  of the wheat during the waiting.
- **Jerome** (father), *In Mt.* II, on 13:36-43, PL 26 cols. 93-94. [A] He
  states the difficulty himself: *Sinite utraque crescere* seems contrary to
  *Auferte malum de medio vestrum*. He answers from the plant: wheat and
  *lolium*, while in the blade, are alike and hard or impossible to tell apart;
  so *ne ubi quid ambiguum est, cito sententiam proferamus; sed Deo judicii
  terminum reservemus*, and God will remove *non suspicionem criminis, sed
  manifestum reatum*. *Ne forte … eradicetis* gives *locus poenitentiae*: the
  one depraved by a harmful doctrine today may defend the truth tomorrow. The
  sleepers are *magistri Ecclesiarum*. At 13:24-30 itself he refuses to
  anticipate the Lord's explanation. Jerome's contribution is the
  epistemology of the command and the boundary it leaves: the ambiguous is
  reserved, the manifest may be expelled.
- **John Chrysostom** (father), *Hom. in Mt.* 46.1. [A] The tares are
  heretical societies sown beside the truth and *"in appearance somewhat like
  wheat"*. The servants are introduced *"that He may pronounce it wrong to slay
  them"*, and the Lord gives two reasons: killing heretics would overthrow
  saints with them, and *"of the very tares it is likely that many may change
  and become wheat"*. He does not forbid *"checking heretics, and stopping their
  mouths"*. Chrysostom's contribution is the boundary of the command (no
  killing) and the hope that grounds it (conversion).
- **Rabanus Maurus** (saint), *In Mt.* IV on 13:24-30, PL 107 cols. 947-948
  *(iteration 1)*. [A] In his moral exposition the householder, knowing the
  enemy's deceit, did not burn with anger or seek quick revenge but waited;
  *"ita et nos cum aliquam laesionem ab inimicis nostris acceperimus, non statim
  ad vindictam prorumpere, sed magis ad virtutem patientiae recurrere
  debemus"*. *"Aequanimiter ergo debemus sufferre malos propter bonos"*, lest by
  retaliating we give the good occasion of scandal; *Sinite* builds
  longanimity *"usque ad finem vitae"*; at the harvest the one who bore
  patiently repays by justice, *"ut in patiendo justitiam meditemur, et in
  judicando patientiam habere non negligamus"*. Rabanus's contribution is the
  moral sense: the parable's patience becomes the patience of one who has been
  wronged.

Supporting: **Thomas Aquinas** (doctor), *Super Mt.* c. 13 on 24-30 [A]: four
reasons the wicked are not uprooted — the good are exercised by them; the
wicked may become good, *ut Paulus*; some seem wicked and are not (*Nolite ante
tempus iudicare*, 1 Cor 4:5); a powerful man drags many with him. He reports
both Chrysostom and Augustine on the *Auferte malum* objection.
**Honorius** (ecclesiastical writer), *Speculum Ecclesiae* [A]: the servants
are prelates who would separate the bad from the good; the mixture is willed
*ut boni per malos exerceantur et pro patientia habundantius remunerari
mereantur*.

The carrying authors agree on the controlling claim: the command rests on the
wheat's safety and the tares' possible conversion, and judgment is reserved to
the harvest. *(Iteration 1)* Augustine's sermon joins Jerome's ground and
Chrysostom's in his own words, so the agreement of the reading's three
Fathers no longer rests on Augustine's Psalm expositions alone. They differ on
how far discipline may go now (below).

### What each element contributes

- **Gospel.** The centre. [A] as above. The detail *cum crevísset herba … tunc
  apparuérunt et zizánia* is the textual basis of Jerome's ambiguity and of
  Augustine's *"As long as it was only in the blade, it did not appear"*.
- **Epistle.** [A] Chrysostom, *Hom. in Col.* 8: *"Forbearing … that is,
  passing things over"*; calling the offence a *"complaint"* makes it petty,
  but *"even as Christ forgave you"* obliges forgiveness of serious wrongs and
  love of the offender; love is *"the bond"* without which the other virtues
  *"fall asunder"*. [A] *(iteration 1)* Aquinas, *Super Col.* c. 3 lect. 3:
  *supportantes invicem* because, though one keeps to justice himself,
  *aliorum mores sunt ei importabiles*, as Lot lived among the Sodomites (2 Pet
  2:8); one forgives when he holds no rancour and procures no evil, *sed quando
  necessitas puniendi est, tunc puniendus est*. [E] The virtues the Epistle
  lists — *víscera misericórdiæ … patiéntiam* — are the practice of the
  patience the Gospel commands toward the field; Rabanus's moral reading of the
  parable as bearing injury, and Aquinas's reading of *supportantes* as bearing
  others' intolerable ways, meet here, though neither names the other text.
  Chrysostom does not mention the parable.
- **Communion.** [A] Bede, *In Marc.* on 11:22-25: those not yet perfect obtain
  what they ask *si tamen in se peccantibus aliis primo ipsi dimittunt*. [E]
  The verse appointed stops before the condition; read in its chapter it
  joins the promise of prayer to the forgiveness the Epistle commands.
- **Introit (and its chapter).** [A] Jerome on Jer 29:7, the verse before the
  appointed words, read tropologically: *Quaeramus quoque pacem Ecclesiae …
  orare praecepit pro inimicis nostris … ut non simus nostra tantum salute
  contenti, sed inimicorum quoque quaeramus salutem.* [E] The exiles are told
  to seek the good of the city that holds them; the reading takes this as the
  same patience extended to enemies. The antiphon itself contributes *cogitatiónes
  pacis, et non afflictiónis*: God's own intention toward his people is peace.
- **Gradual.** [A] Bellarmine on Ps 43:8: safety *"is not owing to my own
  strength"*. [A] *(iteration 1)* Aquinas, *Super Ps.* 43 n. 3: the saints are
  afflicted not only in body but *spiritualiter, quando peccant illi qui
  habitant apud eos*, and God frees them from these afflictions. [E]
  *Liberásti nos … et eos, qui nos odérunt, confudísti*: it is God who frees
  the saints from the affliction of those who live among them and who confounds
  the enemies, not the servants who uproot them.
- **Alleluia and Offertory.** [A] Augustine, *En. in Ps.* 129.2: *"Who is it
  then who crieth? A sinner."* [E] The servants who would weed the field are
  themselves in the deep; the cry is not of the righteous against the tares.
- **Collect.** [E] The family is guarded by God's *contínua pietáte* and leans
  *in sola spe grátiæ*: its protection is asked of God, not secured by
  separation. A clause, not a role.
- **Secret.** [E] The offerers ask absolution for their own *delícta* and the
  direction of their own *nutántia corda*: those who keep the field are not
  exempt from what the field contains. A clause.
- **Postcommunion.** [E] *pignus* now, *efféctus* asked for: the harvest is not
  yet. A clause.

### The difficulty and the disagreement

Scope 7.1 sets out the difficulty and the answers. The reading adopts what
the carrying authors share and keeps their difference visible: Jerome reserves
the ambiguous to God and allows the manifest to be expelled; Chrysostom
forbids killing and allows restraint. *(Iteration 1)* Augustine's own later
position is now stated from his *Ep.* 93 (scope 8.1): his first opinion was
that no one should be coerced into unity, only persuaded by argument; he
yielded to examples of conversions brought about by fear of imperial laws, and
accepted their penalties, *exile and fines*, as *"moderate severity, or rather
clemency"* aimed at correction; in the same letter the parable forbids the
separation of the good from the bad before the harvest, the Church *"correcting
as many as she can, and bearing with those whom she cannot correct"*. His
sermon on the parable names no penalty. Aquinas, in *ST* II-II q. 10 a. 8 ad 1
and q. 11 a. 3 ad 3, reads the whole command as conditional on *ne forte* and
concludes to the death of convicted heretics who persist after admonition;
that conclusion is Aquinas's alone and must not be laid on Augustine. The
study must state these positions as their authors', not as the parable's
sense, and must not use any of them to carry the reading; the reading's claim
does not depend on them. A study that wishes to engage the later doctrinal
development must do so from a source actually read.

### Four senses

- **Literal.** A householder forbids his servants to weed darnel from his wheat
  before harvest, lest they uproot the wheat with it, and appoints the reapers
  to separate them at harvest; Paul tells the Colossians to bear with and
  forgive one another as the Lord forgave them.
- **Allegorical.** Christ sows the good seed and will judge through his
  angels; the Church on pilgrimage holds the good and the bad in *"distinction
  without separation"* (Augustine), in its high seats as among its laity
  (Augustine, *Sermo* 73), until the end of the world (Mt 13:39).
- **Moral.** Judge slowly where the case is uncertain, for we may be deceived
  (Jerome; Augustine); seek the conversion of the erring rather than their
  destruction (Chrysostom; Augustine); bear injuries without revenge (Rabanus);
  bear with, forgive, and love the offender after Christ's pattern
  (Chrysostom and Aquinas on Col 3); pray for enemies (Jerome on Jer 29:7).
- **Anagogical.** At the harvest the angels, *"who cannot make mistakes"*,
  make the separation (Augustine, *Sermo* 73.4); *"In the field they may be with
  you, but they will not be so in the barn"* (*Sermo* 73.1); the wheat is
  gathered into the barn — *mansionibus coelestibus* (Jerome) — when *"the just
  shall shine as the sun"*; the Postcommunion's *efféctus* is that gathering.

### Limits

- No witness read connects the parable to this Mass's other elements; every
  cross-element link is [E].
- The tares are named differently (scope 8.5); the reading must not narrow
  them to heretics only, against Augustine, or widen them to all sinners
  without saying that Chrysostom and Jerome speak of heresy.
- *The field is the world* (Mt 13:38) is not appointed; the reading may use it
  as the Gospel's context and must not make the field simply the Church
  (scope 7.3).
- Rabanus's moral reading concerns injury done to oneself; it does not decide
  the question of public discipline, and must not be set against Jerome's or
  Augustine's position on it.

## 2. `cry-from-captivity` — Thoughts of peace for those who cry from the depths

### Controlling claim

The five chants put words in the people's mouth before and after the
lessons, and the words are those of exiles, martyrs and sinners: *redúcam
captivitátem vestram*, *avertísti captivitátem Iacob*, *Liberásti nos … ex
affligéntibus nos*, *De profúndis clamávi*, *quidquid orántes pétitis*. God's
answer is spoken first, in the Introit, as *cogitatiónes pacis, et non
afflictiónis*; the Communion closes with the promise that prayer made in faith
receives. Between them the people remember a deliverance, cry from the deep
and offer a sacrifice of propitiation. The Fathers who expound these texts
read the captivity as the captivity of sin, the deliverance as Christ's, the
deep as this mortal life, and God's command to pray as the way a promise
already made is received.

### Carrying authors and their reasoning

- **Jerome** (father), *In Hieremiam* V on 29:10-14, PL 24 cols. 893-894.
  [A] Only God knows what he thinks (*futurorum … scientia soli Deo
  competit*), against the false prophets. *Finem et patientiam* is the end of
  captivity and endurance meanwhile, or hope of what is to come. The decisive
  sentence: *Certe absque invocatione et oratione captivorum, poterat implere
  Dominus quod promiserat; sed hortatur eos ad preces, ut quod promissum est,
  mereantur accipere.* He joins v. 13 to Mt 7:7, *Petite, et accipietis*.
  Anagogically: while we live in the confusion of this age, God fulfils his
  promise after repentance and brings us back *ad locum nostrum Ecclesiam*.
- **Augustine** (father), *En. in Ps.* 84.2-4, 43.6-8, 129.1-3. [A] *"Thou
  hast turned away the captivity of Jacob"* is said to Christ, who frees *"from
  our sins, by which Satan held sway over us"*; the past tense is prophecy,
  and the psalmist prays because it is *"not yet done"*. Ps 43 is *"the voice
  of the Martyrs"*, and *"Thou hast saved us"* is the future spoken as past
  *"because … it is as certain as if it were past"*. Ps 129: *"this mortal life
  is our deep"*; the one who cries is a sinner, and his hope is the
  propitiation, which is the sacrifice offered for us (v. 4).
- **Hilary of Poitiers** (father), *Tract. in Ps.* 129.2-3, PL 9 cols. 719-720
  *(iteration 1)*. [A] The prophet cries from the deep of God's inscrutable
  riches and of human calamity; the cry fits *ignorationi, aut passioni, aut
  orationi*. God, present everywhere, hears by his nature, *sed orandus a nobis
  est, ut secundum precem nostram adsit*: *Naturae suae est ut audiat; sed
  fidei nostrae est ut precemur audiri*, that he *qui per naturam suam audit,
  per orantis precem dignetur audire*. Hilary answers, of the Mass's own
  Alleluia and Offertory verse, the question Jerome answers at the Introit.
- **John Chrysostom** (father), *Expositio in Ps.* 129 §1 and Ps 43 §6
  *(iteration 1)*. [A] At Ps 129:1-2: *it is not possible simply to obtain from
  God unless our part be supplied*, so the psalmist says first *I cried* and
  then *Hear*; intent prayer full of compunction has great power to persuade
  God; and in prayer one should seek not only to receive what one asks but to
  make the soul better. At Ps 43:8-9: we need not speak of the forefathers, for
  we ourselves hold pledges of God's providence; we glory in God all our life,
  not only when he helps but also when he forsakes.

Supporting: **Robert Bellarmine** (doctor), *Explanatio* on Ps 84:2, 43:8-9,
129:1-5 [A]: the captivity ends fully *"in the resurrection only"*; Ps 43 is
spoken by the Church and the martyrs of both Testaments; Ps 129 cries from
*"two depths"*, misery and the heart, and v. 5 is read with Deut 4:29, *"if
thou seek him with all thy heart"*. *(Iteration 1)* **Ambrose** (doctor), *In
Ps.* 43.21-22 [A]: the enemies are confounded here, but far more when they see
the saints rise; the saint is praised not in this life but in God.
**Aquinas** (doctor), *Super Ps.* 43 nn. 1-4 [A]: the sons of Core are the
martyrs; deliverance *vel in praesenti … vel in futuro*. **Cassiodorus**
(ecclesiastical writer) [A]: at Ps 43:8 *futura pro praeteritis*; at Ps 84:2
the psalmist rejoices *tanquam de praeterito*, and the captivity is of sin.
**Athanasius** (attributed; no standing row) [A]: Ps 84:2 is the redemption from
spiritual captivity preached first to the Jews (Lk 4:18-21), and the psalm
brings in the Jewish people confessing after the calling of the gentiles.
**Ildefonso Schuster** (blessed) [A]: the Introit is *"the divine answer to
our humble supplications"*, God *"chastises because he loves"*, the Gradual
fits the martyrs' victories.

The liturgical commentators on the chants as a sequence *(iteration 1)*:
**Sicard**, **Rupert** and **Durandus** (ecclesiastical writers), and
**Fromage** reporting Rupert (scope 6). [A] They read the four chants of the
Dicit Dominus Mass as one movement: the Introit announces God's peace and
promise; in the Gradual the delivered give thanks; in the Offertory they ask
pardon (Rupert, Durandus: as Joseph's brothers asked it, Gen 50:17); in the
Communion they receive the assurance (Sicard: *recipiunt certificationem*;
Rupert, Durandus: Joseph's answer, *Nolite timere*). They read it of the
remnant of Israel at the end of the world, prefigured in Joseph's reception of
the brothers who sold him; Durandus glosses *non afflictionis* with *ad hoc
tendunt mala quae vobis infero: ut reconciliem vos mihi*. Their Masses had
other lessons, and they are cited for the chants only.

Jerome, Augustine, Hilary and Chrysostom agree on the controlling claim: the
captivity is turned by God, the people are commanded to pray within a promise
already made, and the full return is still to come. *(Iteration 1)* Hilary
and Chrysostom add the reason prayer is commanded, from God's side (he hears
by nature; faith asks to be heard) and from the petitioner's (one's own part
first; the soul is bettered by praying).

### What each element contributes

- **Introit.** The centre, with its verse. [A] Jerome as above; Augustine,
  Athanasius and Cassiodorus on Ps 84:2. Textual fact: the antiphon omits *ut
  dem vobis finem et patientiam*, *et ibitis … et orabitis me*, v. 13 and *et
  congregabo vos de universis gentibus* (propers/verified.md). The reading may
  say what the antiphon keeps — God's peace, the call, the hearing, the
  return — and must not say why the rest was left out. [A] Durandus reads the
  affliction God sends as ordered to reconciliation.
- **Gradual.** [A] Augustine, Cassiodorus and Ambrose: certainty of the future
  in the form of the past; Bellarmine: remembered deliverance; Chrysostom:
  present pledges of providence; Aquinas: present and future. The Gradual
  stops at v. 9; the psalm goes on *But now thou hast cast us off* (v. 10).
  More than one reading is kept (scope 7.5).
- **Alleluia and Offertory.** [A] Augustine and Bellarmine on the deep;
  Hilary and Chrysostom on why the cry is commanded and how it is heard. The
  same two verses are sung twice; the Offertory repeats its first line, so the
  cry *de profúndis* is said three times before the gifts.
- **Communion.** [A] Bede: prayer obtains, on the condition of forgiving;
  Chrysostom on the parallel (*Hom. in Mt.* 67): the fig tree teaches
  *"being confident in faith and prayer"*. [E] The Communion answers the Introit's
  *invocábitis me, et ego exáudiam vos* with Christ's own promise; Jerome's
  *Petite, et accipietis* at Jer 29:13 is the bridge he himself built between
  Jeremiah and the Gospel's teaching on prayer, though not to Mark 11. [A] The
  liturgical commentators read the Communion as the answer that closes the
  chants' sequence.
- **Secret.** [E] *Hóstias … placatiónis* and Augustine's reading of Ps 129:4,
  *"propitiation … sacrifice"*: the cry from the deep is followed by the
  offering of propitiation. Augustine comments on a verse the Mass does not
  sing; the link is the editor's (scope 7.8).
- **Collect.** [E] The family *in sola spe grátiæ cæléstis innítitur*: the
  exiles lean on a promise, not on their own strength (Bellarmine on Ps 43:8,
  [A], says the same of the psalm; Chrysostom on 43:7, [A], *not in my arms …
  but in God's mercy*).
- **Postcommunion.** [E] The *pignus* received *per hæc mystéria* and the
  *efféctus* still asked for: the return is promised and begun, not complete
  (Augustine: *"not yet done"*; Bellarmine: *"in the resurrection only"*).
- **Epistle.** [E] The life commanded at Col 3:12-17 is the life of a people
  in waiting: Jer 29:5-7 tells the exiles to build, plant and seek the city's
  peace; Paul tells the Colossians to put on mercy and let the peace of Christ
  rule. A clause; neither Father connects the two.
- **Gospel.** [E] *Sínite útraque créscere usque ad messem* fixes the length of
  the waiting: the harvest is the end. The Introit omits *et congregabo vos*;
  the Gospel ends *tríticum autem congregáte in hórreum meum*. That the two
  verbs are the same is an observation, not a design.

### The difficulty

Scope 7.4 and 7.5. The reading turns on Jerome's sentence that God could fulfil
his promise without the captives' prayer and commands it so that they may
receive. If it is accepted, the chants' cry is not a request that God change
his mind but the way the people receive what he has already thought —
*cogitatiónes pacis*. Without it, the Introit's promise and the Communion's
promise would be two unrelated assurances. *(Iteration 1)* Hilary's *Naturae
suae est ut audiat; sed fidei nostrae est ut precemur audiri* and
Chrysostom's *our part first, then Hear*, both said of the Mass's own psalm,
confirm the answer from two further Fathers. The tense of *Liberásti* is left
with its several answers (scope 7.5); all serve the claim.

### What the commentator tradition changes *(iteration 1)*

The review asked whether the commentators' reading of the chants as Israel's
final return, through the Joseph typology, changes this reading's account of
the chants, its anagogy or its limits.

- **The account of the chants.** It confirms the reading's order of the chants
  — promise, thanks, plea, assurance — from three medieval liturgists who
  commented on these chants as a sequence, and supplies a figure for it, the
  brothers' plea and Joseph's forgiving answer. The reading may report that
  sequence as theirs. It does not change the controlling claim, which rests on
  the Fathers' exegesis of each verse.
- **The anagogy.** It adds a second anagogy, recorded beside the first: the
  return of Israel's remnant at the end and the true Joseph's feast with his
  brothers (Rupert, Durandus, Sicard), to which the attributed Athanasius on Ps
  84 gives a patristic analogue (the Jewish people confessing after the
  calling of the gentiles). The study may present it as these writers' reading
  of the chants.
- **The limits.** The commentators are ecclesiastical writers and cannot carry
  the reading; their Masses had other lessons (Jer 23 and Jn 6, or Phil 3 and
  the tribute money), so they say nothing of this Mass's Epistle or Gospel and
  must never be cited for a link between the chants and the tares; their
  Israel anagogy is theirs and is not this formulary's stated intent.

### Four senses

- **Literal.** Jeremiah writes to the exiles in Babylon that God thinks
  thoughts of peace toward them and will bring them back when they call on
  him; Pss 84, 43 and 129 thank God for a captivity turned, recall
  deliverance, and cry from the depths; Jesus tells his disciples that prayer
  made in faith receives.
- **Allegorical.** Christ turns the captivity of sin (Augustine, Athanasius,
  Cassiodorus on Ps 84); the martyrs' voice is the Church's (Augustine,
  Bellarmine and Aquinas on Ps 43); the propitiation is his sacrifice
  (Augustine on Ps 129:4).
- **Moral.** Pray and seek with the whole heart (Jerome on Jer 29:13;
  Bellarmine on Ps 129 and Deut 4:29); pray because faith asks to be heard
  (Hilary), supplying one's own part (Chrysostom); confess oneself in the deep
  (Augustine); forgive in order to obtain (Bede).
- **Anagogical.** The return *ad locum nostrum* (Jerome) and the deliverance
  fully accomplished *"in the resurrection"* (Bellarmine), when the enemies are
  confounded at the rising of the saints (Ambrose); the harvest's ingathering.
  Reported beside it, as the liturgical commentators' reading of the chants:
  the return of Israel's remnant and the true Joseph's reconciliation with his
  brothers (Rupert, Durandus, Sicard).

### Limits

- The commentators' reading of the chants as the end-time salvation of
  Israel's remnant is theirs, at Masses with other lessons; it may be cited for
  the chants only, and never as warrant for joining the chants to the tares.
- Schuster's remark on the Communion's place (scope 2.3) is not used.
- The study may not date Ps 43, 84 or 129 or attach Jeremiah's letter to a
  year beyond what the generated record holds (scope 9).
- Athanasius's expositions are cited as printed under his name.

## 3. `word-in-the-heart` — While men slept: the field kept by the word that dwells

### Controlling claim

The enemy sows *cum dormírent hómines* and goes away; nothing in the parable
blames the sleepers, and the Lord's explanation passes them over. The Fathers
who comment make the detail a warning: the field is lost by sleep, and the
sleep that matters can be avoided. The Epistle names what keeps a person
awake: *Verbum Christi hábitet in vobis abundánter*, and *pax Christi exsúltet
in córdibus vestris*; it locates the Church's singing *in córdibus*. The
reading takes the hearer's own heart as a field in which the word is sown and
over which the same enemy works, and finds its defence in the indwelling word,
the peace of Christ, and the prayer and song of the Mass, which come from the
heart and rouse it.

### Carrying authors and their reasoning

- **John Chrysostom** (father). *Hom. in Mt.* 46.1 [A]: *"while men slept"* is
  a danger hung over *"our rulers … and not the rulers only, but the subjects
  too"*; *"as to natural sleep, it is not possible; but as to that of our moral
  faculty, it is possible"* to stay awake; hence *"continual watchfulness"*.
  *Hom. in Col.* 8 [A]: the peace of God acts as umpire between two thoughts,
  revenge and endurance, *"an arena within, in the thoughts"*, and gives the
  prize to endurance *"by persuading him that God is Peace"*. *Hom. in Col.* 9
  [A]: the word dwells *"richly"* when the Scriptures are read at home, *"not
  … lightly"*; *"This is the cause of all evils, the not knowing the
  Scriptures"*; *"Singing in your hearts to God. Not simply with the mouth, he
  means, but with heedfulness"*; one may sing to God in the market-place, no one
  hearing, as Moses cried in thought and was heard (Ex 14:15); *"it is not
  forbidden one even when walking to pray in his heart"*; do all *"in the
  Name"*. *(Iteration 1)* *Expositio in Ps.* 129 [A]: *out of the depths* is not
  simply from the mouth and tongue, while the mind wanders, but from the
  deepest heart; prayer from the lips alone cannot rise to God.
- **Thomas Aquinas** (doctor). *Super Col.* c. 3 lect. 3 [A]: *(iteration 1, on
  3:12-15a)* peace springs from charity, and *Exultet, quia charitatis effectus
  est gaudium, quod sequitur ex pace*, the peace being Christ's, made between
  God and man; *(on 3:15b-17)* the word of Christ is the origin of wisdom, and
  it must *habitet … abundanter*; its uses are instruction, devotion and
  direction; the psalms, hymns and canticles are sung *in cordibus, et non in
  labiis tantum*, and *cantica ecclesiae cordis principaliter, sed oris sunt ut
  excitetur canticum cordis*. *Super Mt.* c. 13 [A]: the enemy *occultum se
  reddidit* and sows *in medio tritici, et fidelis populi*; Aquinas reports
  Augustine's reading of *cum crevisset herba* as the growth by which one
  becomes *spiritualis* and discerns (1 Cor 2:15).
- **Hilary of Poitiers** (father), *Tract. in Ps.* 129.2 *(iteration 1)*. [A]
  The prophet cries *ex interno cordis sui secreto*; *existima illum non labiis,
  sed secreto, ut jussum est, cordis orare* (Mt 6:6). Hilary's contribution is
  the Mass's own cry *de profúndis*, read as a prayer of the secret heart.

Supporting: **Jerome** (father), *In Mt.* II on 13:36-43 [A]: the sleepers are
the Church's teachers, and the one set over the Church must not sleep. His
restriction to pastors is kept as a narrower answer (scope 7.2). *(Iteration
1)* **Rabanus Maurus** (saint), *In Mt.* IV [A]: the good seed is sown by one
*qui in corpore suo semen bonae voluntatis ad bonorum operum studet perducere
fructum*, and the sleepers warn us *ne dum somno inertiae torpemus, fructum
laboris nostri perdamus*. **Theodoret** and **Theophylact** (ecclesiastical
writers) on Col 3:15-16 [A]: the peace of God as umpire of the inner contest
(both, after Chrysostom); *in your hearts*, *"not with the mouth only"*
(Theodoret).

*(Iteration 1, correcting iteration 0.)* Chrysostom and Aquinas agree on the
claim: the word must dwell abundantly and the heart, not only the lips, must
sing — both say it at Col 3:16, Chrysostom in *Hom. in Col.* 9 and Aquinas in
his lecture. Chrysostom alone of the two draws the warning from the sleepers;
Hilary and Chrysostom say of the Mass's cry from the depths what the two say of
its singing. Rabanus applies the field and the sleep to each person, which is
the reading's extension.

### What each element contributes

- **Gospel.** [A] Chrysostom on the sleepers; Rabanus on the sleep of inertia
  and the seed sown in one's own body; Aquinas on the enemy's secrecy. [E] The
  reading applies the field to the individual hearer as well as to the
  Church; Chrysostom's extension of the warning to *"the subjects too"* and
  Rabanus's moral reading are its warrant, and the Lord's own gloss (*the field
  is the world*) remains the literal frame (scope 7.3).
- **Epistle.** The centre with the Gospel. [A] Chrysostom on the umpire and on
  reading the Scriptures and singing in the heart; Aquinas on the joy that
  follows peace, on the word and on the singing heart. The text difference must
  be stated: Chrysostom, Theodoret and Theophylact read *let it be umpire*; the
  Mass sings *exsúltet*, *let it rejoice*, which Aquinas expounds (scope 7.6).
- **Alleluia and Offertory.** [A] Hilary: the cry from the depths is prayer
  *non labiis, sed secreto … cordis*; Chrysostom: from the deepest heart, not
  from mouth and tongue; Bellarmine: one depth of the cry is *"the depth of his
  heart"*, *"a thorough consideration and deep reflection on his own misery"*.
  [E] The one who does not know he is in the deep does not cry — the sleeper
  again.
- **The other chants.** [E] The Mass's own psalms — *De profúndis* twice,
  *Liberásti … laudábimur tota die* — are what the Epistle calls *psalmis …
  cantántes in córdibus*; Aquinas's sentence that the mouth sings to rouse the
  heart is said of the Church's songs in general, not of these.
- **Secret.** [E] *nutántia corda tu dírigas*: the heart that wavers is the
  field in which the enemy sows; the prayer asks God to direct it.
- **Collect.** [E] *contínua pietáte custódi*: the guarding the sleeping men
  failed to give is asked of God, continually. A clause.
- **Communion.** [E] Its chapter: *non hæsitáverit in corde suo, sed
  credíderit* (Mk 11:23, not appointed) precedes the appointed verse; the
  promise of the Communion is made to the undivided heart. A clause.
- **Introit.** [E] God's *cogitatiónes* are of peace; Jerome [A] stresses that
  only God knows them. The peace the Epistle wants to rule the heart is the
  peace God thinks toward his people. A clause.
- **Postcommunion.** [E] What is received as *pignus* has its *efféctus* in a
  life; the harvest shows what grew (*cum crevísset herba … tunc apparuérunt*).

### The difficulty

Scope 7.2 and 7.6. The reading turns on the judgment that moral sleep is
avoidable and that the warning reaches every hearer (Chrysostom; Rabanus);
with Jerome's narrower answer alone, the reading would be a pastoral rule for
clergy. It also turns on Christ's peace in the heart, which the Greek
witnesses read as an umpire and the Latin as joy: the reading must present
Chrysostom's argument as his reading of the Greek and may give Aquinas's
reading of the word the Mass sings *(iteration 1)*.

### Four senses

- **Literal.** An enemy sows darnel among the wheat at night while the workers
  sleep; Paul tells the Colossians to let the peace of Christ rejoice in their
  hearts, to let the word of Christ dwell in them abundantly, and to sing to
  God in their hearts.
- **Allegorical.** Christ's word is sown in his Church and dwells in it; the
  Church's song is the voice that rouses the heart (Aquinas); the peace is
  Christ's, who *"hath made peace with us"* (Chrysostom), made between God and
  man (Aquinas).
- **Moral.** Keep awake in the moral sense (Chrysostom), not torpid with the
  sleep of inertia (Rabanus); let the Scriptures dwell in the home
  (Chrysostom); settle the inner contest between revenge and endurance by
  Christ's peace (Chrysostom); sing and pray with the heart and not the lips
  only (Chrysostom, Aquinas, Hilary); do all in the Name.
- **Anagogical.** *(Iteration 1, grounded.)* At the harvest the angels, who
  cannot err, separate what grew together (Augustine, *Sermo* 73.4; *"In the
  field they may be with you, but they will not be so in the barn"*, 73.1);
  the one who bore patiently is repaid by justice (Rabanus); what the word
  brought to fruit in the heart is gathered into the barn (Mt 13:30). The
  application of the harvest to each heart is the editor's, on the warrant of
  Rabanus's reading of the field.

### Limits

- Applying the field to the single soul is the editor's extension, warranted by
  Chrysostom's *"the subjects too"* and Rabanus's moral reading, and kept
  beside, not in place of, the Lord's *the field is the world*.
- Aquinas's lecture on Col 3:12-15a is read in the Corpus Thomisticum text
  (a restricted response whose bytes are not retained; no page image of a
  printed edition was read); the Dessain printing lacks it.
- Hilary and Chrysostom speak of prayer from the heart at Ps 129, not of the
  Mass's singing; the link between the Offertory's cry and the Epistle's
  *cantántes in córdibus* is the editor's.

## Comparison of the three readings

| | `patience-of-the-field` | `cry-from-captivity` | `word-in-the-heart` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Governing text | Gospel, *ne forte … Sínite* | Introit and the chants | Gospel's sleepers and Epistle's *Verbum Christi* |
| Principal witnesses | Augustine, Jerome, Chrysostom, Rabanus (Aquinas, Honorius) | Jerome, Augustine, Hilary, Chrysostom (Bellarmine, Ambrose, Aquinas, Cassiodorus, Athanasius, Schuster; Sicard, Rupert, Durandus for the chants) | Chrysostom, Aquinas, Hilary (Jerome, Rabanus, Theodoret, Theophylact) |
| What it reads in the Gospel | the reason for the prohibition | the length of the waiting | the sleep in which the enemy sows |
| What it reads in the Epistle | forbearance and forgiveness | the life of a people in waiting | peace and the indwelling word |
| What it reads in Ps 129 | the servants too are in the deep | why the cry is commanded and how it is heard | the cry from the secret heart |
| The people | servants tempted to uproot | exiles, martyrs, sinners in the deep | each hearer's own field |
| Its consequence | judge slowly; seek conversion; bear injury | pray within the promise | keep the heart awake |

What they share: the harvest as the end that fixes the present (Mt 13:30, 39),
the Epistle as the life of the present, and the Postcommunion's pledge
awaiting its effect. Where they differ: the first and third use the same
Gospel verse-group differently — the first the servants' question and the
householder's answer, the third the sleepers — and they ask about different
objects, others and oneself. The second is the only one that makes the chants
its centre and reads the Gospel at its edge. *(Iteration 1)* The second and
third now share the Offertory's psalm and use different words of the same
Fathers: Hilary's *fidei nostrae est ut precemur audiri* serves the second,
his *non labiis, sed secreto … cordis* the third. No reading needs another's
conclusion, and none contradicts another; the one material disagreement
inside the tradition (coercion, scope 8.1) belongs to the first and does not
affect the others.
