\section*{Note on Sources and Delivery}

{\footnotesize

\textbf{Audience and occasion.} An adult parish assembly, at the Mass
\latin{Dominica decima septima post Pentecosten}, II classis, of the 1962
\work{Missale Romanum}, editio typica, pp.~398--400, nos.~1602--1611, for the
occurrence of 20~September 2026.

\textbf{Length and pace.} The spoken body runs to 1,416 words. At an
unhurried preaching pace of 120 to 130 words a minute that is between eleven
and twelve minutes, and at 110 words a minute just under thirteen. The
figure is arithmetic done on the word count; no speaker has delivered these
words and no delivery has been timed. The prose was read through in full for
sense, sentence length and ease of speech, silently and not aloud.

\textbf{Relation to the reviewed readings.} The expansive study of this
formulary develops three readings of the whole Mass. Two of them carry the
argument preached here: \textit{The whole heart for the one God, and the one
body that love gathers}, which supplies the double commandment, Augustine on
the undivided heart, and the Epistle's unity as the form that love takes in a
people; and \textit{David's son and David's Lord: the one God confessed in his
Word and Spirit}, which supplies Christ's own question and Augustine's answer
to it. What joins them is not an editorial
device but Saint John Chrysostom's reading of the Gospel's two halves in his
seventy-first homily on Matthew, where the question about the Christ follows
from the commandment that names one God. The third reading,
\textit{``Thou art just, O Lord'': the humbled people prays with Daniel for
mercy}, supplies the Introit and the Offertory as the prayer in which the
other two are received, with Jerome on Daniel praying as one of his own
people. Chrysostom joins the two halves of the Gospel; the reading of the
whole formulary that follows from that join is editorial, and no Father is
credited with it. The exegetical argument joins the Gospel in both its
halves, the Epistle appointed with it, and the Collect of the Mass, and draws
in the Introit, the Offertory, the Communion and the Postcommunion; the
Gradual, the Alleluia and the Secret are left to the studies.

\textbf{Texts quoted, and their route.} Scripture is the Douay--Rheims in
Bishop Challoner's revision, at the canonical verses: Mt 22:36, 37, 39, 42,
45, 46; Eph 4:2--6; Ps 118:137 and 124 (Heb.\ 119); Ps 75:12 (Heb.\ 76). The
Collect and the Postcommunion are quoted in the anonymous English printed by
Eugene Cummiskey at Philadelphia in 1861, which is a historical study aid and
not an approved liturgical translation of the 1962 Missal. The Offertory
antiphon is a compilation that no Douay verse renders, so the homily describes
it and quotes no English of it. Augustine's \work{De doctrina christiana} and
Chrysostom's homily are quoted in the nineteenth-century English of the Nicene
and Post-Nicene Fathers; Augustine on Ps~109 and Jerome on Daniel are reported
from their Latin, with Jerome's five words given as he wrote them. That Latin
was read in the tracked digital transcriptions of Migne which this leaf
registers --- the Wikisource and Corpus Corporum text for the
\work{Enarrationes}, the Wikisource text of PL~25 for the commentary on
Daniel --- and not on the printed columns.

\textbf{Exact loci.} Augustine, ``no part of our life is to be unoccupied''
and the one channel of the affections: \work{De doctrina christiana} I.22.21,
the second paragraph of the chapter block I.22.20--21 that the research
record inspects. Chrysostom, ``this makes the way for
that, and by it is again established'', ``not to the rejection of the Son, but
to make the distinction from idols'', and ``to confess Him also to be God'':
\work{Homilies on Matthew} 71. Augustine on answering as the Pharisees
answered and not remaining there, and on the form of God and the form of a
servant: \work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 109, 3--6. Jerome, \latin{quia unus e
populo est}: \work{Commentarii in Danielem}, on 9:5. The Creed is appointed by
the rubric printed at the end of the Gospel, no.~1607.
