\section{The Three Readings Compared}
\label{sec:comparison}

The three readings share more than they divide. All three hear the Mass as
the prayer of one people before the one God. The Collect speaks for
\latin{populo tuo} and asks to follow \latin{te solum Deum}; the Gradual blesses
the \latin{beata gens} whose God is the Lord; the Offertory prays for
\latin{populum istum, super quem invocatum est nomen tuum}. All three take the
Gospel and the Epistle as central. In all three St Augustine is the witness
whose own reading reaches across more than one element: his exposition of
Ps~101 quotes the Epistle's chapter of Ephesians, his sermons on Ps~32 join the
blessed nation to the love that possesses God, and his exposition of Ps~109
begins from this Sunday's Gospel. All three hear the orations as prayer
rather than as the anchors of an argument. Where the first and the third
readings ask what the sacrament those prayers seek actually does, both call on
the same chapter of the Council of Trent (sess.~XIII, cap.~2): the first for
the Eucharist as the sign of the one body, the third for the antidote that
frees from daily faults. The second reading does not need that doctrine, and
does not use it; it hears the same three orations as confession, in the
Collect's \latin{te solum Deum}, the Secret's \latin{maiestas} beside the
Preface appointed after it, and the Postcommunion's \latin{sanctificationes}.

They part on the questions below.

The readings are complementary emphases, not rival identifications. The first
and the second divide the Gospel between them, the commandment and the
question about the Christ, and St John Chrysostom's seventy-first homily on
Matthew is the witness that they belong together. Having praised the
confession of one God, Christ asks about the Christ, so that the God who is to
be loved with the whole heart is shown to be the God whose Son is David's Lord.
The third rests on the chants that have stood together at this Sunday
longest, the Introit, the Offertory and the Communion, and gives the Mass its
tone: the first two supply its doctrine, and the third the posture in which
that doctrine is received.

No witness called on here opposes another's controlling claim. The
disagreements are real but narrow, and each belongs to one text. Augustine
hears Christ in the poor man of Ps~101 and Cassiodorus hears the poor of
Christ. Jerome allows that Daniel, though sinless, joined himself to a sinful
people, and Augustine insists that Daniel confessed sins of his own. Jerome
and Aquinas give ``above all, and through all, and in us all'' to the three
Persons, and Chrysostom and Theodoret read it of the one God and Father.
Hilary and Augustine place the beginning of the servant's mercy differently.
Chrysostom and Jerome give different reasons for the lawyer's question.
Bellarmine takes the Introit's \latin{iudicium} for the law, and Rupert and
Durandus for the judgment that exalts the humble.

Each reading ends at the same place, the silence that closes the Gospel.
``No man was able to answer him a word.'' The first reading answers that
silence with love, the second with confession, the third with prayer; and the
Mass itself, having heard the Gospel, answers it with the Creed.
