# Interpretations — Seventeenth Sunday after Pentecost (1962 Roman Rite)

Research record of the coherent readings proposed for the expansive study of
`liturgy/roman-rite/1962/propers/temporal/57-seventeenth-after-pentecost`.
Written by the research stage of `proper-study` v3, run `1e02dc05f2df9940`,
18 September 2026, and revised on that stage's reentry the same day after cold
research review (the Secret's contribution to reading 2, the anagogical sense of
reading 3, the sacramentary history of the orations, the Epistle's course, and
the later psalm commentators). Revised again on the stage's second reentry: the
Preface clause of reading 2's anagogical sense, two quotations, the standing of
Irenaeus, Theodoret on Ephesians (readings 1 and 2), Hilary on Ps 118 (reading
3), and the 1962 Missal's reuse of the Postcommunion's clause with this Gospel at
St John of God (reading 1). Revised a third time on the stage's third reentry.
Chrysostom's gloss on Eph 4:6 is now quoted. The Fathers' reading of the verse
is restated as two lines: Jerome and Aquinas give the three phrases to the
Persons, and Chrysostom and Theodoret read them as three relations of the one God
and Father. This touches readings 1 and 2, §§1.2, 1.6, 2.1, 2.2, 2.5 and 2.6. It
is reasoning for the authors and reviewers of the three documents, not
reader-facing prose.

Every author claim below rests on a passage this stage read; loci, editions and
routes are in `research/scope.md` §§3 and 6. Each contribution is marked:

- **[A]** an author's own reading of that text, read at the locus given;
- **[T]** a textual observation about the appointed words (`propers/verified.md`);
- **[H]** documented historical orientation (`research/scope.md` §2);
- **[E]** the editor's synthesis: a connection this record draws and no author

No author below is credited with a whole-Mass reading of the 1962 formulary.

elements they share with the 1962 book. Every whole-formulary reading here is
therefore editorial synthesis of a formulary whose chants, orations and Gospel
were transmitted separately (scope §2.6).

## The three readings in brief

| Key | Title | Controlling claim | Principal witnesses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `whole-heart-one-body` | The whole heart for the one God, and the one body that love gathers | The Mass asks that God alone be loved with the whole heart, and shows that love lived as the unity of one people whose members bear one another. | Augustine; John Chrysostom; Gregory the Great; Jerome; Theodoret; Thomas Aquinas |
| `son-and-lord-of-david` | David's son and David's Lord: the one God confessed in his Word and Spirit | Christ's question leads the Church to confess him Son of David according to the flesh and Lord according to his eternal birth, and the Mass's "one Lord … one God" is the confession of the one God in Father, Word and Spirit. | Jerome; Augustine; John Chrysostom; Thomas Aquinas; Irenaeus; Basil; Cassiodorus; Robert Bellarmine |
| `just-judge-merciful-hearer` | "You are just, O Lord": the humbled people prays with Daniel for mercy | The Mass is the prayer of a people that confesses God's just judgment and asks mercy, not according to its own justice; with Daniel it intercedes for the sanctuary and the people called by God's name, and renders its vows. | Augustine; Jerome; Cassiodorus; Hilary; Robert Bellarmine; with Rupert and Durandus for the chants |

The three are complementary. They differ in governing emphasis (love, faith,
penitent prayer), in the element that anchors them (the Gospel's first half, the
Gospel's second half with the Gradual verse and the Epistle's vv. 5–6, the
Introit and Offertory), and in the theological consequence they draw. They do
not dispute an identification. See §4.

## 1. `whole-heart-one-body` — The whole heart for the one God, and the one body that love gathers

### 1.1 Controlling claim

The Gospel answers the lawyer that the whole law hangs on two commandments: the
love of God with the whole heart, soul and mind, and the love of the neighbour
"like to this". Augustine reads "the whole heart" as leaving no part of life free
to enjoy anything but God, and every other love, of self and of neighbour, as
carried into that one current; Chrysostom reads the second as the way that leads
to the first and by which the first is established. The Epistle shows that love
lived: a calling walked worthily in humility, meekness and patience, "supporting
one another in charity", keeping "the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace",
because the people is one body in one Spirit and one hope. The Mass as a whole
can then be heard as the prayer of a people that asks to follow God alone with a
pure mind (Collect), rejoices to have him as its possession and to be his
inheritance (Gradual), prays with one voice of head and members (Alleluia),
renders the vows common to all (Communion), and asks that the sacrament heal it
(Secret, Postcommunion). **[E]** for the whole-Mass form; **[A]** for each author
claim marked below.

### 1.2 Principal witnesses and their reasoning

- **Augustine, *De doctrina christiana* I.22.20–21, I.27.28, I.30.31–33.** [A]
  God alone is to be enjoyed; even oneself and one's neighbour are loved for God's
  sake. "With all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind" means
  "that no part of our life is to be unoccupied, and to afford room, as it were,
  for the wish to enjoy some other object"; loving the neighbour aright means
  urging him to love God wholly, so that "the whole current of his love both for
  himself and his neighbor" flows "into the channel of the love of God". Love has
  an order (I.27.28). The neighbour is every man, since the Lord's parable answers
  "Who is my neighbour?" by mercy; the angels too; and the Lord himself "desired to
  be called our neighbor" (I.30.31–33). Why it matters: Augustine makes the two
  commandments one movement of love, which is what lets the reading hear the
  Collect's *te solum Deum* and the Epistle's charity as one petition. **The
  Collect link is [E].**
- **Augustine, *De moribus Ecclesiae catholicae* I.11.18.** [A] Following after
  God is the desire of happiness and reaching him is happiness; "we follow after
  God by loving Him"; the first commandment is the one "which leads to happy life".
  (English of NPNF1 4; the Latin was not read.) This gives the reading its link
  between the Gospel's love and the Collect's "follow thee" — **[E]** as a claim
  about the Collect.
- **John Chrysostom, *In Mt.* hom. 71.** [A] The second commandment is "like"
  because "this makes the way for that, and by it is again established"; "if
  therefore to love God is to love one's neighbor … with reason doth He say, On
  these hang all the law and the prophets". Jesus answers beyond the question to
  expose that it came "from having no charity, from pining with envy". The
  homily's moral close attacks vainglory, the "many-headed" passion that dissolves
  charity. Chrysostom's mechanism (mutual establishment) differs from Augustine's
  (ordering to God) without contradicting it (scope §4.3).
- **Gregory the Great, *Hom. in Ev.* 38.9–10.** [A] On the wedding garment of Mt
  22:1–13, the parable that precedes this Gospel in Matthew: the garment is charity,
  woven "sicut in duobus lignis" on the love of God and of neighbour. The
  neighbour is loved with a measure (*sicut teipsum*), God without one (*ex
  toto*): "ille veraciter Deum diligit, qui sibi de se nihil relinquit". The
  doubly-dyed scarlet of the tabernacle is love of God that does not neglect the
  neighbour and love of neighbour that does not abandon God. Gregory expounds the
  commandment (cited from Mk 12:30), not Mt 22:34–46; his is doctrinal reception
  of the words this Gospel carries, and it gives the reading its sharpest account
  of "the whole heart" beside Augustine's.
- **John Chrysostom, *In Eph.* hom. 9–11.** [A] The calling is "as His body";
  lowliness is "the basis of all virtue"; "if thou art not forbearing to thy
  neighbor, how shall God be forbearing to thee?"; the Spirit was given "that He
  might unite those who are separated by race and by different manners … and every
  soul become in a manner one"; the fire that fuses dry wood into one blaze and
  cannot fuse wet; "with this bond let us bind ourselves together with one another
  and unto God" (hom. 9). The one body is "the faithful throughout the whole world,
  both which are, and which have been, and which shall be" (hom. 10). "God hath
  called you all on the same terms", and none is "called by the name of a greater
  God, another, of a lesser God", so "whence is it that ye are high-minded?"
  (hom. 11, on vv. 4–6).
- **Jerome, *In Eph.* II on 4:1–4** (PL 26, 492–495). [A] Humility is "non tam in
  sermone, quam in mente"; "sufferentes invicem in charitate" is for those still in
  the beginnings of virtue and means bearing the sick brother, the poor, the widow;
  v. 3 "vel maxime adversum haereticos facit, qui, pacis vinculo dissipato … putant
  se tenere spiritus unitatem"; the one hope is the kingdom, one house of the Father
  with many mansions.
- **Theodoret of Cyrus, *In Eph.* c. 4, vv. 1–6** (PG 82, 532–533, Greek, read on
  the page images; scope §3.3). [A] Paul's bonds are named to rouse even the
  insensible to virtue; walking worthily is a way of life fitting the calling,
  which is not to insult God's benefit by a lawless life. He urges lowliness
  first, because spiritual gifts could puff up the mind. For vv. 2–3: "Μιᾶς
  ἅπαντες τετυχήκατε χάριτος, μία πηγὴ τοὺς διαφόρους ἐκπέμπει κρουνούς"; so
  bear with one another "κατὰ τὸν ἀγάπης νόμον" and keep the bond of peace. On
  vv. 5–6: "Πανταχοῦ τὸ ἓν καὶ εἷς τέθεικεν, εἰς συμφωνίαν συνάπτων τὴν
  Ἐκκλησίαν", and the conclusion he draws is "Προσήκει τοίνυν ὑμᾶς, ὡς
  ἀδελφοὺς, τὴν περὶ ἀλλήλους ἔχειν ὁμόνοιαν". On v. 6 he gives Chrysostom's
  gloss, reading the three phrases as the lordship, providence and indwelling of
  the one God and Father (scope §4.5). Why it matters: the
  diverse gifts flow from one grace as streams from one spring. That makes the
  Epistle's unity a work of love among people unequally gifted, which is the
  reading's moral centre; with Chrysostom's "all … called on the same terms"
  (hom. 11) it is the Greek line for the Epistle.
- **Thomas Aquinas, *Super Eph.* c. 4 lect. 1; *Super Mt.* c. 22 lect. 4.** [A]
  Unity is the end of Paul's exhortation; peace is the bond, defined "secundum
  Augustinum" as a tranquillity of order (optical layer, not checked on the image); *unum corpus … unus spiritus* by faith and charity, or body toward
  neighbour and spirit toward God. On Matthew: the first commandment is "maximum
  capacitate" because love of neighbour is contained in it; the second is like the
  first because man is made to God's likeness and "diligitur Deus in illo"; the
  neighbour includes man and angel (after Augustine).
- **Augustine, *En. in Ps.* 32 II s. 2, 15–18; *En. in Ps.* 101 s. 1, 2–3;
  *En. in Ps.* 75, 16–17.** [A] All men desire happiness; the blessed nation is the
  one whose God is the Lord, "hoc ama, hoc posside"; God is our possession and we
  his inheritance, "alterutrum se possident" (Ps 32). The poor man of Ps 101 is
  Christ head and body, "Vox ergo una, quia caro una", quoting Eph 4:13, 15; "quicumque
  in meo corpore tribulantur, ego tribulor". The vows of Ps 75 begin with what is
  common to all — to believe, to hope for eternal life, to live well — and those
  "round about him" are those who hold truth in common and humbly.

### 1.4 The four senses

- **Literal.** Jesus answers a lawyer in the Temple that the greatest commandment is
  to love God with the whole heart, soul and mind, that the second, to love the
  neighbour as oneself, is like it, and that the law and the prophets hang on both;
  Paul, a prisoner, urges the Ephesians to walk worthily of their calling in
  humility and patience and to keep the unity of the Spirit, since they are one
  body with one hope, one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father.
- **Allegorical.** The one body is Christ and the Church, one voice of head and
  members (Augustine on Ps 101); the blessed nation is the people gathered from all
  nations into the heavenly city (Augustine, Cassiodorus on Ps 32); the neighbour
  who showed mercy is Christ himself (Augustine DDC I.30.33).
- **Moral.** Love God without reserving any part of the heart for another
  enjoyment (Augustine) and without measure, leaving nothing of oneself to oneself
  (Gregory), and love every neighbour for God's sake and toward God; bear one
  another's burdens, not only the congenial; hold truth in common and humbly;
  render the vows common to every Christian and those one has freely made.
- **Anagogical.** The one hope of the calling is the one house of the Father
  (Jerome); the blessed nation will possess God and be possessed by him
  (Augustine, "alterutrum se possident"), the beatitude now begun "inchoative et in
  spe" and then "perfecte" (Aquinas, Super Ps. 32 n. 11); the Postcommunion's
  *remédia ætérna* looks to the healing completed in glory ([T]; its fit **[E]**).

### 1.5 Strongest difficulty and alternative

- **The Gospel has two halves.** A charity reading that ignores Mt 22:41–46 thins
  the Gospel. Chrysostom himself joins the halves (hom. 71), and this reading must
  use that join and leave the full Christological development to reading 2.
- **The Introit and Offertory sit loosely.** They are chants of justice, mercy and
  intercession. Their contribution here is real but secondary; the reading must
  not force "love" into them.
- **Ordinary Time.** Nothing shows the Gospel, the Epistle and the chants were
  chosen for a theme of love. The Epistle stands where the Pauline course puts it,
  between Eph 3:13–21 on the Sixteenth Sunday and Ephesians resumed on the
  Nineteenth (scope §2.5); the Gospel is the least stable element, Luke 14 at this
  Sunday in Rupert's and Durandus's books and read on the next Sunday where
  Durandus's churches read it at all (scope §2.4); Guéranger dates the name
  "Sunday of the love of God" to the later placement of this Gospel. The Collect
  first stands in a Gelasian Sunday Mass with no Gospel (scope §2.3). The reading
  is a theological synthesis of the formulary as the 1962 Missal gives it.

### 1.6 Agreements and differences within the reading

Augustine and Chrysostom agree that the two commandments are inseparable and that
love of neighbour is ordered to God; they differ in the mechanism (scope §4.3).
Chrysostom and Jerome differ on why the lawyer asked (scope §4.4); neither change
the reading's claim. Aquinas gathers both and adds the image of God in the
neighbour. Theodoret agrees with Chrysostom and Jerome that the Epistle calls
first to lowliness and then to concord. At v. 6 he follows Chrysostom's gloss,
the three relations of the one God and Father, and not Jerome's distribution
to the Persons (scope §4.5). From "one Lord … one God and Father of all" both
Greek Fathers draw the moral this reading uses: Chrysostom says none should be
high-minded, and Theodoret says brothers should have concord. The difference
from Jerome does not touch this reading.

## 2. `son-and-lord-of-david` — David's son and David's Lord: the one God confessed in his Word and Spirit

### 2.1 Controlling claim

The Gospel's climax is Christ's own question: "What think you of Christ? Whose son
is he?" Jerome, Augustine, Chrysostom and Aquinas read David's "in spirit … my
Lord" as the revelation that the Christ is David's son according to the flesh and
David's Lord according to his eternal generation from the Father, equal in honour
and power with him. The Epistle's "One Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and
Father of all, who is above all, and through all, and in us all" is read by Jerome
and Aquinas as the one Lord who is the Son, the one faith and threefold baptism in
the Trinity, and the one God above all (Father), through all (Son), in all (Holy
Spirit), Aquinas by appropriation after first reading the phrases as the one
God's dignity. Chrysostom reads the three phrases as the one God and Father
above all, providing for all and dwelling in all. He argues from the verse that
this attribute, which is owned to be the Son's, cannot mark inferiority, since
it is said here of the Father. The Gradual verse "By the word of the Lord the
heavens were established, and all the power of them by the spirit of his
mouth" is the text by
which Irenaeus states the rule of faith in one God who made all things by his Word
and Spirit, and in which Augustine, Basil and Cassiodorus hear Father, Son and
Holy Spirit. The Mass can then be heard as the confession of the one God, loved
with the whole heart (Gospel, first half; Collect *te solum Deum*), whose Son is
David's Lord, and whom the appointed Preface confesses as "one God, one Lord … in a
Trinity of one substance". **[E]** for the whole-Mass form.

### 2.2 Principal witnesses and their reasoning

- **Jerome, *In Mt.* IV on 22:41–46** (PL 26, 166–167). [A] "If he is a mere man and
  only David's son, how does David call him his Lord … not by uncertain error nor by
  his own will, but in the Holy Spirit?" "Dominus igitur David vocatur, non secundum
  id quod de eo natus est, sed juxta id quod natus ex Patre semper fuit, praeveniens
  ipsum carnis suae patrem." He refutes a Jewish application of Ps 109 to Abraham by
  the psalm's own "ex utero ante luciferum genui te" and "sacerdos in aeternum".
- **Augustine, *En. in Ps.* 109, 3–6.** [A] The psalm prophesies Christ "certe
  aperteque"; Christians answer Christ's question as the Jews did, "David", "sed non
  remaneamus ubi Judaei": "in forma Dei … ecce Dominus David … formam servi
  accipiens … inde filius David"; Christ's question "non te filium ejus negasti, sed
  modum quo id fieret inquisisti", and the answer is Isaiah's virgin and Emmanuel.
  The Jews "maluerunt inflata taciturnitate disrumpi, quam humili confessione
  edoceri".
- **John Chrysostom, *In Mt.* hom. 71.** [A] "There is one God" is said "not to the
  rejection of the Son, but to make the distinction from idols"; the counter-question
  leads them "to confess Him also to be God"; David "in spirit" witnesses "to His
  being Lord, and the genuineness of His Sonship, and His equality in honor with His
  Father".
- **Thomas Aquinas, *Super Mt.* c. 22 lect. 4.** [A] Two generations; the Pharisees
  answer of the flesh; the psalm shows the Son's "praeeminentiam ad sanctos,
  aequalitatem ad patrem, et dominium super rebelles"; "sede a dextris meis" is the
  Father's generating Word giving "potestatem, aequalitatem et auctoritatem";
  "filius est secundum carnem … et dominus secundum divinitatem".
- **Jerome, *In Eph.* II on 4:4–6** (PL 26, 494–496). [A] One body (the Church, or
  the body taken of the Virgin); one Holy Spirit, "largitor et sanctificator
  omnium"; "Unus est Dominus, et unus est Deus, quia Patris et Filii dominatio, una
  divinitas est. Propterea et fides una dicitur, quia similiter in Patrem, et in
  Filium, et in Spiritum sanctum credimus. Et baptisma unum … et ter mergimur, ut
  Trinitatis unum appareat sacramentum"; "Super omnes enim est Deus Pater, quia
  auctor est omnium. Per omnes Filius, quia cuncta transcurrit, vaditque per omnia.
  In omnibus Spiritus sanctus, quia nihil absque eo est." Against Sabellius and the
  Arians.
- **Thomas Aquinas, *Super Eph.* c. 4 lect. 2** (v. 6 read on the Dessain page
  images). [A] One Lord is Christ; one faith; one baptism given "in nomine unius,
  scilicet trinitatis"; one God (*Audi Israel, Dominus Deus tuus unus est*) and
  Father. The one God's dignity is commended "ex tribus": "Ex altitudine
  divinitatis … *Qui est super omnes* … Ex amplitudine ejus potestatis … *Per
  omnia* … Ex largitate gratiæ … *Et in omnibus nobis*, sc. per gratiam". "Sed
  primum appropriatur patri qui est fontale principium divinitatis", the second
  to the Son, the third to the Holy Spirit. He reads the phrases of the one God
  first and appropriates them second, so he holds both lines of scope §4.5.
- **John Chrysostom, *In Eph.* hom. 11.** [A] "'There is one God and Father of all,
  who is over all, and through all, and in all.' 'Who is over all,' that is, the
  Lord and above all; and 'through all,' that is, providing for, ordering all;
  and 'in you all,' that is, who dwelleth in you all. Now this they own to be an
  attribute of the Son; so that were it an argument of inferiority, it never would
  have been said of the Father." (NPNF1 13. Migne's Greek, PG 62, 80, glosses the
  first phrase "who is above all", with no word for "the Lord".) He does not give
  the phrases to the Persons. He reads them of the one God and Father, as
  Theodoret does, and supports the confession of the Son's equality by an
  argument: an attribute said of the Father cannot mark inferiority in the Son.
  His "this" does not name the phrase. It fits "through all", but that is the
  record's inference (scope §4.5).
- **Irenaeus, *Adv. haer.* I.22.1** (a Greek author, read only in the ANF
  English; doctrinal use of Ps 32:6, not an exposition of the psalm; scope §3.4).
  [A] for his use of the verse. "The rule of truth which we hold, is, that
  there is one God Almighty, who made all things by His Word … 'By the Word of the
  Lord were the heavens established, and all the might of them, by the spirit of His
  mouth' … by His Word and Spirit, makes, and disposes, and governs all things … the
  God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, above whom there is no
  other God." He uses the Gradual verse as a proof-text for the one God's creation
  by Word and Spirit, against those who divide God. It is doctrinal reception of
  the verse, to be presented as such and not as exegesis of the psalm.
- **Basil, *De Spiritu Sancto* 16.38.** [A] "The Word is He who was with God in the
  beginning and was God, and the Spirit of the mouth of God is the Spirit of truth
  which proceeds from the Father. You are therefore to perceive three, the Lord who
  gives the order, the Word who creates, and the Spirit who confirms." (NPNF2 8
  English; the Greek was not read.)
- **Augustine, *En. in Ps.* 32 II s. 2, 5.** [A] "videte eadem opera Filii et
  Spiritus sancti … Verbum certe Dei Filius est, et Spiritus oris ejus Spiritus
  sanctus est."
- **Cassiodorus, on Ps 32:6.** [A] Besides the apostolic reading, "sanctam hic
  significat Trinitatem. Dicendo enim Verbo, Filium declarat; adiungendo Domini,
  Patrem dicit: spiritu oris eius, … Spiritum sanctum … Et ut in tribus personis
  manifestam intelligeres unitatem, eius dixit, non eorum."
- **Thomas Aquinas, *Super Psalmos*, Ps 32 n. 5.** [A] "Dominus est nomen
  potestatis, et potentia appropriatur patri. … Et verbum est filius. Spiritus
  ejus est spiritus sanctus. Dicitur autem spiritus oris, quia verbo appropriatur
  os: unde idem est dictum, ac si diceret, spiritus verbi; quia ipse est spiritus
  filii et veritatis"; the Trinity's works are undivided, and the psalm speaks
  "secundum appropriationem" — the same mechanism he uses at Eph 4:6 (lect. 2),
  so that the Gradual verse and the Epistle's "above all, through all, in all"
  are read by one Doctor in one way. Mystically the heavens are the apostles, made
  firm by Christ's word and by the Spirit (Lk 24:49).
- **St Robert Bellarmine, on Ps 32:6** (O'Sullivan's English). [A] "there is no
  doubt but the Holy Ghost meant to glance at the mystery of the Holy Trinity to
  be revealed in the New Testament", while "the acts of the Trinity cannot be
  separated, by reason of the unity of essence". A later Doctor who keeps the
  literal sense (God's word of command in Gen 1) and the Trinitarian sense
  together.
- **Durandus, *Rationale* VI, on the Eighteenth Sunday.** [A] (liturgical) Where
  some churches read this Gospel on the next Sunday, Durandus reads its second
  half as Christ reproving the Pharisees "de infidelitate, quia non credebant
  ipsum esse Deum" (scope §2.4). A liturgical commentator on this pericope as a
  Gospel of the season, one Sunday later than the 1962 book.

### 2.4 The four senses

- **Literal.** Jesus asks the Pharisees whose son the Christ is and, when they say
  "David's", asks how David, speaking by the Spirit in Ps 109, can call him Lord;
  none can answer. Paul grounds the Church's unity in one Lord, one faith, one
  baptism, one God and Father of all. The psalmist praises the Lord by whose word
  the heavens were made firm and all their host by the breath of his mouth.
- **Allegorical.** Christ is David's son in the flesh and David's Lord from eternity
  (Jerome, Augustine, Chrysostom, Aquinas); the one God is Father, Word and Spirit,
  above all, through all and in all (Jerome, Aquinas); the heavens made firm by
  Word and Spirit are also the apostles made strong (Augustine, Cassiodorus).
- **Moral.** Answer Christ's question as the Church does and not stop where his
  questioners stopped; confess with the mouth what the heart believes (Augustine,
  citing Rom 10:10); guard the one faith and the unity of the one baptism against
  division (Jerome on v. 3).
- **Anagogical.** The Son sits at the Father's right hand until every enemy is made
  his footstool (Ps 109:1; Aquinas, Chrysostom) **[A]**. The blessed nation of the
  Gradual's psalm is "most happy, 'when we shall see him as he is'" (Bellarmine on
  Ps 32:12, O'Sullivan's English, quoting 1 Jn 3:2, printed "Jn. 3") **[A]**, and
  its beatitude, begun now "in spe", is to
  cleave to God by knowing and loving, perfectly hereafter (Aquinas, *Super Ps.* 32
  n. 11) **[A]**. That this vision is of the Trinity whom the appointed Preface
  confesses, *et in persónis propríetas, et in esséntia únitas, et in maiestáte
  adorétur æquálitas* ("we adore a distinction in the Persons, an unity in the
  essence, and an equality in the Majesty", 1861 Cummiskey), is **[E]**. Neither
  author says it at these loci, and the Preface itself does not speak of vision.

### 2.5 Strongest difficulty and alternative

- **The Offertory's Christological weight depends on verses outside the 1962
  antiphon.** The appointed text ends at Dan 9:19; the seventy weeks and the
  Gabriel verses are not sung. The reading may use Daniel's answered prayer only as
  what the commentators (and, historically, the Offertory's verses) connect with it,
  and must say so.
- **The Alleluia divides the witnesses.** Augustine's Christological reading of Ps
  101 is contested by Cassiodorus; the reading must present both.
- **The Trinitarian distribution of Eph 4:6 rests on Jerome and Aquinas.** The
  Greek Fathers read "above all, through all, in all" differently. Chrysostom
  and Theodoret read the three phrases as three relations of the one God and
  Father: above all (for Theodoret, lordship), providence and indwelling (scope
  §§3.3, 4.5). Chrysostom supports this reading's confession of the Son by his
  argument that an attribute said of the Father implies no inferiority in the
  Son, not by a distribution. Theodoret follows Chrysostom's gloss and draws
  concord from vv. 5–6, not a doctrine of the Persons. Aquinas holds both
  lines: he reads the phrases first of the one God's dignity and then
  appropriates them. The reading may use the verse for the three Persons on
  Jerome's and Aquinas's authority, and Chrysostom's argument for the Son. It must
  not present the distribution as the only patristic reading, and it must not
  credit Chrysostom or Theodoret with it.
- **The Trinity Preface is not proper.** It is the ordinary Sunday preface; it can
  voice the confession but proves nothing about this formulary's design.
- **Lower-case *spíritu*.** In this Sunday's Gradual and Epistle the Missal prints
  *spíritus/spíritu* lower-case; the Trinitarian reading of Ps 32:6 is the Fathers',
  not a typographical signal of this formulary (the votive Mass's capital is
  evidence of the Missal's reading of that verse elsewhere, not here).

### 2.6 Agreements and differences within the reading

All the witnesses agree that the Son is equal to the Father and that the verse of
Ps 109 reveals it. At Eph 4:6 they follow two lines of exegesis (scope §4.5).
Jerome distributes the three phrases to Father, Son and Holy Spirit, and Aquinas
appropriates them to the three. Chrysostom reads them as the one God and Father
above all, providing for all and dwelling in all, and argues that what is said
of the Father cannot mark inferiority in the Son. Theodoret, who is not one of
this reading's witnesses, follows Chrysostom's gloss and draws concord from vv.
5–6; he does not argue about the Son. The witnesses also differ on the persona of Ps
101. Jerome's eschatological reading of "one body" carries an Origenian register
he reports rather than develops (scope §4.6).

## 3. `just-judge-merciful-hearer` — "You are just, O Lord": the humbled people prays with Daniel for mercy

### 3.1 Controlling claim

The Introit opens with the confession that God is just and his judgment right, and
asks to be dealt with "according to thy mercy". Augustine reads that confession as
the penitent's reason for weeping and the petition as "not according to my
justice"; Cassiodorus hears in it the words by which Daniel was saved among the
lions and the three youths in the furnace, and the verse to say in every danger.
The Offertory is Daniel's own prayer for the desolate sanctuary and for "this people
upon which thy name is invoked"; Jerome reads Daniel's prayer as confessing God's
justice and then provoking him to mercy "after the sentence of the judge", Daniel
joining himself to his sinful people; Augustine insists that he truly confessed his
own sins too. The Alleluia, from the fifth penitential psalm, is the cry of the poor
man that intercedes for the calamities of the whole world (Cassiodorus) or of Christ
in his afflicted members (Augustine). The orations ask escape from the devil's
contagion, release from past and future sins, the healing of vices; the Communion
renders the vows of those who stand round the altar to the God who takes away the
spirit of the proud. The Epistle's humility, meekness and patience are the manner of
that people, and the Gospel's lawyers fall silent where the humble confess. The Mass
can then be heard as the prayer of a humbled people that confesses God's justice and
receives his mercy. **[E]** for the whole-Mass form; the medieval commentators
Rupert and Durandus read the chants of this office (with Luke 14) as a lesson of
humility exalted, **[A]** for those chants only.

### 3.2 Principal witnesses and their reasoning

- **Augustine, *En. in Ps.* 118 s. 28.1 (v. 137) and s. 26.5 (v. 124).** [A] Having
  wept for his transgression, the psalmist gives the reason: God's justice and right
  judgment "omni est metuenda peccanti"; no one condemned can justly complain; "inde
  fletus rectus est poenitentis". "Fac cum servo tuo secundum misericordiam tuam: non
  utique secundum iustitiam meam"; the *iustificationes* are those "quibus Deus facit
  iustos, non ipsi se".
- **Hilary of Poitiers, *Tract. in Ps.* 118, *Ain* 10 (v. 124) and *Sade* 1
  (v. 137)** (CSEL 22, pp. 500–501, 515, read on the page images; scope §3.1).
  [A] The servant may say *fac* without presumption because he has done judgment
  and justice. He adds *secundum misericordiam tuam* "quia miseratione eius opus
  est, ut in hac seruitutis nostrae professione maneamus": mercy "uolentes adiuuet,
  incipientes confirmet, adeuntes recipiat". On v. 137, in sickness, persecution,
  loss and bereavement "semper utamur hac, qua nunc propheta usus est uoce dicens:
  iustus es, domine", for "si quid est, quod patimur, ex iudicio bonitatis suae
  accidit". Why it matters: Hilary makes the Introit's confession the word to hold
  under affliction, beside Cassiodorus's word for every danger, and makes its
  petition a plea for mercy to persevere as God's servant. His "ex nobis autem
  initium est, ut illa perficiat" differs from Augustine's account of who begins
  (scope §4.11). The reading uses Hilary for the confession under affliction and
  for perseverance in mercy, and Augustine, not Hilary, for mercy's priority.
- **Cassiodorus, on Ps 118:124, 137.** [A] Asking mercy he asked "infinite things";
  after sins washed in tears the saints praise God's judgments, who afflicts sinners
  to heal them; "Hoc enim dicto Daniel inter ferocissima leonum ora salvatus est, his
  verbis trium puerorum flamma superata est … Dicamus igitur et nos hunc versum in
  quibuslibet periculis, dicamus in gaudiis; quia talis confessio et ab aerumnis
  eripit, et in prosperitate custodit." A patristic link between the Introit's verse
  and the figure of Daniel. **It is Cassiodorus's link, not the Missal's**; that the
  Offertory is Daniel's prayer is a textual fact of the 1962 formulary, and joining
  the two is **[E]**.
- **Jerome, *In Dan.* on 9:2–23** (PL 25, 678–681). [A] Jeremiah's promise provokes
  Daniel to pray, lest security breed negligence; "Peccata populi, quia unus e populo
  est, enumerat ex persona sua"; "Tibi, Domine, justitia … Juste enim patimur, quod
  meremur … nunc quia Dominus non solum justus, sed et misericors est, dicit: Tibi,
  Domine, misericordia, ut post sententiam judicantis, eum provocet ad clementiam";
  on 9:17 "Imple opere, quod sermone pollicitus es"; on 9:20 Daniel confesses "non
  populi tantum peccata, sed et sua … sive humiliter, cum peccatum ipse non fecerit, se
  jungit populo peccatori, ut ex humilitate veniam consequatur"; on 9:21–23 "grandis
  orationis effectus", Daniel *vir desideriorum*.
- **Augustine, *De peccatorum meritis et remissione* II §13.** [A] Daniel "said so
  often in his prayer, 'We have sinned'" and distinguished "My sins … and the sins of
  my people"; the saints' confession is not made only on others' behalf. Against
  Jerome's second option (scope §4.2).
- **Augustine, *En. in Ps.* 101 s. 1, 3; Cassiodorus on Ps 101.** [A] "Si me extollo,
  longe fis; si me humilio, inclinas aurem tuam ad me" (Augustine). The fifth
  penitential psalm; "Tales sunt pauperes Christi, qui non solum pro suis malis,
  verum etiam pro totius mundi calamitatibus intercedere comprobantur" (Cassiodorus).
- **Augustine, *En. in Ps.* 75, 16–18; Cassiodorus on Ps 75:12.** [A] Vow what you
  can and render it, relying not on your strength but on him to whom you vow; the
  spirit of princes is the spirit of the proud, which God takes away, and the humble
  lose their own spirit and receive God's; "Rege terram, et eris rex terrae"
  (Augustine). The faithful "round about him" are those offering at the altar
  (Cassiodorus).
- **Augustine, *En. in Ps.* 109, 6.** [A] The Pharisees "maluerunt inflata
  taciturnitate disrumpi, quam humili confessione edoceri" — the silence of the
  Gospel's last verse as proud refusal of humble confession.
- **Jerome, *In Eph.* II on 4:2; Chrysostom, *In Eph.* hom. 9.** [A] Humility "non
  tam in sermone, quam in mente"; lowliness "the basis of all virtue", "as knowing
  that all is of grace, thou wilt humble thyself".
- **Rupert of Deutz, *De div. off.* XII.17; Durandus, *Rationale* VI.131.** [A] for
  the chants they share with the 1962 formulary: the Introit praises the judgment by
  which the proud are humbled and the humble exalted; Daniel, humbled in confession,
  was exalted by the angel's revelation; the Communion's God takes away the spirit of
  the proud. Their Gospel was Luke 14, and their whole-office reading belongs to that
  formulary (scope §2.4). Both carry the Introit to the end: Rupert hears its
  petition, "Fac cum servo tuo secundum misericordiam tuam", as asking "hanc
  videlicet misericordiam, ut dicas illi: *Amice, ascende superius*", the host's word
  at the heavenly banquet; Durandus hears *Iustus es … rectum iudicium tuum* as "hoc
  iudicium … quo Deus exaltat humiles, et superbos deprimit", in an office "de
  nuptiis aeternis" where the humble "exaltabimur in paradiso". Rupert on the
  Communion: God takes away the spirit of the proud "vel convertendo, vel
  deiciendo", saying to each "Da huic locum".
- **St Robert Bellarmine, on Ps 118:124, 137 and Ps 75** (O'Sullivan's English).
  [A] God is begged to deal "not according to his own merits, 'but according to thy
  mercy'"; "Thou art just, O Lord … you alone have the justice that excludes all
  manner of injustice", though in this psalm "judgment" "always means the law", and
  if taken for "God's allocation of rewards and punishments" is "also most just,
  though we may not see the justice of it" (scope §4.9). On Ps 75 he reads the
  psalm's judgment, in the verses before the Communion's, of "the day of general
  judgment", when God rises "to save all the meek of the earth", and the Communion's
  God as "terrible in his judgments … not only to ordinary men, but even to kings
  and princes"; those "round about him" are those "in the habit of approaching his
  altars and offering your gifts upon them".

### 3.4 The four senses

- **Literal.** In the first year of Darius, Daniel, understanding from Jeremiah that
  the seventy years of Jerusalem's desolation were ending, prays in sackcloth and
  ashes, confessing Israel's sins and God's justice, and begs God to shine his face
  on the desolate sanctuary for his own name's sake; the psalmist confesses God just
  and asks to be dealt with in mercy; the poor man pours out his prayer.
- **Allegorical.** The desolate sanctuary and the people called by God's name are the
  Church in her trials (Schuster applies this to Rome under the Lombards, as his own
  suggestion); the poor man is Christ praying in his members (Augustine) or every poor
  one of Christ interceding for the world (Cassiodorus); Daniel's answered prayer opens
  onto the time of Christ's coming — reached through Dan 9:20–27, which the Mass
  does not sing, and only as Rupert (XII.17, citing the old Offertory verse *Adhuc
  me loquente*), Durandus (VI.131) and Jerome (on 9:24, his computations left
  undecided) read the prayer with its answer (scope §§1.2, 4.7). **[A]** for each
  witness; its use for the 1962 antiphon, which ends at 9:19, **[E]**.
- **Moral.** Confess God's justice and your own sin, not only the sins of others; ask
  mercy, not reward; intercede for the whole people as one of it; vow what you can
  and render it, relying on God; humble the spirit that makes princes proud.
- **Anagogical.** The Introit asks the just God to deal with his servant
  *secúndum misericórdiam tuam*, which Augustine glosses "non utique secundum
  iustitiam meam" (s. 26.5) **[A]**. Rupert and Durandus, reading this chant, carry
  the petition to the end: it asks the mercy by which the host at the heavenly
  banquet says to the humble "Amice, ascende superius" (Rupert XII.17), and the
  *rectum iudícium* is the judgment by which God exalts the humble, "exaltabimur in
  paradiso" (Durandus VI.131) **[A]**, each within an office whose Gospel was Luke
  14, not the 1962 Gospel. The Postcommunion names the healing it asks eternal,
  *remédia nobis ætérna* **[T]**. The Communion's God *terríbili apud omnes reges
  terræ* is, for Bellarmine, "terrible in his judgments … even to kings and
  princes", in a psalm whose preceding verses (the Missal's vv. 9–10, not sung; his
  vv. 8–9) he reads of the general judgment that saves "all the meek of the earth"
  **[A]** (scope §3.9);
  Rupert has God say to the proud "Da huic locum" **[A]**. Augustine reads the same
  verse morally, of the king who rules the earth of his own flesh (En. 75, 18), so
  the anagogical line for the Communion rests on Bellarmine and Rupert, not on
  Augustine. Joining the three elements into one hope of mercy at the judgment is
  **[E]**. No unsung verse supplies this sense: neither Ps 118:124b nor Dan 9:24 is
  used for it.

### 3.5 Strongest difficulty and alternative

- **The Gospel is not penitential.** The reading holds it by Augustine's contrast of
  proud silence and humble confession, which is secondary. Readings 1 and 2 own the
  Gospel's primary sense.
- **The medieval humility reading was built on another Gospel.** Rupert and Durandus
  read these chants with Luke 14, where humility is the Gospel's theme; the 1962
  formulary keeps the chants and changes the Gospel. The reading may use them for the
  chants and must not present them as readers of Mt 22 on this Sunday. Durandus
  did read Mt 22:34–46, as a Gospel of the next Sunday in some churches, and read
  it of the Pharisees' unbelief, not of humility (scope §2.4).
- **What the Introit's *iudicium* names.** Bellarmine says that in Ps 118
  "judgment" "always means the law", and only secondarily God's allocation of
  rewards and punishments; Rupert and Durandus hear the judgment that exalts the
  humble at the end (scope §4.9). The reading's anagogical sense rests on the
  liturgical commentators for the Introit and must say so; Bellarmine supports its
  sense of mercy, not merits, and its Communion.
- **Daniel's own sin.** Jerome allows that Daniel, sinless, joined himself to the
  people in humility; Augustine denies it. The reading must carry the disagreement.
- **Ordinary Time.** The Introit, Offertory and Communion do travel together from the
  earliest graduals (scope §2.1), so a reading anchored in them rests on the oldest
  stable part of the formulary. The Gradual and Alleluia joined them later. The
  orations came by another road: the Collect and Secret from an unnumbered Sunday
  Mass of the Old Gelasian, all three together in the Frankish Gelasian's
  twenty-first Sunday and the Supplement's eighteenth, where O's margin cues them
  with another Sunday's chants (scope §§2.2–2.3, 2.6).

### 3.6 Agreements and differences within the reading

Augustine and Jerome agree that Daniel's is a true confession within the people and
that God is moved by mercy, not merit; they differ narrowly on Daniel's personal sin.
Augustine and Cassiodorus agree that Ps 101 is the prayer of the humble; they
differ on whether the speaker is Christ. Cassiodorus's cross-reference of the Introit
verse to Daniel is his own and is not found in Augustine. Hilary and Augustine agree
that the Introit's petition asks mercy and not reward. They differ on where the
beginning lies: "ex nobis autem initium est" (Hilary) against the justifications
"quibus Deus facit iustos, non ipsi se" (Augustine) (scope §4.11). The reading
keeps both and does not cite Hilary for Augustine's point.

## 4. Comparison

### 4.1 What the three share

- All three hear the Mass as the prayer of one people before the one God: the
  Collect's *pópulo tuo … te solum Deum*, the Gradual's *beáta gens*, the Offertory's
  *pópulum istum, super quem invocátum est nomen tuum*.
- All three take the Epistle's unity and the Gospel as central texts, and all three
  use Augustine as the witness who joins more than one element by his own reading
  (Ps 101 with Eph 4:13, 15; Ps 32 with the love that possesses God; Ps 109 with the
  Gospel).
- All three treat the orations modestly, as prayer rather than as exegetical
  anchors, and use Trent only as doctrinal illumination.

### 4.3 How they relate

The readings are complementary emphases, not rival identifications. Reading 1 and
reading 2 divide the Gospel's two halves, and Chrysostom's hom. 71, which joins them,
is the evidence that they belong together. Reading 3 rests on the chants the older
books kept with this Sunday and gives the Mass its tone of prayer; readings 1 and 2
give it its doctrine. No witness here opposes another's controlling claim; the real
disagreements are narrow and internal (scope §4).

### 4.4 Notes for the concise study's page-1 overview

Four overview rows that are true of all three readings without flattening them:

- **Literal:** the lawyer's question and Christ's answer on the two commandments and on
  David's son and Lord; Paul's plea for unity; Daniel's prayer for the sanctuary.
- **Allegorical:** Christ, David's Lord, head of one body with one voice; the one God
  confessed as Father, Word and Spirit.
- **Moral:** love God wholly and the neighbour for God's sake, bearing one another in
  humility; confess sin and ask mercy.
- **Anagogical:** one hope of the calling, the Father's one house; the enemies made the
  Lord's footstool; eternal remedies.

These are orientation only; each interpretation's own four senses are in §§1.4, 2.4
and 3.4.

### 4.5 Notes for the homily

A compatible combination is available: the Gospel's two questions (what we must love;
who the Christ is) read together as Chrysostom reads them, with the Epistle's unity as
the life that answers both and the Introit's "Just art thou … according to thy mercy"
as the prayer in which the congregation receives them. The homily must not attribute
that combination to a Father; it may name Chrysostom for the join of the two halves,
Augustine for "the whole heart" and for "David's son and David's Lord", and Jerome for
Daniel's praying as one of his people.
