\runninghead{The Whole Heart and the One Body}
\properlane{whole-heart-one-body}{The Whole Heart for the One God, and the One Body That Love Gathers}
\label{sec:whole-heart-one-body}

Heard from the lawyer's question, the Mass is a lesson in one love with two
objects. God is to be loved with the whole heart, and the neighbour within
that same love; the Epistle then shows what such love looks like when it is
lived by many people at once: humility, patience, mutual bearing and a unity
kept in the bond of peace. Around the Gospel and the Epistle the other texts
become the prayer of a people that asks to follow God alone, rejoices to
possess him and to be his possession, prays with the one voice of head and
members, pays the vows common to every Christian, and asks the sacrament to
heal the vices that break charity.

\subsection{Two commandments, one love}

The lawyer asks for ``the great commandment''. Jesus gives him the Shema's
command, ``Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with thy whole heart and with thy
whole soul and with thy whole mind'' (Deut 6:5), and then a second that he did
not ask for, ``Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself'' (Lev 19:18). The
second is ``like to this'', and on the two together ``dependeth the whole law
and the prophets''. The Fathers ask two questions of this answer: what the
threefold ``whole'' requires, and in what sense the second commandment is
``like'' the first.

St Augustine answers the first question in the opening book of \work{De
doctrina christiana}. Only what is eternal and unchangeable is to be enjoyed
for its own sake; everything else, even oneself and one's neighbour, is to be
loved for the sake of God (I.22.20--21). When the commandment says ``with all
thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind'', Augustine writes,
``He means that no part of our life is to be unoccupied, and to afford room, as
it were, for the wish to enjoy some other object.'' Whatever else presents
itself as worthy of love ``is to be borne into the same channel in which the
whole current of our affections flows''. Love of neighbour is therefore not a
second stream beside the first. The one who loves his neighbour aright urges
him to love God wholly, and so ``turns the whole current of his love both for
himself and his neighbor into the channel of the love of God, which suffers no
stream to be drawn off from itself by whose diversion its own volume would be
diminished''. Love so ordered has a measure and an order: ``every man is to be
loved as a man for God's sake; but God is to be loved for His own sake''
(I.27.28). Its reach is universal. When the lawyer of Luke's Gospel asks ``who
is my neighbour?'', the Lord answers with the man who showed mercy, so that
every man is our neighbour; the holy angels are neighbours too, for they show
us mercy; and ``even God Himself, our Lord, desired to be called our
neighbor'', since Christ points to himself in the figure of the one who
brought aid to the man left half dead on the road (I.30.31--33).

St Gregory the Great puts the same answer in the form of a contrast. One of
his homilies on the Gospels expounds the parable that opens the same chapter of
Matthew, the king's wedding feast (Mt 22:1--13).
The wedding garment, he says, is charity. As a garment is woven between two
beams, \latin{sicut in duobus lignis}, so charity is held in two precepts, the
love of God and the love of neighbour. In the love of neighbour a measure is
given, ``as thyself''; the love of God is bound by no measure, for the
commandment says not how much but out of how much, \latin{ex toto}: \latin{quia
ille veraciter Deum diligit, qui sibi de se nihil relinquit}, for he truly loves
God who leaves nothing of himself to himself (\work{Homiliae in
Evangelia} 38.10). The curtains of the tabernacle had to be woven with scarlet
twice dyed, and charity must be dyed twice as well: whoever so loves God that
he neglects his neighbour is scarlet but not twice dyed, and whoever so loves
his neighbour that he abandons the contemplation of God is not twice dyed
either. Gregory cites the commandment from Mark (12:30) and expounds the
parable, not this pericope; what he supplies is the sharpest statement of
``the whole heart'' beside Augustine's.

St John Chrysostom takes up the second question in his seventy-first homily
on Matthew: ``But wherefore `like unto this?' Because this makes the way for
that, and by it is again established.'' Both clauses speak of the second
commandment: the love of neighbour opens the road to the love of God, and the
love of God in its turn re-establishes the love of neighbour. The four texts he
sets beside the sentence run alternately in the two directions. ``Every one
that doeth evil hateth the light'' (Jn 3:20) and ``The love of money is the
root of all evils; which while some coveted after they have erred from the
faith'' (1~Tim 6:10) run from the failure of charity to the loss of God;
``The fool hath said in his heart, There is no God'', with what follows from
it, ``They are corrupt, and become abominable in their ways'' (Ps~13:1;
Heb.\ 14:1), and ``He that loveth me, will keep my commandment'' (Jn 14:15)
run from God back to the neighbour. His conclusion holds both ends at once: ``If therefore to love
God is to love one's neighbor \dots\ but to love one's neighbor worketh a
keeping of the commandments, with reason doth He say, `On these hang all the
law and the prophets.'\,'' Each commandment holds the other up.
He also notices that Jesus answers more than he was asked. By rehearsing the
second commandment unbidden, he shows the questioners ``whence the question had
arisen, that it was from hatred. `For charity envieth not.'\,'' They were led
to their test ``from having no charity, from pining with envy, from being
seized by jealousy''. The homily's moral close turns on the vice that makes
such envy: ``terrible is this passion and many-headed'', he says of
vainglory.

St Thomas Aquinas, lecturing on the same verses, gathers the earlier answers
and adds his own. The first commandment is \latin{maximum capacitate}, greatest in
what it holds, since the love of neighbour is contained in it. The second is
like the first because man is made to the likeness of God, so that in the
neighbour \latin{diligitur Deus in illo}, God himself is loved; and with
Augustine he counts both men and angels as neighbours (\work{Super Matthaeum}
c.~22, lect.~4).

The four witnesses agree that the two commandments cannot be separated and
that the love of neighbour is ordered to God. They differ in what they make the
link. For Augustine it is the one current of love that carries everything
toward the one good. For Gregory it is a measure set against measurelessness.
For Chrysostom it is mutual dependence in both directions: the second
commandment makes the way for the first, and the first establishes the second
again. For Aquinas it is the image of God in the neighbour. None of these
excludes the others.

\subsection{The unity that charity keeps}

Paul's appeal in the Epistle is the same love seen from the side of a
community. He writes as ``a prisoner in the Lord'' and asks the Ephesians to
walk worthily of their calling, ``With all humility and mildness, with
patience, supporting one another in charity''. They are to be ``Careful to
keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace'', because there is one
body and one Spirit, one hope, one Lord, one faith, one baptism, and one God
and Father of all.

Chrysostom reads the calling first. ``Ye were called as His body'', with
Christ as head. The way to walk worthily of that call is lowliness: ``This is
the basis of all virtue'', since the one who remembers ``that all is of grace''
will humble himself. Forbearance follows from the same logic of mercy: ``If
thou, he would say, art not forbearing to thy neighbor, how shall God be
forbearing to thee?'' The unity of the Spirit is a work of God: ``to this end
was the Spirit given, that He might unite those who are separated by race and
by different manners''. He compares it to fire, which works dry wood into one
blazing pile but cannot unite wet timber: ``with this bond let us bind
ourselves together with one another and unto God'' (\work{Homilies on
Ephesians} 9). The one body, he adds, is ``The faithful throughout the whole
world, both which are, and which have been, and which shall be'' (hom.~10).
Its members have one standing: ``God hath called you all on the same terms.
He hath bestowed nothing upon one more than upon another \dots\ whence is it
that ye are high-minded?'' ``For can it be,'' he asks, ``that thou art called by
the name of a greater God, another, of a lesser God?'' (hom.~11).

St Jerome reads the same verses with a teacher's eye for the weak. True
humility is \latin{non tam in sermone, quam in mente}, not so much in speech
as in the mind. ``Supporting one another in charity'' is asked of those
still in the beginnings of virtue, and it means bearing the sick brother, the
poor, the widow. The command to keep the unity of the Spirit ``in the bond of
peace'' tells most against heretics who, having broken the bond of peace,
suppose that they still hold the unity of the Spirit. The ``one hope'' of the
calling is the kingdom, the one house of the Father in which there are many
mansions (\work{Commentarii in epistulam ad Ephesios} II, on 4:1--4).

Theodoret of Cyrus explains why Paul begins with lowliness at all. The gifts
of the Spirit, miracles, tongues and prophecy, could puff up those who
received them. All of them, he says, have received one grace, and one spring
sends out the different streams; so they are to bear with one another
according to the law of love. When Paul sets ``one'' before every term of
verses 5--6, he is binding the Church into concord, and the conclusion
Theodoret draws is that the Ephesians, as brothers, owe one another
agreement (\work{Interpretatio epistolae ad Ephesios}, on 4:1--6). The image of
one spring and many streams makes the Epistle's unity a work of love among
people unequally gifted.

Aquinas names the end of the whole exhortation: that unity be kept among the
faithful. Charity is the joining of minds, \latin{conjunctio animorum}, and no
union holds unless it is bound; the true bond of charity is peace. Bearing one
another in charity means bearing one another's defects, and the one who falls
short is not to be corrected at once, out of season, but awaited with mercy,
\latin{misericorditer expectari, quia charitas omnia sustinet}. The one body
and one spirit are one by the unity of faith and charity; or the body is
turned toward the neighbour and the spirit toward God (\work{Super
Ephesios} c.~4, lect.~1).

Set beside the Gospel, the Epistle shows the second commandment at work
inside the Church. Chrysostom's mercy that forbears because God forbears,
Jerome's bearing of the weak, Theodoret's streams from one spring and
Aquinas's patient waiting describe one kind of love. It is ordered to God,
and for that very reason it holds the neighbour fast. Chrysostom's ``bind
ourselves together with one another and unto God'' names both directions in
one clause, and could stand as a gloss on ``like to this''.

\subsection{The Gospel's second half inside this reading}

A reading built on the first commandment must still account for the question
that follows it. Chrysostom joins the two halves himself. Jesus praises the
confession of one God, then asks whose son the Christ is, ``that they may not
be able to say, that He did miracles indeed, yet was an adversary to the law,
and a foe to God''. The question leads them on ``to confess Him also to be
God''. On Chrysostom's account, then, the one God who is to be loved wholly
is not loved apart from his Christ, and the question about David's son
belongs to the question about the great commandment. The second reading
develops what the question reveals; the first needs only to see that it
follows from the commandment.

\subsection{The prayers and chants of a people that loves}

Around the Gospel and the Epistle, every element of the formulary can be
heard as the prayer of the people these texts describe.

The Collect asks as a gift what the Gospel commands: \latin{te solum Deum pura
mente sectari}, to follow God alone with a pure mind. Augustine, in \work{De
moribus Ecclesiae catholicae}, joins following God to loving him: ``Following
after God is the desire of happiness; to reach God is happiness itself. We
follow after God by loving Him'', and the commandment to love God with all the
heart is the one ``which leads to happy life'' (I.11.18). The Collect's first
petition, to shun the devil's contagion, asks that nothing foreign occupy the
heart; its second asks for the undivided heart Augustine describes, in which
no part of life is left free for another object of enjoyment.

The Gradual blesses that heart's possession. \latin{Beata gens, cuius est
Dominus Deus eorum}: Augustine finds here the happiness all men desire, and
answers it in two words, \latin{Hoc ama, hoc posside}: love this, possess
this. God is his people's possession, and they are his inheritance,
\latin{alterutrum se possident}, each possessing the other
(\work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 32, II, s.~2, 15--18). Cassiodorus reads the
nation as the people gathered from all nations into the heavenly Jerusalem.
The Christian people is an inheritance acquired and not bequeathed, because its
Maker possesses it, \latin{quem praedicationibus sanctis et pretioso sanguine
conquisivit}, won by holy preaching and precious blood (\work{Expositio
psalmorum}, on Ps~32:12). Aquinas adds that the beatitude which all desire,
cleaving to God by knowledge and love, is possessed now \latin{inchoative et in
spe}, in beginning and in hope, and perfectly hereafter (\work{Super Psalmos}
32, n.~11).

The Alleluia gives that people one voice. For Augustine the poor man of Ps~101
is Christ, head and body: \latin{Christus et Ecclesia utrumque unus \dots\ unus
quidam vir perfectus}, and he confirms it with the chapter of Ephesians from
which this Sunday's Epistle is taken: ``Until we all meet into the unity of
faith \dots\ unto a perfect man'' (Eph 4:13). \latin{Vox ergo una, quia caro
una}: one voice, because one flesh. When the next verse asks God to incline his
ear ``in the day when I am in trouble'', Augustine hears the head speaking for
every member in every age, \latin{quicumque in meo corpore tribulantur, ego
tribulor}: whoever are troubled in his body, he is troubled
(\work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 101, s.~1, 2--3). The charity of the one body,
in which one member suffers and all suffer, becomes prayer.

The Offertory shows that charity interceding. Daniel prays for ``this people,
upon which thy name is invoked'', and Jerome notes that when he confesses the
sins of the people in the first person he does so \latin{quia unus e populo
est}, because he is one of the people (\work{Commentarii in Danielem}, on
9:5). The prayer from which the antiphon is drawn opens by naming God as the
one who keeps ``the covenant, and mercy to them that love thee, and keep thy
commandments'' (Dan 9:4). Jerome comments that God fulfils his promises in
those who keep his commandments. These words are not sung at this Mass, but
they stand at the head of the prayer the Offertory condenses.

The Communion calls the people to its vows. Augustine counts first the vows
that are common to all Christians, to believe, to hope for eternal life, to
live well, before the particular vows that some freely add; and he
reads ``all you that are round about him'' of those who hold the truth in
common and humbly (\work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 75, 16--17). Cassiodorus places
the same words at the altar, where the faithful bring their gifts.

The Introit has a smaller place here. Its plea to be dealt with ``according
to thy mercy'', not according to the servant's own justice, as Augustine
glosses it, reminds the people that its love of God begins as mercy received.

The Secret and the Postcommunion ask the sacrament to heal. The Council of
Trent calls the Eucharist the sign of the one body of which Christ is head,
whose members are bound to him by faith, hope and charity, so that there be no
schisms among them (sess.~XIII, cap.~2). The Secret's release from past and
future sins and the Postcommunion's cure of \latin{vitia nostra} are then the
healing of what breaks that bond. The 1962 Missal itself hears the
Postcommunion's clause this way when it borrows it for St John of God, where
the cure is sought through the fire of God's charity, at a Mass that reads
this Sunday's Gospel.

\subsection{The chants that speak of mercy}

The chants of this Sunday speak of justice, mercy, humility and vows more
than of love. The Introit, the Offertory and the Communion already stood
together at this Sunday in the earliest graduals, and the present Gospel came
to the day later; Guéranger dates the name ``the Sunday of the love of God''
from that change. Heard with the Gospel of the great commandment, those chants
become the voice of people who love and know their need: the plea for mercy,
the intercession for a whole people, the vows paid together. Their own theme,
justice confessed and mercy asked, is the subject of the third reading, and
the Gospel's second half, joined here through Chrysostom, is the subject of
the second. The centre of the Gospel's first half and the whole of the
Epistle's appeal belong to this one, and every other text of the Mass can be
heard as the prayer of the people those two passages describe.

\subsection{The four senses of this reading}

\begin{fourSenses}
\item[Literal.] In the Temple, Jesus answers a doctor of the law that the
greatest commandment is to love God with the whole heart, soul and mind, that
the second, to love one's neighbour as oneself, is like it, and that the law
and the prophets depend on both. Paul, a prisoner, urges the Ephesians to
walk worthily of their calling in humility, mildness and patience, bearing
one another in charity and keeping the unity of the Spirit, because they are
one body with one hope, one Lord, one faith, one baptism and one God and
Father of all.

\item[Allegorical.] The one body is Christ and the Church, one voice of head
and members, so that the poor man of the Alleluia is Christ praying in his
members (Augustine). The blessed nation of the Gradual is the people gathered
from all nations into the heavenly city and won by Christ's blood
(Augustine; Cassiodorus). The neighbour who showed mercy to the man left half
dead is Christ himself, who ``desired to be called our neighbor'' (Augustine).

\item[Moral.] Love God without reserving any part of life for another
enjoyment (Augustine) and without measure, leaving nothing of oneself to
oneself (Gregory); love every neighbour for God's sake, and see God loved in
him (Aquinas). Bear with the weak brother, the poor and the widow (Jerome),
wait with mercy on another's defects (Aquinas), forbear as God forbears
(Chrysostom), and refuse the vainglory that breeds envy. Hold the truth in
common and in humility, and pay the vows common to every Christian
(Augustine).

\item[Anagogical.] The one hope of the calling is the kingdom, the one house
of the Father with its many mansions (Jerome). There the blessed nation will
possess God and be possessed by him (Augustine), and the beatitude now held
in beginning and in hope will be held perfectly (Aquinas). The
Postcommunion's \latin{remedia aeterna} look to that same end, the healing
of every vice that divides love completed in glory.
\end{fourSenses}
