\section{``Thou Art Just, O Lord'': The Humbled People Prays with Daniel for Mercy}
\label{sec:just-judge-merciful-hearer}

Heard from its opening chant, the Mass is the prayer of a people that
confesses God's justice and asks his mercy. The Introit begins with a
confession, ``Thou art just, O Lord: and thy judgment is right'', and asks to
be dealt with ``according to thy mercy''. The Offertory is Daniel's own prayer
for the desolate sanctuary and for the people on whom God's name is called,
drawn from a chapter that confesses the same pair: ``To thee, O Lord,
justice'', ``But to thee, the Lord our God, mercy and forgiveness''. The
Alleluia is the cry of the poor man from the fifth penitential psalm. The
orations ask escape from the devil's contagion, release from past and future
sins, and the cure of vices; the Communion renders vows to the God who takes
away the spirit of the proud. The Epistle's humility, mildness and patience
describe the bearing of such a people, and the Gospel ends with questioners who
fall silent where a humble confession was asked of them.

\subsection{The confession of justice and the plea for mercy}

St Augustine reads verse 137 of Ps~118 as the words of a penitent. Having wept
over his transgressions, the psalmist gives the reason for his tears: God's
justice and right judgment \latin{omni est metuenda peccanti}, is to be feared
by every sinner, and no one condemned can justly complain of the just God;
\latin{inde fletus rectus est poenitentis}, hence the penitent's weeping is
right (\work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 118, s.~28, 1). The plea of verse 124
follows from the confession. \latin{Fac cum servo tuo secundum misericordiam
tuam: non utique secundum iustitiam meam}: deal with thy servant according to
thy mercy, not according to my justice. The \latin{iustificationes} the
psalmist then asks to be taught are those \latin{quibus Deus facit iustos, non
ipsi se}, by which God makes men just and they do not make themselves just
(s.~26, 5).

St Hilary of Poitiers, writing in the 360s, reads the same two verses in
the order in which the psalm gives them. The servant may say
\latin{fac} without presumption, because he has done judgment and justice; he
adds \latin{secundum misericordiam tuam} \latin{quia miseratione eius opus est,
ut in hac seruitutis nostrae professione maneamus}, because we need God's pity
if we are to remain in this profession of servitude. Human weakness can obtain
nothing by itself, and it is God's mercy \latin{ut uolentes adiuuet,
incipientes confirmet, adeuntes recipiat}, to help those who will, strengthen
those who begin, and receive those who come (\work{Tractatus super Psalmos}
118, \latin{Ain} 10). On verse 137 he turns the confession into a rule for
suffering. In persecution and sickness, in loss, grief and bereavement,
\latin{semper utamur hac, qua nunc propheta usus est uoce dicens: iustus es,
domine}, we are always to use the prophet's words, ``Thou art just, O Lord'',
for \latin{si quid est, quod patimur, ex iudicio bonitatis suae accidit},
whatever we suffer comes from the judgment of his goodness (\latin{Sade}~1).

Cassiodorus reads the two verses as the prayer of penitents.
Asking mercy, the psalmist \latin{infinita petiit}, asked for things without
end. After sins washed away in tears, the saints praise God's judgments, since
God afflicts sinners in order to heal them. And he adds a link of his own:
\latin{Hoc enim dicto Daniel inter ferocissima leonum ora salvatus est, his
verbis trium puerorum flamma superata est}. By this saying Daniel was saved
among the fiercest jaws of lions, and by these words the flame was overcome
for the three young men. \latin{Dicamus igitur et nos hunc versum in
quibuslibet periculis, dicamus in gaudiis}: let us too say this verse in
every danger, and say it in joy (\work{Expositio psalmorum}, on Ps~118:124,
137). The link with Daniel is Cassiodorus's own, and it joins the Introit's
verse to the prophet whose prayer the Offertory sings.

St Robert Bellarmine adds a precision about the Introit's key word. On verse
124 he writes that the psalmist begs God to deal with him ``not according to
his own merits, `but according to thy mercy'\,'', a mercy that ``especially
consists in teaching him `thy justifications'\,'', which God does ``when God
gives one a great amount of love''. On verse 137: ``you alone have the justice
that excludes all manner of injustice''. But he notes that ``Throughout this
Psalm the word `judgment' always means the law, though taking the word even to
mean God's allocation of rewards and punishments, it is then also most just,
though we may not see the justice of it'' (\work{Commentary on the Psalms},
O'Sullivan's abridged English).

Hilary and Augustine agree that the Introit asks for mercy and not for reward.
They place the beginning differently. Augustine's justifications are those by
which God makes men just; Hilary, writing before the controversy with the
Pelagians, says that mercy perfects what begins from us, \latin{ex nobis autem
initium est, ut illa perficiat}. Augustine's account is the one on which the
priority of grace rests; Hilary's gives the Introit its note of perseverance
in God's service and its word for the day of affliction.

\subsection{Daniel's prayer and Daniel's people}

The Offertory is the heart of this reading. Daniel prays in the first year of
Darius the Mede, when he has understood from Jeremias that the seventy years of
Jerusalem's desolation are almost accomplished. St Jerome notices what the
promise did to him: \latin{quae res Danielem non facit negligentem, sed magis
provocat ad rogandum}, it did not make Daniel careless but provoked him to
pray, so that what God had promised in his clemency might be fulfilled
through Daniel's prayers, lest negligence breed pride and pride give offence
(\work{Commentarii in Danielem}, on 9:2). The prayer is a confession. Jerome
marks its logic. First comes justice: \latin{Tibi, Domine, justitia}, to God
justice and to us confusion of face, \latin{Juste enim patimur, quod meremur},
we suffer justly what we deserve. Then comes mercy: \latin{nunc quia Dominus
non solum justus, sed et misericors est, dicit: Tibi, Domine, misericordia, ut
post sententiam judicantis, eum provocet ad clementiam}, because the Lord is
not only just but merciful, Daniel appeals, after the judge's sentence, to his
clemency (on 9:7--9). At the petition the Offertory sings he writes
\latin{Imple opere, quod sermone pollicitus es}: fulfil in deed what thou hast
promised in word (on 9:17).

The same chapter carries, just outside the antiphon, the reason the
petition dares to ask: ``for it is not for our justifications that we present
our prayers before thy face, but for the multitude of thy tender mercies''
(9:18). St Thomas Aquinas cites this verse, in the Vulgate's words, as the
ground of the confidence every prayer needs: \latin{debet esse cum fiducia
impetrandi, et hoc ex Dei misericordia}, prayer must be made with confidence of
obtaining, and that confidence comes from God's mercy (\work{Super
Philippenses} c.~4, on 4:6). The Introit's ``according to thy mercy'' and Daniel's ``not for our
justifications'' say the same thing in two books.

Daniel prays for ``this people''. Jerome observes that he confesses the
people's sins in the first person, \latin{quia unus e populo est}, because he
is one of the people (on 9:5). When Daniel later says that he was ``confessing
my sins, and the sins of my people'' (9:20), Jerome gives two readings: either
Daniel confessed his own sins as one of the people, or, \latin{humiliter, cum
peccatum ipse non fecerit, se jungit populo peccatori, ut ex humilitate veniam
consequatur}, humbly, though he had not sinned himself, he joined himself to a
sinful people, so as to obtain pardon by humility. St Augustine will not allow
the second. Against those who said that the saints confessed sins only on
others' behalf, he points to the very words of the verse. Daniel, he says,
``expresses himself in language so distinct and precise, as if he were full of
the distinction himself, and wanted above all things to commend it to our
notice: `My sins,' says he, `and the sins of my people.'\,''
(\work{De peccatorum meritis et remissione} II.13). Jerome and Augustine agree
that Daniel's is a true confession made from within the people and that mercy,
not merit, is its ground; they differ over whether the prophet had sins of his
own to confess.

The antiphon's words echo the Introit's psalm. \latin{Illumina faciem tuam
super sanctuarium tuum} has the verb and construction of Ps~118:135,
\latin{Faciem tuam illumina super servum tuum}, a verse the Introit does not
sing. Its ``servant'' (\latin{preces servi tui}) answers the Introit's
(\latin{fac cum servo tuo}). The prophet who in the Introit is Cassiodorus's
example of the confession ``Thou art just'' is in the Offertory the one who
prays.

\subsection{The poor man's cry and the vows of the humble}

The Alleluia comes from the fifth penitential psalm, headed ``The prayer of
the poor man''. Augustine hears in its second verse humility inclining God's
ear: \latin{si me extollo, longe fis; si me humilio, inclinas aurem tuam ad
me}, if I exalt myself, thou art far; if I humble myself, thou inclinest thine
ear to me (\work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 101, s.~1, 3). Cassiodorus, who
hears the psalm in the voice of Christ's poor rather than of Christ, draws its
scope to the whole world: \latin{Tales sunt pauperes Christi, qui non solum pro
suis malis, verum etiam pro totius mundi calamitatibus intercedere
comprobantur}, such are the poor of Christ, who are found interceding not only
for their own ills but for the calamities of the whole world
(\work{Expositio psalmorum}, on Ps~101). Bellarmine calls the verse the
Church's daily preparation for every other petition, and asks with it for the
gift of prayer itself: ``make me so pray that I may be worthy of being
heard''. What hinders prayer, he says, is ``want of faith, of confidence, of
humility, desire''.

The Communion completes the movement in vows. Augustine counsels
\latin{Quisque quod potest voveat, et reddat}, let each vow what he can and
pay it, relying not on his own strength but on the one to whom he vows. The
``spirit of princes'' that God takes away is the spirit of the proud,
\latin{Spiritus enim principum, superbi sunt spiritus}, and he reads the
kings of the earth morally: \latin{Rege terram, et eris rex terrae}, rule the
earth, that is, your flesh, and you will be a king of the earth
(\work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 75, 16--18). Cassiodorus sets the vows at the
altar, where the faithful bring their gifts, and adds that God is terrible
chiefly to the devout, to whom he is also sweet. Bellarmine reads the verses
just before the Communion's, ``When God arose in judgment, to save all the
meek of the earth'', of ``the day of general judgment'', when the meek will no
longer be ``harassed by their proud and cruel persecutors''. The God of the
Communion is then ``terrible'' in his judgments, ``not only to ordinary men,
but even to kings and princes''.

\subsection{The orations, the Epistle, the Gradual and the Gospel}

The three orations are the penitent's own prayers. The Collect asks to shun
\latin{diabolica contagia}; the Secret asks that the holy things we do may strip
us of past sins and of future ones; the Postcommunion asks that our vices be
cured and eternal remedies given. What they ask is what the Council of
Trent teaches of the Eucharist, an antidote that frees from daily faults and
preserves from mortal sins (sess.~XIII, cap.~2).

The Epistle describes the bearing of such a people: the lowliness that
Chrysostom calls ``the basis of all virtue'' because ``all is of grace'', and
that Jerome places in the mind rather than in speech. The Gradual's people is chosen by God and not by its own
merit. Augustine writes of the nation in verse 12, \latin{neque haec a seipsa,
sed Dei munere electa est}: it was chosen not of itself but by God's gift
(\work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 32, I, 12).

The Gospel enters this reading at its end, through Augustine's contrast
between the swollen silence of the Pharisees and the humble confession asked
of them (\work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 109, 6). The people that prays the
Introit confesses where the questioners were silent.

\subsection{The medieval office of humility, and its other Gospel}

Two medieval liturgical commentators read this Sunday's chants as one lesson
of humility exalted. Rupert of Deutz explains the whole office from its Gospel,
the parable of the guest who takes the lowest place (Luke 14). The Introit's
\latin{Iustus es, Domine} praises the Gospel's sentence, ``every one that
exalteth himself shall be humbled: and he that humbleth himself shall be
exalted'' (Luke 14:11); its petition \latin{Fac cum servo tuo secundum
misericordiam tuam} asks \latin{hanc videlicet misericordiam, ut dicas illi:
Amice, ascende superius}, the mercy by which the host says, ``Friend, go up
higher'' (Luke 14:10). Daniel, humbled in the confession ``We have sinned'', was exalted by
Gabriel's revelation; and God takes away the spirit of the proud \latin{vel
convertendo, vel dejiciendo}, by converting them or casting them down, saying
to each \latin{Da huic locum}, ``Give this man place'' (Luke 14:9) (\work{De divinis
officiis} XII.17). William Durandus reads the office as concerned \latin{de
nuptiis aeternis}, the eternal wedding. He hears the Introit's \latin{rectum
iudicium} as the judgment \latin{quo Deus exaltat humiles, et superbos
deprimit}, and promises that if we take the last place here we shall be
exalted in paradise (\work{Rationale} VI.131).

Both commentators read these chants with Luke~14, not with the 1962 Gospel.
Durandus knew Mt 22:34--46 as a Gospel of the following Sunday in some
churches, and read it of the Pharisees' unbelief rather than of humility.
Their witness therefore belongs to the chants they share with the 1962 Mass:
the Introit, the Gradual, the Offertory, the Communion and the Epistle. For
those chants they give the reading its end, the mercy that exalts the humble at
the heavenly banquet. Bellarmine's ``judgment'' as the law and the medieval
commentators' judgment that exalts the humble differ in what the Introit's
word names, not in what they believe of God.

\subsection{A Gospel that is not penitential}

The Gospel is not penitential. It enters here only through Augustine's
contrast of proud silence and humble confession, and its primary sense
belongs to the first and second readings. The medieval office of humility was
built on another Gospel and speaks only for the chants. Jerome and Augustine
differ over Daniel's own sins. The chants themselves are older at this Sunday
than any of these difficulties: the Introit, the Offertory and the Communion
already stand together here in the earliest graduals, and in them the tone of
the day is set by justice confessed, mercy asked and vows paid.

\subsection{The four senses of this reading}

\begin{fourSenses}
\item[Literal.] In the first year of Darius, Daniel, understanding from
Jeremias that the seventy years of Jerusalem's desolation were ending, prays in
fasting, sackcloth and ashes, confesses Israel's sins and God's justice, and
begs God to make his face shine on the desolate sanctuary and to hear for his
own name's sake. The psalmist confesses God just and his judgment right and
asks to be dealt with in mercy; the poor man pours out his prayer; the
faithful are summoned to pay their vows to the God who is terrible to kings.

\item[Allegorical.] The poor man who prays is Christ praying in his afflicted
members (Augustine) or every poor one of Christ interceding for the whole
world (Cassiodorus). The desolate sanctuary and the people called by God's
name are the Church in her trials; Schuster, offering it as his own
suggestion, hears the antiphon as the prayer of Rome under the Lombards. Daniel's answered prayer
opens onto the time of Christ's coming, as Rupert, Durandus and Jerome read it
with the prophecy that followed; the 1962 antiphon itself ends at the
petitions.

\item[Moral.] Confess God's justice and your own sin, and not only the sins of
others (Augustine). Ask mercy and not reward, and pray with confidence
because of God's mercies and not your justifications (Aquinas). Intercede for
the whole people as one of it (Jerome), and for the calamities of the world
(Cassiodorus). Say ``Thou art just, O Lord'' in every danger and in every
affliction (Cassiodorus, Hilary). Vow what you can and pay it, relying on God;
rule the earth of your own flesh, and let God take away the spirit of pride
(Augustine).

\item[Anagogical.] The Introit asks the just God for mercy and not for the
servant's own justice (Augustine). Reading its chant, Rupert hears that mercy
as the host's ``Friend, go up higher'' at the heavenly banquet, and Durandus
hears its judgment as the one by which the humble will be exalted in paradise.
Bellarmine reads the Communion's psalm of the general judgment, which saves
all the meek of the earth, and its God as terrible in judgment even to kings.
The Postcommunion calls the remedies it asks eternal. The judgment the
people confesses as just is the judgment at which it hopes to find mercy.
\end{fourSenses}
