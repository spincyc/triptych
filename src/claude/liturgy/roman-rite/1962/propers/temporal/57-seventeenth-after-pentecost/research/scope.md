## 1. The formulary and the facts the research rests on

The formulary is the ten-element Mass printed at *Dominica decima septima post
Pentecosten*, II classis, Missale Romanum 1962, printed pp. 398–400, marginal
nos. 1602–1611, collated in `propers/verified.md` on the facsimile's native
500 ppi page images. On 20 September 2026 it is the Mass of the day in the
universal 1962 calendar; the commemoration of Ss. Eustace and Companions is
omitted (RG 111 b, RGMR 434 b), the Gloria and Credo are said, and the Preface of
the Most Holy Trinity is appointed by the rubric on printed p. 293 for all
II-class Sundays outside the Christmas and Easter seasons. `research/context.md`
records the resolution; §8 below records what this stage re-checked.

| Key | Element | Appointed text | Kind |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| `collect` | Collect | *Da, quǽsumus, Dómine, pópulo tuo diabólica vitáre contágia* | composed oration |

(Jn 1:1–14). The Preface's proper clause was read on the page image this stage
(`propers/verified.md`, English table).

### 1.1 Lexical threads inside the appointed text

These are textual observations, true of the printed words. None is a claim about
why the texts stand together.

- **"One".** Eph 4:4–6 names *unum corpus … unus spíritus … una spe … unus
  Dóminus, una fides, unum baptísma. Unus Deus*. The Collect prays to follow *te
  solum Deum*. The Gospel's first commandment is addressed to *Dóminum Deum tuum*
  as one Lord (the Shema's *unus* is not printed in Matthew; Aquinas supplies it
  from Deut 6:4 at Super Mt 22, lect. 4). The appointed Trinity Preface says
  *unus es Deus, unus es Dóminus*.
- **"Lord".** *Dóminus* recurs in the Introit (*Dómine*), Gradual (twice), Alleluia
  (*Dómine*), Gospel (*Dóminum Deum tuum*; *Dixit Dóminus Dómino meo*; *vocat eum
  Dóminum* twice), Offertory (*Dómine*), Communion (*Dómino Deo vestro*), Epistle
  (*vinctus in Dómino*; *Unus Dóminus*).
- **Calling and name.** The Epistle's *vocatióne, qua vocáti estis … sicut vocáti
  estis in una spe vocatiónis*; the Offertory's *pópulum istum, super quem
  invocátum est nomen tuum*; the Gospel's *David … vocat eum Dóminum* (twice).
- **Justice and mercy.** Introit *Iustus es … rectum iudícium … secúndum
  misericórdiam tuam*; Offertory *propítius inténde*; the Offertory's source verse
  Dan 9:18 (not sung) *non in iustificatiónibus nostris … sed in miseratiónibus
  tuis multis*.
- **The people.** Collect *pópulo tuo*; Gradual *Beáta gens … pópulus, quem elégit
  Dóminus*; Offertory *pópulum istum*; Communion *omnes, qui in circúitu eius*.
- **Word and Spirit.** Gradual verse *Verbo Dómini … et spíritu oris eius*; Epistle
  *unitátem spíritus … unus spíritus*; Gospel *David in spíritu vocat eum Dóminum*.
  All four printed lower-case in this formulary (the votive Mass of the Holy Spirit
  prints the Gradual's *Spíritu* capitalized, `verified.md` §4).
- **Past and future.** Secret *et a prætéritis nos delíctis éxuant, et futúris*;
  Postcommunion *vítia nostra curéntur, et remédia nobis ætérna provéniant*.
- **Prayer heard.** Alleluia *Dómine, exáudi oratiónem meam*; Offertory *Exáudi,
  Dómine, preces servi tui*; Introit *fac cum servo tuo*. "Servant" joins Introit
  and Offertory (*servo tuo*, *servi tui*).

### 1.1a Threads in the unsung context of the appointed verses

Read in the complete chapters (Clementine and Douay). These words are not sung or
read at this Mass; they are recorded because the commentators read the appointed
verses in their light, and later prose must mark them as context:

- **Dan 9 confesses what the Introit sings.** Dan 9:7 *Tibi, Domine, justitia*;
  9:14 *Justus Dominus Deus noster in omnibus operibus suis*; 9:9 *Tibi autem Domino
  Deo nostro misericordia et propitiatio*. The Introit's *Iustus es, Dómine … secúndum
  misericórdiam tuam* and the Offertory's *propítius* stand beside these in the same
  prayer's context. Jerome's comment on 9:7–8 turns on exactly this pair (scope §3.7).
- **Dan 9:4** opens the prayer from which the Offertory's frame is taken: *Obsecro,
  Domine Deus magne et terribilis, custodiens pactum, et misericordiam diligentibus
  te, et custodientibus mandata tua* — God keeping mercy "to them that love thee, and
  keep thy commandments".
- **Ps 118:135**, in the Introit's psalm, *Faciem tuam illumina super servum tuum*,
  has the same verb and construction as the Offertory's *illúmina fáciem tuam super
  sanctuárium tuum*. Ps 118:132 asks mercy *secundum judicium diligentium nomen tuum*.
- **Mt 22:23–33**, immediately before the Gospel, ends with the God of Abraham,
  Isaac and Jacob, "not the God of the dead but of the living", and the crowds'
  wonder; Mt 23 follows with the scribes and Pharisees on Moses' seat.
- **Eph 2:14–22 and 3:14–21**, before the Epistle, make Jews and gentiles one new man
  and one body through the cross, with access "in one Spirit to the Father", and end
  with a doxology; Eph 4:7–16 continues with diverse gifts for building up the body
  "until we all meet into the unity of faith", "doing the truth in charity".

### 1.2 Threads that are not in the appointed text

Recorded so that later prose does not treat them as appointed:

- the Offertory verses naming Gabriel and Michael (sung with the antiphon in older
  books; not printed in 1962; §2.4);
- the seventy weeks of Dan 9:24–27 (outside the appointed verses; reached only
  through the commentators who read Daniel's prayer with its answer);
- the Ember days and Michaelmas that follow this Sunday in the calendar (separate
  formularies);
- Gerasa, the Sadducees' question and other surrounding Matthean episodes (Mt
  22:23–33 precedes; Mt 23 follows).

## 2. How the formulary was assembled: evidence that its parts travelled separately

The profile's caution for Ordinary Time governs this Sunday: a coherent theological
reading of the whole Mass does not prove that its semi-continuous readings, its
chants and its orations were composed as one unit. The evidence read this stage
shows that they were not transmitted as one unit.

### 2.2 The orations in the Supplement to the Gregorian Sacramentary

H. A. Wilson, *The Gregorian Sacramentary under Charles the Great* (HBS 49, 1915).
Read on page images of the registered tracked PDF
`…wilson-1915.ia-pdf-96fd93c7` (whole file re-hashed, SHA-256 `96fd93c7…`
matched; pages rendered from its 400 ppi scans), on the tracked page image of leaf
n231 (`…wilson-1915.ia-page-image-leaf-231-ad3bbaac`, re-hashed and matched), and
in the registered optical layer `…wilson-1915.ia-djvu-ocr-gregoriansacrame00cath`
(re-fetched 2026-09-18, SHA-256 `3613cce6…` matched). PDF page = printed page + 58
in the edited text, + 10 in the introduction.

- **The edition has two parts, and these orations are in the second.** Wilson,
  intro. p. xviii (PDF p. 28): each of R (Vat. Reg. lat. 337) and O (Ottob. 313)
  "consists of two distinct parts or books; and it is to the first part alone
  that the title and the claim to possess the authority of St. Gregory … really
  belong. The second part is a supplement to the Gregorian *Liber Sacramentorum*,
  arranged by a person not named", preceded in both manuscripts by a list of its
  contents and, in O, by the *praefatiuncula* `Hucusque` (printed p. 145, PDF
  p. 203). Intro. p. xliii (PDF p. 53): Wilson himself attributes "the formation
  of the numbered sections of the supplement" to Alcuin; that is his 1915
  judgment, and later scholarship on the compiler was not consulted. Intro.
  p. xliv (PDF p. 54): he prints "the text of the *Gregorianum* and of the numbered
  sections of the supplement from R", with the variants of C (Cambrai 164) and O
  in footnotes, and O's marginal antiphonary cues "in a separate series of
  footnotes". The Sundays after Pentecost belong to the Supplement: its table of
  chapters on printed p. 147 (PDF p. 205) lists `xviiii. Dominica prima post
  pentecosten` and the following Sundays through `xxxviii. Dominica .xx. post
  pentecosten`, where the page ends.
- **Locus.** The three orations stand at **Supplement sect. XXXVI,
  `DOMINICA .XVIII. POST PENTECOSTEN`, printed p. 175** (PDF p. 233, read at
  300 dpi), as Collect, `Super oblata`, `Ad complendum`; the table of chapters
  (p. 147) lists `xxxvi. Dominica .xviii. post pentecosten`. The neighbouring
  sections are XXXIIII (`DOMINICA .XVI.`) and XXXV (`.XVII.`) on p. 174, and
  XXXVII (`.XVIIII.`) and XXXVIII (`.XX.`) on p. 175. Each heading carries a
  superscript footnote letter before its number, which the optical layer reads as
  `a/x`, `bx` or, at XXXVIII, as a `C` (`CXXXVIII`); there is no section CXXXVI.
- **Readings (R, with O's variants).** Collect: `Da quaesumus domine populo tuo
  diabolica uitare contagia . et te solum dominum¹ pura mente sectari . per
  dominum.` Footnote 1 reads **`dominum` deum O**: O has `te solum deum`, the 1962
  wording, where R has `dominum`. (Wilson's footnotes set the lemma in bold and the
  variant after it, as footnote 2 on the same page, `cognoscimus` cognouimus O; an
  earlier version of this record misread footnote 1 as O `dominum deum`.) Secret:
  `Maiestatem tuam domine suppliciter deprecamur . ut hec sancta que gerimus . et a
  preteritis nos delictis exuant . et futuris . per.` Postcommunion:
  `Sanctificationibus tuis omnipotens deus . et uitia nostra curentur . et remedia
  nobis aeterna proueniant . per dominum.` No other variant is footnoted. Apart
  from R's `dominum`, the three are word for word the 1962 texts, with short
  conclusions and manuscript orthography.
- **The Supplement's count** stands one ahead of the 1962 count in this stretch:
  its XVI carries the 1962 Fifteenth's orations (*Ecclesiam tuam … miseratio
  continuata*), its XVII the 1962 Sixteenth's (*Tua nos … gratia*), its XVIII this
  Sunday's.
- **The Postcommunion is also in the first part, the *Gregorianum* proper.** It is
  the `Ad complendum` of `SABBATUM IN XII. LECTIONES AD SANCTUM PETRUM`, the Lenten
  Ember Saturday (printed p. 32, PDF p. 90), word for word with `aeterna`, and of
  `FERIA .III. AD SANCTAM PRISCAM` in Holy Week (p. 47, PDF p. 105), with
  `sempiterna` for `aeterna`; both read on the page images. The Collect and the
  Secret were not found anywhere else in the volume (tolerant search of the whole
  optical layer, §5.1). The Sunday that follows the Ember Saturday is marked
  `DIE DOMINICO UACAT` on p. 32; its own orations follow there, and its
  `Ad complendum` is `Supplices te rogamus`, not this prayer (page image, re-read
  on the second reentry). The 1962 Missal keeps the Postcommunion at the same two
  Lenten places (§2.5).
- **The antiphonary cues do not pair these orations with these chants.** On the
  page images of pp. 174 and 175 (the tracked leaf n231 and PDF p. 232 for p. 174;
  PDF p. 233 for p. 175, the same 2662 × 4127 scan as the unregistered IA leaf
  n232 an earlier pass read), each footnote letter restarts at `a` on each page
  and is set as a superscript before the section number of the heading it
  annotates:
  - p. 174, `ᵃXXXIIII. DOMINICA .XVI.` (the 1962 Fifteenth's orations) — footnote
    a: `Ant. Iustus es domine. Resp. Unam petii a domino. Off. Oraui deum meum.
    Com. Uouete et reddite. [All. Deus iudex.]` — **this Sunday's chant set**,
    with the Gradual and Alleluia the Sextuplex gives at K S and S;
  - p. 174, `ᵇXXXV. DOMINICA .XVII.` (the 1962 Sixteenth's orations) — footnote b:
    `Ant. Da pacem domine…` (the 1962 Eighteenth's chants);
  - p. 175, `ᵃXXXVI. DOMINICA .XVIII.` (**this Sunday's orations**) — footnote a:

### 2.3 The Gelasian books and the Veronense

**The Old Gelasian.** H. A. Wilson, *The Gelasian Sacramentary* (Oxford, 1894),
edited from Vat. Reg. lat. 316 (Wilson's V.), which Delisle dated on
palaeographical grounds to the seventh or early eighth century and which was
written for a church in the Frankish kingdom (intro. p. xxv; optical layer only).
Wilson's margins and apparatus give the readings of the eighth-century
"Gelasian" sacramentaries of Rheinau (R.) and S. Gallen (S.; S¹ and S² are its
first and second hands), of Gerbert's printed recension (Gerb., *Monumenta
veteris liturgiae Alemannicae*, 1777), and of the Gregorian texts printed by
Pamelius (Pam.) and Ménard (Men.) (preface pp. v–vii; optical layer only). Both
registered optical layers were re-fetched on 2026-09-18 and matched
(`…ia-djvu-ocr-gelasiansacrame00wilsgoog`, `039123ca…`;
`…ia-djvu-ocr-libersacramentorum`, `25586b16…`). Every reading below was then
checked on the page images of Internet Archive item
LiberSacramentorumRomanaeEcclesiae (leaves n323, n324, n451; unregistered; §6.2),
except where "optical layer only" is said.

- **Liber III**, *Orationes et preces cum canone per dominicis diebus* (p. 224,
  optical layer only), is a series of Sunday Masses headed `Item alia Missa`,
  without Sunday numbers. **Sect. XIII, p. 231:**
  - Collect: `Da, quaesumus, Domine, populo tuo diabolica vitare contagia, et te
    solum Dominum² puro corde³ sectari. Per.` Margin: `R. S. Gerb. 185. Pam. 412.
    Men. 182.`
  - A second Collect: `Custodi nos, omnipotens Deus, ut tua dextera gubernante
    nec nostra nobis praevaleant nec aliena peccata. Per.` Margin: `R. S. Gerb.
    185.`
  - Secreta: `Maiestatem tuam, Domine, suppliciter deprecamur, ut haec sancta quae
    gerimus et⁴ praeteritis nos delictis exuant et futuris. Per.` Margin as the
    Collect.
  - Postcommunion, p. 232: `Praesta, quaesumus, omnipotens Deus, ut divino munere
    satiati et sacris mysteriis innovemur et moribus. Per.` Margin: `R.S. (alibi.)
    Gerb. 90. I. xlv supra`. **A different prayer from the 1962 Postcommunion.**
  - Apparatus, p. 232: "¹ The two Collects and Secret of this *Missa* are assigned
    by R. S. Gerb. to the twenty-first Sunday after Pentecost, for which Men. also
    gives the first Collect and the Secret: see the *Missa* for the eighteenth
    Sunday in Pam." "² *Deum* S² Gerb.; V. R. S¹ Pam. Men. as text." "³ *pura
    mente* Pam." "⁴ Pam. inserts *a*, which R. has in place of *et*."

  So V. reads `te solum Dominum puro corde`. `Deum` is the reading of S.'s second
  hand and of Gerbert's recension; V., R., S.'s first hand, Pamelius and Ménard
  read `Dominum`. `pura mente` is recorded only for Pamelius's Gregorian. V.'s
  Secret reads `et praeteritis`, without the `a` that Pamelius inserts and the
  1962 book prints.
- **The Frankish Gelasian.** Wilson's Appendix, "Showing the Contents and
  Arrangement of the Gelasian Sacramentaries of Rheinau (R.) and S. Gallen (S.)"
  (from p. 317, optical layer only: it follows S.'s order; square brackets mark
  matter not in R.; `*` marks a divergence between S.'s original reading and
  Gerbert's later recension). **p. 359**, `Hebd. xxi post Pentecosten.` (Gerb.
  p. 185): `Da q. Dne. populo tuo diabolica vitare contagia` / `Custodi nos o. D.
  ut tua dextera` / `Maiestatem tuam Dne. suppliciter` / `*VD. et suppliciter
  exorare ut sic bonis tuis` / `Sanctificationibus tuis o. D. et vitia`. The entry
  is unbracketed, so it stands in both R. and S. **Here the three 1962 orations
  stand together for the first time in the witnesses read**, with the second
  Collect and a proper preface. That preface (V. `VD. Nos tibi semper et ubique
  gratias agere, et suppliciter exorare sic nos bonis tuis instrui sempiternis`,
  where S.'s second hand, Gerbert and Pamelius insert `ut`) stands in V. at
  Liber I, sect. LVII, the Sunday after the Easter octave (p. 102), and Wilson's
  note 7 there says that R. S. Gerb. Pam. Men. assign it to the twenty-first
  Sunday after Pentecost (optical layer only; `…libersacramentorum` lines
  17147–17169). The preceding `Hebd. xx post Pentecosten` (p. 358,
  optical layer only) carries the 1962 Sixteenth's orations (*Tua nos … gratia*,
  *Munda nos*, *Purifica*); between the two stand Cosmas and Damian, the
  dedication of St Michael's basilica (iii Kal. Oct.), and Mark with Marcellus and
  Apuleius (Non. Oct.); Callistus (prid. Id. Oct.) and `Hebd. xxii` follow.
- **Wilson's index** (optical layers only; `…libersacramentorum` line 42855 reads
  the first entry's page as 231, `…wilsgoog` line 31890 as 331, and the second
  entry's `231` is damaged to `a3i`): `Da q. Dne. populo tuo diabolica
  vitare, 231, 359`; `Maiestatem tuam Dne. suppliciter deprecamur ut haec, 231,
  359`; `Sanctificationibus tuis o. D., 359`. Within this edition the 1962
  Postcommunion appears only in the appendix of R. and S., not in V.
- **Optical-layer loci.** `…wilsgoog`: lines 21519–21535 (p. 231), 21538–21551
  (p. 232, with the apparatus), 30117–30123 (p. 359). `…libersacramentorum`:
  lines 29539–29556 and 29652–29661 (pp. 231–232), 40972–40978 (p. 359). An earlier version of this record reported that
  literal searches of the first layer for `contagia`, `pura mente`,
  `Sanctificationibus` and `suppliciter deprecamur ut haec` returned none of the
  three orations. That report was false (`contagia` stands on line 21522,

### 2.4 The medieval commentators on this Sunday's office

- **Rupert of Deutz, *De divinis officiis* XII.17** (PL 170, cols. 325–326; read
  in the registered optical layer `…latin-migne-pl-170.ia-djvu-ocr-2e2ca850`,
  not on page images). Rupert's *Dominica decima septima* has the **Gospel Luke
  14:1–11** (the wedding feast; the 1962 Missal reads it on the Sixteenth Sunday)
  with this Sunday's Epistle (Eph 4:1), Introit *Iustus es*, Gradual *Beata gens*,
  Offertory *Oravi Deum meum* with its verse *Adhuc me loquente*, and Communion
  *Vovete*. He reads the whole office as teaching humility, and the parable as
  showing "hoc in suo caelesti convivio futurum esse" which it concludes: *omnis
  qui se exaltat humiliabitur*. Paul's *digne ambuletis vocatione* is the
  invitation to the wedding. The Introit *Iustus es, Domine* "manifeste illam
  collaudat sententiam" of the Gospel's last verse, "Simulque oratio est pro illo,
  qui se humiliat: *Fac cum servo tuo secundum misericordiam tuam*, hanc videlicet
  misericordiam, ut dicas illi: *Amice, ascende superius*". *Beata gens* is the
  gentile people, humbled under the sign of the cross, who "ascended higher"; in
  the Offertory Daniel, humbled in confession (*Peccavimus*), was exalted by
  Gabriel's revelation of the seventy weeks and foresaw the times of Christ's
  coming; the Communion bids the humble take the lowest place, and *ei qui aufert
  spiritum principum* is God taking away the spirit "superborum, quos utique vel
  convertendo, vel deiciendo factis ipsis dicit cuique illorum: *Da huic locum*".
  His *Dominica decima octava* (XII.18) has the Gospel of Mt 23 (Moses' seat).
  **Mt 22:34–46 is not this Sunday's Gospel in Rupert's arrangement**, and the
  tolerant search of the PL 170 layer (§5.1) finds no treatment of it anywhere in
  *De divinis officiis*. Role: reception (liturgical).
- **William Durandus, *Rationale divinorum officiorum* VI.131** (1568 composite
  printing, registered optical layer `…1568-durandus-and-beleth-composite.ia-djvu-ocr-2bd5ccb5`,
  "Cap. 131", `Dominica decimaseptima post Pentecost.`; not read on page images).
  Durandus: "agitur de nuptiis aeternis"; the Gospel read "in quibusdam ecclesiis"

  ponimus hic, ut simus humiles, exaltabimur in paradiso"; hence the Introit

  iudicio concordat ecclesia seu consentit, quo Deus exaltat humiles, et superbos
  deprimit"; the Epistle invites to humility; the Gradual's *Beata gens* and *Verbo
  Domini caeli, id est humiles, firmati* (a moralizing gloss); an Alleluia *Qui
  timent Deum* "in quibusdam ecclesiis"; the Offertory is Daniel's humble prayer,
  for which he merited the archangel who "certified him of the incarnation of
  Christ", and the angel is fitting "quoniam hoc officium circa dedicationem beati
  Michaelis cantatur"; the Communion *Vovete* is read of the humble, and *aufert
  spiritum principum* as the pride of the proud, "ecce, quia superbos humiliat".

  Sunday carries this Sunday's 1962 Gospel.** In the chapter *De dominica
  decimaoctava post Pentec.* (the layer damages its number to `Cap. 13 f.`; it

  Epistle 1 Cor 1:4 and Offertory *Sanctificavit Moyses*, the Gospel "in quibusdam
  ecclesiis" is *Super cathedram Mosi* (Mt 23) and in others "Accesserunt ad
  Iesum, quod est Matth. xxii … Quid uobis uidetur de Christo, cuius filius sit? …
  Dixit dominus domino meo"; he reads its second half as Christ reproving the
  Pharisees "de infidelitate, quia non credebant ipsum esse Deum". Found by the
  tolerant search (§5.1); read in the optical layer only. Where Durandus's
  churches read Mt 22:34–46, then, they read it one Sunday later than the 1962
  book, after the September Ember days. Role: reception (liturgical).
- **Prosper Guéranger, *The Liturgical Year*, vol. XI (Duffy's English, 1900),
  pp. 372–387** (registered tracked PDF `…english-duffy-1900-volume-11.ia-pdf-95ba98e2`,
  SHA-256 matched; its text layer read). Guéranger states that the present Gospel
  gave the day "the name of the Sunday of the love of God" "from the time when the
  Gospel of the cure of the dropsy and of the invitation to the wedding-feast was
  anticipated by eight days", and that "from the very first" the Gospel of this
  Sunday was Mt 22:23–33 (the Sadducees on the resurrection). He prints the two

  manuscripts, by the blessed Thomasi", where the next Sunday is "post Sancti
  Angeli". He reads the Epistle with the former Gospel (the wedding invitation).
  He cites Chrysostom, *In Eph.* hom. 9, and Augustine repeatedly. Guéranger is not
  a canonized author; his statements are liturgical-historical testimony, and the
  Tommasi antiphonary he cites was not read. Role: reception (liturgical) and
  context.
- **Bl. Ildefonso Schuster, *The Sacramentary (Liber Sacramentorum)*, vol. III
  (Burns Oates & Washbourne, 1927), pp. 145–150** (registered tracked OCR
  `…ia-volume-3-ocr-4d8c8988`). Schuster: the Sunday is "Secunda post natale Sancti
  Cypriani", with a station in the Würzburg list "ad Sanctos Cosmae et Damiano ante
  natale eorum" (movable, fixed for the Sunday before 27 September); it precedes the
  autumn fast of the seventh month, which St Leo announced in homilies that do not
  refer to the Sunday Gospel; the Introit's Ps 118 was a Roman processional psalm;
  the Gradual is "common to that used on the Wednesday after the Fourth Sunday in
  Lent—the Wednesday of the 'Great Scrutinies'", and "whether by coincidence or
  design, it carries on the idea which the Epistle began"; the Offertory reflects,
  he suggests, Rome under the Lombards (his conjecture, stated as such); the Secret
  expresses the Eucharist as antidote (he refers to Trent); the Communion's
  *terrible* God is terrible to the proud and sweet to the poor. Role: reception
  (liturgical).

### 2.6 Evidentiary consequence

The witnesses read show the formulary's parts travelling separately.

- **The chants.** The Introit, Offertory and Communion stand together at this
  Sunday in all four graduals of the Sextuplex view that carry it (§2.1), and O's
  margin in the Supplement cues the same set, with the Gradual *Unam petii* and
  the Alleluia *Deus iudex*, two Sundays before this Sunday's orations (§2.2). The
  Gradual *Beata gens* and the Alleluia *Domine exaudi* were assigned here later;
  the same Gradual serves the Lenten scrutiny Wednesday, the September Ember
  Wednesday and the votive Mass of the Holy Spirit (§2.5).
- **The orations.** The Collect and the Secret stand first in the Old Gelasian
  Sunday series, Liber III, sect. XIII (Wilson 1894, p. 231), an unnumbered
  Sunday Mass with a second Collect and a different Postcommunion (§2.3). The
  eighth-century Frankish Gelasians of Rheinau and S. Gallen put Collect, second
  Collect, Secret, a proper preface and the Postcommunion *Sanctificationibus
  tuis* together at *Hebd. XXI post Pentecosten* (p. 359). The Supplement to the
  Gregorian Sacramentary gives the three alone at sect. XXXVI, *Dominica XVIII
  post Pentecosten* (Wilson 1915, p. 175), and its O cues them with another
  Sunday's chants (§2.2). The Postcommunion was already a Lenten prayer of the
  *Gregorianum* (pp. 32, 47) and remains one in the 1962 book (§2.5). The 1962
  Missal has the three at the Seventeenth Sunday. The numbers (none; XXI; XVIII;
  XVII) follow different countings of the Sundays after Pentecost. This record
  does not map each count onto the others beyond one check: the orations of the
  1962 Sixteenth Sunday stand one Sunday earlier in both the Frankish Gelasian
  (*Hebd. XX*) and the Supplement (*Dominica XVII*).
- **The Collect's wording** moved as it travelled: V. `te solum Dominum puro
  corde`; `Deum` in S.'s second hand and Gerbert's recension; `pura mente` in
  Pamelius's Gregorian; `dominum … pura mente` in the Supplement's R and `deum …
  pura mente` in its O; `Deum pura mente` in 1962 (§§2.2–2.3). The 1962 wording is
  the later one.
- **The Epistle** stands by the Pauline course of the Sundays after Pentecost
  (§2.5): Ephesians, begun the previous Sunday at Eph 3:13, continued here, and
  resumed after an interruption on three later Sundays. Rupert and Durandus have
  it on this Sunday with Luke 14, and Guéranger reads it with that former Gospel.
  Its place is the course's, not a choice made to match the Gospel.
- **The Gospel** is the least stable element. On the testimony of Rupert,
  Durandus and Guéranger this Sunday's Gospel was Luke 14 in their arrangements,
  and Mt 22:23–33 earlier still (Guéranger); where Durandus's churches read Mt
  22:34–46 they read it on the next Sunday (§2.4). The 1962 book reads the
  neighbouring Matthean pericopes out of order on later Sundays (§2.5).

**Every whole-formulary reading of this Mass is therefore an editorial
theological synthesis of a historically composite formulary.** The medieval
commentators' whole-office readings are checked, at their loci, as their readings
of the formularies they had, which differ from the 1962 formulary in the Gospel.
Their readings of the chants they share with 1962 are their readings of those
chants within a Luke 14 office.

| Verbal echoes | These are echoes noted by this audit, not attested sources of the prayer, and the prayer's history bears on them. **Dominum/Deum.** The oldest reading, `te solum Dominum`, echoes Christ's answer to the tempter, *Dominum Deum tuum adorabis, et illi soli servies* (Mt 4:10, Deut 6:13), and the Gospel's *Díliges Dóminum Deum tuum* (Mt 22:37) through *Dominum*; the later `Deum` (S.'s second hand, Gerbert, the Supplement's O, 1962) keeps the *solum* and loses the *Dominum*. **puro corde/pura mente.** `puro corde` (V.) echoes *Beati mundo corde* (Mt 5:8) and *de corde puro* (1 Tim 1:5) and shares *corde* with Mt 22:37 *ex toto corde tuo*; `pura mente` (Pamelius's Gregorian, the Supplement, 1962) shares *mente* with Mt 22:37 *in tota mente tua*. In its oldest witness the Collect stands in a series of Sunday Masses that carry no Sunday number and no Gospel (§2.3), so the echoes belong to the appointed words and are no evidence of a compiler's design. The 1962 book's reuse of the clause in the Postcommunion of St Jane Frances, *fácias terréna despícere, et te solum Deum pura mente sectári* (§2.5), shows it heard as a petition for undivided love of God. |
| Doctrinal illumination | **Augustine, *De moribus Ecclesiae catholicae* I.11.18**: "Following after God is the desire of happiness; to reach God is happiness itself. We follow after God by loving Him … The greatest commandment, therefore, which leads to happy life, and the first, is this: 'You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart…'" (NPNF1 4, Stothert's English, NewAdvent delivery, §6, unregistered; the Latin was not read this stage, so no Latin wording of Augustine's is claimed). | Liturgical commentators | Guéranger: the devil's jealousy "to rob God of the possession of our souls"; the Collect asks to avoid "the foul contact of the hideous serpent". Schuster applies *diabolical defilements* to the spiritualism of his day (a modern application, not exegesis). |
| Negative results | No patristic or saintly exposition of this prayer itself was found; none was expected. The Collect is used by the readings only through doctrinal illumination and verbal echo. |

## 4. Material disagreements preserved

### 4.1 Who prays Ps 101? Augustine and Cassiodorus

Augustine hears Christ, head and body, as one voice (*En. in Ps. 101* s. 1, 2);
Cassiodorus explicitly declines the Christological persona for this psalm, because
*anxius* and the ashes and tears cannot be fitted to the incarnate Lord, and hears
the prophet speaking as any poor penitent of Christ who intercedes for the whole
world. Cassiodorus's "aliqui" is not named; that Augustine is among them is an
inference and must not be asserted. The two agree that the prayer is the Church's
prayer of the afflicted and that it rises from humility. **Keep both.**

### 4.2 Did Daniel confess his own sins? Jerome and Augustine

Jerome, on Dan 9:20, allows two readings: Daniel recounts his own sins as one of
the people, or, "humiliter, cum peccatum ipse non fecerit, se jungit populo
peccatori". Augustine, *De pecc. mer.* II §13, excludes the second and insists that
Daniel distinguished "my sins" from "the sins of my people". The disagreement is
real and narrow; both agree that the prayer is a true confession within the
people and that mercy, not merit, is its ground (Dan 9:18).

### 4.3 How is "the second like the first"? Chrysostom, Augustine, Aquinas

Chrysostom: the second "makes the way for" the first and "by it is again
established" — mutual dependence, and hatred of neighbour is the root of the
question's malice. Augustine: the neighbour is loved for God's sake; the whole
current of love flows into the love of God (use and enjoyment). Aquinas: the
neighbour is loved as God's image, "diligitur Deus in illo", and to the same end as
oneself. Complementary, not contradictory; they differ in what they make the
mechanism.

### 4.4 Why was the question asked? Chrysostom and Jerome

Chrysostom: to see whether Jesus would amend the first commandment in order to
show himself God. Jerome: because every commandment is great, so any answer could
be attacked. Aquinas reports both lines and adds Augustine's reconciliation of
Matthew's "tempting" with Mark's scribe "not far from the kingdom". Complementary.

### 4.5 How is Eph 4:6 Trinitarian? Two lines: Jerome and Aquinas; Chrysostom and Theodoret

The witnesses read *super omnes / per omnia / in omnibus* in two ways.

**Given to the three Persons: Jerome and Aquinas.** Jerome distributes the three
phrases: "Super omnes enim est Deus Pater, quia auctor est omnium. Per omnes
Filius, quia cuncta transcurrit, vaditque per omnia. In omnibus Spiritus sanctus,
quia nihil absque eo est" (*In Eph.* II, PL 26). Aquinas appropriates them
(*Super Eph.* c. 4 lect. 2, Dessain pp. 316–317, page images): "Sed primum
appropriatur patri qui est fontale principium divinitatis … Secundum filio …
Tertium vero Spiritui Sancto". Before he appropriates, he reads the three
phrases as the one God's dignity "ex tribus": the height of his divinity, the
amplitude of his power, and the largesse of his grace, "in omnibus nobis, sc.
per gratiam". So Aquinas holds both lines, and the appropriation is his second
step.

**Three relations of the one God and Father: Chrysostom and Theodoret.**
Chrysostom (*In Eph.* hom. 11) glosses the three phrases of the Father: "'Who is
over all,' that is, the Lord and above all; and 'through all,' that is,
providing for, ordering all; and 'in you all,' that is, who dwelleth in you
all" (NPNF1 13). In Migne's Greek (PG 62, 80, page image) the first gloss is ὁ
ἐπάνω πάντων, "who is above all", without "the Lord"; the others are προνοῶν,
διοικῶν and ὁ ἐν πᾶσιν οἰκῶν. Theodoret gives the same three relations as
nouns: "Τὸ μέντοι, ἐπὶ πάντας τὴν δεσποτείαν σημαίνει, τὸ δὲ, διὰ πάντων, τὴν
πρόνοιαν, τὸ δέ γε, ἐν πᾶσι, τὴν ἐνοίκησιν" (PG 82, 533), that is, lordship,
providence and indwelling. The second and third glosses share their roots
(προνοῶν and πρόνοια; οἰκῶν and ἐνοίκησις). For the first, Theodoret's
"lordship" matches the NPNF English "the Lord", while Migne's Chrysostom has only
"above all". Theodoret, who wrote after Chrysostom, follows his gloss. This
record does not investigate whether he took it from him. Neither Father gives
the three phrases to the three Persons.

**What each adds.** Chrysostom adds an argument about the Son: "Now this they own
to be an attribute of the Son; so that were it an argument of inferiority, it
never would have been said of the Father" (Καίτοι τοῦ Υἱοῦ, τοῦτο εἶναί φησιν·
ὥστε εἰ ἐλαττώσεως ἦν, οὐκ ἂν περὶ τοῦ Πατρὸς ἐῤῥήθη). His "this" (τοῦτο) does
not name the phrase. The argument fits "through all", because "through" (διά,
Latin *per*) is the preposition Scripture uses of the Son's part in creation
(*Omnia per ipsum facta sunt*, Jn 1:3; *per quem omnia*, 1 Cor 8:6; tracked
Clementine text). That identification is this record's inference, and
Chrysostom does not state it. Aquinas, for his part, cites Jn 1:3 under *per
omnia* before he appropriates the phrase to the Son. Theodoret draws concord
from vv. 5–6: "Πανταχοῦ τὸ ἓν καὶ εἷς τέθεικεν, εἰς συμφωνίαν συνάπτων τὴν
Ἐκκλησίαν … Προσήκει τοίνυν ὑμᾶς, ὡς ἀδελφοὺς, τὴν περὶ ἀλλήλους ἔχειν
ὁμόνοιαν". Chrysostom draws the same practical point from vv. 4–6 just before
his gloss. God "hath called you all on the same terms", and no one is "called by
the name of a greater God, another, of a lesser God". So he asks, "whence is it
that ye are high-minded?"

**Consequence.** The Trinitarian distribution of Eph 4:6 rests on Jerome and
Aquinas. Chrysostom supports the Trinitarian confession by his argument that the
attribute implies no inferiority in the Son, not by a distribution. Theodoret
follows Chrysostom's gloss; he supports neither the distribution nor the
argument about the Son. What he draws from vv. 5–6 is concord. The two lines do
not conflict in doctrine: Aquinas holds both, and neither Chrysostom nor
Theodoret denies the distribution. A reading that uses the verse for the Persons
must still not present the distribution as the only patristic reading.

### 4.6 What is the "one body"? Jerome's alternatives

Jerome gives the Church, or the body Christ took of the Virgin, and then an
eschatological reading introduced with "aut certe illud subtilius indicatur" (the
end in which all become one body and are re-formed into the perfect man). The
last reading carries an Origenian register (*in pristinum statum restituenda sint
omnia*) that Jerome reports rather than develops; later prose must not attribute
to Jerome an apocatastasis doctrine on the strength of this sentence.

### 4.7 The seventy weeks

Jerome sets out several computations and declines to judge between them
("periculosum est de magistrorum Ecclesiae judicare sententiis"). No reading may
present a single computation as the Church's. Rupert and Durandus assert only that
Daniel was shown the time of Christ's coming.

### 4.8 The *caeli* of Ps 32:6

Augustine, Cassiodorus and Aquinas read the heavens both of creation (Word and
Spirit) and of the apostles made firm; Durandus reads them as the humble.
Basil and Bellarmine read the creation line only, and Irenaeus uses the verse
for it only, as a proof-text for the rule of faith. Aquinas speaks of
appropriation and Bellarmine of the Trinity "glanced at", each insisting that the
works of the Trinity are inseparable. These are layers, not conflicts.

### 4.9 What is the *iudicium* of Ps 118:137? Bellarmine and the medieval commentators

Rupert and Durandus, reading the Introit within their Luke 14 office, hear *rectum
iudicium tuum* as the judgment by which God exalts the humble and humbles the
proud, finally at the heavenly banquet (Rupert XII.17; Durandus VI.131: "scilicet
in hoc iudicio … quo Deus exaltat humiles", "exaltabimur in paradiso").
Augustine (s. 28.1) reads it as the just judgment by which the condemned are
condemned, which makes the penitent's weeping right. Bellarmine says that
throughout Ps 118 "judgment" "always means the law", and only if taken for "God's
allocation of rewards and punishments" is it judgment in the other sense. The
difference is in what the verse's *iudicium* names, not in doctrine. A reading
that hears the Introit of the last judgment rests on the liturgical commentators
and on Augustine's penitent, not on Bellarmine. **Keep both.**

### 4.10 Is Ps 75's judgment the last judgment? Bellarmine and Augustine

Bellarmine reads the psalm's rising of God to judgment "to save all the meek of the
earth" (vv. 9–10, before the Communion's verses) of "the day of general judgment",
and the Communion's God "terrible in his judgments … even to kings and princes".
Augustine (En. 75, 18) and Cassiodorus read *reges terrae* morally, of those who
rule their own flesh. The two lines are compatible; they differ in register.

### 4.11 Whose is the beginning of the servant's mercy? Hilary and Augustine on Ps 118:124

Both read the Introit's petition *fac cum servo tuo secundum misericordiam tuam*
as a plea for mercy and not a claim of merit. They place the beginning
differently. Augustine: God is asked to act "non utique secundum iustitiam meam",
and the *iustificationes* are those "quibus Deus facit iustos, non ipsi se"
(s. 26.5). Hilary: human weakness cannot obtain anything by itself, and its part
is only to will and begin to join God's household; mercy helps the willing,
strengthens beginners and receives those who come, "ex nobis autem initium est,
ut illa perficiat" (*Ain* 10). Hilary wrote c. 364–367 (the registered work
record's date), before Augustine's controversy with the Pelagians, and his
sentence is recorded as his; no later label is put on it. A reading that
uses the Introit for grace's priority rests on Augustine; Hilary supports the
plea for mercy and the servant's perseverance, and his sentence on the beginning
must not be cited for Augustine's point. **Keep both** where the question arises.

### 7.6 First audience and place of composition (fourth reentry)

The 1962 profile requires of each dossier "the author or compositional horizon,
first audience, composition place and date or range", and that writer, event and
recipient geography be distinguished (`guidance/liturgy/roman-1962-propers.md`).
Dates belong to the chronology corpus alone (§§7.1–7.4). The other two fields are
not corpus answers and were missing from this record for the Gospel and the
Offertory. They were read on the fourth reentry in the two articles this leaf
already binds — away from those articles' date sections, which stay closed to the
dossier (§7.4). Loci are physical lines of the tracked article-text extractions
whose digests §7.3 records.

**Gospel — "Gospel of St. Matthew" (Jacquier, CE vol. 10, 1911),
`…newadvent-10057a-e7b6ccef-article-text`.** The article gives the destination its
own section, "Destination of the Gospel" (heading line 195, body line 197):
Papias, Irenaeus, Origen, Eusebius and Jerome "agree in declaring that St. Matthew
wrote his Gospel for the Jews", and "Everything in this Gospel proves, that the
writer addresses himself to Jewish readers" — he does not explain Jewish customs
as the other Evangelists do "for their Greek and Latin readers", and "he assumes
that they are acquainted with Palestine, since, unlike St. Luke he mentions places
without giving any indication of their topographical position". Origen "categorically
asserts that this Gospel was published for Jews converted to the Faith", and "St.
Jerome, summarizing tradition, teaches us that St. Matthew published his Gospel in
Judea and in the Hebrew language, principally for those among the Jews who believed
in Jesus" (*In Matt. Prol.*). The same line carries one qualification, from a
non-Catholic authority the article quotes rather than adopts: Zahn holds it
"extremely probable that Matthew wished his book to be read primarily by the Jews
who were not yet Christians", the book being suited both to Jewish Christians "still
exposed to Jewish influence" and to "Jews who still resisted the Gospel". For the
place, Jerome's "in Judea" at line 197 is the article's own statement of it, and two
loci outside the date section stand with it: line 175, where the Evangelist
"proclaimed the Gospel to the Hebrews and, before his departure from Jerusalem,
wrote in his mother tongue the Gospel that he had preached"; and line 221, where the
Biblical Commission's answers hold that the belief "that the first Gospel was written
in the native language of the Jews then in Palestine, is to be considered as based on
Tradition" — a statement about the language and the Jews then in Palestine, which
bears on both fields without being a separate claim of place. Negative results at
this locus: the article reports **no rival place of composition** — a search of its whole text for Antioch, Syria and
Alexandria returns only Theophilus of Antioch and Clement of Alexandria as canon
witnesses (lines 25, 37) — and the one place clause inside its date section
("According to Eusebius, Matthew wrote his Gospel in Hebrew when he left Palestine",
line 201) is a step in a date argument and is not used. None of this touches the
narrated event, which the Gospel locates itself, in the Temple at Jerusalem
(Mt 21:23; §§3.6, 7.4).

**Offertory — "Book of Daniel" (Gigot, CE vol. 4, 1908),
`…newadvent-04621b-2a74931e-article-text`.** The article has no destination section;
its account of both fields is inside "Authorship and date of composition", in the
intrinsic grounds, and is separable from every figure there. Place: the first of
the three indirect intrinsic grounds is that the author was "a resident in Babylon"
(line 37, with the other two at lines 39 and 41), developed at line 43 — "the minute details he refers to, the local
colouring of his descriptions, his exact references to facts, are such as only a
resident in Babylon could be fairly supposed to possess", and the imagery of the
visions suits "one living in Babylon". First audience: at line 45 the linguistic
argument turns on the readers — the easy transition between Hebrew and Aramaic "is
explicable, we are told, only on the supposition that the writer and the readers of
the book were equally familiar with both" — and the same line closes with Daniel,
"owing to his position at the court of Babylon", being "eminently fitted at that
time for writing the prophecies which had been imparted to him for the comfort of
the Jews of his time and of subsequent ages". The book's stated object is "to
comfort God's people under the ordeal of a cruel persecution" (line 19); the
persecution the article names there is a reign, and no reign is carried to the
dossier (§7.4). Negative result: for the later author of the rival theory the
article states **no place of composition at all** (lines 25, 47, 61). Its only
geographical remarks about that theory are the book's place among "the Writings" in
the Palestinian Canon (lines 33, 61), a canon fact and not a place of writing, and
its audience, "the Jews of that period" so oppressed (line 61), which again names
the reign this record does not carry. The narrated setting is distinct from both:
the appointed verses are Daniel's own prayer in the kingdom of the Chaldeans
(Dan 9:1) for the desolate sanctuary of Jerusalem (9:17; §3.7).

**Epistle — already carried, and one limit now recorded.** The dossier's "at Rome
or Caesarea" is the Ephesians article's own alternative at its date locus (§7.3,
line 69), and its "to the saints at Ephesus (1:1)" is the letter's own received
address, not a claim of that article: the bound Clementine verse text reads
"omnibus sanctis qui sunt Ephesi" and the bound Douay "to all the saints who are at
Ephesus" (both read again at 1:1 on this reentry).
The article in fact argues the other way, in its own section "To whom addressed"
(heading line 57, body lines 59–63, read on this reentry, digest matched
`sha256:c101da65…`, its `artifact.toml` value): the words *en Epheso* at 1:1 "do not
belong to the primitive text", Basil attesting that "even in his day, they were not
met with in the ancient manuscripts" and Codices B and Aleph (first hand) lacking
them; the letter has "nothing personal, no greeting, no special recommendation", so
the article reads it as "a circular letter to a group of churches still unknown to
Paul", and proposes a conjectural restoration of the address. That is a modern
critical horizon on the recipients, of the same order as the authorship denial the
dossier already carries, and belongs in the explanatory row beside it, with no
figure taken from the same sentences.

**The four psalms — negative, and the bound of the search.** No source bound in
this leaf states a first audience or a place of composition for Ps 32, 75, 101 or
118. The corpus gives them only the critical boundary (§7.2), its controlling
source for that boundary is a protected text registered as a summarized passage and
not quotable (§7.3), and the psalm commentators read here — Augustine, Cassiodorus,
Hilary, Bellarmine — are bound as reception, not as chronology or introduction
sources; none of them was searched for a compositional audience or place, and the
titles read in the Clementine and Douay (§7.4) give attribution only. The dossier's
psalm rows therefore state the titles, the absence of a traditional date and, where
the psalm itself names one, the place inside the psalm (Sion, Judea), and assert
nothing about hearers or a place of writing. Taking that search further — the
introductions of a critical edition, or a bound patristic *argumentum* to a psalm —
is open work, listed with the unreached corpora of §5.2.

**The bound on all of this.** These are the traditional answers and one reported
critical qualification apiece. They reach the reader through
`sections/80-date-location.tex`, the reviewed home of the chronology the concise
study's physical page 2 reprints (`guidance/liturgy/propers-three-documents.md`),
and they carry no date, era, reign or period with them: every figure in a Date cell
still comes from `research/chronology-annotations.tex` (§7.1). Two consequences for
the documents that use this record. The dossier is a required field per passage, not
an optional expansion: carrying the Gospel's and the Offertory's first audience and
place of writing is what the profile asks, and dropping them again to save a line
would restore the defect this reentry answered. And page 2 of the concise study,
which must hold the whole inventory on one physical page, has to reach that by
compressing the wording — the quotations above can be reduced to their substance
(Judea, the believing Jews; Babylon, the Jews of the Exile and after) — and not by
leaving a required field unanswered.
