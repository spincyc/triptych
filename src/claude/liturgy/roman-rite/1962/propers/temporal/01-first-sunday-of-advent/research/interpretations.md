# Interpretations: First Sunday of Advent (1962)

Proposed readings for the expansive study, written on 25 November 2026 in the
research stage (iteration 0) of proper-study run `77c1ca91ef2d5e93`. Every
locus cited is in research/scope.md (sections 4-8), where its exact text and
state are recorded. The formulary is a compilation: each reading below is the
editor's synthesis across its elements, resting on what each witness says of
the element he expounds. No witness is said to have read this Mass as a whole
except the liturgical commentators, and they only for the elements their Mass
shared (scope.md, section 6).

Revised on 25 November 2026 in the research stage (iteration 1) of the same
run, after the research review: Gregory is cited by his homily's six sections;
reading 2 gives Augustine's and Ambrose's identifications of the lights each
to its own author and rests its controlling claim on what both say; reading 4
labels as the editor's the link between Augustine's two gifts and the two
comings; two editorial links in reading 1 are labelled.

Standing is from `src/sources/inventories/author-standing-v1.toml`. Each
reading has two carrying authors who are Fathers or Doctors; others support.

## 1. `lift-up-your-heads` — The world's ending lifts the heads of those who do not love it

### Controlling claim

The Gospel's signs are real blows on a world that is growing old. The same
signs that make men wither for fear (*arescentibus hominibus præ timore*) are
the reason the disciples lift their heads, because what is ending is a world
they are not to love, and what draws near is their redemption: the body's
incorruption and a creation made new. The Mass's chants of lifted souls and
unconfounded waiting, and its prayer against the dangers of our sins, place
the assembly among those who can look up.

### Carrying authors and their reasoning

- **Gregory the Great** (father), *Hom. in Ev.* 1.1-6. His argument has three
  steps. The Lord foretells the evils of an aging world "that he may wean our
  hearts" from it (1.1); some signs are already seen and others feared. The
  command to look up is read *levate capita, id est exhilarate corda*: when
  the world ends, those who are not its friends (Jas 4:4) rejoice (1.3). The
  fig tree's fruit is the world's ruin: *Ad hoc crescit ut cadat*; the summer
  is the kingdom (1.3). He closes with the judge and Zeph 1:14-16 and the
  conclusion that those who fear now will be the more secure then (1.6).
- **Cyril of Alexandria** (father), *Comm. in Luc.*, sermon 139. Luke turns
  from Jerusalem's capture to "the time of the consummation"; creation
  "begins … to be changed" and its terrors "suffice for the destruction of
  many"; Christ comes "not secretly nor obscurely, but as God and Lord" to
  "renew creation"; the redemption to look up for is the resurrection, when
  "this earthly and infirm body shall put off corruption"; therefore watch and
  pray (21:36; 2 Pet 3:10-12).
- Supporting: **Cyril of Jerusalem** (father), *Cat.* 15.3, on the parallel:
  the heavens are rolled up "not that He may destroy them, but that He may
  raise them up again more beautiful"; "the creatures are not as precious as
  the Master's words". **Bede** for the passing heaven as the aerial heaven,
  renewed (on v. 33); not for v. 28, which is Gregory's.

The two carriers agree that the signs are real, cosmic and terrible, and that
their purpose for the disciple is joy. Gregory supplies the moral turn (the
world as an object of love); Cyril the doctrinal content of the redemption
(the resurrection and new creation).

### What each element contributes

- **Gospel**: the signs, the withering, the command to look up, the fig tree,
  the word that does not pass (Gregory 1.2-1.4; Cyril).
- **Introit**: *Ad te levávi ánimam meam*. The psalm's lifted soul and the
  Gospel's lifted heads are the editor's juxtaposition, not a witness's.
  Augustine's reading of the verse supports its content: the soul lifted by
  spiritual desire from the earthly desires that trampled it (*En. in Ps.*
  24.2). Durandus, as a liturgical witness, hears hope raised by the Lord's
  coming in the Introit.
- **Gradual and Offertory**: *Univérsi, qui te exspéctant, non confundéntur*.
  Set against the Gospel's men who wither *præ timóre et exspectatióne*, the
  waiting that is not confounded (Bellarmine: those who patiently expect God's
  help "were never disappointed") is the editor's contrast; the words are the
  Missal's.
- **Collect**: the danger the Collect names is that *of our sins*
  (*ab imminéntibus peccatórum nostrórum perículis*), not of the sky; Schuster
  paraphrases "the dangers to which we are exposed through our sins". Setting
  this prayer beside Gregory's fear of judgment is the editor's connection.
- **Epistle**: the night past and the day at hand (Rom 13:12); Chrysostom's
  runner who rouses himself near the goal.
- **Alleluia**: *Osténde nobis, Dómine, misericórdiam tuam*: the prayer to be
  shown mercy and given salvation. Joining it to the Gospel's *leváte cápita*
  is the editor's connection.
- **Secret**: cleansing by the sacrament's power to come purer to its source.
- **Communion**: *Dóminus dabit benignitátem*: the goodness given now.
- **Postcommunion**: mercy received in the temple, to go before the coming
  solemnities.

### The difficulty and the disagreement

The difficulty is 7.1: Gregory reads the signs as portents, some visible in
his own time. Augustine's Letter 199 is the strongest objection: present
calamities are common to all ages, and to say the end is "sooner" is to speak
desirably and err dangerously (199.36-37, 54). The reading keeps Gregory's
realism about the signs and his moral use of them, and does not adopt his
dating of the end from his own city's storm and wars. That limit is Augustine's,
and it must be stated, not smoothed. Gregory himself marks a distance: present
terrors are *sequentis irae praecones*, as unlike the last tribulation as a
herald is unlike the judge (1.6).

### Four senses

- **Literal**: Christ foretells real signs before his coming in power and
  majesty, and tells his disciples to lift their heads because their
  redemption is near.
- **Allegorical**: the world that grows to fall is the fig tree whose fruit is
  ruin (Gregory); the redemption is the resurrection of the body and the
  renewing of creation (Cyril of Alexandria; Cyril of Jerusalem).
- **Moral**: do not love what is ending; let fear of the judge now make one
  secure then (Gregory 1.6); turn from the dangers of sin, which are the real
  threat (Collect).
- **Anagogical**: the summer of the kingdom, when heaven and earth pass and
  Christ's words remain.

### Limits

Gregory's homily was preached on another Sunday at another basilica as his
edition prints it (scope 8.4); the study cites what he says of Lk 21, not his
occasion. Gregory's event of three days before is a whirlwind sent by the
judge that shook the ground and threw down buildings (1.5-6); he does not call
it an earthquake, and Schuster's "earthquake" is not his word. Bede's
words on v. 28 are Gregory's. The Introit-Gospel pairing of lifted soul and
lifted head is editorial.

## 2. `coming-through-the-church` — The signs are read in the Church, and the hour is hidden

### Controlling claim

The Gospel's signs need not be read only as the last day's portents.
Augustine and Ambrose both read them of the Church under persecution: the
heavenly lights fail when, under persecution, many fall from the faith and the
Church's light is hidden (Augustine) or dimmed (Ambrose); Ambrose adds that so
the good are proved and the weak exposed. Both also let the coming the signs
announce be one already under way: Christ comes daily in his body, which is
the Church (Augustine), and his presence comes about in each one who receives
him with all his affections (Ambrose). They assign the lights differently, and the reading keeps each
identification with its author (below; scope 8.2). Chrysostom adds, on the
parallel, that "this generation" is the generation of the faithful, which no
trial destroys; Augustine adds that the time is hidden, so the watch is daily.
The Mass's waiting that is not confounded, and its prayer to be shown God's
ways, are the Church's part in that age (the editor's connection).

### Carrying authors and their reasoning

- **Augustine** (father), Ep. 199. He distinguishes three comings in the
  discourse: in the fall of Jerusalem, daily in his body the Church, and at
  the end in the head (199.25-26). Luke separates the city's siege (vv. 20-24)
  from the last coming (199.27). The cosmic signs are better understood of the
  Church (199.39): *Ecclesia est enim sol et luna et stellae* (Cant 6:9), and
  when sun, moon and stars fail, *Ecclesia non apparebit*, while persecutors
  rage; the falling stars are *multi, qui gratia fulgere videbantur*, who give
  way to persecutors and fall; *pressura gentium* the nations on the left
  (199.40); *in nube* the Church or his body (199.41). Perhaps the whole can
  be read of the coming by which he *quotidie venit in corpore suo, quod est
  Ecclesia*, except what is plainly the last (199.45).
  And no one knows the time: *in quo enim quemque invenerit suus novissimus
  dies, in hoc eum comprehendet mundi novissimus dies* (199.2).
- **Ambrose** (father), *Expos. in Luc.* X.36-45. When many fall from religion,
  bright faith is darkened by the cloud of faithlessness, for *sol ille
  coelestis mea fide vel minuitur, vel augetur*: the sun is Christ as each
  believer's faith receives him (X.37). The moon is the Church, which cannot
  borrow light from Christ's rays when the vices of the flesh obstruct it; in
  persecutions love of this life shuts out God's light (X.37). The stars that
  fall are the patriarchs and prophets, *resurrectionis gloria iam micantes*,
  who *excident … hominibus* if the bitterness of persecution grows strong:
  *sic enim probantur boni, sic produntur infirmi* (X.38). In a comparison he
  offers with *Fortasse*, the Lord's awaited coming is a presence in the whole
  world that *fit utique in singulis, cum omnibus affectibus receperint
  Christum*, and so the powers of heaven are moved by an increase of grace
  (X.39-40); the clouds are prophets and saints, and Christ came *in nube
  levi incarnatus ex Virgine* (X.41-42); the fig tree bears the fruit of faith
  or the leaves of the man of iniquity (X.44-45).
- **John Chrysostom** (father), *Hom. in Mt.* 77, on the parallel: "this
  generation" is "not … the generation then living, but … that of the
  believers"; over it "shall nothing prevail".
- Supporting: **Bede** on v. 32 (the human race or the Jews), with the limit
  in scope 8.5; **Cyril of Jerusalem** 15.4: "as to the time let no one be
  curious … nor on the other hand supinely slumber".

Augustine and Ambrose make independent figurative readings of vv. 25-27. What
they share carries the reading: the signs figure the Church under
persecution, in which many fall from the faith (Ambrose, *plurimis … a
religione deficientibus*; Augustine, *multi … cedent et cadent*) and the
Church's light fails or is hidden; and Christ's coming is already under way in
the Church and in those who receive him. They differ on two identifications,
and the difference is kept (scope 8.2). The sun: for Ambrose, Christ as the
believer's faith receives him; for Augustine, the Church, which is sun, moon
and stars together. The stars: for Ambrose, the patriarchs and prophets
already shining with resurrection glory, lost to men under persecution; for
Augustine, the many who seemed to shine with grace and give way. Their
emphases differ too: Ambrose on the believer's faith, Augustine on the
Church's history and on the hidden time. Chrysostom gives the reading of
v. 32 on which the reading's lasting generation depends.

### What each element contributes

- **Gospel**: the signs as figures, the generation that lasts, the words that
  do not pass (Augustine, Ambrose, Chrysostom).
- **Introit and Offertory**: Augustine reads Ps 24 as Christ speaking in the
  person of the Church (*En. in Ps.* 24.1); the enemies who would laugh are
  hidden suggestions (24.3). The psalm is the Church's voice in the age the
  Gospel describes.
- **Gradual**: *Vias tuas, Dómine, notas fac mihi*: Aquinas: the ways are the
  commandments, and *per has … vias venit dominus ad nos* (*Super Ps.* 24
  n. 4); Bellarmine: taught "practically", by grace that moves the will. The
  coming through the Church is a coming along the ways of his law.
- **Alleluia**: *Osténde nobis … misericórdiam tuam* (Augustine 84.9: we are
  already sons of God, but by faith; in hope, and not yet in reality).
- **Collect**: the Son is asked to come and rescue from the dangers of sin.
  Hearing that request as a prayer for the daily coming Augustine names is the
  editor's connection.
- **Epistle**: the night is past in the Church's time of grace (Aquinas's
  second and third nights).
- **Secret** and **Postcommunion**: the mercy received *in médio templi tui*
  (Ps 47:10, Schuster) is mercy received in the Church's worship.
- **Communion**: the earth's fruit as the good works of the faithful
  (Augustine 84.15; Durandus).

### The difficulty and the disagreement

Difficulties 7.1, 7.2 and 7.3. The reading rests on the Fathers'
distinction between Jerusalem's fall and the later signs (Augustine 199.27;
Cyril of Alexandria) and not on the Missal's lesson boundary, which is a
textual fact only. Its disagreement with reading 1 is real: Gregory and the
Cyrils read the signs as cosmic portents, Augustine and Ambrose chiefly as
figures in the Church, and Augustine rejects reading one's own calamities as
proof of nearness. Cyril of Jerusalem's warning that the darkened sun is not
Christ answers the Manichees, not Ambrose (scope 8.2).

### Four senses

- **Literal**: Christ foretells signs and his coming, and says that his words
  outlast heaven and earth and that the generation of believers will not pass
  until all is done.
- **Allegorical**: the signs figure the Church under persecution, in which
  many fall from the faith (Augustine; Ambrose) and, Ambrose adds, the good
  are proved. Each assigns the
  lights his own way: the Church is sun, moon and stars, and the falling stars
  are those who seemed to shine and give way (Augustine 199.39); the sun is
  Christ as faith receives him, the moon the Church, the stars the patriarchs
  and prophets lost to men (Ambrose X.37-38). The cloud is the Church or his
  body (Augustine 199.41), the prophets and saints, and the flesh he took of
  the Virgin (Ambrose X.42).
- **Moral**: watch daily, because each one's last day is his share of the
  world's (Augustine 199.2); keep faith so that the sun is not darkened in you
  (Ambrose).
- **Anagogical**: the coming in the head completes the coming in the body; the
  generation of the faithful stands at the end.

### Limits

Augustine himself hesitates (199.42, 45), as Ambrose does at X.39
(*Fortasse*), and neither excludes the last coming; the reading must not
make the signs merely figurative. It must not merge Augustine's and
Ambrose's identifications of the lights into one figure or credit either
Father with the other's. Chrysostom's
"generation" comments on Matthew. The figurative reading is the Fathers', not
a claim about what the Missal's compilers meant.

## 3. `hour-to-rise` — It is the hour to rise, and God must rouse the sleeper

### Controlling claim

The Epistle tells the Church it is the hour to rise from sleep because
salvation is nearer than when we believed. The Collect asks the Lord to stir
up his power and come. The two belong together: the sleep is of sin and
negligence, the waking is by grace, and the rising is to put on Christ. The
nearness that urges it is of time (each day brings the end closer), of
disposition (charity grows), and, as the Church reads these words in Advent,
of the first coming that the season remembers.

### Carrying authors and their reasoning

- **John Chrysostom** (father), *Hom. in Rom.* 24 on 13:11-14. Resurrection
  and judgment draw nearer each day; as runners near the goal rouse
  themselves, so should we (v. 11). When the day dawns, neighbours wake one
  another (v. 12); the arms of light are given for the day. Not drink but
  excess is forbidden (v. 13). Christ is put on as a garment that covers all
  and dwells within (v. 14).
- **Thomas Aquinas** (doctor), *Super Rom.* 13 l. 3. The sleep is not of
  nature or of contemplation but of sin (Eph 5:14) or negligence (Prov 6:9);
  rising is by penance and by care for good works. *Propior … salus* is
  eternal life, near in two ways: in time, as the just approach the end of
  life, and by disposition, as charity grows (Mt 25:10). *Sed secundum quod
  Ecclesia haec verba adventus tempore legit*, they speak of the salvation of
  Christ's first coming, the Apostle speaking *ex persona omnium fidelium qui
  fuerunt ab initio mundi* (Isa 56:1). Three nights fit *nox praecessit*:
  this life's ignorance, sin, and the time before the Incarnation; *dies
  appropinquavit* can mean the day of grace drawing near *per fidem et
  devotionem* (Phil 4:5).
- Supporting: **Augustine**, *Conf.* VIII.5.12, the sleeper who wishes to
  wake and says "*modo, ecce modo, sine paululum*", freed only *gratia tua*;
  and VIII.12.29, where Rom 13:13-14 ends the delay. This is Augustine's
  narrative of his conversion, not an exegesis of the verse. **Schuster**
  (blessed, liturgical commentator): the Church rouses us "by those inspired
  words of the Apostle which once wrought the conversion of Augustine";
  Christ as "a secret and supernatural garment of the soul". **Durandus**: the
  cantor's rising voice at *Ad te levavi* is *proprium … excitantis*, as the
  Epistle's *Hora est*.

Chrysostom and Aquinas carry the reading; Aquinas's distinction of nearness
and his Advent sense of the verse is the reading's distinctive inference.

### What each element contributes

- **Epistle**: the hour, the night past, the works of darkness put off, Christ
  put on.
- **Collect**: *Excita … poténtiam tuam, et veni*. The verb that the Epistle
  addresses to us (*súrgere*) the Collect asks God to exercise: the waking is
  his work (Augustine's *nisi gratia tua*). Addressed to the Son.
- **Introit**: the soul lifted; Aquinas: prayer is the mind's ascent to God
  (*Super Ps.* 24 n. 1).
- **Introit verse and Gradual**: the ways shown and made known are the
  commandments by which the Lord comes to us (Aquinas n. 4), the "ways to
  walk and not to walk" the Epistle then lists (Rupert, Sicard, on their
  shared elements).
- **Alleluia**: mercy and salvation asked, not achieved.
- **Gospel**: the fig tree's leaves show summer near (Lk 21:30-31); the
  kingdom near is the day Paul announces (Durandus joins *prope est regnum
  Dei* with *propior est nostra salus*).
- **Offertory**: the lifted soul repeated at the offering.
- **Secret**: *poténti virtúte mundátos … purióres*: the cleansing that the
  Epistle's casting off requires is the sacrament's work.
- **Communion**: the goodness the Lord gives.
- **Postcommunion**: *ventúra solémnia* of our restoration, prepared for by
  fitting honours: Aquinas's Advent sense of the Epistle.

### The difficulty and the disagreement

Difficulty 7.4. Chrysostom's nearness is chronological and eschatological;
Aquinas keeps that as the Apostle's intention and adds the Church's Advent
sense; Augustine (Ep. 199.1) refuses to measure the time. These are
complementary, but the study must keep Aquinas's order: the Apostle's sense
first, the Church's reading second.

### Four senses

- **Literal**: Paul tells the Romans it is time to wake, for the day of
  salvation is near, and to put on Christ.
- **Allegorical**: the night is the time before the Incarnation and the day
  the coming of the Sun of justice (Aquinas's third night; Durandus).
- **Moral**: rise from the sleep of sin by penance and from negligence by
  good works; put off excess; put on Christ (Chrysostom; Aquinas).
- **Anagogical**: the day of glory, nearer each day (Chrysostom; Aquinas on
  the Apostle's intention).

### Limits

Augustine's *Confessions* is reception of the verse in a life, not
commentary. Schuster's sentence on Augustine is a liturgical commentator's
connection, not Augustine's. Aquinas's Advent sense is his reading of how the
Church reads the verse, not a claim about why it was appointed.

## 4. `mercy-shown-salvation-given` — Christ given as man, to be given as God

### Controlling claim

The Psalm 84 chants ask God to show his mercy and give his salvation, and
promise that the Lord will give goodness and our earth its fruit. Augustine
reads the salvation as Christ: God has given us Christ as man and will give
us the same Christ as God (84.9). He explains the second gift by the adoption
not yet made manifest: we are already sons of God, but by faith; we are so in
hope, and not yet in reality; and *cum apparuerit, similes ei erimus,
quoniam videbimus eum sicuti est* (1 Jn 3:2, in 84.9). Bellarmine reads v. 8
as the request for the Saviour's coming. That the Mass prays between
Augustine's two gifts, and that those gifts answer to the first and second
comings of Advent, is the editor's connection: Augustine does not call the
two gifts comings. Where he names two comings of the Son of God, on Ps 24:10,
they are of mercy and of judgment, *unus miserantis, alter iudicantis*
(*En. in Ps.* 24.10), not of man and of God. What the earth's fruit is divides
the witnesses: Christ born of the Virgin, or the good works that God's
sweetness makes grow in us.

### Carrying authors and their reasoning

- **Augustine** (father), *En. in Ps.* 84. On v. 8: *Hoc est quod
  cantavimus*; *salutare tuum, Christum tuum … Dedit enim nobis Christum, sed
  hominem: quem nobis dedit hominem, eum ipsum nobis daturus est Deum*; *Ad
  ipsam adoptionem renovamur, ut efficiamur filii Dei. Jam quidem sumus, sed
  per fidem: sumus quidem in spe, nondum sumus in re*, with 1 Jn 3:2 (84.9).
  On v. 13 (his text *dabit suavitatem*): God gives the sweetness of justice,
  so that our earth, which confesses, yields good works; his rain falls on the
  seed of the word (84.15). Separately, *En. in Ps.* 24.10 (context, v. 10 not
  appointed): *duo adventus Filii Dei, unus miserantis, alter iudicantis*, the
  two comings of mercy and of judgment; Augustine does not join this to 84.9.
- **Robert Bellarmine** (doctor), *Explanatio* 84. The psalm's title, "The
  Coming of Christ to Bring Peace and Salvation to Man". On v. 8: having asked
  for reconciliation, "he now asks for the coming of the Savior … 'and grant
  us thy salvation.' Send us your Son for a Savior". On v. 13: "he will send
  his Holy Spirit from heaven, who will overshadow a virgin, and thus our
  land, which was never ploughed nor sown … will yield her fruit".
- Supporting: **Cyril of Jerusalem** (father), *Cat.* 15.1: "We preach not one
  advent only of Christ, but a second also"; the first "unobserved, like rain
  on a fleece", the second "His open coming". **Rupert** and **Sicard**
  (liturgical commentators, ecclesiastical writers) on the Communion: *terra
  nostra, id est, uterus Virginis concipiens* (Rupert, III.2). **Schuster**:
  the Alleluia's salvation "is Jesus Incarnate"; the Gospel joins the second
  coming in majesty with the first in humility. **Bernard** (*In Adventu*
  5.1): the middle advent as "the way … from the first to the last"; context
  only, since the standing file has no row for him (scope 11).

### What each element contributes

- **Alleluia**: the request for mercy and salvation (Augustine 84.9;
  Bellarmine expl. 7).
- **Communion**: the promise of goodness and fruit, read of the Incarnation
  (Bellarmine; Rupert) or of grace and good works (Augustine; Durandus).
- **Postcommunion**: *Suscipiámus … misericórdiam tuam … ut reparatiónis
  nostræ ventúra solémnia … præcedámus*: mercy received now, and the coming
  feast of our restoration. That the Alleluia and the Postcommunion both say
  *misericórdia* is a textual observation; joining them is the editor's.
- **Collect**: *Excita … et veni*: the prayer for the coming, to the Son.
- **Secret**: the sacrament that cleanses so that we come to its source.
- **Gospel**: the second coming *cum potestáte magna, et maiestáte*;
  Gregory's contrast with the humility once refused (1.2); Ambrose's *nube
  levi* of the Incarnation (X.42).
- **Epistle**: Aquinas's Advent sense, the first coming near *ex persona
  omnium fidelium*.
- **Introit, Gradual, Offertory**: waiting not confounded (Bellarmine's
  "certain hope"); the ways made known by the one who comes.

### The difficulty and the disagreement

Difficulty 7.5 is a real disagreement. Augustine (whose psalter read
*suavitatem*) reads the fruit morally; Rupert, followed by Sicard, and
Bellarmine read it of the Virgin and Christ; Durandus reads the flesh and good
works. The study gives each reading to its author and does not merge them.
Augustine's two gifts (man, then God) and Bellarmine's Incarnation reading are
compatible: both read v. 8's salvation as Christ given in the flesh (Augustine:
*dedit … hominem*; Bellarmine: "Send us your Son for a Savior"). Augustine
looks further, to Christ to be given as God, which he explains by the sight of
1 Jn 3:2, *cum apparuerit … videbimus eum sicuti est*; he does not call that
second gift a coming.

### Four senses

- **Literal**: the psalmist asks God, who has turned away his anger, to show
  mercy and give salvation, and promises that the land will yield its harvest.
- **Allegorical**: the salvation is Christ (Augustine; Bellarmine); the earth
  that yields its fruit is the Virgin (Bellarmine; Rupert).
- **Moral**: the earth that confesses yields good works under God's
  sweetness (Augustine 84.15).
- **Anagogical**: Christ, given to us as man, will be given as God; we are
  sons of God now by faith and in hope, and when he appears we shall see him
  as he is (Augustine 84.9, with 1 Jn 3:2).

### Limits

Augustine's Latin of v. 13 differs from the Missal's (*suavitatem*); the study
says so if it quotes him. Rupert and Sicard had another Gospel; they are
cited only for the Communion. Bernard cannot carry. Whether the compilers
chose Ps 84 for its Christological sense is not established.

## Comparison

- **1 and 2** give rival answers to the Gospel's chief difficulty. Reading 1
  takes the signs as coming portents and draws joy from the world's end;
  reading 2 takes them chiefly in the Church's whole age and draws daily
  watchfulness from the hidden time. They differ in exegesis (portent or
  figure), in what the hearer is told to look at (the world's ruin, or the
  state of faith), and in their use of present events (Gregory reads them;
  Augustine forbids the inference). A study must present both and must not
  merge them into "the signs are both".
- **3** is about the Epistle's nearness and the Collect's request. It differs
  from 1 in that its urgency comes from the sleeper's condition and the
  Church's Advent memory, not from the signs; from 2 in that it gives the
  nearness a positive content (disposition, the first coming) rather than
  leaving the time hidden.
- **4** is about the Psalm 84 chants and the gift of Christ, given as man and
  to be given as God; relating those gifts to the two comings is the editor's
  framing. It differs from 3 in that its first gift is Christ himself given as
  man, not a season of preparation, and it carries a disagreement of its own
  (the earth's fruit) that the other readings do not touch.
- All four use the Collect, Secret and Postcommunion, each in its own way:
  as prayer against the dangers of sin (1), as the Church's daily coming (2),
  as grace that rouses and cleanses (3), and as mercy given now toward the
  feast of restoration (4).

## Notes for the author

- Each reading's whole-Mass construction is editorial; say so once per
  reading, and give each element's sense to the witness who gives it.
- Quote the 1962 Latin and the English display of propers/verified.md.
  Gregory's lesson text reads *in nubibus*; the Missal *in nube*.
- Do not cite Bede for Lk 21:28 (Gregory's words), Durandus for the orations'
  first-coming sense without Rupert and Sicard before him, or Schuster for the
  station of Gregory's homily or an earthquake (scope 8.4-8.5).
- Rupert and Sicard read Mt 21 as their Gospel; cite them only for the
  elements they share.
- The Gradual's *notas fac* and the Communion without *Etenim* need their
  English stated as in propers/verified.md.
