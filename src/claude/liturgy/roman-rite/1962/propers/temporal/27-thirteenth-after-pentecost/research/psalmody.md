# Psalmody reception record — Ps. 73:1, 19, 20, 22, 23; Ps. 89:1; Ps. 30:15–16

Verified reception research for the three psalms the Sunday's chants sing:
Ps. 73 (Introit and Gradual), Ps. 89:1 (Alleluia), Ps. 30:15–16 (Offertory).
Vulgate numbering throughout; the Hebrew numbers are 74, 90 and 31. Every
witness below was opened and read at its own work and locus on 2026-08-20
unless the entry says otherwise; nothing rests on a catena or an aggregator.
The record keeps strictly to the appointed verses and flags every place where
a witness's exposition runs past them.

## Boundary and use

This record feeds the Introit, Gradual, Alleluia and Offertory treatments in
the per-element sweep, the covenant thread in the source-grounded synthesis
(Ps. 73 *testamentum* → Gal. 3 *diatheke*/*berith* → the Collect → *Panem de
caelo*), and the `times-in-the-hands-of-the-refuge` relation between Alleluia
and Offertory.

Quotation discipline: the NPNF first-series vol. 8 English of Augustine's
*Enarrationes* ("Expositions on the Book of Psalms," tr. members of the
English Church, Oxford/NPNF), Shepherd's Guéranger (1900) and Lagarde's
*Psalterium iuxta Hebraeos* (1874) are public domain and may be quoted in
English or Latin. O'Sullivan's 1866 English of Bellarmine is public domain
and quotable, **but it deliberately omits every philological and text-critical
passage** (see negative 10), so Bellarmine's textual notes are quoted from the
1611 Latin. Cassiodorus has **no public-domain English** (P. G. Walsh, ACW
51–53, is in copyright and was not consulted or quoted); Jerome's *Tractatus*
and *Commentarioli* have no public-domain English (Ewald's FOTC 48 and 57 are in
copyright); Theodoret's English (R. C. Hill) is in copyright; the Glossa
ordinaria has none at all. For all of those the guide quotes the Latin or Greek
only. Any English
rendering in this file that is not attributed to a named public-domain
translation is a researcher's working aid and is not published.

## Direct exegesis, witness by witness

### Augustine, *Enarratio in Psalmum 73* (PL 36)

Latin read complete at augustinus.it,
`https://www.augustinus.it/latino/esposizioni_salmi/esposizione_salmo_092_testo.htm`
(48,562 bytes of extracted text; note the file-number offset — the site's
`092` is *In Psalmum 73*, `109` is *In Psalmum 89*, `035`–`038` are the four
enarrationes on Ps. 30). Public-domain English from NPNF 1/8 at
newadvent.org/fathers/**1801074**.htm — newadvent titles by the **Hebrew**
number, so Vulgate 73 is its "Psalm 74," Vulgate 89 its "Psalm 90," Vulgate 30
its "Psalm 31"; its inline scripture references, however, are Vulgate. The PL
36 columns were **not** read from a scan and are not asserted.

- **Whose voice.** The title *Intellectus Asaph* is the hinge: "Asaph latine
  Congregatio, graece Synagoga dicitur" — the psalm is the voice of the
  Synagogue *come to understanding*, the saved remnant, not of the Church
  simply. Augustine grounds this at once on the Epistle's own conclusion:
  "quia et nos semen Abrahae sumus; Gentibus quippe loquebatur Apostolus, cum
  diceret: **Ergo semen Abrahae estis, secundum promissionem haeredes**"
  (Gal. 3:29). The Introit psalm's exposition opens by quoting the verse that
  closes this Sunday's Epistle chapter.
- **The destroyed sanctuary is Roman, and is history, not prophecy.** The
  editorial section heading printed above §6 in the augustinus.it text reads
  *Ierusalem a Romanis eversa* (an editor's rubric, not Augustine's words); at v. 5
  Augustine identifies the standards: "Habebant signa quae ibi ponerent,
  vexilla sua, aquilas suas, dracones suos, **signa Romana**; aut etiam
  statuas suas, quas primo in templo posuerunt." At §3 he ties the sack to
  Jn. 11:48: "Si dimiserimus eum, venient Romani, et tollent nobis, et locum,
  et gentem … et ubi occiderunt Christum, ibi occisi sunt," the city falling
  at Passover.
- **v. 1, `Ut quid repulisti in finem`.** Augustine's lemma is *Utquid
  repulisti **nos**, Deus, in finem? … **Iratus est animus tuus super oves
  gregis tui**" — *animus*, not the Clementine's *furor*, and *oves gregis
  tui*, not *oves pascuae tuae*. His gloss turns the question into a
  christological one: "Non reprehendit, sed quaerit … Quid est, in finem?
  Forte usque in finem saeculi. **An repulisti nos in Christum, qui finis est
  omni credenti?**" (Rom. 10:4). NPNF: "He censures not, but inquires
  wherefore … Have You repelled us unto Christ, who is the End to every one
  believing?"
- **v. 19.** Lemma: "Ne tradideris bestiis animam confitentem tibi … **Animas
  pauperum tuorum ne obliviscaris in finem**." The beasts are the devil and
  his angels; the *confessio* is Pentecost's, Acts 2:37–38 ("compuncti sunt
  corde"). The poverty is the poverty of grace against merit: "Divites eramus,
  fortes eramus … iam modo non constituimus iustitiam nostram, sed agnoscimus
  gratiam tuam; **pauperes sumus, exaudi mendicos tuos**," closing on the
  publican of Lk. 18:13.
- **v. 20, `Respice in testamentum tuum` — the decisive text for this
  Sunday.** Augustine reads *testamentum* as the **New** covenant, explicitly
  and by name, and refuses the old one clause by clause:

  > "Respice in testamentum tuum. Redde quod promisisti: **tabulas tenemus,
  > haereditatem exspectamus**. Respice in testamentum tuum; **non illud
  > vetus**: non propter terram Chanaan rogo; non propter inimicorum
  > temporalem subiectionem, non propter filiorum carnalem fecunditatem, non
  > propter divitias terrenas, non propter salutem temporalem; Respice in
  > testamentum tuum, **quo promisisti regnum coelorum**. Iam agnosco
  > testamentum tuum; iam intellectus est Asaph, non est pecus Asaph, iam
  > videt quod dictum est: *Ecce dies veniunt, dicit Dominus; et consummabo
  > domui Israel et domui Iuda testamentum novum, non secundum testamentum
  > quod disposui patribus eorum* [Jer. 31:31] … Respice ergo in testamentum
  > tuum; **et reliquiae salvae fiant**" [Rom. 9:27].

  NPNF renders it: "Have regard unto Your Testament. Fulfil that which You
  have promised: the tables we have, for the inheritance we are looking. Have
  regard unto Your Testament, not that old one … wherein You have promised the
  kingdom of Heaven."
- **v. 22.** Augustine's lemma is **`Exsurge, Domine; iudica causam meam`** —
  *Domine* with the chant, but ***meam*, not *tuam***, against both chant and
  Clementine. NPNF independently confirms the variant in English: "**Arise, O
  Lord, judge my cause**." His exposition depends on the possessive: the speaker's own
  cause is at issue because he is mocked for believing what he cannot show
  ("dum dicitur mihi quotidie: Ubi est Deus tuus?"), and the answer is the
  Resurrection: "**Non enim venturus est ad iudicium, nisi qui exsurrexit a
  mortuis.**" The long roll of promises kept — "promisit, exhibuit" eight
  times over, virgin birth, precious blood, risen flesh, believing Gentiles —
  ends: "ista omnia cum praedixisset et exhibuisset, de solo die iudicii
  mentitus est?" Note that when the chant sings *causam tuam* it is **not**
  singing Augustine's text, and his exegesis does not transfer without
  adjustment.
- **v. 23.** Lemma: **`Ne obliviscaris vocem deprecantium te`** — the
  suppliants, with the chant's sense (*quaerentium te*) and against the
  Clementine's *inimicorum tuorum*. Gloss: "Ingemiscentium, et exspectantium
  iam quod promisisti **de Novo Testamento**, et ad ipsam fidem ambulantium."
  The covenant reading of v. 20 is carried straight into v. 23. Of the enemies
  ("Superbia eorum qui te oderunt, ascendat semper ad te"): "Noli et eorum
  superbiam oblivisci. Nec obliviscitur: omnino aut punit, aut corrigit."
- **A leprosy typology inside the Introit psalm (v. 11, outside the appointed
  verses — flagged).** Expounding "Utquid avertis manum tuam et dexteram tuam
  de medio sinu tuo," Augustine takes Moses' second sign (Ex. 4:6–7): "Mitte
  manum tuam in sinum tuum … et inventa est alba, id est immunda. **Albor enim
  in cute, lepra est, non candor.** Ipsa enim haereditas Dei, id est, populus
  eius foras ab eo missus, immundus factus est … Revoca eam; **redeat ad
  colorem, agnoscat salvatorem**," closing on Rom. 11:25 ("Caecitas ex parte
  Israel facta est, ut plenitudo Gentium intraret, et sic omnis Israel salvus
  fieret"). This is the same colour-theology of leprosy Augustine uses on this
  Sunday's Gospel at *Quaestiones Evangeliorum* II.40 (see
  `research/gospel-luke-17.md`), here applied to the Introit psalm. The verse
  is **not** in the appointed centonisation; the guide may use it only as a
  cross-reference, clearly labelled as v. 11.
- Supersessionist formulations are present in Augustine's own voice ("In a
  word, all things which to the Jews had been promised have been taken away")
  and are attributed to him where used; but the *Enarratio* ends, like the
  Gospel record's witnesses, on Rom. 9–11 and "reliquiae salvae fiant."

### Augustine, *Enarratio in Psalmum 89* (PL 37)

Latin at augustinus.it `esposizione_salmo_109_testo.htm` (23,696 bytes
extracted); public-domain English NPNF 1/8, newadvent.org/fathers/1801090.htm.
PL 37 columns not read from a scan.

- **The Moses title, and a critical judgement about it.** "Oratio Moysi
  hominis Dei, psalmus iste praenotatur: per quem hominem suum Deus Legem
  dedit populo suo … Fuit ergo **Moyses minister Testamenti Veteris, et
  propheta Testamenti Novi**." Then, flatly: "**Non enim credendum est ab ipso
  omnino Moyse istum psalmum fuisse conscriptum**, qui ullis eius litteris
  inditus non est, in quibus eius cantica scripta sunt: sed alicuius
  significationis gratia tam magni meriti servi Dei nomen adhibitum est, ex
  quo dirigeretur legentis vel audientis intentio." NPNF: "Now it is not to be
  believed that this Psalm was entirely the composition of that Moses … but
  the name of the great servant of God is used for the sake of some
  intimation, which should direct the attention of the reader or listener."
- **v. 1, `a generatione in generationem` — read as the two covenants.**
  Augustine's lemma is *in generatione et generatione*, and he gives two
  readings, the second of which is the Sunday's whole argument in one clause:

  > "Domine, inquit, refugium factus es nobis in generatione et generatione:
  > **sive in omni generatione, sive in duabus generationibus, vetere et
  > nova**; quia, sicut dixi, minister fuit ille Testamenti pertinentis ad
  > generationem veterem, et Propheta Testamenti pertinentis ad generationem
  > novam. **Cuius Testamenti sponsor, sponsusque coniugii** quod de illa
  > generatione sortitus est, ait ipse Iesus: *Si crederetis Moysi, crederetis
  > et mihi; de me enim ille scripsit*" [Jn. 5:46].

  NPNF: "either in every generation, or in two generations, the old and new …
  Jesus Himself, the Surety of that covenant, and the Bridegroom in the
  marriage which He entered into in that generation."
- **The refuge is God's eternity.** v. 2 supplies the reason the refuge can be
  "made" without God's changing: "Tu ergo qui semper es, et antequam essemus,
  et antequam mundus esset, refugium ex quo ad te conversi sumus factus es
  nobis … **Ecce quae aeternitas facta est nobis refugium, ut in ea mansuri,
  ad eam de hac temporis mutabilitate fugiamus.**" *Priusquam montes fierent*
  distinguishes angels (made) from man (formed of clay); *a saeculo usque in
  saeculum* would be better said "ab aeterno in aeternum," and the present
  tense *tu es* insinuates "Dei substantiam omni modo incommutabilem, ubi non
  est: Fuit, et Erit; sed tantum: Est" (Ex. 3:14).
- **Direct bearing on the Offertory.** Augustine's word for the refuge is the
  contrast of *aeternitas* with *temporis mutabilitas* — the exact axis on
  which the Offertory's *tempora mea* turns. The connection is Augustine's
  vocabulary, not a modern conceit, though he does not himself make the
  cross-chant link.
- Only vv. 1–4 of Ps. 89 bear on the appointed Alleluia; everything after
  §5 (the thousand years, the days of our years, *Repleti sumus mane*) is
  outside the appointed text and is flagged where used.

### Augustine, *Enarrationes in Psalmum 30* — **four**, not three (PL 36)

augustinus.it prints them as *In Psalmum 30 enarratio* I, II, III and IV
(files `035`, `036`, `037`, `038`; in Maurist/CCSL terms: *enarratio* I, and
*enarratio* II *sermones* 1–3). Verse coverage, verified by listing every
`[v n]` lemma in each file:

| Piece | file | verses treated |
|---|---|---|
| Enarr. I | 035 | 1–19, 22–25 |
| Enarr. II (s. 1) | 036 | 1–9 |
| Enarr. III (s. 2) | 037 | 11–16 |
| Enarr. IV (s. 3) | 038 | 16–25 |

So the Offertory's vv. 15–16 are treated in **Enarr. I and Enarr. III only**;
Enarr. IV picks up at v. 16b (*Erue me de manibus inimicorum meorum*) and has
nothing on the *sortes*. **All four read `In manibus tuis sortes meae`.**

- **Enarr. I, §§15–16** (the piece NPNF translates): "Ego autem in te speravi,
  Domine; dixi: Tu es Deus meus: non enim mutatus es, ut non salvum facias qui
  emendas. … **In manibus tuis sortes meae**: in potestate tua sunt sortes
  meae. Non enim ullum video meritum, quo de universa impietate generis humani
  me potissimum elegisti ad salutem: et si est apud te iustus et occultus ordo
  electionis meae, ego tamen quem hoc latet, **ad tunicam Domini mei sorte
  perveni**" (Jn. 19:24). NPNF: "In Thy hands are my lots. In Thy power are my
  lots. For I see no desert for which out of the universal ungodliness of the
  human race You have elected me particularly to salvation … yet I, from whom
  this is hid, have attained by lot unto my Lord's vesture." Note the lemma
  agrees with the chant in word order — *dixi: Tu es Deus meus*, not the
  Clementine's *Deus meus es tu*.
- **Enarr. III, §§12–13** (Latin only; NPNF omits this sermon — see negatives)
  is the fullest patristic exegesis of the Offertory's second clause, and it
  is an exegesis of *lots*:

  > "In manibus tuis sortes meae: non in manibus hominum, sed in manibus tuis.
  > Quae sunt istae sortes? quare sortes? Audito nomine sortium, **non debemus
  > sortilegos quaerere**. Sors enim non aliquid mali est: sed res est in
  > dubitatione humana divinam indicans voluntatem" — the apostles' lot for
  > Matthias (Acts 1:26) — "Sortes dixit, quantum ego existimo, **gratiam qua
  > salvi facti sumus**. Quare sortis nomine appellat gratiam Dei? **Quia in
  > sorte non est electio, sed voluntas Dei** … quando autem Deus nulla merita
  > nostra invenit, **sorte voluntatis suae nos salvos fecit, quia voluit, non
  > quia digni fuimus**. Haec est sors." The seamless tunic follows again, and
  > then Eph. 2:8–10 ("Gratia salvi facti estis per fidem … *vide sortem* …
  > non ex operibus"), and the conclusion: "Haec quodam modo sors occulta est
  > voluntas Dei … non enim ille personas accipit, sed **occulta illius
  > iustitia tibi sors est**."

  Augustine's §12 on v. 15 sets this up as the alternative to imitating men:
  "cum attenderem homines iustos et quaererem, et deessent quos imitarer, quid
  feci? … Ego autem, in te speravi, Domine … Tolle ab homine spem, quia
  *maledictus omnis qui spem suam ponit in homine*" (Jer. 17:5).
- **The Samaritan inside the Offertory psalm (v. 3, outside the appointed
  verses — flagged).** Enarr. II (s. 1), on *esto mihi … in domum refugii*,
  brings in Lk. 10: "Quem transiens sacerdos praeteriit, transiens Levita
  praeteriit, transiens Samaritanus miseratus est, id est ipse Dominus …
  **Samarites enim custos interpretatur.** Et quis nos custodit, si ille
  deserit? … cum Iudaei conviciantes dicerent: *Nonne verum dicimus, quia
  Samaritanus es, et daemonium habes*, unum respuit, alterum amplexus est …
  sic intellegi volens **nostrum se esse custodem**." The *Samaritanus =
  custos* etymology on which Augustine's Gospel exposition and Rupert's
  reading of the whole Office rest (see `research/gospel-luke-17.md`) occurs
  in Augustine's exposition of the Offertory psalm itself, and it occurs there
  attached to the word *refugium*, which is the Alleluia's word. Verse 3, not
  15–16: cite only as a cross-reference.
- **Augustine's own lexical bridge between *sortes* and *testamentum* — at
  another psalm.** At *Enarr. in Ps. 67*, §16 (on Ps. 67:14 *si dormiatis
  inter medios cleros*; augustinus.it `esposizione_salmo_084_testo.htm`) he
  explains why *lots* can mean *covenants*: "Cur autem in cleris Testamenta
  intellegantur, cum hoc nomen graecum sit quidem, sed Testamentum non ita
  dicatur; haec causa est, **quia per testamentum datur haereditas**, quae
  graece appellatur [κληρονομία], et haeres [κληρονόμος]. [κλῆρος] autem sors
  graece dicitur, et **sortes ex promissione Dei, partes haereditatis
  vocantur** … Proinde propter haereditatem quae fit testamento … **nomine
  clerorum Testamenta ipsa significata sunt**." (The Greek words are dropped
  by the augustinus.it transcription and are supplied here from the NPNF
  translation of the same sentence, which is public domain: "kleronomia … an
  heir kleronomos. Now kleros in Greek is the term for lot … by the name of
  'lots' the Testaments themselves are signified.") NPNF adds Augustine's
  second reading: "understanding by *cleri* the inheritances themselves: so
  that, whereas the inheritance of the Old Testament … is earthly felicity;
  but the inheritance of the New Testament is everlasting immortality."
  **This is not a comment on Ps. 30:16** and must never be cited as one; it is
  evidence that for Augustine *sors* and *testamentum* are the same
  inheritance-word — which is precisely the pair the Introit and the Offertory
  put on either side of this Mass.

### Cassiodorus, *Expositio Psalmorum* (PL 70)

Latin read complete at monumenta.ch for all three psalms:
`text.php?tabelle=Cassiodorus&rumpfid=Cassiodorus,+Expositio+in+Psalterium,+2,+++73`
(51,851 bytes of HTML, 31,326 of extracted text), `…,+2,+++89` (37,105 bytes)
and `…,+1,+++30` (57,685 bytes). The site carries a manuscript apparatus
(siglum `bnf12957` observed inline) but **no statement of the underlying
edition was found on the pages, and no PL 70 column marker was read**; this is
therefore content verified in an electronic transmission, not at a
column-bearing scan. No public-domain English exists; Walsh (ACW) is
copyrighted and was not consulted.

**Cassiodorus is the most important witness in this file, for two reasons: his
psalter lemmata agree with the appointed chants at four points where the
Clementine does not, and his exegesis of `Respice in testamentum tuum` matches
Augustine's.**

*On Ps. 73:*

- **Prophecy, and the Roman fulfilment named.** The psalm is the Church's
  lament over a destruction still to come: "Asaph … interpretatur Congregatio,
  quae nunc vocatur Ecclesia. **Ista futuras clades civitatis Ierusalem
  miseranda lamentatione deplorat.** Mira pietas, stupenda clementia, malum
  futurum praesentem sibi facere planctum; **et sic dolere ventura, quasi iam
  probentur esse suscepta!**" He classes it with Pss. 78 and 136 as a
  *lamentatio* distinct from a penitential psalm.
- **v. 1.** Lemma: "**Utquid, Deus, repulisti in finem? iratus est furor tuus
  super oves gregis tui**" — *furor* with the Clementine and the chant, *oves
  gregis tui* against both (chant and Clementine: *oves pascuae tuae*).
  Exposition: "Introducitur populus Iudaeorum Deo supplicans, ut averteret ab
  Israeliticis quod imminebat exitium. **Utquid? quaerentis est, non
  imputantis** … timens ne, quia permissa est templi vastatio, Iudaicus quoque
  populus funditus interiret … *In finem*, hic interitum significat et
  captivitatem, **quam Ierusalem temporibus Vespasiani atque Titi principum a
  Romanis legitur pertulisse**." The Roman destruction, named by emperor, read
  as the thing foretold.
- **v. 19.** Lemma: "Ne tradas bestiis animas confitentes tibi: animas pauperum
  tuorum ne obliviscaris in finem" — *obliviscaris* with the Clementine and
  the Gradual, against the Introit's *ne derelinquas*. Gloss: the *confitentes*
  are the believing remnant in that people — "Erant enim in illo populo etiam
  tunc vasa misericordiae, ex quibus fuit **Simeon, Nicodemus, Nathanael**"
  (Jn. 1:47) — and the poor are those "qui superbia mundi repudiata, ad
  humilitatem se sanctissimam contulerunt … **sed quia pauperes eius facti
  sunt, ipsius largitate ditescant**."
- **v. 20, `Respice in testamentum tuum`.** Cassiodorus reads it exactly as
  Augustine does, with the same Jeremiah proof-text:

  > "**Respice, dixit, ut digneris implere quae cognosceris ante promisisse**;
  > sicut scriptum est: *Ecce dies venient, dicit Dominus, et consummabo domui
  > Israel et domui Iuda testamentum novum, non secundum testamentum quod
  > disposui patribus eorum* [Jer. 31:31]. In illo enim **pollicitationes
  > temporales** sunt, ut fuit terra repromissionis et inimicorum subiectio …
  > **In Novo autem Testamento promittitur imperturbabilis vita, regnum cuius
  > non erit finis, beatitudo perpetua, et Domini contemplatio gloriosa.** In
  > hoc ergo testamento respicere dicit Dominum debere … Revera intelligens
  > factus est Asaph, qui talia petit, **unde totus mundus potuisset absolvi**.
  > Et vide qua sapientia cuncta discurrit: **quia non erant bona, quae
  > delinquentis populi commemorare potuisset, meminit promissionis Dominicae
  > quae semper impletur.**"

  The last sentence is the rhetorician's point and is the best single sentence
  in the file for the Introit: with no merits to plead, the psalmist pleads
  the promise.
- **v. 22.** Lemma: "**Exsurge, Domine, iudica causam tuam**: memor esto
  improperiorum tuorum, eorum quae ab insipiente sunt tota die" — ***Domine*
  and *causam tuam*, the chant's reading against the Clementine's *Exsurge,
  Deus***. Gloss: "nunc facit **Iudicis causam**; ut eum efficacius commoveret,
  cui negotii sui qualitas intimatur … **Iudicat enim causam suam, dum errantes
  facit manifesta cognoscere, ut conversi praedicent quod stultis cogitationibus
  abnuebant**" — God judges his own cause by converting, not by destroying.
  (The Gradual's second half, *memor esto opprobrii servorum tuorum*, is not
  Cassiodorus's lemma here; see `propers/verified.md` no. 5.)
- **v. 23.** Lemma: "**Ne obliviscaris voces quaerentium te**: superbia eorum
  qui te oderunt ascendat semper ad te" — ***quaerentium te*, the Introit's
  reading, against the Clementine's *inimicorum tuorum***. Gloss: "miscuit
  iterum vota fidelium, ne abiiciat preces eorum qui ad ipsum toto cordis
  affectu clamare noscuntur; **ut inveniant quem quaerunt, et mereantur cernere
  quem expetunt**." Of the pride: "**Hoc congrue dictum de Romanis
  advertimus**, de quibus superius ait: *In medio atrio tuo posuerunt signa
  sua*."
- **The Moses-leprosy typology again (v. 11, outside the appointed verses —
  flagged).** As in Augustine: "sicut Moysi datum fuerat per virgam miracula
  facere, ita illi praeceptum est ut dexteram suam in sinum mitteret,
  exindeque prolata, **leprosa** conspecta est … Significans quia populus
  Iudaeorum exiens a Domino Christo immundus fieret; **ad eum vero conversus
  reciperet pristinam sospitatem**," and *in finem* at v. 10 is glossed
  "significat mundi vesperam, **quando gens Iudaeorum pro maxima parte
  creditura est**." The Introit psalm's two Latin expositors both find a
  leprosy healed by return at v. 11 — a genuine, and unexpected, contact with
  the Gospel of the day, but at a verse the chant does not sing.

*On Ps. 89:*

- **v. 1.** Lemma: "**refugium factus es nobis, a generatione et progenie**" —
  ***the Alleluia's exact reading***, against the Clementine's *a generatione
  in generationem*, and against Augustine's and Jerome's *in generatione et
  generatione*. And he glosses the two nouns separately:

  > "**A generatione, significat ex quo generari coeptum est genus humanum.
  > Progeniem vero dixit usque ad finem saeculi, cum progenies sibi humani
  > generis per aetates singulas reparata succedit; ac si diceret, semper.**"

- **The refuge is Moses' own biography, then the Church's.** "Moyses ille
  venerabilis **in typo sanctae Ecclesiae** … Domino fusa oratione gratias
  agit, quod in hoc saeculi salo factus fuerit eius insigne refugium," with
  the four perils enumerated (exposure among the Hebrew children, Pharaoh, the
  Red Sea, the murmuring people). Then the grammatical argument: "Verum ut hoc
  non tantum de uno homine, sed de universali Ecclesia debuissemus advertere,
  **nobis posuit, non mihi**: quoniam cunctae Ecclesiae, quae in hac saeculi
  tempestate versatur, **tutissimum refugium semper est Dominus**."
- **The Moses title.** "**Moysen … manifestum est ministrum fuisse Testamenti
  Veteris, et prophetam NOVI.** De ipso enim in Evangelio dicit: *Si crederetis
  Moysi, crederetis et mihi, de me enim ille scripsit* [Jn. 5:46]. Et quia
  utraque psalmus iste coniunxit, merito illi tale nomen appositum est, **quod
  duo sacramenta vi sui nominis indicaret**." This is Augustine's sentence and
  Augustine's proof-text, taken over; Cassiodorus adds the point that the title
  is put there because the psalm *joins* both covenants. He also notes this is
  the third of the psalms titled *oratio*.

*On Ps. 30:*

- **The whole psalm is the voice of Christ.** "Verba quae titulus continet, ad
  Dominum Christum frequenter diximus esse referenda, cui totus psalmus
  aptandus est, quando eius **passionem resurrectionemque** decantat … **Per
  universum psalmum verba sunt Domini Salvatoris.**"
- **vv. 15–16 — and the `tempora` reading, found.** Cassiodorus's lemma is
  ***`Dixi, tu es Deus meus; in manibus tuis tempora mea`*** — the Offertory's
  reading, word for word, including the word order *dixi, tu es Deus meus*
  against the Clementine's *Deus meus es tu*, and ***tempora* where every
  other Latin witness in this file has *sortes***. His exposition turns on
  *tempora* and cannot be transposed to *sortes*:

  > "Dominus Christus dicit: *Tu es Deus meus*, sed **a parte scilicet
  > humanitatis assumptae, quae, sicut ait inferius, et tempori subiacebat et
  > morti**. Non enim, quemadmodum inimici putabant, eorum persecutione vitam
  > suam finiendam esse commemorat, sed **in potestate ponit Domini tempora
  > vitae suae**. Ipso enim creante existimus, ipso disponente vegetamur,
  > eodem etiam iubente transimus."

  His lemma at v. 15a is likewise the chant's: "ego autem in te speravi,
  Domine," glossed against the plotters of v. 14 ("Cum inimici circumhabitarent
  synagogam, et spem suam haberent in viribus suis, se dicit sperasse de
  Domino").
- **v. 6 in the same psalm (outside the appointed verses — flagged).** "*In
  manus tuas commendo spiritum meum: redemisti me, Domine, Deus veritatis.*
  Consideremus cur hic sermones ipsi sint positi, **quos Evangelii textus
  eloquitur** … scilicet ut et hic cognosceres fuisse locutum, qui post tot
  saecula, eadem erat in cruce positus verba dicturus … **Commendat itaque
  Patri inaestimabilem thesaurum**, animam videlicet illam quae voluntates
  ipsius pura intentione faciebat." The Offertory's *in manibus tuis* is, in
  Cassiodorus's reading, the same hands into which the dying Christ commends
  his spirit nine verses earlier.

- **The *Commentarioli* do treat Ps. 73 — but only the title and v. 5, and
  they date the sack to Babylon, not Rome** (printed p. 60, running head

  > "**LXXIII.** *Intellectus Asaph.* Ideo intellectus in titulo praenotatur,
  > **quia duplex captivitas sequitur, et carnalis videlicet et spiritalis.**
  > *Posuerunt signa sua, signa: et non cognoverunt sicut in exitu desuper.*
  > **Sic Babylonii**, in adrogantiam victoriae tumore sublati, in templo tuo
  > sua signa posuerunt, quomodo solent super portarum fornices de hostibus
  > trophaea constitui. Quod **Symmachus** planius interpretatus est, dicens:
  > 'Posuerunt signa sua manifeste in edito introitu portarum.'"

  Jerome has **nothing** on vv. 1, 19, 20, 22 or 23 of Ps. 73 — a clean
  bounded negative on the Introit and Gradual text, and a real disagreement
  with Augustine and Cassiodorus about which army is meant.
- **The *Commentarioli* on Ps. 89 comment only on vv. 9 and 12** (printed
  p. 69): the cobweb years, with Aquila's "Anni nostri similes loquenti," and
  Symmachus's "Dies nostros sic notos fac, ut veniamus corde sapienti."
  **Nothing on v. 1**, nothing on the *refugium*, nothing on the Moses title
  there.
- **Jerome's *Tractatus de psalmo LXXXVIIII* (first series; Morin 1897,
  printed pp. 106–108, the running head `TRACTATUS DE PSALMO LXXXVIIII. 107`
  legible in the OCR) is the file's best cross-proper find.** Expounding
  the Alleluia's verse, Jerome explains what a refuge is by quoting the
  Introit's verse:

  > "*Domine, refugium factus es nobis in generatione et generatione.* Qui
  > refugium quaerit, aut ab aestu nimio quaerit et calore, aut bestia illum
  > persequitur, aut hostes illum persequuntur, ut interficiant. Et nos igitur
  > in aestu sumus istius saeculi; **et nos bestia persequitur, de qua dicimus
  > *Ne tradas bestiis animam confitentem tibi*** [Ps. 73:19]; et nos hostes
  > persequuntur; propterea subter alas tuas refugimus. **In generatione et
  > generatione: et in praeterito, et in praesenti, et in futuro. Aliter *In
  > generatione et generatione*: et in vetere populo Iudaeorum, et in nostro
  > Xpistianorum.**"

  Morin's apparatus note identifies the citation (`8. illi] Ps. 73, 19`).
  Jerome thus (a) gives the same two-generations = two-peoples reading as
  Augustine, and (b) joins the Alleluia's psalm to the Introit's psalm at the
  very verse the Introit sings, on the ground that the refuge is sought from
  the *beasts*. Against the Origenist reading of v. 2 he insists on the
  punctuation: "Male distinguis, haeresis. Quare calumniam facis Spiritui
  sancto? … **Sic ergo legendum est: *Domine, refugium factus es nobis in
  generatione et generatione*: et hucusque distinctio.** Rursum aliud
  principium: *Priusquam montes fierent*…"
- **Jerome's second homily on Ps. 89** (*series altera*; Morin 1903, printed
  pp. 61ff.) adds the canonical placing and the Hebrew ascription: "Quarti
  libri exordium est, quoniam psalterium in quinque libros divisum scire
  debemus … **Hebraei non solum istum psalmum, sed et alios decem qui
  sequuntur, quoniam in eis titulus non habetur, putant a Moyse esse
  conscriptos.**" On *hominis Dei*: "**Si omnes homines essent homines Dei,
  numquam additum esset, *Oratio Moysi hominis Dei*** … Grande est ergo, dici
  homines Dei aut servos Dei … **nec enim condicio servitutis ostenditur, sed
  bene servientis dignitas demonstratur**." And on the refuge: "**Refugium
  numquam dicitur, nisi fuerit persecutio**: nisi enim nos alius persequatur,
  non indigemus ad alium refugere … quoniam nos grandis tribulationum aestus
  fatigat et cauma, sub umbra alarum tuarum refugimus … **In generatione et
  generatione. In isto loco serpens sibilat.**"
- Morin's own note at the first homily points the reader to **Jerome, *Ep.*
  140 ad Cyprianum**, an entire exposition of Ps. 89, and quotes Augustine's
  *Enarr. in Ps. 89* n. 2 against Mosaic authorship — the same sentence
  transcribed above from augustinus.it, which independently corroborates that
  text. *Ep.* 140 itself was **not opened**; unverified lead.

- **The hypothesis: prophecy of the Roman destruction — VERIFIED at the locus
  (col. 1453), and argued by triple exclusion.** The earlier researcher's
  report is confirmed, and it is stronger than reported: Theodoret does not
  merely assert the Roman reading, he eliminates the alternatives.

  > "**Συνέσεως τῷ Ἀσάφ.** Τὴν ἐσομένην τῆς Ἱερουσαλὴμ πανωλεθρίαν ὁ
  > προφητικὸς προθεσπίζων λόγος, ἐπιστῆναι καὶ συνιέναι κελεύει τοῖς
  > ἐντυγχάνουσι τοῦ ψαλμοῦ τὴν διάνοιαν. Τινὲς μέντοι τοῖς ὑπὸ Ἀντιόχου τοῦ
  > Ἐπιφανοῦς γεγενημένοις προσήρμοσαν τὸν ψαλμόν, οὔτε τῇ ἱστορίᾳ
  > προσεσχηκότες, οὔτε τοῦ ψαλμοῦ τὴν προφητείαν ἀκριβῶς νενοηκότες … Οὔτε μὴν
  > τῇ Βαβυλωνίων ἁρμόττει πολιορκίᾳ· προφήτας γὰρ εἶχον τηνικαῦτα πολλούς, τὸν
  > Ἱερεμίαν, Οὐρίαν τὸν Σαμαίου, τὸν Ἰεζεκιήλ, Δανιήλ. Ὁ δὲ ψαλμός φησιν·
  > *Οὐκ ἔστιν ἔτι προφήτης* … **Εἰ δὲ μήτε Βαβυλωνίοις, μήτε Μακεδόσιν
  > ἁρμόττει, δῆλον ὅτι τὴν ὑπὸ Ῥωμαίων ἐπενεχθεῖσαν αὐτοῖς πανωλεθρίαν
  > προαγορεύει**, μεθ' ἣν οὐδεμιᾶς ἀνακλήσεως ἔτυχον, τῆς κατὰ τοῦ Σωτῆρος
  > ἀσεβείας τίνοντες δίκας."

  The reasoning: the psalm describes the burning of the temple, the demolition
  of houses and a total sack, which history does not record under Antiochus;
  and it says "there is no longer a prophet," which cannot be the Babylonian
  siege, when Jeremiah, Uriah son of Shemaiah, Ezekiel and Daniel were alive.
  He adds a note of wonder at God's goodness in foretelling the disaster in
  order to avert the impiety that causes it.
- **Titus, by name (col. 1457).** At v. 4: "Ἐν τῇ τοῦ Πάσχα γὰρ ἑορτῇ, παντὸς
  τοῦ λαοῦ κατὰ τὸν νόμον συναθροισθέντος, **τὴν στρατιὰν ὁ Τῖτος
  παρακαθίσας, ἐπολιόρκησε τὴν ἐκείνων μητρόπολιν**. Ἐπειδὴ γὰρ ἐν τῇ τοῦ
  Πάσχα ἑορτῇ τῷ σταυρῷ τὸν Σωτῆρα προσήλωσαν, κατὰ τὸν αὐτὸν καιρὸν τίνουσι
  τῆς ἀσεβείας ποινήν." At v. 5 the *signa* are Pilate's imperial standards
  brought into the city against the law, and the boar's head the Roman emperors
  carved on the gate-stones, with Mt. 24:15 (τὸ βδέλυγμα τῆς ἐρημώσεως). The
  Passover timing is the same detail Augustine gives in Latin — the two
  traditions converge on Rome, at the feast, as punishment for the crucifixion.
- **v. 1 (col. 1453).** "**Ἱνατί, ὁ Θεός, ἀπώσω εἰς τέλος;**" (Migne prints
  *Ἱνατί* as one word.) The load-bearing phrase is *εἰς τέλος*: "Οὔτε γάρ, ὡς
  ἐπὶ Βαβυλωνίων, τῇ δουλείᾳ **τὸν ἑβδομηκοντούτην ὥρισας χρόνον**, οὔτε ὡς ἐπὶ
  Ἀντιόχου, **τὰ ἑπτὰ ἥμισυ ἔτη**, κατὰ τὴν τοῦ Δανιὴλ προφητείαν, ἀλλ' εἰς
  τέλος ἀπώσω, καὶ πανωλεθρίαν κατεψηφίσω." On the second clause: "Ὠργίσθη ὁ
  θυμός σου ἐπὶ πρόβατα νομῆς σου. **Ἐπίμονον ἔσχες τὴν κατὰ τῶν προβάτων τῶν
  σῶν ἀγανάκτησιν.**" (Note that Theodoret's Greek has *πρόβατα νομῆς σου* =
  the Clementine's *oves pascuae tuae*, the chant's reading, against Augustine's
  and Cassiodorus's *oves gregis tui*.)
- **v. 19 (cols. 1464–1465).** "Οὐχ ἁπλῶς ὁ προφητικὸς πᾶσιν ἐπεύχεται λόγος·
  ἀλλὰ **τοῖς τὸ θεῖον ὁμολογοῦσιν ὄνομα αἰτεῖ τὰ ἀγαθὰ** … Θηρία δὲ καλεῖ [οὐ
  μόνον] **τὴν τῶν πολεμίων ὠμότητα** …" and on the poor: "**Καὶ πένητες δὲ
  εἶεν ἂν οἱ τὸ φρόνημα ταπεινοί**," sealed with Mt. 5:3. The square brackets
  are **Migne's own**, marking material absent from one or both of his codices;
  the bracketed expansions here are all anti-Jewish and Christological
  (`τοῖς ὁμολογοῦσι τὸν σταυρωθέντα Θεὸν ἐκ Θεοῦ …`), and should not be
  attributed to Theodoret without the flag.
- **v. 20 — a flat, important negative, and a textual surprise (col. 1465).**
  Theodoret's printed lemma is **not** `ἐπίβλεψον εἰς τὴν διαθήκην σου`. It is:

  > "**Ἐπίβλεψον εἰς τὴν διαθήκην τῶν δούλων σου**, ὅτι ἐπληρώθησαν οἱ
  > ἐσκοτισμένοι τῆς γῆς οἴκων ἀνομιῶν."

  and his whole note expounds only the **second** half: "Οἱ γὰρ τῆς σῆς αἴγλης
  οὐκ ἀπολαύσαντες … τὸ τῆς ἀγνοίας ἀγαπήσαντες σκότος, ἀνομίας καὶ τῆς
  ἐντεῦθεν φυείσης τιμωρίας μεστὰς ἔχουσιν οἰκίας … Παντοδαποῖς κακοῖς
  παρεδόθησαν, **διὸ προείλοντο σκότος**."

  **Theodoret says nothing whatever about what διαθήκη means here.** No
  Abraham, no Sinai, no New Covenant, no gloss of any kind on the word. This is
  the sharpest contrast in the file: the two Latin expositors make v. 20 the
  hinge of the psalm and identify the covenant with Jeremiah's new one, while
  the Antiochene passes the clause without comment. The guide should say so.
  Two subsidiary facts, both from Migne's own apparatus as reported in the
  parallel pass that read the page image: the intrusive `τῶν δούλων` is flagged
  in the *Variae Lectiones* (fn. 83) as "**Abest ab utroque cod., a textu Hebr.
  et a versione τῶν Ο΄**"; and Migne's facing Latin renders the clause
  "**Respice ad foedus servorum tuorum**" — *foedus*, not *testamentum*, so the
  Latin of PG 80 does not even carry the Introit's word.
- **v. 22 — Theodoret defends precisely the chant's `causam tuam` (col.
  1465).** This is the file's most useful single sentence for the Gradual:

  > "Ἀνάστα, ὁ Θεός, δίκασον τὴν δίκην σου … **Σφόδρα δὲ ἁρμοδίως τὴν
  > ἀντωνυμίαν ἐνήλλαξεν· οὐκ εἶπε τὴν δίκην μου, ἀλλὰ τὴν δίκην σου.** Ἐγὼ μὲν
  > γάρ, φησίν, **ἐνδίκως ὑπέμεινα ταῦτα**· ἐκεῖνοι δὲ πολλὰς ἐτόλμησαν κατὰ
  > σοῦ βλασφημίας."

  Working rendering: *"Very aptly he changed the pronoun: he did not say 'my
  case' but 'thy case.' For I, he says, suffered these things justly; but they
  dared many blasphemies against thee."* The exchange of pronouns is the whole
  point of his note — the suppliant concedes that his own punishment is
  deserved and re-founds the plea on God's injured honour. Set beside
  Augustine's lemma *iudica causam **meam***, this makes the Introit's and
  Gradual's *causam tuam* a reading with an argued patristic defence, and a
  substantive one.
- **v. 23 — the `quaerentium te` / `inimicorum tuorum` split explained (col.
  1465).** Theodoret's lemma is "**Μὴ ἐπιλάθῃ τῆς φωνῆς τῶν ἱκετῶν σου**" —
  *thy suppliants*, which is what the chant's *quaerentium te* and Augustine's
  *deprecantium te* render, and what Cassiodorus's lemma reproduces. Migne's
  apparatus (fn. 2, read from the page image in the parallel pass) records the
  whole textual situation in one line: "**Ἱκετῶν. cod. 1 οἰκετῶν. Hebr. צרריך,
  inimicorum tuorum. Aqu. ἐχθρῶν. Symm. πολεμίων.**" So the Clementine's
  *inimicorum tuorum* is the Hebrew, followed by Aquila and Symmachus, while
  the Septuagint's *suppliants* is what the Old Latin, Augustine, Cassiodorus
  and the Introit all sing. The divergence at Introit no. 4 in
  `propers/verified.md` is not a chant liberty at all: **it is the Septuagint
  against the Hebrew.** Theodoret's own gloss: "τῷ ἑαυτῶν προσώπῳ τὴν τῶν
  πολεμίων ἀλαζονείαν συνέζευξαν, **οὐ δι' ἑαυτούς, ἀλλὰ δι' ἐκείνους**, φειδοῦς
  τινος ἀξιωθῆναι παρακαλοῦντες."
- **Ps. 89:1 (cols. 1597–1601).** The lemma is **dative** — "**Προσευχὴ τῷ
  Μωσεῖ ἀνθρώπῳ τοῦ Θεοῦ**," *a prayer for Moses*, not *of Moses* — and that
  dative is what makes his second option grammatically possible: "Τινὲς μὲν τῶν
  συγγραφέων τὸν μέγαν ἔφασαν Μωσῆν τήνδε ποιῆσαι τὴν προσευχήν· **τινὲς δὲ
  συγγραφέα αὐτῆς εἶπον τὸν μακάριον Δαβὶδ εἶναι, προσαρμόσαι δὲ αὐτὴν Μωσῇ τῷ
  θεσπεσίῳ**." He reports both without deciding; a bracketed (hence
  text-critically suspect) addition pushes the Davidic view. He then declares
  the psalm to have a **double oracle** (`διπλῆν … χρησμολογίαν`), one about
  the calamities of the Jews and one about the corruptibility of human nature
  at large, both ending in a change for the better.
- **`ἐν γενεᾷ καὶ γενεᾷ`, expounded as two scopes of history, not two dates.**
  For the Jews it is enumerated as a succession of rescues — "οὐκ ἐν Αἰγύπτῳ
  μόνον, ἀλλὰ καὶ ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ καὶ ἐπὶ Ἰησοῦ τοῦ Ναυῆ, καὶ ἐπὶ Γεδεών, Βαράκ τε
  καὶ Ἰεφθάε, καὶ Σαμψών, καὶ Σαμουήλ, καὶ Δαβίδ." For **the whole human race**
  ("Πᾶσα δὲ τῶν ἀνθρώπων ἡ φύσις τούτοις κέχρηται τοῖς λόγοις") it is a second
  and wider list: Enoch translated; Noah preserved as "**σπινθῆρα τῷ γένει**," a
  spark for the race, when all had embraced a bestial life; and then — the one
  place at any of these three psalms where Theodoret does reach for the covenant
  with Abraham — "**ὅπως τὸν πατριάρχην Ἀβραὰμ ἐκλεξάμενος, πᾶσιν ὑπέσχετο τὴν
  εὐλογίαν τοῖς ἔθνεσιν**." The point is polemical: against those who say the
  Lord came to the Gentiles out of a change of mind (`ἐκ μεταμελείας`), the
  refuge "in generation and generation" proves he always provided for all. The
  Abrahamic promise-to-the-nations that Gal. 3:16 argues from is thus in
  Theodoret **at the Alleluia's verse, not at the Introit's**.
- **Ps. 30:15–16 (cols. 1081–1084) — the `tempora` reading, in Greek, with the
  variant named.** Verified in both transmissions:

  > "«Ἐγὼ δὲ ἐπὶ σέ, Κύριε, ἤλπισα· εἶπον, Σὺ εἶ ὁ Θεός μου. Ἐν ταῖς χερσί σου
  > οἱ κλῆροί μου.» Ἐκεῖνοι μὲν οὖν ἀθροισθέντες τὸν ἐμὸν καττύουσι θάνατον,
  > ἐγὼ δέ σε Θεὸν οἶδα καὶ πρύτανιν. Τοῦτο γὰρ λέγει· «Ἐν ταῖς χερσί σου οἱ
  > κλῆροί μου», **ἢ ὡς οἱ λοιποί, ἐν ταῖς χερσί σου οἱ καιροί μου**, ἀντὶ τοῦ,
  > **Σὺ ἀποκληροῖς ὡς ἐθέλεις ἑκάστῳ καὶ ἀθυμίας καὶ θυμηδίας, καὶ μεταβάλλεις
  > ταῦτα πάλιν πρὸς τὸ δοκοῦν. Καιροὺς γάρ, ἢ κλήρους, καλεῖ τὰς τῶν πραγμάτων
  > μεταβολάς**, πλοῦτον καὶ πενίαν, δουλείαν καὶ δεσποτείαν, εἰρήνην καὶ
  > πόλεμον, καὶ τἄλλα τὰ τοιαῦτα ὁμοίως."

  Theodoret cites "**my times**" as the reading of **οἱ λοιποί** — "the rest of
  the translators," i.e. Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion against the
  Septuagint — and then **refuses to choose**, expounding the verse on the two
  together: *times, or lots*, means the vicissitudes of circumstance — wealth
  and poverty, slavery and mastery, peace and war. The gloss `Σὺ ἀποκληροῖς ὡς
  ἐθέλεις ἑκάστῳ` puns on *κλῆρος* while carrying the sense of *καιρός*. **This
  is the one ancient witness who holds both of the Offertory's readings in view
  at once**, and it supplies the guide with a patristic warrant for preaching
  *tempora mea* without discarding Augustine's *sortes*.

### Robert Bellarmine, *Explanatio in Psalmos* — and what O'Sullivan's English leaves out

Two texts were opened and both were read **on page images**, not merely in OCR.

- **Latin, the Rome first edition:** *Explanatio in Psalmos*, "AVCTORE ROBERTO
  BELLARMINO … ROMAE, Apud Bartholomaeum Zannettum, ANNO M.DC.XI" (engraved
  title page read; the year line is partly obscured by the Florence library
  stamp, and 1611 is confirmed from the catalogue and the Paul V privilege).
  archive.org item `ita-bnc-mag-00002877-001` (Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale di
  Firenze, shelfmark CFMAGL 2.6.552; `_djvu.txt` 3,886,864 bytes, but **the OCR
  of this folio is unusable** — the target phrases are not findable in it, and
  every quotation below was transcribed from the page image). The images carry
  a ProQuest "Early European Books" notice; the 1611 **text** is public domain.
  Long *ſ* silently expanded; *v/u* and *j/i* left as printed.
- **English, public domain and quotable:** *A Commentary on the Book of Psalms*,
  translated by **Archdeacon John O'Sullivan, P.P. of Kenmare and V.G. of
  Kerry**, 1866; archive.org item `commentaryonpsal0000bell` (`_djvu.txt`
  2,731,137 bytes; Graduate Theological Union copy, shelfmark BS 1430 .B44
  1866). **The title page is not in this scan**, so no imprint could be read off
  the page; the translator's identity rests on Bishop David Moriarty's
  approbation at p. v, which names him. **On the imprint the repository already
  has the answer, and it corrects the brief:** this guide's own registered
  record `edition.robert-bellarmine.commentary-on-the-book-of-psalms.osullivan-duffy-1866`
  gives the publication as "**Dublin and London: James Duffy, 1866**" — not
  M. H. Gill — and notes the same defect ("Its first surviving leaves are
  damaged or incomplete around the title, so the publication identity was
  cross-checked against the National Library of Ireland catalog"). Note also
  that the repository's registered facsimile
  (`…commons-ia-facsimile-78b290d4`, 56,448,883 bytes, 482 pages) is a Commons
  copy of a **different** Internet Archive item, `commentaryonbook0000bell`,
  whose printed pagination it describes as damaged; the printed page numbers
  cited below come from the `commentaryonpsal0000bell` scan and have **not**
  been reconciled against the registered artifact's PDF-page ranges.

**The load-bearing caveat, and it is severe.** O'Sullivan cut out exactly the
material this Sunday needs. Moriarty's approbation, p. v: the Archdeacon "has
undertaken to translate this Commentary, **omitting those portions which are
purely philological, or which relate to the discrepancy and reconciliation of
the texts and versions**." O'Sullivan's own preface, same page: the original "is
encumbered with endless disquisitions on Hebrew roots, and different versions
and readings"; and "the Cardinal's **prefaces to the several Psalms** … I have,
therefore, **substituted the simple, substantial headings in the Douay**, for
the elaborate and learned disquisitions of the Cardinal." So Bellarmine's
*Titulus & Argumentum* — where he settles the psalm's occasion — and all his
variant notes are **absent from the only public-domain English**. The guide can
quote O'Sullivan for the exposition and must quote the 1611 Latin for
everything textual. (O'Sullivan also renumbers: his Ps. 30 verses 14 and 15 are
Vulgate 15 and 16; Bellarmine's Latin numbers Ps. 73:20 as *XXI*.)

*On Ps. 73:*

- **Occasion: Antiochus IV, against Augustine and Theodoret — and argued.**
  Latin, p. 529 (page number and heading `PSALMVS SEPTVAGESIMVS TERTIVS. /
  Titulus, & Argumentum Psalmi. / Intellectus Asaph.` read on the image):

  > "**In hoc Psalmo praedicitur, & deploratur desolatio Hierosolymae** … Quaestio
  > est inter expositores, an Psalmus intelligi debeat de persecutione
  > Nabuchodonosor, an Antiochi, an Titi, & Vespasiani, an Diocletiani &
  > Maximiani, an Antichristi … **Sed verior sententia omnibus consideratis
  > videtur eorum, qui hunc Psalmum exponunt de persecutione Antiochi
  > illustris, de quo agitur in libris Machabaeorum** … In tempus Titi, &
  > Vespasiani omnia conuenire videntur … & ideò hanc expositionem amplexi sunt
  > S. Augustinus, Theodoretus, & Euthymius. Sed verba huius Psalmi non
  > conueniunt Iudaeis perfidis, qui non erant oues pascuae Dei … **neque
  > Ecclesia frequentaret hunc Psalmum, si contineret voces infidelium
  > Iudaeorum**. quare improbabilis est expositio Theodoreti, & Euthymij, qui
  > verba Psalmi tribuunt populo Iudaico."

  Two things here matter to the guide. **(a)** He says the psalm *foretells*
  (`praedicitur`) the desolation, so it is prophecy for him too — but of the
  Maccabean persecution, not of AD 70. **(b)** His decisive reason for
  rejecting the patristic Roman reading is **liturgical**: the Church would not
  sing this psalm if it were the voice of unbelieving Jews, and the psalm calls
  its speakers *the sheep of thy pasture* and *the souls that confess to thee*.
  A Counter-Reformation commentator rejects Augustine and Theodoret **because
  of how the Roman Rite uses the Introit's own psalm.** He also concedes the
  hard verse: at v. 7 (English, p. 224) "This seems to be the only passage
  forbidding the application of this Psalm to the persecution of Antiochus …
  for, though the entire temple was burnt and razed down by Nabuchodonozor and
  by Titus, and was not burnt by Antiochus; still the gates of the temple were
  burned by him."
- **v. 1 (English, p. 223 — quotable).** "The holy prophet, speaking in the
  person of the Jewish people, deplores that universal calamity that was
  inflicted on them by the gentiles, **under king Antiochus, who was called
  Epiphanes** … The words, '*thou hast cast us off unto the end*,' do not imply
  that God, in reality, by a fixed decree, cast off his people for ever; but
  that the people thought he had done so … for, when he says in a subsequent
  verse of this same Psalm, '*and forget not the souls of thy poor*,' **he shews
  that he was not at all so sure of their having been cast off**." The Latin
  (p. 529–530) adds a note O'Sullivan cut: "*Iratus est furor tuus*, in hebraeo
  eleganti metaphora dicitur, **Fumauit nasus tuus**, quam metaphoram recte
  explicauerunt Septuaginta Interpretes."
- **v. 19 (English, p. 225 — quotable).** "**This verse should decide on
  Antiochus being the one the Psalmist had in view.** It was in the persecution
  of Antiochus that such prayer was most appropriate; because he never succeeded
  in entirely annihilating them, as did Nabuchodonosor, Titus, and Vespasian.
  '*Deliver not up to beasts*,' to cruel men as ferocious as beasts … '*and
  forget not, to the end, the souls of thy poor*;' forget not to defend and
  deliver the souls of your poor, **who daily beg at the gate of your mercy**."
  The Latin at p. 538 adds the Hebrew note (cut in English): the Seventy read
  *thodecha* where the Masoretic has *thoreca*, an easy scribal confusion of
  *resh* and *daleth*, "neque est causa, cur anteponamus textum, quem nunc
  habemus, textui, quem habuerunt Septuaginta Interpretes."
- **v. 20, `Respice in testamentum tuum` — Bellarmine's answer is the Old
  Covenant, and it is territorial.** English, p. 225 (quotable):

  > "He brings forward two other arguments to appease God; one **from the
  > covenant entered into with the fathers** … '*Have regard to thy covenant.*'
  > If you will not regard us, regard, at least, **thy covenant or testament
  > which you made with our fathers, that you would be a God to us, and that you
  > would protect and defend us in the land you gave us**."

  Latin, p. 538 (verse `XXI`, read on the image): "Adducit duo alia argumenta ad
  placandum Deum, **vnum ex parte foederis initi cum Patribus** … saltem respice
  **pactum, siue testamentum quod fecisti cum Patribus nostris, vt esses nobis
  in Deum, nosque protegeres, atque defenderes in terra, quam nobis dedisti**."

  **He makes no connection to Christ, to the New Covenant or to the kingdom of
  heaven at this verse, and he does not name Abraham here** (he says *Patres*;
  Abraham is named earlier, at v. 2, on *virga hereditatis* — "the land you
  promised to Abraham and his descendants" — which is the land-promise, not the
  *testamentum*). This is a **flat contradiction of Augustine, Cassiodorus and
  the Glossa** at the same clause, and it is the single most useful disagreement
  in the file: the Latin patristic tradition reads *thy testament* as the new
  one; the greatest Counter-Reformation commentator reads it as the old one.
- **v. 22 — and he corrects Augustine's text.** English, p. 225–226: "the
  people's cause is God's cause, for the reproaches heaped upon them actually
  fall on God himself … '*Arise, O God, judge thy own cause*,' defend it; **the
  Hebrew has, plead your own cause**." Latin, p. 539 (verse `XXIII`), the part
  O'Sullivan cut: "in hebraeo est ad verbum, **Litiga litem tuam** … tamen quia
  Deus non potest litigare nisi apud tribunal iustitiae suae, cum non habeat
  superiorem … proinde rectè versio Septuaginta, & vulgatae editionis habet,
  iudica causam tuam. **S. Augustinus legit, Iudica causam meam: sed nescio quid
  secutus sit: nam hebraeus, & graecus codex habet secundam personam, & sic
  inuenitur in latinis libris.**" So Bellarmine noticed exactly the Augustinian
  lemma recorded above and judged it unsupported — a second, independent
  vindication of the chant's *causam tuam*.
- **v. 23 — he adjudicates the `quaerentium`/`inimicorum` split the other way
  from the chant.** O'Sullivan (p. 226) prints "*Forget not the voices of thy
  enemies*" with no note. The Latin, p. 539 (verse `XXIV`), has the note:
  "**Illud, inimicorum, in graecis libris est, seruorum, siue supplicum tuorum;
  & sic etiam legit S. Augustinus. Sed in hebraeo est, inimicorum**, & sic etiam
  vertisse Septuaginta Interpretes testatur S. Hieronymus in epist. ad Suniam,
  & Fretellam: **proinde necesse est in textibus graecis irrepsisse errorem**, &
  positum esse οἰκετῶν, pro, ἐχθρῶν." Bellarmine therefore knows the reading the
  Introit sings, attributes it to Augustine and the Greek books, and **rejects
  it as a corruption** — reading, as Migne's apparatus independently records, the
  variant `οἰκετῶν` rather than `ἱκετῶν`. The Sunday's Introit sings the reading
  Bellarmine judged secondary; this is worth saying, and worth saying calmly.

*On Ps. 89:* — **Bellarmine denies Mosaic authorship, and the English does not
show it.** O'Sullivan gives only the Douay heading at p. 277 and calls the
author "David" throughout (e.g. p. 279). The Latin, pp. 665–666 (read on the
images), argues it out: Jerome *Ep. ad Cyprianum* held Moses wrote this psalm
and the ten following; Augustine denied it, because it is not in the Pentateuch
with his other canticles; "**Addere possumus aliam rationem; quoniam in hoc
Psalmo definitur vita hominis, vt plurimum, annis septuaginta, & in
robustioribus, annis octoginta: at Mosis tempore plurimi centesimum annum
transcendebant**" — Moses died at 120, was 80 at the call, Aaron 83 at his
consecration, and men so near death would not have been chosen. Conclusion:
"**Quare videtur nobis, titulum huius Psalmi inscribi Moysi, quòd Mosem
loquentem Dauid introducat**. Introducitur autem Moses fortasse, quòd ipse
scripserit creationem Mundi, peccatum primorum parentum, & poenas inde
secutas." The psalm's *argumentum* is "**oratio ad Deum pro humano genere**."
On **v. 1** the English is quotable and good (p. 278): "he that seeks for fresh
favours can make use of no argument so convincing as the shewing himself
grateful for the past … not once or twice, but always, '*from generation to
generation.*' **The Hebrew for refuge signifies a well-fortified house, placed
on an eminence, the tenants of which are quite secure from their enemies, from
beasts, from flood, and from storms**" — and those who take refuge in God
"dwell as they would in a city fortified by faith, hope, and charity," which is
this Sunday's Collect triad arriving at the Alleluia's verse. Latin, p. 666, has
the Hebrew word O'Sullivan dropped: "Vox hebraica, *mahon*, propriè
habitationem significat."

*On Ps. 30:15–16 — the `tempora mea` question, and the strongest single answer
in this file:*

- **English: a clean negative.** O'Sullivan, p. 78, numbering the verses 14 and
  15: "'*My lots are in thy hands* …' **My life does not depend on lot or
  chance, but depends on your will and power.** '*Deliver me out of the hands of
  my enemies.*' The meaning is quite plain, and needs no explanation." No
  mention of *tempora* whatever; the translator has visibly compressed
  Bellarmine's note and cut its philological middle.
- **Latin: a full discussion, and a liturgical witness.** p. 180 (verse `XVIII`;
  page number and text read on the image):

  > "Nam, in manibus tuis sortes meae, id est, **sortes vitae, vel mortis meae
  > non pendent à sorte, sed à manu tua**, id est, à potentia & voluntate tua.
  > **In hebraeo habetur, in manibus tuis tempora mea**; & fortasse quod nunc
  > legimus in graeco, κλῆροι, quod significat sortes, **legendum esset,
  > καιροί, quod significat tempora**. nam **non desunt antiquae versiones ex
  > graeco, quae habeant, tempora mea. Sic certè legitur in officio Missae non
  > semel, & in Psalterio Ecclesiae S. Petri Romae.** Sensus autem est idem.
  > nam, in manibus tuis tempora mea, nihil est aliud, nisi à tua voluntate
  > pendent tempora vitae, & mortis meae, **non viuam, nisi quamdiu tu volueris,
  > neque moriar, nisi quando tu volueris**."

  This is the answer to the Offertory's textual puzzle from the inside of the
  Roman Rite. Bellarmine (i) reports the Hebrew as *tempora mea*; (ii) conjectures
  that the Greek κλῆροι should be καιροί; (iii) states that the reading *tempora
  mea* is found in ancient Latin versions from the Greek, **"and so it is
  certainly read more than once in the Office of the Mass, and in the Psalter of
  the Church of St Peter at Rome"** — i.e. he names the Mass propers themselves
  and the Vatican basilica's psalter as the witnesses for the very word the
  Offertory sings; and (iv) holds that the sense is the same either way.
  Bellarmine's `Psalterium Ecclesiae S. Petri Romae` is the *Psalterium Romanum*
  by another name; that identification is standard but is **not** asserted here
  as verified, since no Roman-Psalter witness has been collated (see leads).

### Glossa ordinaria on the Psalter — medieval reuse, and it sides with the Fathers

Read in the *Glossae Scripturae Sacrae electronicae* (Gloss-e, IRHT-CNRS, ed.
Martin Morard), whose **Psalter coverage is complete** (Pss. 1–151), at
`gloss-e.irht.cnrs.fr/php/editions_chapitre.php?id=liber&numLivre=26&chapitre=26_73`
and the parallel `26_89`, `26_30` (289,132 / 178,734 / 271,271 bytes). The
edition keys each gloss to the Erfurt witness and the Rusch facsimile (e.g. at
Ps. 30:16, `{t. 2: Erfurt, f. 245rb; facsim., p. 489b}`); **the Rusch facsimile
itself was not opened**, and those markers are reported as Gloss-e's own
references. The parallel retrieval pass corroborated three of the glosses in the
archive.org scan of **PL 113** (`patrologiaecur113mign`, `_djvu.txt` 4,246,823
bytes), reading the running heads on the page images at cols. 885/886, 961/962
and 995/996; those column numbers were **not** read by the author of this file.
Classification, as with the Pauline Gloss in `research/epistle-galatians-3.md`:
**reuse** — a mosaic of Augustine and Cassiodorus — but the mosaic is decisive
for the Introit.

- **Ps. 73:20 — the Glossa states the New-Covenant reading as the plain sense.**
  Interlinear on *Respice in testamentum tuum*: "**non vetus de terrenis sed
  tuum quod promisisti regnum celorum**." Marginal, AUGUSTINUS: "Reliquie salve
  fient quia multi qui tendunt ad terram obscurati sunt et repleti terra."
  Marginal, CASSIODORUS: "**Imple que promisisti ut testamentum novum quod est
  de eternis constituas. Meminit promissionis quia merita deficiunt** …" That
  second sentence is Cassiodorus's own argument, compressed to eight words, and
  it is the sentence the Sunday needs: *he remembers the promise because the
  merits fail*.
- **Ps. 73, the historical setting: Titus and Vespasian.** Marginal on v. 1 *in
  finem*: "Nec templo parcitur. Interitum et captivitatem significat que a Tito
  et Vespasiano." Interlinear on v. 4: "tunc eversa est Hierusalem quando
  sollemnitas agebatur paschalis in qua crucifixerunt Dominum"; on v. 5,
  "**vexilla romana**," and AUGUSTINUS "**Aquilas**"; on v. 8, "**Romani**."
  The title gloss carries the covenant frame: Asaph laments "intelligens
  dicernensque terrena a celestibus **vetus testamentum a novo** ut scias quid
  ex his eligas," and CASSIODORUS "Monet relictis figuris transire ad
  veritatem." The Glossa also classes the psalm as the first of three
  lamentations (with 78 and 136) and as spoken "**nondum eversa necdum visa
  captivitate**" — prophecy, of AD 70. Every point at which Bellarmine departs
  from the Fathers, the Glossa stays with them.
- **Ps. 73:23 — Gloss-e's apparatus records the very split** the chant sits on:
  `inimicorum` (Ps-G, with Symmachus ἐχθρῶν) against `querentium` (Ps-R, with
  the LXX ἱκετῶν), citing Jerome *Ep.* 106 §46. **This is the file's one
  explicit statement that `quaerentium te` is the *Psalterium Romanum* reading**
  — reported here as Gloss-e's apparatus, since no Roman-Psalter witness was
  collated directly.
- **Ps. 30:16 — the Glossa knows both readings too.** Marginal: "**SORTES.
  CASSIODORUS. Vel tempora vite. AUGUSTINUS. quia quando vis vivimus vel morimur
  ideo eripe.**" Then Augustine's grace-reading in compressed form: "ad tunicam
  Domini inconsutilem … sorte perveni id est gratia electionis. Sors non est
  aliquid mali sed res in humana dubietate divinam indicans voluntatem. **Sors
  hic est gratia que sine meritis quos vult eligit.**" Interlinear on *in
  manibus tuis*: "in tua potestate"; on *sortes mee*: "Causa et ordo electionis
  mee hominibus incognitus." So the standard medieval Psalter gloss holds the
  Offertory's two readings side by side — *lots, or the times of life* — and
  attributes the *tempora* alternative to **Cassiodorus**, exactly where this
  file found it.
- **Ps. 89:1 — Augustine's two-generations reading, transmitted whole.**
  Interlinear on the title: Moses "qui fuit **minister Veteris Testamenti et
  propheta Novi Testamenti** secundum quam dispensationem iste psalmus
  inspiciendus est **non quod Moyses eum conscripserit** sed quia in hoc psalmo
  tractantur que in Veteri Testamento per Moysen dato continentur." Interlinear
  on v. 1, AUGUSTINUS: "id est **in omni generatione vel veteri cuius ille
  minister et nova cuius ille propheta**" — Augustine's alternative preserved
  verbatim as the schoolroom gloss. Marginal, CASSIODORUS: "Primo eternum
  proponit refugium temporali homini." Note that the Glossa denies Mosaic
  authorship for Augustine's reason and Bellarmine denies it for his own; the
  two negatives are independent.

## Liturgical reception

### Guéranger, *The Liturgical Year*, Time after Pentecost II (tr. Shepherd, Duffy 1900, vol. 11)

Thirteenth Sunday, printed pp. 307–326; read in the OCR of archive.org item
`V11TheLiturgicalYear` (1,169,523 bytes), the repository's held edition
`english-duffy-1900-volume-11`. Public domain and quotable.

- **The Introit is put in the mouth of the just of the old law, and tied to
  the Gospel by *forgetting*** (p. 308): "Now that she is in possession of the
  promises so long waited for by the world, the Church loves to repeat the
  words wherewith the just men of the old law used to express their sentiments
  … There is a danger, however … it is the danger of forgetting all these
  blessings. **Ingratitude is the necessary outcome of forgetfulness, and
  to-day's Gospel justly condemns it.** On this account, the Epistle, and here
  our Introit, remind us of the time when man had nothing to cheer him but
  hope: a promise had, indeed, been made to him of **a sublime covenant** which
  was, at some distant future, to be realized; but, meanwhile, **he was very
  poor, was a prey to the wiles of satan, his cause was to be tried by divine
  justice**, and yet he prayed for loving mercy." The three petitions of the
  Introit — the poor, the beasts, the cause — are read off in order.
- **The Gradual, and the covenant referred to Abraham, via Rupert** (p. 316):
  "It is on this account that the Gradual repeats the supplication of the
  Introit: *Respice Domine, in testamentum tuum*; for, as **Rupert** observes,
  it was the cry of the ancient people, who, sighing at the weakness of the
  powerless Law of Sinai, **besought God to fulfil the covenant He had promised
  to Abraham's faith**. They cried out to Christ, as the poor creature might
  have done to the good Samaritan, after he had seen the priest and the levite
  pass him by." Guéranger's footnote gives the locus precisely: **"Rup., *De
  Div. Off.*, xii. 13."** Rupert of Deutz's own text (PL 170) was **not**
  opened; the citation is recorded here so the lead can be run down (it is the
  same lead left open in `research/epistle-galatians-3.md` and
  `research/gospel-luke-17.md`, now with a book-and-chapter reference).
- **Material negative within Guéranger:** he prints the Alleluia (*Domine,
  refugium factus es nobis, a generatione et progenie*, p. 317) and the
  Offertory (*In te speravi, Domine … in manibus tuis tempora mea*, p. 325)
  **without a word of comment on either**. The paragraph that follows the
  Offertory is about the oblation and the Secret, not about the psalm.

### Schuster, *The Sacramentary* (Liber Sacramentorum) vol. III (Burns Oates & Washbourne, 1927)

Thirteenth Sunday ("Tertia post Sancti Laurentii"), printed pp. 132–135; read
in the OCR of the repository's held archive.org item `LiberSacramentorum`,
vol. 3 text file (1,149,699 bytes). US public domain (1927); may remain
protected in life+70 jurisdictions — quoted in short compass.

- **Introit** (p. 132): "The cause of the Christian people is the cause of
  God … In spite, however, of this widespread inheritance of hate, the final
  victory will always remain with Christ and his faithful followers, **for God
  promised this to the Patriarchs of old** … It seems, at times, as though God
  had forgotten his Church, and that is when our sins have placed a kind of
  barrier between him and us."
- **Gradual — the clearest statement anywhere in the reception that the
  *testamentum* pleaded is the covenant with the Patriarchs** (pp. 133–134):
  "The Gradual is derived from the same psalm as the Introit — the
  seventy-third — and is common also to the **Thursday after the Fourth Sunday
  in Lent**. **It recalls to God the promises made to the Patriarchs, and draws
  his attention to their merits**, which, in a certain measure, cover our own
  unworthiness. In other words, we say thus to God: 'O Lord, we have not
  deserved thy grace, but grant it to us, **as an inheritance due to us because
  of thy promises to our Fathers the Patriarchs of old** … We have no personal
  merits which can avail us, but their merits are also ours, since we are their
  sons and their heirs. **Our cause is their cause, therefore it is thine**.'"
  This is the *Respice in testamentum tuum* → Abraham link stated outright, and
  it is the same move Cassiodorus makes ("non erant bona quae … commemorare

## The chants' psalter, against the Fathers' lemmata

`propers/verified.md` collates the appointed chants against the Clementine and
records twelve divergences without asserting a source. The witnesses read for
this file supply, for the first time, **patristic lemmata that agree with the
chant where the Clementine does not** — and, equally important, places where
they do not. Every cell below is a lemma actually read in the text cited above,
not an inference.

Two later witnesses belong beside that table rather than in it, because they
report readings instead of carrying them: **Bellarmine** (1611) prints *sortes
meae* as his lemma but records the Hebrew as *tempora mea* and names the Mass
and St Peter's psalter for it; the **Glossa ordinaria** prints *sortes* with the
marginal alternative "Vel tempora vite," and its Ps. 73:23 apparatus names
`querentium` as the Ps-R reading against Ps-G's `inimicorum`.

**What this shows.** Cassiodorus's psalter agrees with the appointed chants at
four of the divergences the Clementine collation flagged (Ps. 73:22 *Domine …
causam tuam*; Ps. 73:23 *quaerentium te*; Ps. 89:1 *a generatione et progenie*;
Ps. 30:15–16 *dixi, tu es Deus meus … in manibus tuis tempora mea*), and
disagrees at two (Ps. 73:1 *oves gregis tui*; no *Domine* at Ps. 73:20).
Augustine agrees at Ps. 30:15 and stands with the chant's *sense* at Ps. 73:23
(*deprecantium* for *quaerentium*, both against the Clementine's *enemies*),
but not at Ps. 73:22 or Ps. 89:1. That the chants therefore descend from the
*Psalterium Romanum* rather than the Gallican is the obvious hypothesis and the
one a specialist would test; **this record does not assert it**, no *Psalterium
Romanum* witness having been collated here or in `propers/verified.md`. Two
second-hand pointers were found and are worth having: **Gloss-e's apparatus at
Ps. 73:23 assigns `querentium` to Ps-R and `inimicorum` to Ps-G**, and
**Bellarmine, defending *tempora mea* at Ps. 30:16, names as his witnesses "the
Office of the Mass, more than once, and the Psalter of the Church of St Peter at
Rome"** (1611, p. 180). Both point where the lemmata point; neither is a
collation.

**The `tempora`/`sortes` question, answered.** The brief asked whether any
witness commenting on the Latin knows a *tempora* reading. **Cassiodorus does
not merely know it: it is his lemma, and his whole exposition of the verse
turns on it** — the assumed humanity "et tempori subiacebat et morti," and the
*tempora vitae suae* placed in the Father's power. Independently, **Jerome's
*Psalterium iuxta Hebraeos* reads `in manu tua tempora mea`**, verified in
Lagarde's edition (archive.org `psalteriumiuxta00lagagoog`,
`_djvu.txt` 470,784 bytes; the surrounding verses read "ego autem in te
speravi, domine: dixi 'deus meus es tu': in manu tua tempora mea. libera me de
manu inimicorum meorum et persequentium me"). The appointed Offertory is thus a
hybrid of the two traditions — the Septuagint/Old-Latin **plural** *in manibus
tuis* with the Hebrew **tempora** — which is exactly Cassiodorus's text and not
exactly Jerome's. **Bellarmine reached the same conclusion in 1611** and
explained the mechanism: the Greek κλῆροι should perhaps be read καιροί, and
*tempora mea* is what the Mass and St Peter's psalter actually sing. The Glossa
ordinaria carries the alternative as a marginal gloss, attributed to
Cassiodorus: "SORTES. CASSIODORUS. **Vel tempora vite.**" And in Greek,
Theodoret cites both and refuses to choose. So the Offertory's *tempora* is
attested continuously — Old Latin/Roman Psalter, Cassiodorus, the Glossa,
Bellarmine — and the reading the Clementine prints is the one that had to be
defended, not the one the Roman Rite sings. Augustine, in all four of his expositions, has *sortes*, and
his celebrated reading of the verse (*sors* = unmerited grace and election)
belongs to a word the Roman Mass does not sing.

## Cross-cutting findings

1. **`Respice in testamentum tuum` has three answers in the reception, plus a
   silence.** The **patristic Latin** line identifies the covenant pleaded with
   the **New** Testament and proves it from Jer. 31:31 — Augustine ("Respice in
   testamentum tuum; non illud vetus … quo promisisti regnum coelorum") and
   Cassiodorus ("ut digneris implere quae cognosceris ante promisisse")
   independently, with the same proof-text — and the **Glossa ordinaria**
   hardens it into the schoolroom gloss ("non vetus de terrenis sed tuum quod
   promisisti regnum celorum"). The **liturgical** commentators (Rupert *apud*
   Guéranger; Schuster) refer the plea to the covenant **promised to Abraham and
   the Patriarchs**. **Bellarmine** reverses the Fathers outright: it is the old
   covenant with the fathers, "vt esses nobis in Deum, nosque protegeres, atque
   defenderes **in terra, quam nobis dedisti**." And the **Greek** expositor at
   the same verse says **nothing at all** about *diatheke* — his printed lemma
   is not even *thy* covenant but *the covenant of thy servants*, an intrusion
   Migne's own apparatus flags, and his Latin column reads *foedus*, not
   *testamentum*. Four positions; the guide should present them as four rather
   than blend them, because the disagreement is the interesting thing.
2. **The Abraham link is real but it sits where one would not expect.** No
   witness read for this file glosses *testamentum* at Ps. 73:20 by Abraham.
   Augustine reaches Abraham at the **opening** of the *Enarratio*, and reaches
   him by quoting **Gal. 3:29** — the last verse of this Sunday's Epistle
   chapter. Theodoret reaches Abraham at **Ps. 89:1**, in the list of what God's
   being "a refuge in generation and generation" has meant for the race, and
   reaches him by the promise of blessing "to all the nations" — Gal. 3:16's own
   proof-text. The covenant thread between Introit and Epistle is therefore
   documented, but it runs through the Fathers' framing of the psalms rather
   than through their comment on the pleaded clause.
3. **The two-generations reading of Ps. 89:1 is a covenant reading, and it is
   unanimous in the Latin witnesses.** Augustine: "sive in omni generatione,
   sive in duabus generationibus, vetere et nova." Jerome: "et in praeterito, et
   in praesenti, et in futuro. Aliter … et in vetere populo Iudaeorum, et in
   nostro Xpistianorum." Cassiodorus: the Moses title is put there "quod duo
   sacramenta vi sui nominis indicaret." All three ground it on Moses as
   *minister Testamenti Veteris et propheta Novi*, and Augustine and Cassiodorus
   both prove it from Jn. 5:46. The Alleluia therefore carries the same
   old-covenant/new-covenant hinge as the Introit, on the authority of three
   independent Latin witnesses.
4. **The Offertory's `tempora mea` is not a chant-book eccentricity.** It is
   Cassiodorus's psalter text and the basis of his exegesis; it is the reading
   of Jerome's *iuxta Hebraeos*; and in Greek it is the reading of *hoi loipoi*
   (Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion) which **Theodoret cites by name and refuses
   to choose against**, expounding *καιροί* and *κλῆροι* together as "the
   vicissitudes of circumstance." Augustine's magnificent *sortes = gratia*
   exposition belongs to the other reading and should be introduced as such.
5. **Four witnesses read Ps. 73 as being about the Roman destruction of
   Jerusalem; they differ about whether it is prophecy — and the two later
   authorities split.** Theodoret: prophecy,
   argued by eliminating Antiochus and Babylon, with **Titus named** and the
   Passover timing. Cassiodorus: prophecy — "futuras clades … et sic dolere
   ventura, quasi iam probentur esse suscepta" — with **Vespasian and Titus
   named**. Augustine: the sack is treated as accomplished fact, the *signa
   Romana* identified and Jn. 11:48 applied, the speaker being the Synagogue
   that has come to understanding. The **Glossa ordinaria** keeps the patristic line
   ("Interitum et captivitatem significat que a Tito et Vespasiano," *vexilla
   romana*, *aquilas*) and reads it as spoken "nondum eversa." **Jerome
   dissents** one way: in the *Commentarioli* the standard-planters at v. 5 are
   "Babylonii." **Bellarmine dissents the other way, and most sharply**: the
   psalm foretells the **Maccabean** persecution under Antiochus, and he rejects
   the Titus reading *because the Church sings this psalm* — "neque Ecclesia
   frequentaret hunc Psalmum, si contineret voces infidelium Iudaeorum." That is
   a liturgical argument against a patristic consensus, made about this Sunday's
   own Introit, and it deserves quoting rather than smoothing away. The Asaph
   title is read as
   *Synagoga/Congregatio* by Augustine, Cassiodorus and (at v. 18) Theodoret
   alike; none of them raises the modern question of a later Asaphite guild.
6. **The Introit's most-questioned readings turn out to be Septuagint readings
   with patristic lemmata behind them — and Bellarmine, who knew it, voted the
   other way.** `voces quaerentium te` is the LXX
   ἱκετῶν, carried by Augustine (*deprecantium*), Cassiodorus (*quaerentium*)
   and Theodoret (ἱκετῶν) — the Clementine's *inimicorum tuorum* is the Hebrew,
   which Migne's apparatus assigns to Aquila (ἐχθρῶν) and Symmachus (πολεμίων).
   `Exsurge, Domine … causam tuam` is Cassiodorus's lemma exactly, and
   Theodoret's note on the pronoun is an explicit defence of *tuam* against
   *meam* — the very form Augustine's Old Latin has, and the very form Bellarmine
   later pronounced unsupported ("nescio quid secutus sit"). Two of the
   collation's twelve divergences are thus not chant liberties but transmission
   history. Bellarmine, at Ps. 73:23, knew the *supplicum* reading, attributed
   it to Augustine and the Greek books, and judged it a corruption of ἐχθρῶν —
   so the Roman Rite goes on singing, in this Introit, the reading its own
   greatest controversialist thought secondary.
7. **Leprosy at these psalms: an unexpected positive, and a clean Greek
   negative.** Theodoret never uses the word λεπρ- anywhere in the whole
   *Interpretatio in Psalmos* (see negatives). But Augustine **and** Cassiodorus
   both expound **Ps. 73:11** by Moses' leprous hand (Ex. 4:6–7), and Augustine's
   gloss — "Albor enim in cute, lepra est, non candor … redeat ad colorem,
   agnoscat salvatorem" — is the same colour-theology of leprosy he applies to
   this Sunday's Gospel in *Quaestiones Evangeliorum* II.40. Cassiodorus turns
   the hand restored to the bosom into a Rom. 11 hope: "quando gens Iudaeorum
   pro maxima parte creditura est." The verse is **outside the appointed
   centonisation**, and the guide must say so whenever it uses this.
8. **Thanksgiving-for-healing is absent.** No witness read for this file
   connects any of the three psalms to the Gospel's *gratias agens*. The one
   Gospel-shaped contact besides the leprosy typology is Augustine's Good
   Samaritan and *Samarites = custos* at **Ps. 30:3** — again outside the
   appointed verses, but inside the Offertory psalm and attached to the word
   *refugium*, which the Alleluia sings.

**Three answers to the Introit's question, not one.** *Testamentum* = the **New**
covenant (Augustine, Cassiodorus, the Glossa); = the **Old** covenant with the
fathers, about the land (Bellarmine); = the covenant **promised to Abraham's
faith** (Rupert *apud* Guéranger, Schuster). And **Theodoret says nothing at
all** — a silence that is itself a finding, since he is the witness an earlier
pass expected to carry the covenant argument.

**Two answers to the Offertory's word, held together by three witnesses.**
*sortes* = grace and election (Augustine, the Glossa); *tempora* = the times of
life and death in God's will (Cassiodorus, Bellarmine, Jerome *iuxta Hebraeos*).
Theodoret, the Glossa and Bellarmine each hold both in view; only Augustine is
committed to *sortes* alone, and only Cassiodorus to *tempora* alone.

## Unverified leads

1. **Rupert of Deutz, *De divinis officiis* XII.13 (PL 170)** — Guéranger's own
   footnote gives the book and chapter (p. 316). This is the single most
   valuable open lead in the whole Sunday: Rupert is the witness who reads the
   entire Office through *Respice in testamentum tuum* and through
   *Samaritanus = custos*, and he has never been opened at first hand for this
   guide. (The same lead stands open in `research/epistle-galatians-3.md` and
   `research/gospel-luke-17.md`, there without the chapter number.)
2. **Jerome, *Epistula* 140 ad Cyprianum** — a full exposition of Ps. 89,
   cited repeatedly in Morin's apparatus to the two homilies. Not opened.
   Whether NPNF 2/6 carries it in public-domain English was not checked.
3. **A *Psalterium Romanum* witness.** Every one of this file's textual findings
   points at it and none of them proves it directly. Two second-hand pointers
   were found and are recorded as pointers: **Bellarmine** names "*officio
   Missae non semel, & in Psalterio Ecclesiae S. Petri Romae*" as reading
   *tempora mea* (1611, p. 180), and **Gloss-e's apparatus** at Ps. 73:23
   assigns `querentium` to **Ps-R** against `inimicorum` in Ps-G. Weber's
   edition (Collectanea Biblica Latina 10, 1953) is in copyright; a pre-1929
   Roman-Psalter witness, or the Gregorian *Graduale* sources, would settle
   whether the chants' text is simply the Roman Psalter, and would turn the
   file's central hypothesis into a fact.
4. **PL column numbers.** None was read from a scan for Augustine (PL 36, 37),
   Cassiodorus (PL 70) or Jerome. For Theodoret the numbers rest on the Aegean
   text's embedded markers, corroborated at four anchors by running heads read
   from page images in the parallel retrieval pass.
5. **The Rusch facsimile of the Glossa** was not opened; Gloss-e's
   `{Erfurt, f. …; facsim., p. …}` markers are its own internal references. The
   PL 113 columns (885/886, 961/962, 995/996) were read from page images in the
   parallel retrieval pass, not by the author of this file.
6. **Jerome, *Ep. ad Sunniam et Fretelam* (= *Ep.* 106), §46**, cited both by
   Bellarmine on Ps. 73:23 and by Gloss-e's apparatus on the same verse. It is
   Jerome's own defence of his psalter readings and would settle the
   `quaerentium`/`inimicorum` history at first hand. Not opened.
7. **A second copy of the 1866 O'Sullivan with its title page**, to read the
   Dublin/M. H. Gill imprint (see negative 11).
8. **Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion at Ps. 30:16** are known here only through
   Theodoret's `οἱ λοιποί` and Migne's apparatus; Field's *Hexapla* (public
   domain) would give the direct citation and was not consulted.
