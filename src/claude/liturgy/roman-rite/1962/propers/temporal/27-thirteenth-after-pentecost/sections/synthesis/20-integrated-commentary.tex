\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

This companion edition does not walk the formulary element by element. It
takes the same evidence and organises it by the six questions the ten
appointed texts put to each other, so that every element is doing work in the
argument even where it has no heading of its own. The complete appointed texts
and the per-element reception sweep are in the canonical full edition; the
loci below are sufficient to check every claim made from them.

\subsection*{One noun, and why it is not a coincidence}

The Introit begs God to look upon his \latin{testaméntum}. The Gradual begs it
again, after the lesson has been read. The Epistle then argues, for seven
verses, about a \latin{testaméntum confirmátum a Deo}. That is a textual
observation about the printed page and nothing more; what makes it an argument
is philological, and Jerome supplies it. Commenting on this very passage, he
records that anyone comparing the Hebrew volumes ``inveniet ubi testamentum
scriptum est, non testamentum sonare, sed pactum, quod Hebraeo sermone dicitur
BERITH.'' One Latin noun therefore carries both things at once --- the covenant
a psalmist begs God to remember and the ratified instrument Paul says cannot be
touched --- and Paul's whole proof works by pressing the legal end of the
ambiguity. Theodoret notices the same convergence from the other side: the
ancient Scripture, he says, itself calls the promise made to Abraham God's
\latin{diatheke}.

The psalm's own commentators had already made that verse the hinge of the whole
psalm, and they had already told the reader which covenant it means. Augustine
begins his exposition of Psalm 73 by quoting this Sunday's Epistle four verses
past where the lesson stops --- ``quia et nos semen Abrahae sumus \dots\ Ergo
semen Abrahae estis, secundum promissionem haeredes'' (Gal. 3:29) --- and at
v.~20 he refuses the old covenant clause by clause: ``Respice in testamentum
tuum; \emph{non illud vetus}: non propter terram Chanaan rogo \dots\ Respice in
testamentum tuum, quo promisisti regnum coelorum,'' with Jeremiah's new covenant
quoted in full. Cassiodorus repeats the structure and adds the sentence that
best explains why this psalm opens this Mass: ``quia non erant bona, quae
delinquentis populi commemorare potuisset, meminit promissionis Dominicae quae
semper impletur'' --- having no merits to plead, the psalmist pleads the
promise. Thirteen centuries later Schuster says the same thing of the Gradual
in almost the same words, and says it of Abraham by name.

The disagreement travels with the agreement, and it runs on the same fault line
as the Epistle's. Theodoret's printed lemma at Psalm 73:20 is corrupt --- it
reads ``the covenant \emph{of thy servants},'' words Migne's own \latin{Variae
Lectiones} flag as absent from both his codices, from the Hebrew and from the
Septuagint --- his note expounds only the verse's second half, and he says
nothing at all about what the covenant is. Migne's facing Latin does not even
render the word with the Introit's noun; it prints \latin{foedus}. The clause
that is the hinge of the psalm for the Latin tradition is passed in silence by
the Antiochene.

And the greatest of the later Latins reverses it. Bellarmine reads the same
clause as a plea to remember ``pactum, siue testamentum quod fecisti cum
Patribus nostris, vt esses nobis in Deum, nosque protegeres, atque defenderes
\emph{in terra, quam nobis dedisti}'' --- the old covenant, and what it promised
was a land. He makes no connection here to Christ or to the kingdom of heaven,
and it is of a piece with his reading of the psalm's occasion, which is not AD
70 at all but the persecution under Antiochus. His reason for rejecting the
patristic reading is worth having in full, because it is a liturgical argument
made against the Fathers about this Sunday's own Introit: the words of the
psalm ``do not suit unbelieving Jews, who were not the sheep of God's pasture
\dots\ neque Ecclesia frequentaret hunc Psalmum, si contineret voces infidelium
Iudaeorum'' --- the Church would not keep singing this psalm if it were the
voice of unbelievers. Four positions on one pleaded noun, then: the new
covenant, the covenant with Abraham, the old covenant about a land, and
silence. The guide prints four.

The Latin commentators develop the will-image with relish. Ambrosiaster turns
it into a doctrine of inheritance --- ``ut post mortem Christi credentes,
haeredes essent fidei Abrahae per testamentum sanguinis Christi'' --- and
charges the judaizers as \latin{quasi reos falsati testamenti}, defendants
accused of forging the instrument. Aquinas states the underlying axiom in one
line: ``nihil humanum tantam firmitatem habet sicut ultima voluntas hominis,''
and the promises are ``quasi testamentum Dei, quia est quaedam ordinatio de
haereditate danda.''

That the liturgy itself heard the chants and the lesson as question and answer
is documented rather than deduced. Guéranger, citing Abbot Rupert, reads the
Gradual's repetition of \latin{Réspice \dots\ in testaméntum tuum} as ``the cry
of the ancient people, who, sighing at the weakness of the powerless Law of
Sinai, besought God to fulfil the covenant He had promised to Abraham's
faith.'' The Collect stands between the plea and the answer and asks for
exactly the capacity the answer requires: the promise is given
\latin{credéntibus}, and the first thing the day asks for is more of the
believing.

\subsection*{Who stands in the middle}

The formulary is unusually preoccupied with intermediaries, and it does not
settle who they are.

Paul's \latin{ordináta per Angelos in manu mediatóris} generated the one real
disagreement in this Sunday's reception. Jerome's mediator is Christ as the
pre-incarnate Word: before the Incarnation he is called \latin{mediator}
without addition, and only afterwards is he \latin{mediator Dei et hominum
homo Christus Jesus}. Augustine's mediator is Christ precisely \textit{as man}
--- ``Mediator ergo inter Deum et Deum esse non posset, quia unus est Deus.''
Chrysostom's is Christ the Giver of the law himself. Ambrosiaster's is Christ
as arbiter of two peoples, ``non est unius, sed duorum,'' cutting away the many
gods from one side and the works of the law from the other. Marius Victorinus,
whose exposition of the rest of this lesson is lost in a lacuna, has Christ
alone joining the members to the heavenly Church. And Theodoret says, without
qualification, that the mediator is \textbf{Moses}: the law was laid down with
angels serving and Moses ministering, ``for Him he called mediator.''

Aquinas refuses the choice and builds a bridge instead. \latin{In manu
mediatoris, id est, in potestate Christi} --- and then: ``Iste mediator
significatus est per Moysen, in cuius manu est lex data,'' citing Deuteronomy
5:5. He prints Ambrosiaster's reading of the angels as human messengers,
Moses and Aaron, beside the literal angelic reading, as alternatives; the
\work{Glossa ordinaria} had already handed both on unresolved. Liturgical
reception then took the Antiochene side without argument: Guéranger describes
the law as made known ``through the mediation of Moses and the angels'' and
contrasts it with a covenant made with Abraham directly.

The Gospel asks the same question in narrative. Ten lepers are sent to
intermediaries --- \latin{Ite, osténdite vos sacerdótibus} --- and are cleansed
before they arrive. Bruno of Segni draws the conclusion the later sacramental
theology would need: they were sent ``non ut sacerdotes eos mundarent, sed ut
mundos eos esse judicarent,'' not that the priests should cleanse them but that
they should judge them clean. Cyril of Alexandria, in the surviving fragments,
reads the same sending as evidential: the priests, ``ever envious of His
glory,'' are made witnesses precisely because they knew the marks of leprosy
and its healing --- ``He sent them to the priests, and with them He sent also
the healing.'' And the Secret, at the altar, says \latin{propitiáre} twice, once
over the people and once over the gifts, so that the oblation never becomes the
sole intermediary: God is asked to be favourable to the offerers before he is
asked to be appeased by what they offer.

\subsection*{Cleansed, and not yet saved}

\latin{Augméntum} is the day's operative noun. The Collect asks for an
increase of faith, hope and charity; the Postcommunion, after Communion, asks
that we may advance \latin{ad redemptiónis ætérnæ \dots\ augméntum}. The pair
is older than the Sunday: both prayers stand in one Mass in Book III of the
Vatican Gelasian and together again in the Gregorian supplement, so the
bracket is a fact about the received formulary rather than a recovered
intention. The Council of Trent, at the sixth session, chapter 10, quotes the
Collect as the Church's own evidence that received justification increases ---
\latin{Hoc vero iustitiae incrementum petit sancta ecclesia, quum orat: Da
nobis, Domine, fidei, spei et caritatis augmentum} --- and the 1863 edition
footnotes the citation to this Sunday. It is the decree's one liturgical
citation in that chapter. Aquinas supplies the theology of the noun without,
so far as this guide's bounded search reached, quoting the prayer: charity
increases by the soul's approach to God, every act of charity disposes to
increase even where it does not increase charity in act, and there is no fixed
terminus in this life.

Between those two petitions the Gospel gives increase an arithmetic. Ten men
ask, obey and are cleansed; nothing in the text takes their cleansing back.
One returns, and what he receives is named with a different verb and with the
Epistle's own word --- \latin{fides tua te salvum fecit}. Bede draws the
distinction from that sentence: ``Si autem fides salvum fecit eum \dots\ ergo
perfidia perdidit eos qui de acceptis beneficiis Deo gloriam dare
neglexerunt.'' What saves is faith's working, not its bare acknowledgement.
Bruno guards the same distinction against presumption in the other direction
--- ``multi a peccatorum lepra mundati, qui \dots\ in electorum numero non
computabuntur'' --- and Augustine, preaching on the pericope, turns it on the
congregation rather than on anyone else: ``Noli tibi tribuere, ne sis inter
novem qui gratias non egerunt.'' Do not attribute it to yourself, lest you be
among the nine.

\subsection*{Two words that carried a doctrine}

The single most consequential thing in this formulary's reception is not an
interpretation of the Samaritan but of a subordinate clause. \latin{Et factum
est, dum irent, mundáti sunt}: cleansed on the way, before the priests were
reached.

Augustine saw the pattern first outside the pericope, in Cornelius, whose
household received the Spirit before baptism and was therefore cleansed while
going. Bede transcribed him. The eleventh-century treatise \work{De vera et
falsa poenitentia}, transmitted among Augustine's works and printed under
\latin{Auctor incertus} --- it is certainly not Augustine's --- built two
things on the same verse at once: oral and personal confession, grounded on
\latin{osténdite vos sacerdótibus} (``docens corporali praesentia confitenda
peccata, non per nuntium, non per scriptum manifestanda''), and the priority
of contrition over the keys (``Mundati enim sunt leprosi, dum ibant ostendere
ora sacerdotibus, antequam ad eos pervenirent \dots\ Unde patet Deum ad cor
respicere''). That treatise fed Gratian and Peter Lombard. Bruno of Segni states
the same thing at its sharpest --- ``prius multoties peccatori debita
remittuntur, quam veniat ad sacerdotem \dots\ quia cor contritum et humiliatum
Deus non spernit'' --- and Guéranger carries it to its sacramental conclusion,
that the old law's priesthood could discern leprosy while the new priesthood's
sentence of absolution removes it.

The chain matters for how the rest of the Mass is heard. The Secret asks for
pardon and for the things asked; the Postcommunion asks that the communicants
advance. Both are petitions of people who have been told, in the Gospel, that
the giving can precede the arriving.

\subsection*{The chant is older than the Bible printed beside it}

Twelve verified divergences separate this formulary's appointed text from the
Clementine Vulgate, and their distribution is not random: five fall in the two
Psalm 73 chants and three more in the Offertory. The Introit adds a vocative
the psalm has not; sings \latin{ne derelínquas} where the psalter has
\latin{ne obliviscáris}; sings \latin{exsúrge, Dómine} where the psalter has
\latin{Exsurge, Deus}; and turns the psalm's \latin{voces inimicorum tuorum}
into \latin{voces quæréntium te}, so that God is asked to remember what his
seekers say rather than what his enemies shout. The Gradual sings the psalter's
verb where the Introit did not, and its verse's second clause ---
\latin{memor esto oppróbrii servórum tuórum} --- is verbally identical, minus
the vocative, with Psalm 88:51 rather than with the Psalm 73 the missal cites.
The Alleluia sings \latin{a generatióne et progénie} for \latin{a generatione
in generationem}. The Offertory sings \latin{témpora mea} where the Clementine
has \latin{sortes meæ} --- \textit{my times} for \textit{my lots}, agreeing at
that point with the Hebrew against the Latin Bible. And the Communion is an
adaptation rather than a quotation: Wisdom's third-person \latin{præstitísti
illis} becomes \latin{dedísti nobis, Dómine}, with the verb of Psalm 77:24 and
a vocative the verse does not have.

Four consequences follow, and all four are practical. First, a reader working
from an English Bible is not reading these chants, and this guide therefore
prints the Douay verses whole with the reused fragments marked rather than
composing an English antiphon of its own. Second, the divergences are not a
1962 novelty: five of them, including \latin{témpora mea}, are already in print
in the Pustet Missal of Ratisbon of 1862.

Third, and more consequentially, several of them are not chant liberties at all
but older text with commentary attached. Cassiodorus's lemmata agree with the
appointed chants at four points where the Clementine does not --- including
\latin{a generatióne et progénie} at the Alleluia and \latin{in manibus tuis
tempora mea} at the Offertory --- and his exposition of the Offertory turns on
the very word the Clementine lacks: Christ speaks ``a parte scilicet humanitatis
assumptae, quae \dots\ et tempori subiacebat et morti,'' and places
\latin{tempora vitae suae} in the Father's power. Jerome's translation from the
Hebrew reads \latin{in manu tua tempora mea}, so the antiphon is a hybrid: the
Septuagint's plural hands with the Hebrew's times. Theodoret names
\textit{times} as the reading of Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion, refuses to
choose, and expounds the verse on both --- lots or times, he says, are the
changes of circumstance, wealth and poverty, slavery and mastery, peace and
war. The medieval Psalter gloss keeps both on the page and credits the
alternative to the right man: ``SORTES. CASSIODORUS. Vel tempora vite.'' And
Bellarmine, asked in effect where the reading comes from, points at this Mass:
the Hebrew has \textit{tempora mea}, the Greek word for lots should perhaps be
read as the word for times, ancient Latin versions from the Greek have it, and
``sic certè legitur in officio Missae non semel, \& in Psalterio Ecclesiae S.\
Petri Romae.'' The one commentator who names the Roman Mass propers as a
textual witness for this word is a commentator whose only public-domain English
cut the passage in which he does it. And Augustine, who reads \latin{sortes} in all four of his expositions of
this psalm and builds on it one of his great pages on unmerited election, is
expounding a word this Sunday does not sing. The Introit's \latin{voces
quæréntium te}, meanwhile, which looks like the boldest liberty in the
formulary, is simply the Septuagint against the Hebrew: Migne's apparatus at
Theodoret records that the Hebrew has \textit{thy enemies}, that Aquila and
Symmachus follow it, and that the suppliants are the Greek --- which is what
Augustine, Cassiodorus and the Roman chant all had.

Fourth --- and this is what makes the point more than antiquarian --- one of
them is quotable history. The bull by which Leo X opened proceedings against
Luther in 1520 begins with the chant's liturgical form, \latin{Exsurge, Domine,
et judica causam tuam}, and not with the Clementine's \latin{Exsurge, Deus};
and Theodoret, at that verse, makes the pronoun the point of his whole note ---
the psalmist did not say \textit{my} case but \textit{thy} case, conceding that
his own punishment was deserved and re-founding the plea on God's injured
honour. No derivation from any particular older psalter is asserted anywhere in
this guide, because no such witness was collated for it.

\subsection*{What the day declines to say}

Three refusals are worth stating, because the surrounding literature does not
always share them.

The formulary does not oppose law to promise. Paul asks \latin{Lex ergo
advérsus promíssa Dei?} and answers \latin{Absit}, and every checked account of
the law's purpose is positive in its own register: custody rather than
subversion for Jerome; a humbling of the proud so that grace may be sought for
Augustine; a bridle for Chrysostom; the care of the race from which the seed
would come for Theodoret; four distinct ends for Aquinas.

It does not identify the nine with a people. Luke does not, and the sentence
that does is Augustine's own --- ``quia illi erant Iudaei, amisisse per
superbiam declarati sunt regnum caelorum'' --- which Bede deliberately dropped
when he transcribed the rest of the passage almost verbatim, and which the
\work{Catena aurea} silently restored four centuries later. The harshest
formulations in this dossier are not patristic at all: they are Guéranger's
(``his now dead Law'') and Schuster's, and they are attributed to them by name
wherever this guide cites them. Guéranger himself ends the chapter in a Romans
11 key, praying for the time that will be glorious ``for the two peoples.''

And it does not answer the question it opens with. \latin{Ut quid, Deus,
repulísti in finem?} is sung exactly as the Clementine has it, question mark
included, and nothing later in the Mass replies to it in its own terms. What
the day offers instead is three other things: a refuge attested \latin{a
generatióne et progénie}; an instrument that later law cannot annul; and, at
the offering of the gifts, a pair of hands into which a man may put his times.

The first of those is less general than it sounds. Augustine, expounding the
Alleluia's verse, offers two senses of ``in generation and generation'' ---
either every generation, or two of them, ``vetere et nova,'' because Moses was
minister of the covenant belonging to the old and prophet of the covenant
belonging to the new; Jerome gives the same pair, and Cassiodorus, whose lemma
is the chant's exactly, glosses its two nouns as the whole span from the first
begetting to the end of the world, ``ac si diceret, semper.'' On the first of
those readings the Alleluia is not a general comfort at all but the Epistle's
thesis sung in one line, on the Sunday that argues it. Augustine adds the
reason a refuge can be \emph{made} without God changing: it is his eternity,
and what one flees to it from is \latin{temporis mutabilitas}, the mutability
of time --- which is the axis the Offertory turns on a few minutes later.

The Communion then names what is given in place of an explanation --- bread
from heaven having in it every delight and the sweetness of every taste, a
verse Rabanus Maurus was already reading as John 6 in the same century in which
a scribe first wrote \latin{Com. Panem de caelo} in the margin of this Mass.
