# Page-2 dossier facts — verified composition and event orientation

Facts gathered for the `Scriptural Date and Location` sheet, each checked at
the named source on 2026-08-20 unless dated otherwise. Traditional attribution
and modern historical judgment are kept distinct; the sheet prints both and
settles nothing. Six distinct directly appointed passages, in Catholic
canonical order: Ps 30:15–16 (Off.); Ps 73:1, 19–20, 22–23 (Int., Grad.);
Ps 89:1 (All.); Wis 16:20 (Comm.); Gal 3:16–22 (Ep.); Lk 17:11–19 (Gosp.).
Composed orations are not inventoried; their biblical echoes are not
quotations.

## Psalm superscriptions (Clementine, drbo.org, 2026-08-20)

- Ps 30 (31): `In finem. Psalmus David, pro extasi.` Verse 6 is `In manus
  tuas commendo spiritum meum` — the Compline and crucifixion verse of the
  same psalm (Lk 23:46; Acts 7:59 alludes).
- Ps 73 (74): `Intellectus Asaph.` The verse the Introit takes as its psalm
  verse (v. 1) follows directly: `Ut quid, Deus, repulisti in finem…`.
- Ps 89 (90): `Oratio Moysi, hominis Dei.` The only psalm in the Psalter
  bearing Moses' name.

## Modern judgments (New American Bible, vatican.va ENG0839)

- **Ps 74 (73 Vulg.)** — NAB footnote (page `_PHU.HTM`): "A communal lament
  sung when the enemy invaded the temple; it would be especially appropriate
  at the destruction of Jerusalem in 587 B.C." The community recounts creation
  to move God to act again.
- **Ps 90 (89 Vulg.)** — NAB heading "A prayer of Moses, the man of God";
  footnote (page `_PIA.HTM`): "A communal lament that describes only in
  general terms the cause of the community's distress"; contrasts God's
  eternity with human brevity.
- **Ps 31 (30 Vulg.)** — NAB footnote (page `_PGN.HTM`): "A lament with a
  strong emphasis on trust, ending with an anticipatory thanksgiving." NAB
  renders v. 16 (modern numbering 31:15) "My times are in your hands" — the
  Hebrew-based rendering agreeing with the missal's Old-Latin `témpora mea`
  against the Clementine's `sortes meæ` (see `propers/verified.md`,
  divergence row 9).

## Traditional attributions

Titles as printed above: David for Ps 30; Asaph for Ps 73; Moses for Ps 89.
The 1910 *Catholic Encyclopedia*, "Psalms" (newadvent.org/cathen/12533a.htm),
defends the titles as ancient and canonical while conceding many are obscure;
Asaph is David's chief musician (1 Par 16), so a title-based Davidic-era
horizon (c. 1000 BC) sits in visible tension with the sanctuary-destruction
content of Ps 73 — witnesses since antiquity have read the psalm as prophecy
of a later destruction or as the voice of a later Asaphite generation; the
sheet reports the tension rather than resolving it (the psalmody reception
record carries the witnesses).

## Wisdom 16:20

- Catholic Encyclopedia, "Book of Wisdom" (newadvent.org/cathen/15666a.htm):
  Solomonic attribution is a literary fiction ("its author, through a literary
  fiction, speaks as if he were the Son of David"); the author "not a
  Palestinian Jew, but an Alexandrian Jew"; composition in the time of "either
  Ptolemy IV Philopator (221–204 B.C.), or Ptolemy VII Physicon (145–117
  B.C.)"; original language Greek; place Alexandria.
- NAB introduction (vatican.va ENG0839 `__PLH.HTM`): "The Book of Wisdom was
  written about a hundred years before the coming of Christ"; author "a member
  of the Jewish community at Alexandria, in Egypt"; "He wrote in Greek, in a
  style patterned on that of Hebrew verse"; primary purpose "the edification
  of his co-religionists in a time when they had experienced suffering and
  oppression."

## Galatians 3:16–22

- Catholic Encyclopedia, "Epistle to the Galatians"
  (newadvent.org/cathen/06336a.htm): favors the **South** Galatian theory
  ("the South-Galatian Theory has proportionately gained ground"); reports
  Ramsay/Weber for composition "from Antioch, before the council (A.D.
  49–50)" while noting Weber's arguments "very plausible, but not quite
  convincing," and "about A.D. 53, 54" among South-Galatian defenders;
  occasion: "to counteract the influence of a few Judaizers."
- NAB introduction (vatican.va ENG0839 `__P100.HTM`): addressees "most likely
  among the descendants of Celts who had invaded western and central Asia
  Minor in the third century B.C." (North Galatia, around Ancyra, modern
  Ankara); "If it is addressed to the Galatians in the north, the letter was
  probably written around A.D. 54 or 55 … most likely from Ephesus"; "On the
  South Galatian theory, the date would be earlier, perhaps A.D. 48–50";
  occasion: converts from paganism "being enticed by other missionaries" to
  adopt circumcision and the law.
- The sheet therefore prints the North/South disagreement as a real
  competing-judgment pair (CE favoring South, NAB leaning North), with the
  date range A.D. 48–55 spanning both.
- Pauline authorship is both traditional and the mainstream modern judgment;
  neither source disputes it.

## Luke 17:11–19

- Composition: Catholic Encyclopedia, "Gospel of Saint Luke"
  (newadvent.org/cathen/09420a.htm): Luke "the most dear physician"
  (Col 4:14), companion of Paul, likely a native of Antioch; cites Julius
  Africanus for composition "in Achaia"; argues the Gospel was finished
  before Acts, i.e. by about A.D. 62–63 (before the end of the Roman
  imprisonment). NAB introduction (vatican.va ENG0839 `_PWJ.HTM`): "Early
  Christian tradition, from the late second century on, identifies the author
  of this gospel and of the Acts of the Apostles as Luke, a Syrian from
  Antioch"; "many propose A.D. 80–90 as the time of composition"; no place
  stated; "Luke was a non-Palestinian writing to a non-Palestinian audience
  that was largely made up of Gentile Christians."
- Narrated event: on the final journey to Jerusalem, "through the midst of
  Samaria and Galilee" (Lk 17:11) — the border country, in present-day
  northern Israel/West Bank; an unnamed village (`quoddam castéllum`). Within
  the last months before the Passover of the Passion; the Passion year is
  AD 29 on the *Catholic Encyclopedia*'s computation ("Chronology of the Life
  of Jesus Christ," newadvent.org/cathen/08377a.htm, as used for the
  neighboring Sundays), commonly AD 30–33 in later discussion; not settled
  here.
