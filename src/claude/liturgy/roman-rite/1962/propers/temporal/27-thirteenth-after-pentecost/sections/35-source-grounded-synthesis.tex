\section*{Source-Grounded Synthesis Across the Propers}

These are conclusions, not fresh evidence: each rests on the appointed texts,
their documented history, and the witnesses checked in the commentary. Nothing
here is a proposal; the proposals are in the next section and are labelled.

The formulary is held together by a noun that carries two senses at once.
\latin{Testamentum} is what the Introit and the Gradual beg God to look at and
what the Epistle proves cannot be revoked, and the double sense is a fact of
the Latin rather than an invention of this guide: Jerome records that behind
it stands \latin{berith}, which means pact and not will, and Theodoret notes
that Scripture itself calls the promise to Abraham God's covenant. The chants
ask God not to forget; the lesson answers that the question is not memory but
instrument. That the Church heard them in that order is documented in Rupert
of Deutz as Guéranger reports him, and Schuster states it outright at the
Gradual: the chant ``recalls to God the promises made to the Patriarchs.''

The psalm commentaries make that reading older than the liturgical commentators
--- and they do not agree with each other. Four positions on the pleaded word
were located, and the guide prints them as four. Augustine and Cassiodorus each
make Psalm 73:20 the hinge of the whole psalm and identify its
\latin{testamentum} as the \emph{new} covenant, both proving it from Jeremiah
31:31, and the \work{Glossa ordinaria} hardens that into the schoolroom gloss;
Augustine's exposition of the psalm opens by quoting Galatians 3:29, four
verses past where this Sunday's lesson stops. Rupert, as Guéranger reports him,
and Schuster refer the plea instead to the covenant promised to Abraham and the
patriarchs. Bellarmine reverses the Fathers outright: the covenant pleaded is
the old one made with the fathers, and what it promised was protection
\latin{in terra, quam nobis dedisti}. And Theodoret says nothing at all --- his
lemma at the verse is corrupt, his note expounds only the verse's second half,
and Migne's facing Latin does not even use the Introit's noun. The hinge of the
psalm for the Latin tradition is a clause the Antiochene passes in silence, and
the greatest of the later Latins reads it against them.

The day asks twice for one thing, and it asks for growth rather than for a
first gift. \latin{Augméntum} opens the Mass in the Collect and closes it in
the Postcommunion, and the two prayers had already travelled together in the
Gelasian and Gregorian books before any Sunday number was attached to them.
The Council of Trent then took the Collect --- and, in that chapter, only the
Collect --- as the Church's own evidence that received justification
increases. Between those two petitions stands a Gospel about ten men who
receive a grace entire and one who goes further, and Bede draws from its last
sentence the distinction the whole day turns on: cleansing is not salvation,
and what saves is faith's working rather than its bare acknowledgement.

Where the tradition genuinely disagrees, this formulary sits on the fault
line. Paul's \latin{in manu mediatóris} produced two readings that both
survived --- the Latin answer, Christ, in five distinct forms, and the
Antiochene answer, Moses --- and Aquinas declines to choose, keeping Christ as
mediator and Moses as the figure in whose hand the law was given. Liturgical
reception took the Antiochene side without appearing to notice. A guide that
reported only the Latin answer would be reporting a majority as a consensus.

Two further conclusions carry practical weight. The appointed chants are not
the Bible text beside them at twelve verified points, five of them in the two
Psalm 73 chants; a reader working from an English Bible is not reading this
Sunday's chants. What the psalm commentaries add is that several of those
readings are not chant liberties but older text. Cassiodorus's lemmata agree
with the appointed chants at four of them, including \latin{a generatióne et
progénie} at the Alleluia and \latin{in manibus tuis témpora mea} at the
Offertory, and his exposition of the Offertory turns on the very word the
Clementine does not have. Theodoret names \textit{times} as the reading of
Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion and declines to choose between it and
\textit{lots}; the medieval Psalter gloss keeps both on the page and credits
the alternative to Cassiodorus. Bellarmine goes further than any of them: he
reports the Hebrew as \textit{tempora mea}, conjectures that the Greek word for
lots should be read as the word for times, and names as his witnesses ``the
Office of the Mass, more than once, and the Psalter of the Church of St Peter
at Rome.'' The Introit's \latin{voces quæréntium te}, meanwhile, which looks
like the boldest liberty in the formulary, is the Septuagint against the
Hebrew, as Migne's apparatus sets out and the Glossa's psalter apparatus
confirms --- though Bellarmine, who knew that, judged the Greek reading a
corruption and voted for the Hebrew. This guide still asserts no derivation
from a particular psalter, because none was collated; what it can now say is
that these readings stood in front of named commentators centuries before the
missal printed them, and that one commentator pointed at the Mass itself when
asked where the Offertory's word came from.

The Gospel's most consequential reception, meanwhile, hangs on a subordinate
clause: \latin{dum irent, mundáti sunt}
became, through Augustine's Cornelius, an eleventh-century treatise passing
under Augustine's name, Bruno of Segni and the scholastic penance tradition,
one of the load-bearing texts of the Latin doctrine of contrition and the
keys.

What the formulary does not say is worth stating too. It does not oppose law
to promise --- Paul's own answer is \latin{absit}, and every checked account of
the law's purpose is positive. It does not identify the nine with a people:
Luke does not, and the sentence that does is Augustine's own, dropped by Bede
and restored by the \work{Catena aurea}, while the harshest formulations in
this dossier are nineteenth- and twentieth-century. And it never answers the
Introit's question on the Introit's terms. What it offers instead is a refuge
attested across every generation, an instrument that cannot be revoked, and a
pair of hands to put one's times into.
