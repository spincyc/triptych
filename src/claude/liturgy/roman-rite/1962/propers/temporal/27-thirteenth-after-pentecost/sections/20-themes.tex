\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\label{triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

\movementthesis{This formulary is built on one word said in two testaments.
The chants plead \latin{testaméntum} --- look upon thy covenant, do not
forsake thy poor --- and the Epistle answers with the same noun: the covenant
God confirmed to Abraham stands, and no law given four hundred and thirty
years later can void it. Between the plea and the answer the day inserts a
prayer for the only capacities that can receive a covenant --- faith, hope and
charity, asked as one increase --- and a Gospel in which ten are cleansed but
one act of returning faith is what hears \latin{salvum fecit}. What began as
a cry from ruins ends as bread of every sweetness and a second asking of the
same increase.}

The order in which the book prints the ten texts is itself the argument. Read
as four stages:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=0.1em]
  \item A covenant pleaded from the ruins, twice, and a refuge confessed. \cue{Int.}, \cue{Grad.}, \cue{All.}
  \item The covenant answered: promise before law, and the virtues that receive it. \cue{Ep.}, \cue{Coll.}
  \item Ten cleansed on the way; one comes back, and faith is named. \cue{Gosp.}
  \item Times handed over, God appeased, the bread of every taste, increase asked again. \cue{Off.}, \cue{Sec.}, \cue{Comm.}, \cue{Postcomm.}
\end{enumerate}

English quoted in this section is the Douay--Rheims (Challoner) for scriptural
propers and the 1861 Cummiskey hand missal for orations, as throughout; the
sources are identified in the detailed commentary and in the references.

\subsection*{1. A covenant pleaded from the ruins}

The Introit is the prayer of a devastated people: ``Have regard to thy
covenant \dots\ forget not to the end the souls of thy poor \dots\ Arise, O
God, judge thy own cause.'' Its psalm verse does not soften the situation; it
sharpens it into the question the whole day must answer: ``O God, why hast
thou cast us off unto the end?'' The Gradual then sings the same two verses
over again --- the one chant of the day that repeats the other --- so that the
plea is made twice before the Epistle is allowed to speak. That repetition was
noticed: Guéranger, citing Abbot Rupert, reads the Gradual's
\latin{Réspice \dots\ in testaméntum tuum} as ``the cry of the ancient
people, who, sighing at the weakness of the powerless Law of Sinai, besought
God to fulfil the covenant He had promised to Abraham's faith.''

The Alleluia answers the fear of being forsaken \latin{in finem} with the one
psalm in the Psalter that bears Moses' name: ``Lord, thou hast been our refuge
from generation to generation.'' The lawgiver's own prayer confesses a refuge
older and steadier than anything the day will say about the law he gave.

\subsection*{2. Promise before law}

The Epistle takes up the pleaded word and answers it as an argument. The
promises were spoken to Abraham ``and to his seed \dots\ which is Christ'';
the \latin{testaméntum} God confirmed cannot be disannulled by ``the law
which was made after four hundred and thirty years.'' The law was set
``because of transgressions, until the seed should come''; it could not give
life; Scripture ``hath concluded all under sin, that the promise, by the
faith of Jesus Christ, might be given to them that believe.'' The chants
pleaded a covenant as if it might be forgotten; Paul answers that it cannot
even be amended.

The Collect stands between plea and argument as the day's act of receiving.
It asks ``an increase of faith, hope, and charity'' --- the promise is given
\latin{credéntibus}, so the first thing to ask is more of the believing ---
and then binds attaining to loving: ``that we may deserve what thou promisest,
make us love what thou commandest.'' The prayer is older than any Sunday
number it has carried, and the Church has quoted it back: the Council of
Trent, teaching on the increase of justification, wrote that ``this increase
of justification holy Church begs, when she prays, `Give unto us, O Lord,
increase of faith, hope, and charity''' --- this Collect, cited by this
Sunday, the decree's one liturgical quotation in that chapter.

What that pleaded word means was not settled by the men who expounded the
psalm. Augustine and Cassiodorus make this verse the hinge of the whole psalm
and read its \latin{testaméntum} as the \emph{new} covenant, both proving it
from Jeremiah; Rupert and Schuster refer it to the covenant promised to
Abraham; Bellarmine reads it as the old covenant made with the fathers about a
land; and Theodoret, who might have been expected to carry the argument, does
not gloss the word at all. The Introit sings one noun that its commentators
answer four ways.

\subsection*{3. Ten cleansed, one saved}

The Gospel narrates the covenant's grammar as an event. Ten lepers cry
``Jesus, Master, have mercy on us''; all ten obey the command to show
themselves to the priests; all ten are cleansed ``as they went.'' One ---
``and this was a Samaritan'' --- turns back, glorifying God, and falls on his
face giving thanks. To him alone is it said: ``Arise, go thy way; for thy
faith hath made thee whole.'' The Venerable Bede drew the line that the
formulary's placement makes audible: the cleansing ran through the Law's
priests and reached all ten, but what the returning one receives is named
with the Epistle's own word --- faith --- and Bede concludes that faith's
working, not its bare knowledge, is what saves. Nine were cleansed under the
Law's form; the stranger outside it went home saved.

The oldest reading of the scene is Augustine's, and it is not about ingratitude
in general. Leprosy, he says, is a defect of colour rather than of health ---
which is why Luke writes that the ten were \emph{cleansed} and not healed --- so
the ten stand for those who profess doctrines with truth and error mixed
together, and the one who comes back is named by a word the tradition heard as
``keeper.'' Bruno of Segni guards the reading from the opposite error: many are
cleansed of the leprosy of sin, he writes, who will not be counted among the
elect. And the cure happens on the road. They were made clean
\latin{dum irent}, while they were going, before they reached the priests they
had been sent to --- two words on which the Latin Middle Ages built the
doctrine that contrition precedes the keys.

\subsection*{4. Times handed over, and the increase asked again}

What the Church sings at the offering is trust with time itself inside it:
``I have put my trust in thee, O Lord: I said: Thou art my God. My times are
in thy hands'' --- the missal's old psalter singing \latin{témpora mea},
``my times,'' where the Clementine reads ``my lots.'' A people that has just
heard the four-hundred-thirty-year argument hands its own chronology over at
the altar.

The Secret says \latin{propitiáre} twice --- ``Be thou propitious, O Lord, to
thy people: and mercifully receive their offerings'' --- and asks pardon and
petitions granted through the one oblation. The Communion then sets the
oldest food in the day's mouth: ``thou didst \dots\ give them bread from
heaven \dots\ having in it all that is delicious, and the sweetness of every
taste'' --- Wisdom's memory of the manna, recast by the liturgy as a
first-person address, \latin{dedísti nobis, Dómine}, at the moment of
Communion. A ninth-century witness already attaches this antiphon to this
Sunday's Mass. And the Postcommunion closes the bracket the Collect opened,
asking the day's noun one last time: having received the heavenly mysteries,
that we may advance toward the increase --- \latin{augméntum} --- of eternal
redemption. The day asked an increase of the three virtues before the
covenant was argued; having eaten, it asks the increase of the covenant's
whole content.
