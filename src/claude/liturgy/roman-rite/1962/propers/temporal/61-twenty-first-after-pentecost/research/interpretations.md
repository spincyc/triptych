## The rules this record keeps

- **The formulary is a compilation.** A reading of the whole Mass is the
  editor's synthesis. Every Father below commented on his own passage — a
  psalm, Job, Ephesians, the parable — not on this Mass. Each reading marks
  what is *textual observation* (in the appointed words), *documented
  reception* (a named witness at a checked locus, said of his own passage or,
  for a liturgical commentator, of the Mass as his books gave it) and
  *editorial synthesis* (a connection between elements that no witness
  makes). Where a witness himself joins two passages that this formulary also
  joins, that is said explicitly with the locus (Hilary on Ps 118:84 and Eph
  6:12; Chrysostom on the wronged man and the devil; Chrysostom on Eph 6:14 and
  the Exodus; Aquinas on the parable's servant and Job 1:8). Aquinas, *Super
  Eph.* 6 lect. 3, joins Eph 6:12 to Ps 118:157 (*Multi qui persequuntur me*),
  which this formulary does not appoint; a link from the Communion to Eph 6:12
  through him is editorial synthesis, and Hilary's is the documented join.
- **Unsung context is labelled as such.** A reading rests its question, its
  controlling claim and its senses on the words the Missal appoints. Words of
  an appointed passage that the chant or reading does not sing are *textual
  observation of the unsung context*, so marked wherever they are used. For
  this Mass that includes Esth 13:12–17 (Introit); Ps 89's title *Oratio
  Moysi*, its thousand years, the shortness of life and *numerare dies
  nostros* (vv. 3–12; the Gradual sings vv. 1–2); and, in the Communion, the
  verb *Defecit* of v. 81, v. 84a *Quot sunt dies servi tui?* and v. 86a *Omnia
  mandata tua veritas*; Peter's question and *septuagies septies* (Mt
  18:21–22), which the Gospel's opening *Dixit Iesus discipulis suis parabolam
  hanc* replaces; and the Epistle's continuation, prayer *omni tempore in
  spiritu* and Paul *in catena* (Eph 6:18–20) (`propers/verified.md`;
  `research/scope.md` 1.2). No study may say that the Communion asks how many
  are the servant's days, or that in this Mass's Gospel Jesus answers Peter.
  A witness who comments on a full verse is cited for the sung words only
  where his comment bears on them, and that bearing is stated. That holds for
  Hilary as for Augustine: his ground for a present rather than final
  judgment is the unsung v. 84a (`research/scope.md` 3.10).
- **Editors' words are not the Father's.** In PL 9 each numbered paragraph of
  Hilary's *Tractatus* opens with an italic summary by the Maurist editors
  (Caph 1 *Salutaris Jesus*; Caph 5 *Judicium non hic poscitur extremum … sed
  virtus qua diabolus cum ministris conteratur*). None is quoted as Hilary's;
  his own words follow the dash.
- **Commentators whose Mass had another Gospel** (Rupert, Durandus, Honorius,
  Sicard: Jn 4 at this Sunday) are cited only for the chants, Collect and
  Epistle their Mass shares with this one, never for a link between those and
  the parable (`research/scope.md` 2.1). Fromage and Schuster expound this
  formulary as the 1962 Missal gives it.
- **Carrying authors** are Fathers, canonized saints or Doctors, or the
  Blessed paired with one of them (`author-standing-v1.toml`). Origen has no
  standing row and is used only as context.
- **The added orations** (*pro Fidei propagatione*; St Luke at low Mass) are
  not formulary elements; no reading depends on them.

## The three readings in brief

1. `armour-under-providence` — **The war God permits and bounds.** The
   Christian's enemy is the adversary, not the brother; he fights only by the
   leave of the God whose will none resists, and the Christian stands in the
   armour that is Christ.
2. `debt-forgiven-from-the-heart` — **The king's reckoning and the forgiven
   debtor.** Mercy received without measure must become mercy given from the
   heart, or the debt returns.
3. `last-exodus-of-the-church` — **The Church's exodus toward the judgment.**
   The Church, brought out of Egypt (*In exitu Israel de Aegypto*) and girded
   for departure, asks *quando facies de persequentibus me iudicium?* while it
   stands *in die malo* before a king who *voluit rationem ponere*, with the
   eternal God (*a saeculo et usque in saeculum tu es*) for refuge and the
   food of immortality (*immortalitatis alimoniam*) for the journey.

Each answers a different exegetical question (sections 1.1, 2.1, 3.1), turns
on a different difficulty, and leads to a different consequence (section 4).

## 1. `armour-under-providence` — The war God permits and bounds

### 1.1 Question and controlling claim

**Question.** The Introit says *non est qui possit resistere voluntati tuae*;
the Offertory says Satan *petiit ut tentaret: et data est ei potestas a
Domino*; the Epistle says *non est nobis colluctatio adversus carnem et
sanguinem*; the Communion asks judgment on *persequentibus*. Who is the
Christian's enemy, and how can the enemy's work stand *in voluntate tua*?

**Controlling claim.** The adversary is real and wars on God's people, but he
asks before he strikes and strikes only within the power given him; God bridles
what he looses and turns the trial into the proving of his servant. The
Christian's warfare is therefore not against flesh and blood — not against the
human persecutor or the offending brother — but against the spirits that move
them, and he fights it clothed in Christ, who is the armour of God, under a
will that nothing escapes.

### 1.2 Principal witnesses and their reasoning

- **Gregory the Great** (Father), *Moralia*, Preface 7–8, 11; II.18–19;
  IX.4–5. *Him the adversary sought indeed to obtain that he might prove
  deficient … but obtained that he might prove a proficient … The Lord in
  loving-kindness permitted that to be done, which the devil in his wickedness
  required* (Pref. 7). On Job 1:12: God *lets go our enemy, and keeps him in …
  looses, and yet bridles him*, and divides the contests of *His soldier*
  (II.19). On Job 9:4: whoever resists the Creator *had* no *peace* (IX.5).
  Inference: the devil's petition and God's permission are two wills; the
  first aims at ruin, the second at proving, and the second governs the
  outcome. Gregory speaks of Job, not of this Mass.
- **John Chrysostom** (Father), *Homilies on Ephesians* 22 and 24. The devil
  fights by *wiles*; to the persecuted disciples Paul says, *imagine not that
  it is they who war with you. They that really war with you, are the spirits
  that work in them*; therefore *If any one hath an enemy, if any one hath been
  wronged by him … pour it out upon the head of the devil … here revenge is
  praiseworthy*; *if we are at war with the devil, we are at peace with God*
  (Hom. 24). In *Homilies on Matthew* 61.5 he says the devil *hath become a
  cause of crowns … and Job showeth it*. Inference: hostility is redirected
  from the human offender to the adversary; the trial becomes a crown.
  Chrysostom joins the wronged man, the devil and Job himself (Hom. 61.5) and
  the persecutor and the spirits (Hom. 22) — documented reception for those
  joins, not for the whole Mass.
- **Jerome** (Father), *In Eph.* III, PL 26, col. 543A–B (page image): *omnia
  arma Dei … intelligi Salvatorem … quasi dixerit, Induite Dominum Jesum
  Christum*; Israel's wars against the nations were written as figures of the
  spiritual war (cols. 545–546, text layer).
- **Hilary of Poitiers** (Father), *Tract. in Ps.* 118, Caph 5, PL 9, cols.
  573C–574B (page image; his own text, not the editors' italic summary that
  heads the paragraph). On the sung *quando facies de persequentibus me
  iudicium?*: the prophet *fieri judicium ab his qui se persequuntur
  desiderat, a diabolo scilicet, ministrisque ejus. Sunt enim spiritales
  nequitiae in caelestibus* (Eph 6:12); what he asks is *ut … conterat intra
  se diabolum, conterat adversantes sibi inimicasque virtutes*, the power the
  Lord gave the apostles *calcare super serpentes et scorpiones* (Lk 10:19:
  *Hanc potestatem judicii Dominus apostolis suis dedit*). Hilary himself
  joins the Communion's verse to the Epistle's verse — documented reception
  of that pair. Why he takes this judgment as present and not final: *Judicium
  post consummationem saeculi Propheta esse non ignorat* (Ps 1:5); what he
  now asks is *Non illud utique quod in resurrectionis saeculo meminit
  futurum*, because he knows he is *paucorum … dierum*: *Ita enim ait: Quot
  sunt dies servi tui?* That step rests on v. 84a (and v. 83), unsung in this
  Mass — *textual observation of the unsung context* in Hilary's comment on
  the full verse. He also affirms that the devil is to be judged *in
  resurrectionis saeculo*.
- **The exposition printed under Athanasius's name** (PG 27, cols. 495B–496B,
  page images; attribution the printing's): on v. 84a (unsung; the lemma
  prints only 84a), few are men's days on earth, *in quibus rogat statim
  conteri Satanam sub pedibus ejus*. It converges with Hilary on the present
  crushing of the adversary, and it is said of the unsung half; its bearing
  on the sung 84b is this record's inference. Contributing only.
- **Thomas Aquinas** (Doctor): *Super Eph.* 6 lect. 3 answers the objection
  from Ps 118:157 *Multi qui persequuntur me* (not appointed) that men do
  fight us: they do, but *a superiore movente, sc. a diabolo* — said of Eph
  6:12, and applied to the Communion's persecutors only by editorial
  synthesis; ST I q. 103 a. 7 *sed contra* proves from
  Esth 13:9 that nothing occurs outside the order of divine government; *Super
  Iob* 1 (unregistered web text): Satan harms *non quantum vult sed quantum
  permittitur*; *voluntas nocendi* is his own, *potestas non nisi a Deo*; he
  departs *a facie domini … ab intentione providentiae … recedens licet ordinem
  providentiae effugere non valens*.
- **Rabanus Maurus** (saint), *In Esther* XI, PL 109, 664B–C: the heavenly
  king's edict rules the whole world, *in cujus ditione cuncta sunt posita, nec
  est qui possit resistere voluntati ejus*; *superbia mundi superatur potentia
  Dei*; Aman prefigures the enemies of the Church (XIII).
- **Robert Bellarmine** (Doctor), Ps 89:1: *refuge* means *a well fortified
  house, placed on an eminence*; those who dwell in God are *in a city
  fortified by faith, hope, and charity*.
- **Liturgical commentators on this Mass's shared elements.** Durandus,
  *Rationale* VI.138: the Church speaks of *bello contra diabolum* and our
  armour; Mardochai's prayer is the Church's in sackcloth and ashes; God
  *liberas eos de manu diaboli, et adiuvas eos in bello*; *quia etiam bene armati
  non possunt perfecte resistere, nisi Deus adiuvet*, the Gradual asks refuge;
  patience above all conquers the devil (Offertory). Rupert, *De div. off.*
  XII.21: the office *voces continet hominis, in conflictu virtutum vitiorumque
  positi*; the tempter strikes him *occulta Dei dispensatione permissus*; what
  befell Job *historialiter* befalls him *moraliter*; the Epistle comforts him;
  the Introit is his humble confession with Job. Sicard VIII.21 follows Rupert.
  Schuster (Blessed), III pp. 178–180: the evil God permits serves Providence;
  the history of Job is *repeated again and again throughout the history of
  the Church*. Fromage, XI pp. 457–462: the armour is first the Spouse's.

Carrying authors: **Gregory the Great**, **John Chrysostom** (Fathers);
contributing: Jerome, Hilary, Aquinas, Rabanus, Bellarmine, the exposition
printed under Athanasius's name (on the unsung v. 84a); Rupert, Durandus,
Sicard (ecclesiastical writers) as witnesses to the shared chants; Schuster
and Fromage to the Mass.

### 1.3 The difficulty followed through

**Words.** *Non est qui possit resistere voluntati tuae* (Introit) against
*quem Satan petiit … et data est ei potestas a Domino* (Offertory).

**Inferences.** Gregory: resistance happens, but it destroys the resister's
peace (IX.5); the devil's will to ruin and God's will to prove both act on Job,
and God's governs (Pref. 7; II.19). Aquinas: the will to harm is the devil's
own; the power is given; leaving the intention of Providence, he cannot leave
its order (*Super Iob* 1); nothing occurs outside the universal cause (ST I
103.7). Rabanus applies the Introit's sentence to the world's pride conquered
by God's power.

**What follows if accepted.** The Introit becomes the ground of the Epistle's
confidence (*Confortamini in Domino, et in potentia virtutis eius*): the war is
fierce but bounded; the Communion's cry for judgment on *persequentibus* is,
in Hilary's reading of the sung words, a plea against the devil and his
ministers (Eph 6:12) for the power to tread them down (Lk 10:19), not a wish
against men; the Collect's *a cunctis adversitatibus … libera* asks what God's
bridle grants. That the power is asked for *now*, within this life, is
Hilary's inference from the unsung v. 84a (*Quot sunt dies servi tui?*, few
days), and a study that uses it says so; reading 1 does not need it, since
the identification of the persecutors and the power asked stand on the sung
v. 84b, and Hilary himself keeps the devil's judgment at the end. Without
this answer the Introit would sit beside the Offertory as a contradiction, or
the adversary would become a second power.

### 1.5 The four senses

- **Literal.** Mardochai, under Aman's sentence, confesses that all things lie
  in God's will; Paul arms the Ephesians against spiritual powers (that he
  writes *in catena*, Eph 6:20, is textual observation of the unsung
  context); the Job narrative tells that Satan asked and was given power over
  Job's goods and flesh; the psalmist asks when God will judge his
  persecutors.
- **Allegorical.** The armour is Christ (Jerome); Job is a type of the Redeemer
  together with his Body (Gregory, Pref. 14); the Church is the people under
  sentence whom God delivers (Rabanus, Durandus).
- **Moral.** Stand firm; do not take the brother or the persecutor for the
  enemy; pour the hostility on the devil (Chrysostom); turn trial into proving
  by patience (Gregory, Durandus).
- **Anagogical.** The devil, already bridled, is judged at the end (Hilary's
  own words: *Sed scit et diabolum in resurrectionis saeculo judicandum*, set
  beside the power over him asked in this life), and the soldier who stands
  in the evil day is crowned (Chrysostom Hom. 61.5; Fromage).

### 1.6 Limits

- Chrysostom's *revenge is praiseworthy* is aimed at the devil only; it must
  not be generalized.
- Hilary's present judgment and Augustine's eschatological reading of Ps
  118:84 differ in time (`research/scope.md` 4.4). This reading follows
  Hilary on the sung v. 84b (the persecutors, the power asked) and should say
  so. Where it also uses his *now, not the last judgment*, it says that Hilary
  draws it from the unsung v. 84a read as *few days* and from Ps 1:5, and that
  he affirms the devil's judgment at the end.
- Quote Hilary only from his own text after each paragraph's dash; the italic
  summaries (*Judicium non hic poscitur extremum …*; *Salutaris Jesus*) are
  the Maurist editors'. His own words for the latter are *Salutaris enim
  noster Jesus est* (Caph 1).
- Aquinas's *Super Iob* is read on an unregistered web text; quote at most a
  short phrase, or describe.
- The Alleluia's contribution here is editorial.

### 1.7 Agreements and differences within the reading

Gregory, Aquinas and Rupert agree that the adversary acts by permission;
Gregory speaks of lost peace, Aquinas of an order not escaped (4.1 of
`research/scope.md`). Chrysostom and Hilary agree that the real enemies are
spirits; Aquinas adds that men are instruments. Jerome alone makes the armour
Christ himself; Aquinas makes it the gifts and virtues; Fromage holds both.

## 2. `debt-forgiven-from-the-heart` — The king's reckoning and the forgiven debtor

### 2.1 Question and controlling claim

**Question.** The parable ends *Sic et Pater meus caelestis faciet vobis, si
non remiseritis unusquisque fratri suo de cordibus vestris*. The king had
forgiven *omne debitum*; he then exacts *universum debitum*. What does the
king's mercy require of the forgiven, and does a pardon once given return?

**Controlling claim.** Every hearer is the debtor of ten thousand talents,
forgiven beyond what he asked; the only condition the king attaches is that the
forgiven forgive. The servant's cruelty revokes his own pardon; forgiveness
must be given *from the heart*, not with the lips, and it does not abolish
correction. The Mass sets this demand within prayers that ask God's continual
mercy and a pure following of what the mouth has received.

### 2.2 Principal witnesses and their reasoning

- **John Chrysostom** (Father), *Homilies on Matthew* 61: sins against God and
  against man differ *as … ten thousand talents, and a hundred pence*; the
  threat to sell wife and children was *unspeakable tenderness*, to bring the
  servant to supplication; he asked delay and received remission; by seizing
  his fellow he *revok[ed] the sentence and the gift* — it was not the lord who
  revoked it *but the one who had received it*; *delivered … that is, for ever*;
  *not merely to forgive with the lips, but from the heart*.
- **Augustine** (Father), *Sermon* 83: every man is God's debtor and has a
  debtor; two works of mercy; the Lord's Prayer is *the covenant and
  agreement*; the talents and denarii both of the Decalogue's number; *every
  man begins from Baptism … the ten thousand talents are forgiven him*; *I will
  call thee back, and put upon thee again all that I had remitted to thee*;
  *Say not with the tongue, "I forgive," and put off to forgive in the heart*;
  *from your hearts* lets discipline stand *through affection*, as a surgeon
  cuts. *Sermon* 58.8: the martyrs *are not yet avenged. Still doth the
  patience of God wait … Forgive therefore; from the heart forgive*.
- **Jerome** (Father), *In Matth.* III, PL 26, cols. 132C–133C (page images):
  *quanto magis servi conservis suis debent minora dimittere?*; the debtor is
  not the devil; *Formidolosa sententia, si juxta nostram mentem sententia Dei
  flectitur atque mutatur … omnem simulationem fictae pacis evertit*.
- **Thomas Aquinas** (Doctor), *Super Matth.* 18: the king is God; every man
  a servant (*Numquid considerasti servum meum Iob*); the reckoning begins
  with tribulation and ends at the judgment; forgiven sins do not return as
  such, since remission has its efficacy from the sacraments, but are said to
  return *per ingratitudinem*.
- **Hilary** (Father), *In Matth.* 18.10–11: pardon without measure through
  the Gospel's grace (text layer; brief).
- **Rabanus Maurus** (saint), *In Matth.*: the king Christ; with Jerome and a
  sentence under *(Greg.)*: what is not forgiven from the heart is exacted
  again (text layer).
- **Alphonsus Liguori** (Doctor), *Sermons for All the Sundays*, Sermon L for
  this Sunday (Callan, Dublin 1882, text layer, unregistered): v. 34 *a
  description of the sentence of the eternal death*; those who die in sin
  *remain for ever debtors to the divine justice*. He preaches eternity, not
  the fellow servant; he agrees with Chrysostom's *for ever*.
- **Liturgical commentators.** At this formulary: Fromage, XI pp. 464–468 (the
  *Dies irae*; *We are all of us … that negligent servant*; Augustine *Sermon*
  83 quoted); Schuster, III p. 180 (the parable *the most authoritative
  commentary on … Forgive us our trespasses*). At this Gospel's own locus in
  their books: Rupert XII.22 (*Dimitte nobis debita nostra*), Durandus VI.139
  (the day of judgment), Honorius IV.93 (Christ the king coming to judgment).

Carrying authors: **John Chrysostom**, **Augustine** (Fathers); contributing:
Jerome, Aquinas, Hilary, Rabanus, Alphonsus; Fromage and Schuster for the
Mass.

### 2.3 The difficulty followed through

**Words.** *omne debitum dimisi tibi … tradidit eum tortoribus, quoadusque
redderet universum debitum. Sic et Pater meus caelestis faciet vobis.*

**Inferences.** Chrysostom: the gift was revoked by the receiver's cruelty;
the delivery is *for ever* because the debt can never be paid. Augustine: God
*puts upon thee again* what he remitted, as the covenant of the Lord's Prayer
states. Aquinas: Origen thought forgiven sins return; Thomas denies that they
return as such, since sacramental remission is efficacious, and says they
return *per ingratitudinem*. Jerome: the sentence is fearful because it shows
God's judgment following our own measure; it unmasks a feigned peace.

**What follows.** The reading must keep Augustine's and Chrysostom's strong
formulation beside Aquinas's distinction (`research/scope.md` 4.2): the hearer
is not told that baptism is undone by a grudge, but that ingratitude brings a
debt as great as the one forgiven. And *from the heart* (Augustine 83.8) does
not forbid correction; it forbids rancour.

### 2.5 The four senses

- **Literal.** Jesus tells his disciples a parable (*Dixit Iesus discipulis
  suis parabolam hanc*): a king forgives a servant an unpayable debt; the
  servant refuses a fellow a small one; the king reverses his sentence. In
  Matthew the parable answers Peter's question and *septuagies septies*
  (18:21–22), which this Mass does not read: textual observation of the
  unsung context, so labelled if used.
- **Allegorical.** The king is God (Aquinas), or Christ (Rabanus, Origen); the
  debt forgiven is sin remitted in Baptism (Augustine); the fellow servants who
  grieve are the Church's members (Aquinas: when one member suffers the others
  suffer with it). Rabanus's Israel–Gentiles allegory is his own and is
  reported only as such.
- **Moral.** Forgive every debt, from the heart, as one forgiven far more;
  correct without rancour (Augustine 83.8).
- **Anagogical.** The reckoning ends at the judgment (Aquinas; Durandus;
  Honorius), when God's measure will be the measure we used; the unforgiven
  debtor *for ever* a debtor (Chrysostom 61.4; Alphonsus, Sermon L); the *Dies
  irae* prayer *grant us the gift of forgiveness, before the Day of reckoning*
  (Fromage).

### 2.6 Limits

- *Sermon* 83's numerology (10, 11, 77) is Augustine's; it may be reported but
  must not carry the argument.
- The Lord's Prayer link is the Fathers' (Augustine, Rupert at his locus,
  Schuster), not the Missal's.
- The Postcommunion join is editorial; say so in the records, not by
  disclaimer in prose.

### 2.7 Agreements and differences within the reading

Chrysostom and Augustine agree on the heart; they differ on who revokes
(Chrysostom: the receiver; Augustine: God calls back) — complementary in their
own grammar. Aquinas's distinction on the return of sins is narrower than
either, and the study must keep it. Jerome's rejection of the devil-debtor
reading against Origen is a real disagreement (`research/scope.md` 4.3).

## 3. `last-exodus-of-the-church` — The Church's exodus toward the judgment

### 3.1 Question and controlling claim

**Question.** The Communion sings *In salutari tuo anima mea, et in verbum
tuum speravi: quando facies de persequentibus me iudicium? iniqui persecuti
sunt me, adiuva me*; the Epistle bids the Ephesians *resistere in die malo*;
the Gospel's king *voluit rationem ponere cum servis suis*; the Alleluia sings
*In exitu Israel de Aegypto*; the Gradual confesses *a saeculo et usque in
saeculum tu es, Deus*; the Postcommunion names *immortalitatis alimoniam*.
When does the judgment the Communion asks for come, and where does that leave
the people that sings this Mass in the meantime?

**What the reading does not rest on.** The Communion does not sing v. 84a,
*Quot sunt dies servi tui?*, and the Gradual stops before the rest of Ps 89 —
man turned to dust, the thousand years, the shortness of life and *numerare
dies nostros* (vv. 3–12). Neither
is appointed in this formulary (`propers/verified.md`; `research/scope.md`
1.2). The reading's question is the sung *quando*, when the judgment comes;
it is not a question about how many days are left. Where Ps 118:84a or Ps
89:3–12 appear below, they are *textual observation of the unsung context*,
so labelled, as Esth 13:16–17 is.

**Controlling claim.** The Church is Israel going out of Egypt: delivered,
girded, fed on the way, not yet arrived. Hoping in God's word, it asks when he
will judge those who persecute it, and it is answered that the judgment is the
end toward which it travels and that it remains on earth, persecuted and
helped, until then. The evil day is the present life and, above all, the last
day; the king's reckoning begins in tribulation and is finished at the
judgment; the God who is *a saeculo et usque in saeculum* is its refuge from
changing time; and the food it receives is the food of immortality, eaten as
the Passover was eaten, girded for departure.

### 3.2 Principal witnesses and their reasoning

- **Augustine** (Father), *Enarr. in Ps.* 118, Caph, commenting on the full
  verses of the Vulgate:
  - v. 81, whose sung form is *In salutari tuo anima mea, et in verbum tuum
    speravi*: the speaker is *the chosen generation … longing for Christ from
    the origin of the human race even unto the end of this world*, and *these
    times, since He hath ascended into heaven, have Saints who desire His
    manifestation to judge the quick and the dead*; *I have hoped because of
    Thy word* is *a hope which causeth us to await with patience that which is
    not seen*. His *laudable* failing is said of *Defecit*, which the Missal
    omits; the longing for Christ's manifestation to judge and the patient
    hope bear on the sung *in salutari tuo* and *in verbum tuum speravi*.
  - v. 84, the full verse: *In the Apocalypse, these are the words of the
    Martyrs, and long-suffering is enjoined them until the number of their
    brethren be fulfilled* (NPNF note: Rev 6:10–11, the martyrs' *how long …
    dost thou not judge*, answered by rest *for a little time*). *The body of
    Christ then is asking concerning its days* is said of v. 84a, unsung, and
    is reported only as his comment on the full verse. Of the sung v. 84b he
    says that the question about the judgment was added *showing indeed that
    the Church would exist on earth until the judgment, when vengeance shall
    fall upon Her persecutors*. That is how his comment bears on the sung
    half: *quando facies … iudicium?* is the martyrs' plea for judgment,
    answered by long-suffering until the end, and the assurance that the
    Church endures on earth until the judgment. Those two points stand on the
    words this Mass sings; the counting of days does not.
  - v. 86, sung *iniqui persecuti sunt me, adiuva me*: *the whole sense
    dependeth upon the foregoing*; *help Thou me, that I may struggle for the
    truth even unto death*. His *persecuted nothing save the truth* leans on
    the unsung *Omnia mandata tua veritas*.
  - *Enarr.* 113: *Egypt … an emblem of this world; from which we must
    spiritually withdraw*; *no man is turned away in heart from this world,
    unless aided by the gift of the Divine mercy*; fulfilled *in the daily
    travailings of the Church, in this end of the world*.
  - *Enarr.* 89.2, on the sung v. 2: *the eternity that is our refuge, that we
    may fly thither from the mutability of time*.
- **John Chrysostom** (Father), *Homilies on Ephesians* 22–23: *By "evil day"
  he means the present life*; on the girded loins, the Israelites ate the
  Passover girded; *we too eat a Passover, even Christ … that we too may be
  ready for our Exodus, for our departure hence*; *the leader of this our
  Exodus is not Moses, but Jesus*; *We have manna; yea rather not manna, but far
  more than manna*; *The present life is nothing else than a march and an
  encampment*. Chrysostom himself joins Eph 6:14 to the Exodus and the
  Passover — documented reception of that pair.
- **Thomas Aquinas** (Doctor), *Super Matth.* 18, on *voluit rationem
  ponere*: *Finis huius rationis erit in die iudicii; principium quando primo
  inducit aliquam tribulationem*.
- **Robert Bellarmine** (Doctor): on the sung Ps 89:2, *Thou art*, God's
  eternity without past or future; his contrast with the shortness of human
  life is drawn from vv. 4–6, unsung context. On Ps 118:84b, *When will you
  judge and condemn those that persecute and tempt me, that my persecution
  may cease on their condemnation?*; and of the whole verse, *He makes no
  curious inquiries as to the number of the days of his life, or of those of
  the world, he merely expresses his desire of life everlasting, when all the
  wicked … shall cease to harass the just*. Bellarmine himself turns the verse
  from counting days to desiring the end, which is the reading the sung half
  carries on its own.
- **Gregory the Great** (Father), *Moralia* Pref. 16: Job's grief sets forth
  *the travails of Holy Church, which is harassed by the manifold toils of this
  present life*.
- **Cassiodorus** (ecclesiastical writer), *Expositio Psalmorum*: Ps 118 Caph
  is sung by *peregrinus in hac terra populus*; on the full v. 84 the
  assembly of the saints asks *quando mundus iste finietur*, so that the
  vengeance prepared for the persecutors, *id est diabolo cum ministris*, be
  completed — *non quia vitae suae tempus volebat agnoscere: sed celeritatem
  futuri iudicii postulabat*. His comment bears directly on the sung *quando
  facies … iudicium?* and expressly sets aside the counting of one's own
  days. On Ps 113:1 the exodus is release *a peccatorum vinculis* and from
  *a daemonum turba*; on Ps 89:1 the Church, *quae in hac saeculi tempestate
  versatur*, has the Lord as its refuge (a reading he reaches through the
  unsung title's Moses).
- **The exposition printed under Athanasius's name** (PG 27, page images;
  attribution the printing's; contributing only). On the sung Ps 118:81,
  *Ecquid aliud fuerit salutare Dei, nisi adventus Domini?* and *in verbum
  tuum … intellige, in promissionem* (cols. 493D–495A); on the sung v. 86b,
  the one persecuted unjustly, *gnarus se in divino tribunali mercedem
  accepturum*, *gestit illud tempus videre* (col. 495C). On the sung Ps 89:1,
  God was refuge *generatione* under Pharaoh, under Joshua and in Babylon
  (col. 395B–C). Its comment on v. 84 is said of the unsung 84a and is used
  in reading 1, not here.
- **Liturgical commentators on this formulary.** Fromage, XI pp. 455–463: *the
  near approach of the general judgment … is the very soul of the Liturgy
  during these last Sundays*; the evil day *evil beyond all the others … the
  last day*; Gradual and Alleluia: *preparing the last exodus of the Church,
  which is leaving this world … and is hastening to the true land of promise*;
  at the Gospel, the *Dies irae*. Schuster, III p. 181: the Communion's
  enemies already judged; St Michael and Antichrist. At this Gospel's own locus:
  Honorius IV.93 (*Christus rex verus tunc ad iudicium veniet*) and Durandus
  VI.139 (*in diei iudicii ponet Dominus rationem nobiscum*).

Carrying authors: **Augustine**, **John Chrysostom** (Fathers); contributing:
Aquinas, Bellarmine, Gregory, the exposition printed under Athanasius's name;
Cassiodorus (ecclesiastical writer); Fromage and Schuster for the Mass.

### 3.3 The difficulty followed through

**Words.** *quando facies de persequentibus me iudicium? iniqui persecuti sunt
me, adiuva me* — a plea for judgment on persecutors, sung after a Gospel that
demands forgiveness.

**Inferences.** Augustine reads the plea for judgment as the martyrs' (Rev
6:10–11), answered by *long-suffering … until the number of their brethren be
fulfilled*, and takes the question about the judgment to show that the Church
remains on earth until it (*Enarr.* 118.84, the part of his comment that
bears on the sung half). Elsewhere he asks whether the martyrs plead *against
the men themselves or against the dominion of sin*, and calls the overthrow
of sin's dominion *a genuine avenging of the martyrs*, which may wish *the end
of this world* (*De serm. Dom.* I.22.77); *Christ is not yet avenged, the holy
martyrs are not yet avenged. Still doth the patience of God wait … Forgive
therefore* (*Sermon* 58.8). Bellarmine reads the sung question as the desire
for everlasting life, when the wicked cease to harass, and expressly not as a
curious inquiry into the number of days.

**What follows.** The Communion is not a counter-command to the Gospel; it is
the Church's longing for the end, which God's patience delays for the
conversion of enemies, so that the Church forgives *while* it waits. Hilary's
present-tense reading (reading 1) and Augustine's end-directed reading are
both kept; they differ in the time of the judgment, and each draws his time
from his own reading of the unsung v. 84a (`research/scope.md` 4.4). This
reading asks *when*, as the Communion sings; for its answer it rests on what
Augustine, Cassiodorus and Bellarmine say of the sung v. 84b and on the PG 27
exposition at the sung v. 86b, and it neither needs nor uses the unsung
*Quot sunt dies*.

### 3.5 The four senses

- **Literal.** Israel went out of Egypt (*In exitu Israel de Aegypto*);
  Mardochai prays for a people under sentence of death; the psalmist, hoping
  in God's word, asks when God will judge his persecutors and begs help
  (*quando facies de persequentibus me iudicium? … adiuva me*); Paul tells the
  Ephesians to resist *in die malo*; the king *voluit rationem ponere*.
- **Allegorical.** The Exodus is the Church's leaving the world, led by Jesus
  (Chrysostom), its Passover Christ; the Church, whose saints desire Christ's
  manifestation to judge, asks when the judgment comes and remains on earth
  until it (Augustine, *Enarr.* 118.81 and 84, on the sung words); the
  eternal God is its refuge from the mutability of time (Augustine, *Enarr.*
  89.2).
- **Moral.** Live as on a march and in a camp; do not build in the desert
  (Chrysostom); withdraw in heart from Egypt by God's mercy (Augustine);
  forgive while God's patience waits (Augustine *Sermon* 58.8).
- **Anagogical.** The judgment the Communion asks for and the king's
  reckoning completed at it (Augustine; Aquinas *Super Matth.* 18); the
  promised land; the food of immortality fulfilled in life everlasting
  (Bellarmine's *desire of life everlasting* on v. 84; Fromage).

### 3.6 Limits

- Ps 118:84a (*Quot sunt dies servi tui?*) and Ps 89:3–12, *numerare dies
  nostros* among them, are not sung in this formulary. Augustine's *the body
  of Christ … asking concerning its days* is his comment on the full verse; a
  study may report it, attributed and marked as said of words this Mass does
  not sing, but may not make it the Communion's question, the reading's image
  or an overview sense.
- Augustine's *laudable* failing (v. 81) is said of *Defecit*, which the
  Missal omits; his *persecuted nothing save the truth* (v. 86) leans on the
  unsung *Omnia mandata tua veritas*. Both are reported as his, with that
  mark.
- Fromage's *the very soul of the Liturgy during these last Sundays* is his
  judgment about the season; a study may attribute it, not adopt it as the
  formulary's intention.
- Honorius's and Durandus's eschatological readings of the parable belong to
  their reading of Mt 18 at its own locus; they may be cited for the parable,
  not for its pairing with these chants.
- The Introit–Alleluia link through Esth 13:16 is a textual observation about
  unsung context; it is not a claim about why the chants were chosen.

### 3.7 Agreements and differences within the reading

Augustine and Chrysostom agree that the Exodus is the Christian's departure;
Augustine emphasizes the heart's withdrawal by mercy, Chrysostom the march and
the food. Augustine's eschatological reading of Ps 118:84 differs from Hilary's
present-tense reading (used in reading 1); Bellarmine stands with Augustine,
and Cassiodorus joins Hilary's persecutors (the devil and his ministers) to
Augustine's time (the end of the world). The exposition printed under
Athanasius's name holds both: on the unsung v. 84a a present crushing of
Satan, on the sung v. 86b a longing for the divine tribunal. A study that
uses this reading reports Hilary's difference (with its ground in the unsung
v. 84a) and does not present the end-directed reading as the only one.
Theodoret is censured and is not offered as a voice in this comparison; his
literal setting (David persecuted, a short life) is context only
(`research/scope.md` 3.10).

## 4. Comparison

### 4.1 What the three share

All three read the formulary under one sovereign God who is refuge, and all
three need the Gospel's mercy and the Communion's plea to be heard together.
All three use Chrysostom; two use Augustine; Gregory and Aquinas serve readings
1 and 3.

### 4.2 Where they differ

| | Reading 1 | Reading 2 | Reading 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question | Who is the enemy, and how is his work within God's will? | What does the king's mercy require, and does pardon return? | When does the judgment asked for (*quando facies … iudicium?*) come, and where is the people meanwhile? |
| Governing element | Offertory with Introit and Epistle | Gospel | Communion with Alleluia and Postcommunion |
| Decisive inference | Permission and bridle (Gregory; Aquinas *Super Iob*); the spirits behind men (Chrysostom; Hilary) | The receiver revokes the gift; forgive from the heart (Chrysostom; Augustine) | The Church's exodus; the martyrs' plea for judgment answered by long-suffering, the Church remaining on earth until the judgment (Augustine on the sung v. 84b; Chrysostom) |
| Identification of the persecutors (Communion) | The devil and his ministers (Hilary on the sung v. 84b, with Eph 6:12); the judgment asked is the power of Lk 10:19 to crush them, asked in this life — the *in this life* resting on Hilary's reading of the unsung v. 84a | The dominion of sin; men to be forgiven (Augustine) | The Church's persecutors until the judgment (Augustine *Enarr.* 118 on the sung v. 84b) |
| Consequence | Steadfastness; hostility turned from men to the devil | Forgiving every debt, with correction without rancour | Pilgrim readiness; forgiving while God's patience waits |

### 4.3 How they relate

Readings 1 and 2 meet in Chrysostom's own join (the wronged man pours his
anger on the devil): reading 1 makes the enemy the adversary, reading 2 makes
the brother the forgiven debtor. They are complementary; they are distinct
because reading 1's argument would stand without the parable (it rests on
Introit, Epistle, Offertory, Communion) and reading 2's would stand without
the Offertory's permission. Reading 3 differs in its question (when the
judgment comes, not who the enemy is or what the debt requires) and in its
reading of the Communion (Augustine against Hilary on the time of the
judgment): a real but narrow disagreement, not a conflict of doctrine.
Hilary affirms the final judgment and the devil's judgment at the end; what
divides him from Augustine is what the prophet asks *now*, and that he draws
from the unsung v. 84a. On the sung v. 84b reading 1 rests on Hilary's
identification of the persecutors and the power asked, reading 3 on
Augustine's martyrs' plea answered by long-suffering; neither rests on the
editors' summary or, without saying so, on the unsung half.

### 4.4 The appointed texts' most demanding features, and where each is met

- *Non est qui possit resistere voluntati tuae* beside Satan's petition and the
  servant's refusal: reading 1 (1.3), with the witnesses' different formulas
  kept (`research/scope.md` 4.1).
- The reversed pardon and *universum debitum*: reading 2 (2.3), with
  Aquinas's distinction kept (`research/scope.md` 4.2).
- The plea for judgment on persecutors after a Gospel of forgiveness: readings
  1 and 3 (1.3, 3.3), Hilary and Augustine kept apart, with Cassiodorus and
  the exposition printed under Athanasius's name beside them, and the
  dependence of each witness's *when* on the unsung v. 84a stated
  (`research/scope.md` 4.4). Theodoret is context only.
- The Introit's source in the Greek chapters of Esther and the Missal's own
  wording: textual history for the expansive study (`propers/verified.md`).
- The Offertory's narrative is not a Scripture text but a summary: all three
  readings cite Job by its chapters, not the chant as a verse.

### 4.5 The Introit verse Ps 118:1 in the three readings

Reading 1: the soldier's undefiled way; reading 2: Augustine's admission that
those who walk undefiled still confess sin (*Enarr.* 118 s. 1.3), which keeps
the debtor universal; reading 3: the people on the way. Only Augustine was read
on the verse (`research/scope.md` 3.2).

### 4.6 Notes for the concise study's page-1 overview

A four-row overview may draw: Literal — Mardochai's prayer, Paul's armour,
Job's trial, the king's reckoning; Allegorical — Christ the armour and the
king, Job the type of Christ and the Church, the Church's exodus; Moral —
stand against the adversary, forgive the brother from the heart; Anagogical —
the judgment the Communion asks for (*quando facies de persequentibus me
iudicium?*) and the king's reckoning, the food of immortality. Each row must
keep the readings' differences rather than merge them. Ps 118:84a (*Quot sunt
dies servi tui?*) and Ps 89's numbering of days are not sung in this
formulary and supply no overview sense.

### 4.7 Notes for the homily

The most speakable single argument is reading 2 sharpened by reading 1's
redirect: *our wrestling is not against flesh and blood* — the man who wronged
me is my fellow debtor, the enemy is the one who wants my grudge; forgiving
from the heart is how the forgiven stand in the evil day. Textual details that
make the turn possible: *Patientiam habe in me* said twice in the same words;
the servant asked for time and received remission; *de cordibus vestris*;
*non est nobis colluctatio adversus carnem et sanguinem*; *quod ore percepimus,
pura mente sectemur*. Chrysostom (*Hom. in Matth.* 61; *Hom. in Eph.* 22) and
Augustine (*Sermon* 83) can be named naturally. No anecdote, and no Father's
approval of the editor's joins.

## 6. The commemoration and the added oration

The oration *pro Fidei propagatione* is said in every Mass this Sunday unless
the Ordinary moved the mission day; St Luke is commemorated in low and
conventual Masses only (`research/context.md`). No reading depends on either.
A study may note, as rubrical fact, that the added Collect's *qui omnes
homines vis salvos fieri* (1 Tim 2:4) is prayed beside the Introit's *non est
qui possit resistere voluntati tuae*; any theological comment on that pair
must rest on a checked witness (Rupert's use of Augustine's *De spiritu et
littera* is a lead only, `research/scope.md` 5.2).
