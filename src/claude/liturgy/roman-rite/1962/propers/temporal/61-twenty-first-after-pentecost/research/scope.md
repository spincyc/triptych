## 1. The formulary and the textual facts the research rests on

Ten appointed elements, nos. 1699–1708 of the 1962 Missal, printed pp.
413–415: Introit Esth 13:9–11 (an adapted text) with Ps 118:1; Collect
*Familiam tuam*; Epistle Eph 6:10–17; Gradual Ps 89:1–2; Alleluia Ps 113:1;
Gospel Mt 18:23–35; Offertory *Vir erat in terra Hus* (a narrative cento cited
*Iob 1*); Secret *Suscipe, Domine, propitius hostias*; Communion Ps 118:81, 84,
86 (adapted); Postcommunion *Immortalitatis alimoniam*. The rubrics add the
oration *pro Fidei propagatione* in every Mass and, in a low or conventual
Mass, the commemoration of St Luke (`research/context.md`). Neither added set
belongs to the formulary.

### 1.1 Threads inside the appointed text

Textual observations only; whether any pairing was designed is not asked
(the three-document profile settles that every formulary is a compilation).

- **Will and power.** The Introit begins *In voluntate tua … universa sunt
  posita, et non est qui possit resistere voluntati tuae*. The Offertory says
  of Satan *quem Satan petiit, ut tentaret: et data est ei potestas a Domino*.
  The Gospel's king *voluit rationem ponere*. The verb *volo* and the giving of
  power by the Lord run through three elements; the Introit's claim that no one
  resists God's will stands beside an adversary who asks and receives power and
  a servant who refuses the king's mercy. This is the formulary's principal
  theological difficulty (section 4.1).
- **The adversary and the wrestling.** The Epistle names the *insidias
  diaboli* and a *colluctatio* not *adversus carnem et sanguinem* but against
  *principes … potestates … rectores tenebrarum … spiritualia nequitiae*; the
  Offertory names *Satan*; the Communion names *persequentibus* and *iniqui*;
  the Collect asks freedom *a cunctis adversitatibus*; the Gospel's king hands
  the servant *tortoribus*.
- **Protection and refuge.** Collect *custodi … te protegente*; Gradual
  *refugium factus es nobis*; Communion *adiuva me*; Epistle *armaturam Dei*.
- **Debt, reckoning, judgment.** Gospel *rationem ponere … debitum … donec
  redderet … quoadusque redderet universum debitum*; Communion *quando facies
  de persequentibus me iudicium?*; Epistle *in die malo*.
- **Mercy, patience, release.** Gospel *Patientiam habe in me* (twice),
  *misertus … dimisit*; Secret *propitius … potenti pietate*; Collect *continua
  pietate*; Postcommunion *immortalitatis alimoniam*.
- **Mouth and heart.** Gospel *de cordibus vestris*; Postcommunion *quod ore
  percepimus, pura mente sectemur*; Epistle *in veritate*; Introit verse
  *immaculati in via*. The Postcommunion's *ore … pura mente* has the shape of
  the Order of Mass's *Quod ore sumpsimus, Domine, pura mente capiamus* (no.
  1130, `propers/verified.md`), where the object is the sacrament received.
- **Creation and eternity.** Introit *tu enim fecisti omnia, caelum et terram
  … Dominus universorum tu es*; Gradual *Priusquam montes fierent … a saeculo
  et usque in saeculum tu es, Deus*.
- **Israel delivered.** Alleluia *In exitu Israel de Aegypto*; the Introit's
  speaker, Mardochai, prays for Israel under sentence (section 1.2).
- **Walking and standing.** Introit verse *ambulant in lege Domini*; Epistle
  *stare … perfecti stare … State ergo*; Gospel *procidens* (twice).

### 1.2 Threads in the unsung context of the appointed verses

- **Esther.** Mardochai's prayer (Esth 13:8–18 Clementine) is said when Aman's
  decree has condemned the Jews of the Persian empire (Esth 3; 13:1–7, the
  decree's text in the Greek chapters). Its unsung lines refuse to adore Aman
  (13:12–14), ask *miserere populi tui* (13:15), recall *partem tuam, quam
  redemisti tibi de Aegypto* (13:16) and ask *ut viventes laudemus nomen tuum
  … et ne claudas ora te canentium* (13:17). The prayer recalls the Exodus that
  the Alleluia sings. The prayer stands in the Clementine's chapters 10:4–16:24,
  which Jerome placed after the Hebrew book's end; Esth 11:1 is a colophon
  dating the delivery of the Greek *epistolam phurim* to the fourth year of a
  Ptolemy and Cleopatra. Rabanus Maurus states that he expounds only the
  Hebrew chapters and leaves the obelized Greek additions unexpounded
  (*Expositio in librum Esther*, preface, PL 109, 635C–D), yet he quotes the
  Introit's sentence at XI (664B–C) (section 3.1).
- **Ephesians.** The Epistle stops at v. 17; vv. 18–20 ask for prayer *omni
  tempore in spiritu* and for Paul *pro quo legatione fungor in catena*. The
  letter is written from captivity (3:1; 4:1; 6:20).
- **Matthew.** The Missal's opening replaces *Ideo*, so Peter's question and
  *septuagies septies* (18:21–22) are unsung. The discourse of chapter 18 is
  spoken after the arrival at Capernaum (17:23 Clementine) *in illa hora* (18:1)
  and closes before Jesus *migravit a Galilaea* (19:1).
- **Job.** The Offertory narrates Job 1:1, 1:12, 1:13–19 and 2:6–7 in its own
  words; the unsung context includes the heavenly council (1:6–11; 2:1–5),
  Job's *Dominus dedit, Dominus abstulit* (1:21) and *In omnibus his non
  peccavit Job labiis suis* (1:22; 2:10). The land of Hus is joined to Edom by
  Lam 4:21 (*filia Edom, quae habitas in terra Hus*).
- **Psalm 89.** The Gradual sings vv. 1–2 only. Unsung: the title *Oratio
  Moysi, hominis Dei* and vv. 3–17, among them man turned to dust (v. 3), a
  thousand years as yesterday (v. 4), the shortness of life (vv. 5–10) and
  *numerare dies nostros* (v. 12).
- **Psalm 113.** The verse opens a psalm of the Red Sea and the Jordan (vv.
  3–6); the Vulgate's Ps 113 joins Hebrew Pss 114 and 115.
- **Psalm 118.** The Communion comes from the eleventh section (Caph, vv.
  81–88) and sings only v. 81 without its verb *Defecit* (*In salutari tuo
  anima mea, et in verbum tuum speravi*), v. 84b (*quando facies de
  persequentibus me iudicium?*) and v. 86b (*iniqui persecuti sunt me, adiuva
  me*), adding *Domine Deus meus* (`propers/verified.md`). Unsung: the
  soul's fainting (*Defecit*, v. 81), vv. 82–83 (the eyes failing, the
  wineskin in the frost), v. 84a *Quot sunt dies servi tui?*, v. 85 (the
  wicked telling tales), v. 86a *Omnia mandata tua veritas*, and vv. 87–88
  (*paulo minus consummaverunt me in terra*; *secundum misericordiam tuam
  vivifica me*). A study may use any of these only as unsung context, so
  labelled, and may not say that the Communion asks how many are the
  servant's days.

### 2.3 The Offertory's verses

The 1962 and 1862 Missals print only the antiphon *Vir erat* (verified). The
commentators read the Offertory with its verses, and the verses carry their
interpretation: Honorius (IV.91) and Sicard (VIII.21) explain the repetition of
words in the verses as the speech of the sick Job, whose words break off and
are begun again; Honorius adds *Per Job autem Christus designatur, qui pro
nobis doluit*; Durandus refers the repetition to an earlier chapter; Fromage
prints the verses entire and cites Amalarius, *De ecclesiasticis officiis*
III.39, for the distinction between the historian's antiphon and Job's own
words in the verses; Schuster quotes *ut videam bona* repeated and calls the
1962 antiphon *a mere narrative* without its *primitive antiphonic hemistichs*.
The verses are not part of the formulary. A study may cite these commentators
for their reading of the Offertory's narrative and of Job, and may say that
the older chant books carried verses; it may not quote the verses as
appointed text.

### 2.4 Continuity from 1862 to 1962

Every element stands in the 1862 Pustet in the same words (`propers/verified.md`).
The 1862 adds pre-1960 second and third orations; the 1962 formulary has none.

### 3.1 Introit — Esth 13:9–11 (adapted), no. 1699

- **Direct ancient exegesis.** None located. The *Catholic Encyclopedia*
  article *Esther* (fetched 2026-10-15; see section 7.3) says that among the
  early Fathers *no one among them found it to his purpose to write a
  commentary on it*. No Greek or Latin patristic commentary on Esther is
  registered.
- **Rabanus Maurus (saint)**, *Expositio in librum Esther*, PL 109, tracked
  Latin text (`…2026-10-12-migne-pl109-wikisource.esther-text-6ee8f395`), read
  on the text layer:
  - preface (635C–D): Esther *in Ecclesiae typo populum de periculo liberat*;
    Aman *interpretatur iniquitas*; he expounds the chapters *ex Hebraeorum
    fonte* and passes over the Greek additions, *obelo praenotata* — which
    include the Introit's prayer;
  - XI (664B–C): *Et edictum regis pependit in Susis* signifies the heavenly
    king's rule over the whole world, *in cujus ditione cuncta sunt posita,
    nec est qui possit resistere voluntati ejus (Esth. XIII)*; *superbia mundi
    superatur potentia Dei*; John 16:33 *ego vici mundum*;
  - VI (652B–C): Aman signifies the pride of the powerful of this world who
    persecute those who refuse them divine honour; XIII (668D–669A): Aman *qui
    hostes praefigurat Ecclesiae*.
  Use: a direct, brief witness to the Introit's sentence applied to God's
  sovereignty over the persecutors of his people. His standing (saint) lets him
  carry a reading with a Father.
- **Aquinas (Doctor)**, *Summa theologiae* I q. 103 a. 7 *sed contra*, English
  Dominican translation, tracked (`…summa-theologiae…pg17611-body-9c99a67a`,
  lines 47145–47190): *It is written (Esther 13:9): "O Lord, Lord, almighty
  King, all things are in Thy power, and there is none that can resist Thy
  will"*, to prove that nothing occurs outside the order of the divine
  government; the body distinguishes particular causes from the universal
  cause. Use: doctrinal illumination of the Introit's claim.
- **Rupert of Deutz (ecclesiastical writer)**, *De omnipotentia Dei* 9–10,
  PL 170, cols. 461–462 (tracked OCR, lines 33360–33440): *Et non est qui
  possit resistere voluntati tuae* (he cites it as Rom 9) is to be completed
  *et pacem habere*, with Job 9:4 and Gregory's *Moralia* on it; chapter 10
  quotes Augustine, *De spiritu et littera*, that unbelievers act against God's
  will but do not conquer it (*voluntas Dei semper invicta est*). Use: the
  answer to the Introit's difficulty, through Gregory, whom he quotes (Gregory
  read directly, below); Augustine's *De spiritu et littera* was not read in
  this stage and stays a lead.
- **Gregory the Great (Father)**, *Moralia* IX.v.5 on Job 9:4 (*Who hath
  resisted Him, and had peace?*), Oxford English, tracked
  (`…moralia-in-iob…complete-text-d9f9ed86`, lines 14355–14385): whatever
  resists the Creator breaks its own peace; the apostate angel and Adam lost
  peace by resisting. Use: the answer Rupert adopts, read at its source.
- **Liturgical commentators.** Durandus VI.138: the Church speaks *voce
  Mardochaei in cilicio et cinere*, sackcloth and ashes being *arma
  poenitentiae*; *Dominus universorum tu es: et ideo beati qui tibi serviunt:
  quia tu liberas eos de manu diaboli, et adiuvas eos in bello contra
  diabolum: sicut … liberasti Mardochaeum de manu Aman*. Honorius IV.90: the
  Sunday *de historia Esther inofficiatur, per quam populus Dei ab imminenti
  periculo liberatur*. Rupert XII.21: the Introit is *vox … ejusdem militis*,
  the soldier of the virtues, falling with Job to the earth in humility and
  saying *Dominus dedit, Dominus abstulit*. Fromage (pp. 455–456): the Church
  makes her own Mardochai's prayer for a people doomed to extermination; the
  closing petition of the prayer (*ut viventes laudemus nomen tuum*) explains
  the Collect's wish for peace. Schuster (pp. 178–179): Mardochai's humble
  prayer; the evil God permits serves Providence, *like the devil, who,
  despitefully winnowing the grain, renders a helpful service to the great
  Householder*.
- **Searched and not found.** Augustine, *De gratia et libero arbitrio* and the
  Anti-Pelagian works were not opened; the library search for the English
  *resist thy will* in tracked texts returned the Summa, Bellarmine (a
  different phrase), Lasance, the Douay and NPNF1-6 (Augustine, *Sermon* 56,
  NPNF *Sermon* 6, on the Lord's Prayer, line 26183: *that I may not resist Thy
  will*, unrelated to Esther).
- **Not used.** Cornelius a Lapide on Esther is not registered (his held
  records are on the Pauline Epistles and the Pentateuch).

### 3.2 Introit verse — Ps 118:1, no. 1699

- **Augustine (Father)**, *Enarr. in Ps.* 118, sermon 1 (Aleph) 1–3, NPNF1-8,
  tracked (lines 56727–56790): bliss is what all desire; *if thou wouldest be
  blessed, be undefiled … In what way, save in the law of the Lord?*; the
  saints walk in the Lord's ways yet say *If we say that we have no sin, we
  deceive ourselves* (1 Jn 1:8–9). Use: the verse's moral sense; and the

### 3.5 Gradual — Ps 89:1–2, no. 1702

- **Augustine (Father)**, *Enarr. in Ps.* 89, NPNF1-8, tracked (lines
  45083–45160), and the tracked Latin (`…wikisource-part-9-latin-text`, line
  196): the title names Moses, *Minister of the Old, and the Prophet of the New
  Testament*; *from one generation to another* is either every generation or
  the old and the new; *it is not to be believed that this Psalm was entirely
  the composition of that Moses* (line 45103); God *hast become our refuge ever
  since we turned to Thee*; *from age to age Thou art* in the present tense,
  *the eternity that is our refuge, that we may fly thither from the
  mutability of time* (line 45151); v. 3 is the prayer *Lead us not into
  temptation*. His Latin lemma reads *in generatione et generatione*.
- **Bellarmine (Doctor)**, *Explanation of the Psalms*, O'Sullivan, tracked
  (lines 3209–3226): *The Hebrew for refuge signifies a well fortified house,
  placed on an eminence*; God *Thou art* to show eternity without past or
  future (v. 2, sung); the shortness of man's life after the fall (vv. 3–7,
  unsung context).
- **Bellarmine on the authorship of Ps 89** (the same text, two prefaces, read
  in this repair pass). Both prefaces first refute the Hebrew rule that an
  untitled psalm belongs to the author of the one before it: under that rule
  the ten untitled psalms after Ps 89 would be Moses', and Ps 98:6 names Samuel
  as one who went before the writer. That refutation concerns Pss 90–99, not
  Ps 89. On the titled psalms themselves he then gives his own judgment:
  - Preface II §4 (physical line 61; a preface the translator's note, citing
    the 1931 critical edition, says survives only in manuscript and was not in
    the original printed edition; English by Michael J. Miller): *As for the
    remaining Psalms, which bear in their titles the name of Asaph or Ethan or
    Moses or others, it is not unlikely that those named in the titles were the
    authors, as Saint Athanasius maintains in the Synopsis, Saint Hilary in the
    Preface to the Psalms, and Saint Jerome in the Preface to the Psalms to
    Sophronius and in his letter to Cyprian on Psalm 89.* For Ps 89, whose
    title is *Oratio Moysi, hominis Dei*, Mosaic authorship is therefore *not
    unlikely* in his judgment.
  - Preface I §5 (physical lines 45 and 47, the printed preface): he sets out
    two patristic opinions — named authors in the titles (Athanasius, Hilary,
    Jerome, the last also in the letter to Cyprian on Ps 89) and David as sole
    author (Chrysostom, Theodoret, Euthymius, Cassiodorus, Augustine *De civ.
    Dei* XVII.14) — and concludes of the psalms *which bear the title Moses or
    Solomon or Asaph or Idithun or Ethan or the sons of Core*: *I consider as
    acceptable the opinion of Athanasius, Hilary and Jerome, but more probable
    that of Chrysostom, Augustine, Theodoret*, because it is the more common;
    the reason he then argues (singers named in titles because the psalms were
    given them to sing) is stated of Asaph, Idithun, Ethan and the sons of
    Core, not of Moses.
  Use: in Preface II Bellarmine supports the title's attribution of Ps 89 to
  Moses as *not unlikely*; in Preface I he admits it as *acceptable* while
  preferring, for all the titled psalms alike, the Davidic view as *more
  probable*. He gives no reason specific to Ps 89 against Moses. His judgment
  stands against Augustine's *Enarr.* 89.2 (above, and section 4.7). A study
  that cites him for Moses must keep his probability language and not make him
  hold it as certain; the expansive study, if it reports Preface I, keeps the
  *more probable* preference beside it.
  The authorities he names — Athanasius's *Synopsis*, Hilary's preface
  (*Instructio Psalmorum*), Jerome's letter to Sophronius and his letter to
  Cyprian on Ps 89, and on the other side Augustine *De civ. Dei* XVII.14 and
  Theodoret's preface — were not read; they are leads (section 5.2).
- **Cassiodorus (ecclesiastical writer)**, *Expositio Psalmorum*, Ps 89
  (Corpus Corporum web text of the registered Corpus Corporum edition's
  route, fetched 2026-10-15 in iteration 1, unregistered delivery, SHA-256

  sinners*: dying in sin, they die *debtors to God*, cannot satisfy in the

### 3.9 Secret — *Suscipe, Domine, propitius hostias*, no. 1706

Composed text. Verbal and doctrinal observations only: *hostias quibus et te
placari voluisti* (the sacrifice by which God willed to be appeased) and
*salutem potenti pietate restitui*. Schuster (p. 181) reads it of the
Eucharistic sacrifice that appeases divine justice and restores the primitive
state, mercy triumphing over judgment (Jas 2:13). Fromage (p. 470): the
salvation of the world is virtually in the Sacrifice, *whose power restores
man, by appeasing God*. Chrysostom's *God, whom by mere prayer thou hast
reconciled* (*Hom. in Matth.* 61.4) is said of the servant's plea, not of the
Secret; any joining is editorial synthesis.

### 3.11 Postcommunion — *Immortalitatis alimoniam*, no. 1708

Composed text. Verbal observations: *immortalitatis alimoniam*; *quod ore

## 4. Material disagreements preserved

### 4.1 Can anything resist God's will?

The Introit says no one can resist God's will; the Offertory shows Satan
asking and receiving power; the Gospel shows a servant who refuses the mercy
he received. The witnesses do not answer in the same way:

- **Gregory** (*Moralia* IX.5): resistance is possible, but whoever resists
  has no peace; the resister confounds himself (Satan, Adam). On Job (Preface
  7; II.19), what the devil asks for Job's ruin God permits for his proving,
  and God bridles the enemy he looses.
- **Rupert** (*De omnipotentia Dei* 9–10) adopts Gregory's completion *et
  pacem habere* and adds, through Augustine, that the will of God is resisted
  but not conquered.
- **Aquinas** (ST I q. 103 a. 7, with Esth 13:9): nothing escapes the order of
  the universal cause; in *Super Iob* 1, Satan departs from the intention of
  Providence without escaping its order; power to harm comes only from God.
- **Schuster**: the malice of creatures enters the plan of Providence, which
  uses even its enemies.
These are complementary emphases (peace lost; order not escaped; malice used),
not a dispute. A study should keep each witness's own formula.

### 4.2 Does forgiveness once given return?

- **Chrysostom** (61.4): the lord did not revoke the gift; *the one who had
  received it* revoked it; the servant is delivered *for ever*.
- **Augustine** (*Sermon* 83.7): God says *I will call thee back, and put upon
  thee again all that I had remitted to thee*.
- **Jerome**: a *formidolosa sententia* if God's sentence bends to our mind.
- **Aquinas** (*Super Matth.* 18): Origen held that forgiven sins return;
  Thomas answers that remission has its efficacy from the sacraments, and sins
  are said to return *per ingratitudinem*.
- **Rabanus**, quoting Gregory: what was forgiven is exacted again if we do not
  forgive from the heart.
A real difference of formulation (Augustine's and Gregory's *exacted again*;
Aquinas's denial that forgiven sins return as such) that a study must not
flatten. Aquinas names Origen as holding the stronger view; Origen's text was
not read for this point.

### 4.3 Who is the debtor of ten thousand talents?

- **Origen** (XIV.10–11): perhaps *the man of sin* or *the devil*, in a higher
  sense he declines to fix.
- **Jerome**: *Scio quosdam … diabolum interpretari*; such a reading *nec
  Ecclesiasticae interpretationis est, nec a prudentibus viris recipiendae*.
- **Augustine** and **Chrysostom**: every man, and the hearer.
- **Rabanus**: allegorically the Jewish people, the fellow servant the Gentile
  people; morally (through Jerome) every one.
- **Aquinas**: every man, and in particular prelates.
A real disagreement. The readings use the moral identification (Augustine,
Chrysostom, Jerome) and may report Rabanus's allegory as his; the devil reading
is reported only as what Jerome rejects.

### 4.4 Who are the persecutors of the Communion?

- **Hilary** (*Tract. in Ps.* 118, Caph 5, cols. 573C–574B, his own text,
  not the editors' italic summary): on the sung v. 84b, the persecutors are
  *diabolus … ministrique ejus* (Eph 6:12), and the judgment asked is the
  power of Lk 10:19, that the saint *conterat intra se diabolum*. He knows
  the judgment *post consummationem saeculi* (Ps 1:5) and that the devil is
  to be judged *in resurrectionis saeculo*; what the prophet asks *now* is
  not that judgment, because he knows his days are few. That last step rests
  on the unsung v. 84a, *Quot sunt dies servi tui?*, read as *non plures*
  (with v. 83, also unsung).
- **Augustine** (*Enarr.* 118.84): the persecutors of the martyrs and of the
  Church until the judgment; (*De serm. Dom.* I.22.77) the martyrs' plea may be
  against the *dominion of sin*.
- **Bellarmine**: the wicked who tempt and persecute, whose cessation comes
  with life everlasting.
- **Aquinas** (*Super Eph.* 6, on Ps 118:157, not appointed): men are the
  instruments, the devil the principal agent; applied to the Communion only
  by editorial synthesis.
- **Honorius**: Mardochai thanks God for judgment done on his persecutors.
- **Cassiodorus** (*Expositio* 118, Caph, v. 84): the devil and his ministers,
  as Hilary; but the judgment asked is the future one, at the end of the
  world, *non quia vitae suae tempus volebat agnoscere*.
- **The exposition printed under Athanasius's name** (PG 27, cols.
  495B–496C): on the unsung v. 84a (the lemma prints only 84a), few are the
  days of men on earth, in which he prays that Satan be crushed at once under
  his feet; on the sung v. 86b, knowing he will be recompensed at the divine
  tribunal for what he suffered unjustly, he longs to see that time. It holds
  both a present crushing of Satan (from the unsung half) and a longing for
  the final tribunal (on the sung v. 86b).
- **Context only: Theodoret** (censured, 3.5; PG 80, cols. 1847–1848): the
  literal-historical setting, David persecuted by Saul (v. 83) within a short
  life (v. 84a, unsung); recorded as context, not as a voice in this
  disagreement.
Complementary for the most part. The witnesses differ on the time of the
judgment asked, and the difference is driven by how each reads the unsung
v. 84a: Hilary takes *Quot sunt dies* as *few days* and so the judgment asked
as present power over the devil, while affirming the final judgment; the
exposition under Athanasius's name likewise draws a present crushing of Satan
from the few days; Augustine takes it as the body of Christ asking about its
days and so the judgment as the end, until which the Church remains;
Cassiodorus and Bellarmine expressly deny that the verse asks about the length
of one's life and take the judgment as the end. On the sung v. 84b alone the
difference is narrower: Hilary and Cassiodorus agree that the persecutors are
the devil and his ministers; Hilary reads the judgment asked as power given
now, Cassiodorus, Augustine and Bellarmine as the end. The readings keep both
and say, wherever Hilary's *now* is used, that it rests on the unsung half.

### 4.5 What is the *dies malus*?

- **Chrysostom** (Hom. 22): the present life.
- **Fromage**: the evil days of each life, and above all *the last day*.
- **Aquinas**: the evils that happen in the day.
A difference of emphasis used by reading 3.

### 4.6 Whose voice is the Introit?

- **Durandus**: the Church in Mardochai's voice, in sackcloth and ashes.
- **Rupert**: the tried soldier, humbled with Job.
- **Honorius**: God's people in Esther's history.
- **Fromage**: the holy men in a world condemned like Mardochai's people.
Complementary.

### 4.7 Who wrote Ps 89?

The Gradual sings Ps 89:1–2 without the title *Oratio Moysi, hominis Dei*;
the question bears on the page-2 chronology and on any study sentence about
the psalm's author, not on any reading's argument.

- **The title** names Moses.
- **Bellarmine** (Preface II §4): it is *not unlikely* that those named in the
  titles, Moses among them, were the authors, with Athanasius, Hilary and
  Jerome (letter to Cyprian on Ps 89); (Preface I §5) that opinion is
  *acceptable*, the Davidic-sole-author opinion *more probable*.
- **Augustine** (*Enarr.* 89.2): *it is not to be believed that this Psalm was
  entirely the composition of that Moses*; the name serves *some intimation*.
- **The exposition printed under Athanasius's name** (PG 27, cols. 395B /
  396A–B, *Hypothesis*): *Hanc, ait, orationem in arcanis descripsisse
  Moysen*, for those who would fall into calamities *in extremis temporibus*
  after disobeying and killing the Lord. For Moses, under its printed
  attribution, reported in catena form (*ait*). It is not the *Synopsis*
  that Bellarmine cites for Athanasius, which was not read.
- **Context only: Theodoret** (censured, 3.5; PG 80, cols. 1597–1598):
  reports the two received opinions, that Moses made the prayer and that
  David wrote it and fitted it to Moses; a sentence assigning it to David, as
  all the psalms, stands in brackets from one codex. Recorded as a report of
  the opinions, not as a judgment that carries weight here.
- **Cassiodorus** (*Expositio*, Ps 89): treats Moses as the one who prays the
  psalm and reads the name as interpretive; he does not argue authorship.
A real disagreement between a Doctor's probable judgment for the title (with
the PG 27 exposition's plain statement for Moses, under its printed
attribution) and a Father's judgment against Moses' entire authorship;
Theodoret's report of both opinions is context. A study keeps them
attributed, and keeps Bellarmine's probability language; no witness read
dates Moses or names an occasion (section 7.4).

### 4.8 Where the checked witnesses do not disagree

All who comment on the Epistle read the enemy as spiritual (Chrysostom,
Jerome, Aquinas, Durandus, Fromage, Schuster); all who comment on the parable
make it a lesson about forgiving from the heart; all who comment on Job read
his trial as permitted by God. No witness read disputes these.

### 7.4 The Psalms: attribution, occasion and composition kept apart

- **Ps 89.** Title *Oratio Moysi, hominis Dei* (Clementine; Douay *A prayer of
  Moses the man of God*); the Missal's Gradual does not sing the title. The
  corpus carries no attribution or occasion for Ps 89, so the projection shows
  only the collection-wide composition boundary and *Occasion date
  unresolved*. The traditional witnesses read disagree on Moses (section 4.7):
  - **For Moses:** the title itself, and Bellarmine, who judges it *not
    unlikely* that those named in the titles, Moses among them, were the
    authors (Preface II §4, line 61), and in his printed preface calls that
    opinion *acceptable* while finding the Davidic-sole-author opinion *more
    probable* (Preface I §5, lines 45–47). He names Athanasius, Hilary and
    Jerome (with Jerome's letter to Cyprian on Ps 89) as holding named
    authors; those works are leads, not read. The PG 27 exposition printed
    under Athanasius's name (cols. 395B / 396A–B) says that Moses wrote the
    prayer *in arcanis* and handed it on for those of later times; it gives
    the psalm a prophetic purpose, not an occasion or a date.
  - **Against:** Augustine (*Enarr.* 89.2, NPNF1-8 lines 45103–45108): *it is
    not to be believed that this Psalm was entirely the composition of that
    Moses*, since it lacks his songs' expressions; the name is used *for the
    sake of some intimation*.
  What the studies may say of Ps 89's attribution: the title names Moses;
  the title and Bellarmine are for Moses (Bellarmine: *not unlikely*, Preface
  II; *acceptable*, Preface I, where he prefers the Davidic view as *more
  probable* for all titled psalms); Augustine is against Moses' entire
  authorship; neither the
  corpus nor any witness read supplies a date for Moses or for an occasion of
  the psalm, so no date is printed for either, and the occasion stays
  unresolved. The page-2 row may name the title's attribution and these two
  judgments in prose; it may not give Moses a date. Reported in section 10.
- **Ps 113.** No attribution in the title beyond *Alleluja*; the corpus dates
  only the retrospective event (the Exodus) and the composition boundary.
- **Ps 118.** No title author; the corpus's Davidic attribution is received
  commentary (Haydock), shown as a reference era, with occasion unresolved.
  Augustine and Hilary name no occasion; Bellarmine speaks of *David*
  introducing one tempted.
