\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

This edition's commentary is organised by what the ten appointed texts do
together, not by the order in which they are sung. Five units carry the
evidence: the state of the text, the verse that has had the longest career,
the parable and its real reception history, the day's three prayers of
petition, and what the formulary finally asks a sacrament to do. Every
appointed element informs the whole; none receives a heading of its own.
\latinpolicynote

English quoted below is the Douay--Rheims (Challoner) for scriptural propers
and the anonymous English of the 1861 Philadelphia Cummiskey hand missal for
the three orations, both public domain in the United States and both named
again in the references. This guide has composed, translated, adapted and
paraphrased nothing.

\subsection*{1. What the books actually print, and where the appointed text is not the Bible's}

Two facts govern everything after them, and both are collation results rather
than judgments. First, ``the 1962 Latin'' is not one artefact: the Vatican
\latin{editio typica} (printed pp.~392--393) and the Benziger \latin{editio
iuxta typicam} of the same year (printed pp.~386--388) differ at eight places
in this formulary --- headings, commas, a conclusion cue, and, the one that
matters to a singer, Benziger's printed antiphon-repetition cue \latin{Deus.}
after the \latin{Glória Patri}. The typical edition governs here and nothing
is blended.

Second, and more consequential, the appointed text diverges from the
Clementine Vulgate at thirteen verified places, and the divergences are of
three different kinds. The Introit's are chant-psalter readings: the antiphon
names the enemies (\latin{inimíci mei}) where Clementine Ps.~69:3 does not,
and the verse sings \latin{qui cógitant mihi mala} --- who \textit{devise}
evils --- where the Clementine, and therefore the Douay, has \latin{qui volunt
mihi mala}. That is not a corruption but an inheritance, and it can be proved
from the expositors: Augustine's own lemma reads \latin{qui cogitant mihi
mala}, and Cassiodorus's psalter carries both of the chant's readings word for
word. The Mass sings the psalter the Fathers commented on, after Jerome's
revision displaced it in the Bible.

The Epistle's and Gospel's divergences are liturgical framing --- \latin{Fratres:}
displacing Paul's \latin{autem}, \latin{In illo témpore: Dixit Iesus
discípulis suis} replacing Luke's own turning to the disciples --- with one
real variant: the missal prints \latin{ex ómnibus víribus tuis} at Lk.~10:27
where the Clementine reads \latin{ex omnibus virtutibus tuis}, verified at
300~dpi on the facsimile and in the Benziger images. The Douay's ``with all
thy strength'' renders either and shows neither.

The Offertory's divergences are of a third kind altogether, and they are the
finding of this leaf. \latin{Precátus est Móyses} is not loose quotation of
the Vulgate but an Old-Latin, Septuagint-shaped adaptation: \latin{in conspéctu
Dómini} where Jerome has a direct object; a second-person \latin{irásceris}
where Jerome has \latin{irascitur furor tuus}; \latin{Parce iræ ánimæ tuæ},
which carries the matter of a verse the printed citation does not even name;
\latin{Iacob} for \latin{Israël}; the milk-and-honey formula in the accusative
\latin{lac et mel}, where Jerome's ablative \latin{lacte et melle} stands at
Ex.~3:8 and 33:3; and a closing clause that follows the Greek syntax. The
checkable proof that this form circulated in Latin before Jerome is
Augustine's lemma in the \work{Quaestiones in Heptateuchum}: \latin{Et
propitiatus est Dominus de malitia quam dixit facere populo suo} --- the
chant's construction, not the Vulgate's. The Communion, by contrast, is a
clean centonisation whose path the missal's own citation declares
(\latin{13 et 14-15}): v.~13's second clause inverted with an added vocative,
v.~14a omitted, then vv.~14b--15 entire. The Gradual and the Alleluia verse
match the Clementine word for word.

\subsection*{2. \latin{Líttera enim occídit}: one verse, five readings, and a Father who overrules himself}

The Epistle's second move --- \latin{non líttera, sed spíritu: líttera enim
occídit, spíritus autem vivíficat} --- has had one of the great careers in the
history of reading, and the checked witnesses do not agree about it.

Origen fixed the equation the Latin West inherited: ``by letter are meant
bodily things, and by spirit intellectual things'' (\work{De principiis}
I.1.2), and in \work{Contra Celsum} VII.20 the verse dissolves the alleged
contradiction of Moses and Christ at the spiritual level. A bounded negative
belongs beside that: the great hermeneutical manifesto of \work{De principiis}
IV grounds the spiritual sense on 1~Corinthians 2 and Proverbs 22 and does not
quote this verse there.

The Antiochenes and the oldest Latin commentator read it juridically instead.
Chrysostom: ``The Law, if it lay hold on a murderer, puts him to death; the
Gospel, if it lay hold on a murderer, enlightens, and gives him life.''
Ambrosiaster, in Latin and before Augustine: \latin{Litterae enim ad hoc datae
sunt Moysi, ut contemnentes Legem occideret secundum jus naturae. Spiritus
autem, id est, lex fidei quae non scribitur, sed animo continetur, vivificat
reos mortis} --- and his summary of vv.~7--9, \latin{multum ergo interest
inter legem et legem}. On this reading the letter kills the guilty by
sentence.

Augustine holds both readings, in that order, and rules between them. In 396
(\work{De doctrina christiana} III.5.9) the verse is a rule of exegesis: to
take figurative expressions literally is ``a miserable slavery of the soul.''
In 412, against the Pelagians, he quotes that interpretation back and demotes
it without retracting it --- the verse is to be understood ``not in that way
only \dots\ but also, and indeed \textit{especially}'' (\latin{eoque vel
maxime}) of the law without grace: \latin{doctrina quippe illa, qua mandatum
accipimus continenter recteque vivendi, littera est occidens, nisi adsit
vivificans spiritus}. The killing letter is now the Decalogue itself, and the
treatise's formula follows: \latin{Lex ergo data est, ut gratia quaereretur:
gratia data est, ut lex impleretur}.

Aquinas supplies the mechanics and then the corollary. In the commentary the
letter kills \latin{occasionaliter} --- \latin{littera legis dat solam
cognitionem peccati \dots\ non reprimit concupiscentiam, sed magis
occasionaliter auget} --- knowledge without healing of the cause. In
\work{Summa theologiae} I-II q.~106 a.~2 he formalises Augustine's own
definition of ``letter'' as \latin{quaelibet Scriptura extra homines existens,
etiam moralium praeceptorum qualia continentur in Evangelio}, and concludes:
\latin{unde etiam littera Evangelii occideret, nisi adesset interius gratia
fidei sanans}. The line no longer separates the Testaments; it runs through
every written norm, this page included. Bernard, eight centuries after Origen,
has the verse seasoning the choir office: \latin{Mel in cera, devotio in
littera est. Alioquin littera occidit, si absque spiritus condimento
glutieris}.

Two boundaries are kept: Theodoret's commentary on the passage (PG~82) was
verified as publicly reachable but his sentences were not verified, so nothing
is printed as his words; and no Gregory the Great locus for this verse was
located in the corpora checked.

That doctrinal history is what makes the Gospel's opening scene more than a
morality tale. Augustine's exposition of the parable, verified in the Latin,
reads priest and Levite as the \latin{sacerdotium et ministerium Veteris
Testamenti, quod non poterat prodesse ad salutem} --- the same noun as the
Epistle's \latin{ministrátio mortis}, and the same judgment, reached by a
witness who was not thinking about 2~Corinthians. And the Collect has already
conceded the Epistle's premise in its opening clause, \latin{de cuius múnere
venit, ut tibi a fidélibus tuis digne et laudabíliter serviátur}; Schuster's
commentary on this Sunday quotes 2~Cor. 3:5 against any suspicion that the
oration overstates.

\subsection*{3. The parable: an older allegory than is usually claimed, a dissenting Greek, and Bede's rule}

Four attributions that a careful guide could make from the standard delivery
of the sources are wrong, and correcting them is most of the reception
history.

The Samaritan-as-Christ reading is not Latin allegorising imposed on a moral
tale. It is attested in the second century: Irenaeus already has the Lord
compassionating the man fallen among thieves and giving ``two royal
denaria'' bearing the image of the Father and the Son, and Clement of
Alexandria asks of the neighbour who showed mercy, ``Who else can it be but
the Saviour Himself?'' Origen then reports the whole scheme --- Adam,
paradise, the world, the hostile powers, the Law, the prophets, Christ, the
Church's president --- as something ``one of the elders'' said, corrects it
(not every man descends from Jerusalem to Jericho), and adds two signatures of
his own: the etymology, the Samaritan \latin{qui interpretatur custos}, keyed
to Jn.~8:48 where the Lord denies the demon and does not deny the name; and
the two denarii as the knowledge of Father and Son.

The Fathers did not all allegorise. Cyril of Alexandria's surviving sermon on
this pericope works entirely at the moral level and never identifies the
Samaritan with Christ: the lawyer, praised, ``breaks forth into pride,
thinking that he had no neighbour,'' and Cyril's conclusion is that priesthood
and Law-learning are vain unless confirmed by works. The major Greek
commentator declines the reading the Latin West systematised.

Gregory the Great never preached on this Gospel --- the forty
\work{Homiliae in Evangelia} contain no lection from it, verified across the
whole series --- and his famous oil-and-wine reading belongs to the
\work{Moralia} and the \work{Regula pastoralis}: wine and oil are applied
\latin{ut per vinum mordeantur vulnera, per oleum foveantur}, so that anyone
set over the healing of wounds must mix \latin{lenitas cum severitate}. Nor is
there a genuine Chrysostom treatment: the long allegorical extracts under his
name in the \work{Catena aurea} are flagged in the Oxford edition's own
margins as \latin{Hom. in loc. Ed. Lat.}, and its preface assigns such
material to an early imitator.

What the Latins did add is substantial and various. Ambrose grounds the figure
in history first --- \latin{haec simplicis historia veritatis est} --- makes
Jericho \latin{figura istius mundi} and the exile one of conduct rather than
place, re-reads the two denarii as the two Testaments, makes the innkeeper
St~Paul, and closes with the sentence the whole tradition kept: \latin{Non enim
cognatio facit proximum, sed misericordia: quia misericordia secundum
naturam}. Augustine fixes the classic table, including his own reading of
Jericho as the moon and so as mortality, the man left \latin{semivivum}
because \latin{ex parte qua potest intellegere et cognoscere Deum vivus est},
the oil \latin{consolatio spei bonae} and the wine \latin{exhortatio ad
operandum ferventissimo spiritu}; and in \work{Sermo} 131 he turns the table
anti-Pelagian and pastoral at once --- baptism forgives all sins, yet
\latin{adhuc curatur}, the man is still under treatment in the inn.

Bede legislates the balance, and his sentence is this guide's rule: the Lord
framed the parable \latin{ut omnem qui misericordiam faceret cuilibet proximum
doceret}, and it also specially designates the Son of God, but the rules of
allegory must not \latin{moralia mutuae fraternitatis instituta \dots\
extenuare et auferre} --- must not thin out or carry off the moral institutes
of mutual brotherhood. What Calvin would later treat as rival readings, and
reject wholesale, Bede had held together five centuries earlier; and
\work{Salvifici doloris} 28--30 completes the circle from the other side,
setting Christ in the wounded man's place rather than the Samaritan's.

The two chants between the lessons belong to this unit, because their
reception answers the parable's lawyer without knowing him. Cassiodorus, on
the Gradual's \latin{áudiant mansuéti}, draws a pointed exclusion ---
\latin{non dixit, lege docti, non ieiunántes, non psalléntes, sed mansuéti} ---
not the law-learned, not the fasters, not the psalm-singers, but the meek are
bidden rejoice; Basil, on the same clause, makes the meek those who win
``without battle'' and guesses ``perhaps, prophetically, the disciples of
Christ.'' The Gospel's \latin{legisperítus} is exactly \latin{lege doctus},
and the Gospel's blessed eyes are the disciples'. Neither expositor had a
lectionary in view; the juxtaposition is the missal's.

\subsection*{4. Three prayers that are not self-supplied, and the books that carry them}

The Introit is the sentence with which the Western Church has begun her prayer
for fourteen centuries, and this is the one place the 1962 Missal sings it at
Mass. Cassian hands the verse over as the formula of unceasing prayer because
``it embraces all the feelings which can be implanted in human nature \dots\
it contains the thought of one's own weakness, confidence in the answer, and
the assurance of a present and ever ready help''; Cassiodorus, citing Cassian
by name and chapter, records that monks ``do not begin'' anything
\latin{sine huius versiculi trina iteratione}; the Rule of St~Benedict makes
it law at cc.~17--18 and sends even the kitchen rotation through it at c.~35;
and Bellarmine, looking back, simply describes the accomplished fact --- ``a
verse celebrated in the Catholic Church, as all the divine offices commence
with it.'' Augustine grounds the plea where the psalm does: \latin{ego vero
egenus et pauper sum}, ``help I seek, always mine infirmity I confess.''
Theodoret, characteristically, keeps it in history --- David fleeing Absalom,
``the memory of his sin stung him more sharply than his enemies did.''

The Offertory carries the same confession into narrative. Its reception is
about how a prayer for the undeserving works. Augustine, on God's
\latin{Sine me} spoken before Moses has said a word, hears the whole
mechanism: the sentence means \latin{ac si diceretur \dots\ dilectio tua in
illos intercedit mihi} --- as if to say, your love intercedes with me for them
--- and God ``was loving them the more secretly while terrifying them aloud.''
He also fixes the doctrinal guard-rails: \latin{malitiam hic poenam
intellegi voluit}, the ``evil'' God is appeased of is penalty and not malice;
and \latin{ira Dei non perturbatio animi eius est, sed iudicium quo inrogatur
poena peccato}. Gregory reaches the same place from the other side --- to say
``let me alone'' to a servant is to give him boldness to plead, and God
``bears the supplicant's contradiction which He inspires'' --- and makes Moses
the pattern ruler, ``mercy united with severity.'' Tertullian alone among the
checked witnesses names the type outright: Moses deprecating the Father's
wrath prefigures ``Christ \dots\ the deprecator of the Father, and the offerer
of His own life for the salvation of the people.'' That is one witness, not a
tradition, and preaching commonly credits it to more.

The orations themselves are older than every book that now carries them, and
the transmission is documented rather than inferred. The Collect stands in the
Verona collection among the July Masses in a shorter form (\latin{tribue ut},
without \latin{quǽsumus nobis}); in the Gelasian it opens the Mass-set that
already contains all three of this Sunday's orations together; and the
Gregorian Hadrianum has the 1962 wording exact under \latin{Dominica XIII post
Pentecosten}, the old count running one ahead of the missal's. The Secret is
not in the Verona collection and reads \latin{largiáris} in the Gelasian
against the later \latin{largiéndo}. The Postcommunion stands in the Verona
collection with one different word --- \latin{participatio \textbf{tui} sancta
mysterii}. A footnote in the same Gregorian witness records, for the Mass
before those orations, the chant cues \latin{Ant. Deus in adiutorium. Resp.
Benedicam dominum. Off. Precatus est moyses. Com. De fructu operum}: this
Sunday's exact four chants, paired a thousand years before the 1962 printing.
That proves early association. It says nothing about why. And all three
orations outlived the 1962 books: the postconciliar Missal appoints the
Collect for the Thirty-first Sunday \latin{per annum}, the Secret as the
\latin{super oblata} of Thursday after Ash Wednesday, and the Postcommunion
after Communion on Tuesday of the third week of Lent.

\subsection*{5. What the formulary finally asks a sacrament to do}

The Secret's whole argument is in a gerund. \latin{Ut, nobis indulgéntiam
largiéndo, tuo nómini dent honórem}: God's act of lavishing pardon is the very
mode in which the offerings render him honour --- not pardon traded for glory
but pardon \textit{as} glory. The two ends of sacrifice a treatise would
distinguish, propitiation and praise, are fused in one clause of liturgical
grammar; Schuster reads it exactly so. The 1861 English reverses the order and
is therefore quoted with that caution attached.

The Communion's three verses have drawn the widest spread of reading of any
text in this Mass, and the spread is the commentary. At the literal pole
Theodoret: God supplies rain so that the earth yields ``bread to strengthen
and nourish human nature, wine to gladden and make life sweeter, oil to
nourish within and brighten without'' --- providence, and no sacramental turn
at all, from an expositor who allegorises freely elsewhere. Bellarmine agrees
and moralises: gratitude, wonder that the Father gave ``even the luxuries,''
and frugal meals. At the other pole Augustine --- ``That it may bring forth
bread out of the earth. What bread? Christ. Out of what earth? From Peter,
from Paul'' --- with the sober inebriation of the Lord's cup and the chrism
that anoints ``over His whole Body''; and Cassiodorus, the most explicitly
sacramental witness this guide has met: wine gladdens the heart \latin{cum
sacratum fuerit in sanguinem Domini Christi}, the face is brightened with oil
\latin{cum regale chrisma conficitur}, and his proof-text for the illumined
face is Ps.~33:6 --- the Gradual's own psalm quoted inside the Communion's, in
a sixth-century commentary. That the 1962 books appoint these verses as a
\textit{Communion} chant sits squarely on the Augustine--Cassiodorus line;
whether any compiler chose them for that reason is not documented and is not
claimed here.

The Postcommunion closes the formulary on the Epistle's own verb. The lesson's
hinge was \latin{spíritus autem vivíficat}; the last oration opens
\latin{Vivíficet nos}. That is a textual observation about shared vocabulary
on two collated texts and nothing more; but what the prayer asks with that
verb is exact. \latin{Et páriter nobis expiatiónem tríbuat, et munímen}: one
participation, two gifts held level --- cleansing for what is past, a rampart
for what is coming --- with \latin{páriter} refusing to rank them. Schuster
calls the prayer, in its Latin brevity, ``an epitome of Eucharistic
doctrine.'' Augustine's \latin{adhuc curatur} is its pastoral counterpart: the
Mass that opened with an unarmed cry for help ends not by thanking God for a
finished rescue but by asking that the rescue keep working.
