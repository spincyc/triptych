\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\label{triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

\movementthesis{This Mass is built on the difference between what the letter
can do and what mercy does. The Law's own ministers see the half-dead man and
pass by; the despised outsider binds his wounds and pays. From the first cry
--- help, and quickly --- to the last petition --- give us life --- nothing
decisive in this formulary is self-supplied: worthy service is God's gift
before it is our act, sufficiency is God's, rescue arrives from outside every
expected channel, and what the warning alone could never produce is handed
over at the altar.}

The order in which the book prints the ten texts is itself the argument. Read
as four stages:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=0.1em]
  \item Help begged before anything else is said; service confessed as gift. \cue{Int.}, \cue{Coll.}
  \item The doctrine stated: sufficiency from God; the letter kills, the spirit gives life. \cue{Ep.}
  \item The receiver's posture sung, and the story told: unbroken praise, the meek invited, a cry day and night --- then the road, the passers-by, and the guardian. \cue{Grad.}, \cue{All.}, \cue{Gosp.}
  \item Intercession carried to the altar and answered in kind: Moses' prayer, pardon as glory, bread and wine and oil, life with cleansing and defence. \cue{Off.}, \cue{Sec.}, \cue{Comm.}, \cue{Postcomm.}
\end{enumerate}

English quoted in this section is the Douay--Rheims (Challoner) for scriptural
propers and the 1861 Cummiskey hand missal for orations, as throughout; the
sources are identified in the detailed commentary and in the references.

\subsection*{1. The Mass that begins by shouting for help}

The Introit is the sentence the Western Church has used to begin prayer for
fourteen centuries: Cassian commended this one verse as fitting ``every
condition, and all assaults,'' Benedict wrote it into the opening of the
Hours, and the Office still starts there daily. On this one Sunday the Mass
itself begins with it --- ``O God, come to my assistance; O Lord, make haste
to help me'' --- and the chant's own psalter sharpens what the Vulgate leaves
general: the enemies are named (\latin{inimíci mei}), and they no longer
merely wish evil but devise it (\latin{cógitant}). The Church walks into this
liturgy as the psalter's poor man, with nothing in hand.

The Collect then blocks the one exit self-sufficiency might still take. It
does not ask that the faithful manage to serve God worthily; it states, in
its opening clause, that worthy and laudable service \textit{comes of God's
gift} --- \latin{de cuius múnere venit} --- and only then asks for something:
that we may \textit{run} to the promises without stumbling. Even the running
is requested, not reported.

\subsection*{2. The letter kills; the spirit gives life}

The Epistle supplies the doctrine under the day. Paul disclaims not merely
achievement but the interior act itself --- not sufficient ``to think any
thing of ourselves, as of ourselves'' --- and then names the two economies:
ministers of a new testament, ``not in the letter but in the spirit. For the
letter killeth: but the spirit quickeneth.'' The tradition spent its best
minds on that sentence. Chrysostom read it forensically: the Law seizes a
murderer and executes him; the Gospel seizes a murderer, baptises him, and
gives him life. Augustine, against the Pelagians, drove it inward: the
killing letter is any command without grace --- the Decalogue itself ---
because the bare precept shows what to do and gives no power to do it; ``the
law was given that grace might be sought; grace was given that the law might
be fulfilled.'' Aquinas carried the point to its edge in the \work{Summa}:
even the letter of the \textit{Gospel} would kill, were the inward healing
grace of faith not present. The Sunday's lesson is not an archaeology of
Sinai; it is a warning about every text, this page included.

The two chants between the lessons answer as receivers answer. The Gradual
takes the acrostic psalm's opening --- ``I will bless the Lord at all
times'' --- the praise of a man whose standing is received, with the meek,
not the strong, invited to hear and rejoice. The Alleluia frames in double
Alleluias the one bright verse of the psalter's darkest psalm: a cry ``in
the day, and in the night'' that does not stop, addressed to \latin{Deus
salútis meæ}, the God of my salvation --- salvation named as his property,
not the singer's.

\subsection*{3. The road, the passers-by, and the guardian}

The Gospel then stages the whole doctrine on a real road. The Law's reader
answers his own citation exam perfectly --- love God, love your neighbour ---
and, ``willing to justify himself,'' asks the question the parable will
refuse to answer: and who is my neighbour? What follows is the letter's
failure enacted in its own personnel: priest and Levite --- Augustine reads
them as the priesthood and ministry of the Old Testament, ``which could not
profit unto salvation'' --- see the half-dead man and pass by. The Samaritan,
whose very name the tradition from Origen to Gregory heard as \latin{custos},
guardian, ``being on his journey, came near him'': mercy arrives from
outside, binds wounds with oil and wine, carries the man on its own beast,
pays, and promises to return. The question comes back reversed --- not
``who is my neighbour?'' but ``which of these three \dots\ was neighbour to
him?'' --- and the lawyer's own verdict closes it: ``he that shewed mercy to
him.'' \latin{Vade, et tu fac simíliter}.

The reception holds two readings in one hand, and Bede legislated the
balance this guide keeps: the parable teaches that everyone who shows mercy
is a neighbour, \textit{and} it specially designates the Son of God; the
allegory must not thin out the moral command. Blessed, the pericope began,
are the eyes that see what prophets and kings desired --- and what they see,
by the end, is the guardian at work on the road.

\subsection*{4. Intercession answered at the altar}

What can be done for people who cannot supply their own rescue? The
Offertory answers with the Old Testament's boldest scene, sung in a Latin
older than the Vulgate: Moses \latin{in conspéctu Dómini}, asking God why
his anger burns, reminding God of God's own oath, ``and the Lord was
appeased.'' Augustine heard in God's strange ``Let me alone'' the whole
secret --- \latin{dilectio tua in illos intercedit mihi}, your love
intercedes with me for them --- and Gregory made it a rule: to say ``let me
alone'' to a servant is to give him boldness to plead. Tertullian named the
figure: Moses deprecating the Father's wrath prefigures Christ, ``the
offerer of His own life for the salvation of the people.''

The three prayers around Communion then convert intercession into exchange.
The Secret asks God to attend the offerings so that, \textit{by lavishing
pardon on us}, they may give honour to his name: pardon not traded against
glory but constituting it. The Communion feeds the rescued from the creation
psalm's pantry --- bread out of the earth, wine that gladdens, oil that
brightens, bread that strengthens the heart --- verses the tradition's Latin
doctors read toward the chalice and the chrism, and that this Mass sets at
the moment of reception. And the Postcommunion begins with the Epistle's own
verb turned into a petition: \latin{Vivíficet nos} --- may the holy
participation give us \textit{life} --- and asks it to bestow, \latin{
páriter}, equally, expiation for what is past and a rampart for what comes.
The Mass that opened with an unarmed cry for help closes by asking that the
help just received keep working: cleansing and defence, neither yet
finished.

\textbf{What controls this reading.} Three limits, stated here so that the
movement above is not mistaken for more than it is. First, its only evidence of
design is the order the book prints; the one piece of compositional evidence in
hand is a ninth-century cue line pairing this Sunday's four chants, and that
proves early association, not purpose. Second, the reception is genuinely
divided at two of the four stages: Augustine holds two readings of ``the letter
killeth'' and himself ranks the later above the earlier, while Chrysostom and
Ambrosiaster read it juridically instead; and the Alleluia's psalm is heard as
the voice of Christ in the Passion by Augustine, Cassiodorus and Bellarmine and
as exilic Israel's by Theodoret. The commentary keeps those disagreements
rather than resolving them. Third, and governing the whole, Bede's rule: the
parable specially designates the Son of God \textit{and} teaches that everyone
who shows mercy is a neighbour, and the rules of allegory must not thin out or
carry off the moral institutes of mutual brotherhood. A reading of this Sunday
that ended in doctrine about grace, and not in the imperative the Gospel closes
with, would have failed on the tradition's own terms.
