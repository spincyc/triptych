\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

Each appointed element is named by its Latin incipit, its printed heading and
reference, and the missal's marginal number; its text is then given in
English, quoted from an identified public-domain witness; and the evidence
follows. Latin is quoted only where an argument turns on the wording.
\latinpolicynote

\textbf{Where the English comes from, and what it is not.} Scriptural
elements --- Introit, Gradual, Alleluia, Epistle, Gospel, Offertory,
Communion --- are given in the Douay--Rheims as revised by Bishop Challoner
(1749--1752), which is the English of the Clementine Vulgate the missal
prints, in the Project Gutenberg e-text of that revision. The orations ---
Collect, Secret, Postcommunion --- are given in the anonymous English of
\work{The Roman Missal translated into the English language for the use of the
laity}, first revised edition (Philadelphia: Eugene Cummiskey, 1861), quoted
from the three lines that book supplies for this formulary. Both are in the
public domain in the United States. Neither is an official liturgical
translation, and this guide has composed, translated, adapted and paraphrased
nothing: where the registered English does not answer the missal's Latin, the
gap is stated and left open.

\subsection*{The text: what two 1962 books actually print}

The Latin of this formulary was collated at 300 and 600 dpi against the CMAA
facsimile of the Vatican \latin{editio typica} (printed pp.~392--393), and
independently against the page images of the Benziger \latin{editio iuxta
typicam} of the same year (printed pp.~386--388). The two books are not two
photographs of one page; they are two editions, and they differ. Nothing below
is blended, and the typical edition governs.

\begin{receptiontable}{Place}{Vatican \latin{editio typica}}{Benziger \latin{iuxta typicam}}
Introit close & \latin{℣. Glória Patri.} & \latin{℣. Glória Patri. Deus.}\\
Introit heading & \latin{Ant. ad Introitum} & \latin{Antiphona ad Introitum}\\
Alleluia & \latin{Dómine, Deus salútis meæ} & \latin{Dómine Deus salútis meæ}\\
Lk. 10:34 & \latin{in iuméntum suum, duxit} & \latin{in iuméntum suum duxit}\\
Secret & \latin{ut, nobis \dots\ largiéndo,} & \latin{ut nobis \dots\ largiéndo,}\\
Secret conclusion & \latin{Per Dóminum nostrum.} & \latin{Per Dóminum.}\\
\end{receptiontable}

None of these changes a meaning; the first is the only one substantive for the
singer, since Benziger prints the antiphon-repetition cue the typical edition
leaves to the general rubrics. All of them show that ``the 1962 Latin'' is not
a single artefact, and that a guide which does not name its witness has not
told the reader what it checked.

\subsubsection*{Where the appointed text is not the Bible's text}

The more consequential collation is against the Clementine Vulgate itself.
Every divergence below was verified directly and is a textual observation, not
an inference. They are gathered here once, and the commentary returns to each
where it bears on an argument. This is the point at which the Latin has to be
on the page: an argument about wording cannot be conducted in translation.

\begin{receptiontable}{Proper and locus}{Appointed text}{Clementine Vulgate}
1. \cue{Int.} Ps. 69:3 & \latin{confundántur et revereántur \textbf{inimíci mei}, qui quærunt ánimam meam} & \latin{Confundantur, et revereantur, qui quærunt animam meam} --- the chant names the enemies the psalm verse leaves unnamed\\
2. \cue{Int.} Ps. 69:4 & \latin{qui \textbf{cógitant} mihi mala} & \latin{qui volunt mihi mala} --- a different verb: devising, not desiring, and the one the Douay renders\\
3. \cue{Ep.} 2 Cor. 3:4 & \latin{Fratres: Fidúciam talem habémus} & \latin{Fiduciam \textbf{autem} talem habemus} --- the liturgical address displaces Paul's connective\\
4. \cue{Gosp.} Lk. 10:23 & \latin{In illo témpore: Dixit Iesus discípulis suis} & \latin{Et conversus ad discipulos suos, dixit} --- the incipit removes the evangelist's turning\\
5. \cue{Gosp.} Lk. 10:27 & \latin{ex ómnibus \textbf{víribus} tuis} & \latin{ex omnibus \textbf{virtutibus} tuis} --- a different noun for ``strength''; the Douay's ``with all thy strength'' renders either without showing which\\
6. \cue{Off.} Ex. 32:11 & \latin{Precátus est Móyses in conspéctu Dómini Dei sui, et dixit} & \latin{Moyses autem orabat Dominum Deum suum, dicens}\\
7. \cue{Off.} Ex. 32:11 & \latin{Quare, Dómine, irásceris in pópulo tuo?} & \latin{Cur, Domine, irascitur furor tuus contra populum tuum, quem eduxisti de terra Ægypti\dots} --- the verb becomes second person and the Egypt clause is dropped\\
8. \cue{Off.} (v.~12) & \latin{Parce iræ ánimæ tuæ} & carries the matter of a verse the citation does not name: \latin{quiescat ira tua, et esto placabilis super nequitia populi tui}\\
9. \cue{Off.} Ex. 32:13 & \latin{meménto Abraham, Isaac, et Iacob} & \latin{Recordare Abraham, Isaac, et Israël servorum tuorum} --- \latin{Iacob} for \latin{Israël}\\
10. \cue{Off.} Ex. 32:13 & \latin{quibus iurásti dare terram fluéntem lac et mel} & the verse's oath is \latin{Multiplicabo semen vestrum sicut stellas cæli\dots}; the milk-and-honey formula belongs to Ex. 3:8 and 33:3, and there in the ablative, \latin{lacte et melle}\\
11. \cue{Off.} Ex. 32:14 & \latin{Et placátus factus est Dóminus de malignitáte, quam dixit fácere pópulo suo} & \latin{Placatusque est Dominus ne faceret malum quod locutus fuerat adversus populum suum}\\
12. \cue{Comm.} Ps. 103:13 & \latin{De fructu óperum tuórum, \textbf{Dómine}, satiábitur terra} & \latin{de fructu operum tuorum satiabitur terra} --- inverted order, and the vocative is the antiphon's own\\
13. \cue{Comm.} Ps. 103:14 & antiphon runs \latin{\dots terra: ut edúcas panem} & v.~14's first clause, \latin{producens fœnum iumentis\dots}, is omitted --- exactly what the printed citation \latin{13 et 14-15} declares\\
\end{receptiontable}

Three negative results belong with that table. The Gradual (Ps. 33:2--3) and
the Alleluia verse (Ps. 87:2) match the Clementine psalter word for word, and
the Epistle and Gospel match it throughout apart from rows 3--5 above; the
missal's \latin{i} for the Clementine's \latin{j} (\latin{Iacob, iurásti,
iustificáre}) and its accents are orthographic policy, not readings. The
Introit's two divergences have the same natural explanation as every chant
psalter divergence in this series: the Roman Church sang an older Latin
psalter before Jerome's Gallican recension displaced it in the Bible, and the
\work{Catholic Encyclopedia} records that the Roman recension remained in
liturgical use at Rome long after: of the \work{Psalterium Romanum} its
article on the Breviary says that ``it is still in use in St.\ Peter's at
Rome, and many of the texts of our Breviary and Missal still show some
variants.'' The Offertory's five rows are of another
kind and are treated at the Offertory: that chant is an Old-Latin,
Septuagint-shaped adaptation older than the Vulgate beside it. This guide
records all of these as verified textual observations and does not assert
derivations, because no \work{Psalterium Romanum} or Old-Latin witness was
collated for them.

One cross-edition observation is recorded at the level of a lead only: in the
degraded OCR text layer of the tracked Venice Missal of 1570, this Introit's
psalm verse appears with the Gallican \latin{qui volunt mihi mala}, and the
Collect's word order appears as \latin{tribue nobis, quesumus}, where the 1962
book prints \latin{qui cógitant mihi mala} and \latin{tríbue, quǽsumus,
nobis}. Neither was collated against page images, and nothing is built on
either.

\subsection*{Introit \cue{Int.}}

\elementid{Deus, in adiutórium meum inténde}{\latin{Ant. ad Introitum} --- Ps. 69, 2-3, with \latin{Ps. ibid.,} 4}{1552}

\begin{namedtranslation}{English: Douay--Rheims (Challoner), Ps. 69:2--3 and 69:4}
\textbf{Antiphon} (vv.~2--3): O God, come to my assistance; O Lord, make haste
to help me. Let them be confounded and ashamed that seek my soul.

\textbf{Psalm verse} (v.~4): Let them be turned backward, and blush for shame
that desire evils to me.
\end{namedtranslation}

\englishgap{That English is the English of the Clementine psalm, and at two
points it is not the English of the chant: the antiphon's \latin{inimíci mei}
has no counterpart in Clementine v.~3, and the verse sings \latin{qui cógitant
mihi mala} --- who \textit{devise} evils --- where the Clementine, and
therefore the Douay, has \latin{qui volunt mihi mala}, who \textit{desire}
them. Neither gap is filled in here.}

\textbf{And this time the Fathers sing with the missal.} On the Ninth Sunday
of this series the chant and the expositors part company over a verb; here
the opposite happens, and it is worth saying plainly. Augustine's own lemma at
this verse reads \latin{Avertantur retrorsum, et erubescant qui cogitant mihi
mala}, and Cassiodorus's psalter carries both of the chant's readings ---
\latin{inimici mei} and \latin{cogitant} --- word for word. The Introit is not
a deviation from the psalter the tradition expounded; it \textit{is} the old
psalter the tradition expounded, standing in the Mass after Jerome's revision
replaced it in the Bible. The commentary below can therefore quote the
expositors on exactly the words the assembly sings.

The psalm itself is the psalter's own doublet --- its verses stand nearly
verbatim as the close of Ps.~39 --- and its speaker is the psalter's
recurring poor man: the plea for speed is grounded, three verses later, in
\latin{ego vero egenus et pauper sum}. Bellarmine states the doublet plainly
(``The remainder of the Psalm is almost word for word with Ps.~39, which
see''), and Cassiodorus cites the twin verse with a theological division of
labour: in Ps.~39 the words are Christ's in his own person, here his
members'. Augustine, for all nine sections of his exposition, never mentions
the other psalm --- a bounded negative verified against the full Latin of
both expositions.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Augustine, \work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 69 (New Advent ``Psalm 70''), §§1--4, 7}
Preached on a martyrs' feast, and built on the grain of wheat: ``Thanks to the
Grain of wheat, because He willed to die and to be multiplied \dots\ Many
therefore members of Him, under one Head \dots\ are one man: and of the same,
as of one man, the voice is ofttimes heard in the Psalms.'' The cry for help
is therefore never off duty --- \latin{opus enim habemus sempiterno adiutorio
in isto saeculo} --- ``for need we have of an everlasting aid in this world.
But when have we not?'' On the antiphon's second clause he hears Christ
whole: ``He is speaking that has said, Why do you persecute Me?'' And on the
verse he draws a precise historical line through the chant's own verb: open
persecution has passed, ``now there has remained the malice of them
\textit{thinking}'' --- the enemies who once sought the soul now merely devise,
\latin{cogitant}. At §7 the speaker's ground: ``help I seek, always mine
infirmity I confess.'' English from the public-domain NPNF first series,
vol.~8; Latin checked at the same sections.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Cassiodorus, \work{Expositio Psalmorum} 69 (PL 70, coll.\ 493--494)}
The whole psalm is \latin{ex persona martyrum}: after the Head narrated his
Passion in Ps.~68, ``after their Head the members likewise speak.'' His gloss
on the two opening verbs is the closest reading this verse has received:
\latin{inténde} asks God to \textit{look favourably}, ``since the Lord knows
all things even when he is not asked''; \latin{festína} is prayed ``as though
divine mercy were delaying, and by the delay of its coming the torments were
growing heavier.'' And he records what the verse had already become by the
sixth century, citing Cassian by name and chapter: the most eloquent Cassian
so celebrates this verse in his tenth Conference ``that whatever monks
undertake, they do not begin without a threefold repetition of this little
verse'' (\latin{sine huius versiculi trina iteratione non inchoent}). Latin
from the public-domain Migne text; the copyrighted ACW translation was not
used.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ John Cassian, \work{Conferences} X.10}
The chapter Cassiodorus meant. Abba Isaac hands over this one verse as the
formula of unceasing prayer: ``it embraces all the feelings which can be
implanted in human nature, and can be fitly and satisfactorily adapted to
every condition, and all assaults \dots\ it contains the thought of one's own
weakness, confidence in the answer, and the assurance of a present and ever
ready help''; it is to be prayed ``in adversity that we may be delivered, in
prosperity that we may be preserved and not puffed up.'' English from the
public-domain NPNF second series, vol.~11.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{The Rule of St.\ Benedict, cc.\ 17--18 and 35}
Cassian's counsel became law. The Rule orders the Hours to open with this
verse --- c.~18: \latin{In primis dicatur versu: Deus, in adiutorium meum
inténde; Dómine, ad adiuvándum me festína} --- and c.~35 sends even the
kitchen rotation through it: the brother entering his week of service says
this verse three times before all. From Benedict the verse passed to the
whole Latin Office, where it opens every hour except Matins. The Twelfth
Sunday's Introit is thus the one place the 1962 Missal sings at Mass the
sentence with which the Western Church has begun her prayer for fourteen
centuries. Latin from the public-domain text; English cross-checked in the
1902 Verheyen translation.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Theodoret of Cyrus, \work{Interpretatio in Psalmos} 69 (PG 80, coll.\ 1415--1418) --- Greek-tradition sample}
The Antiochene counterweight: David composed the psalm fleeing Absalom, and
``the memory of his sin stung him more sharply than his enemies did.'' The
opening plea he paraphrases without figure: many and various calamities
engulf me; therefore I ask that help be given quickly. Migne's apparatus at
this verse carries a text-historical note the Introit's singers have an
interest in: the second clause, \latin{Dómine, ad adiuvándum me festína}, is
absent from the Septuagint's two great codices and stands in the Hebrew, in
Apollinaris --- and in Augustine. Read in the registered facsimile of PG 80
after a fresh hash match; Migne's parallel Latin is quoted, and the
copyrighted modern English translation was not consulted.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Robert Bellarmine, \work{Explanatio in Psalmos}, Ps.~LXIX, tr.\ John O'Sullivan (Dublin, 1866), artifact p.~220}
One compressed paragraph, aimed straight at the liturgical fact: ``A verse
celebrated in the Catholic Church, as all the divine offices commence with
it. For though it is peculiarly applicable to Christ hanging on the cross, it
may be used by all the faithful in any danger whatever \dots\ it is not only
lawful, but expedient to repeat this verse very frequently.'' Read on the
registered facsimile of the 1866 edition.
\end{witnessnote}

\subsection*{Collect \cue{Coll.}}

\elementid{Omnípotens et miséricors Deus}{\latin{Oratio}, composed oration}{1553}

\begin{namedtranslation}{English: Cummiskey, \work{The Roman Missal \dots\ for the use of the laity} (Philadelphia, 1861), Collect of this formulary, printed p.~420}
O Almighty and merciful God, from whose gift it proceedeth that thy people
worthily serve thee; grant we beseech thee, that we may run on, without
stumbling, to the obtaining the effects of thy promises. Thro'.
\end{namedtranslation}

\textbf{A caution about that English before anything is built on it.} The 1861
rendering gives one adverb where the Latin prays two: \latin{digne et
laudabíliter serviátur} asks that God be served worthily \textit{and
laudably} --- service that is right and service that is praise --- and
``worthily serve thee'' carries only the first. It also flattens \latin{a
fidélibus tuis}, ``by thy faithful,'' into ``thy people.'' The argument below
is conducted on the Latin.

The prayer's whole theology is in its opening subordinate clause. Before it
asks anything, it states where the thing it wants comes from: \latin{de cuius
múnere venit, ut tibi a fidélibus tuis digne et laudabíliter serviátur} ---
it comes of God's own gift that God is well served at all. Worthy service is
not the price of grace but its product; the Collect concedes, in oration
grammar, exactly what the day's Epistle will argue --- \latin{non quod
sufficiéntes simus cogitáre áliquid a nobis \dots\ sed sufficiéntia nostra ex
Deo est}. Schuster's commentary on this Sunday makes the link explicit,
quoting that very verse of 2 Corinthians 3 against any thought that the
prayer exaggerates. Then the petition, with the printed semicolon marking
the join: \latin{tríbue, quǽsumus, nobis; ut ad promissiónes tuas sine
offensióne currámus} --- grant that we may \textit{run} to thy promises
\latin{sine offensióne}, without stumbling, the participant's verb rather
than the spectator's. And the promises the Church asks to run toward are, on
this Sunday, the ones her own Offertory will name three chants later:
\latin{quibus iurásti}.

\textbf{Where the prayer has been, on documented witnesses.} The oration is
older than every book that now carries it. It stands in the Verona
(``Leonine'') collection among the July Masses in a shorter form ---
\latin{tribue ut}, without \latin{quǽsumus nobis} (Feltoe's edition, p.~74);
in the Gelasian Sacramentary it opens the Mass-set that already contains all
three of this Sunday's orations together (Wilson's edition, Book III, no.\
VIII, pp.~228--229); and in the Gregorian Sacramentary of the Hadrianum
tradition it stands under \latin{Dominica XIII post Pentecosten} with the
1962 wording exact (Wilson's 1915 edition, p.~173) --- the old count running
one ahead of the missal's. It also outlived the 1962 books: the postconciliar
\work{Missale Romanum} appoints the same collect, verbatim, for the
Thirty-first Sunday \latin{per annum}. A prayer first attested in a
fifth-to-sixth-century Roman collection is still being said over the same
petition fifteen centuries later.

\textit{Source boundary.} No patristic commentary on this oration's wording
was located; composed Roman collects carry no ancient commentary tradition
comparable to the Scriptures'. The transmission facts above are documented at
the cited editions; the grammatical analysis is source-grounded synthesis on
the printed Latin.

\subsection*{Epistle \cue{Ep.}}

\elementid{Fidúciam talem habémus}{\latin{Léctio Epístolæ beáti Pauli Apóstoli ad Corínthios} --- 2 Cor. 3, 4-9}{1554}

The lesson makes two moves, and the second has had one of the great careers
in the history of reading. First, sufficiency: not that we are sufficient to
\textit{think} anything of ourselves, as of ourselves --- Paul disclaims even
the interior act --- but our sufficiency is from God, who made us fit
ministers of a new testament. Second, the contrast that names the covenants:
\latin{non líttera, sed spíritu: líttera enim occídit, spíritus autem
vivíficat}, and then the a fortiori of the two ministrations: if the
ministration of death, engraved in letters on stones, came with such glory
that Israel could not gaze at Moses' face --- a glory \latin{quæ evacuátur},
being done away --- how shall the ministration of the Spirit not abound the
more in glory? The missal's text follows the Clementine exactly, down to the
shift from \latin{ministrátio} to \latin{ministérium} in the last clause;
only the incipit differs, as tabulated above.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ John Chrysostom, \work{Homilies on Second Corinthians} 6 (on 3:1--6) and 7 (on 3:7--18)}
The Antiochene reading is forensic and sacramental at once. On sufficiency:
Paul ``refers and ascribes the whole to Him.'' On the letter that kills:
``In the Law, he that has sin is punished; here, he that has sins comes and
is baptized and is made righteous \dots\ The Law, if it lay hold on a
murderer, puts him to death; the Gospel, if it lay hold on a murderer,
enlightens, and gives him life'' --- with the Sabbath stick-gatherer executed
under the one and forgiven thousands under the other. On the two
ministrations (Hom.~7): since the New Covenant's glory cannot be shown to
bodily eyes as Moses' shining face could, Paul ``by this logical inference
established its superiority''; and the tables have changed material: ``This
table is of the flesh, and spirit, and righteousness, and remains; and unto
all of us is it vouchsafed, not to one only, as to Moses.'' English from the
public-domain NPNF first series, vol.~12.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Ambrosiaster, \work{Commentaria in Epistolam ad Corinthios Secundam}, ad 3:6--9 (PL 17)}
The oldest complete Latin commentary on Paul reads the verse juridically:
\latin{Litterae enim ad hoc datae sunt Moysi, ut contemnentes Legem occideret
secundum jus naturae. Spiritus autem, id est, lex fidei quae non scribitur,
sed animo continetur, vivificat reos mortis} --- the letters were given to
Moses precisely to execute despisers of the Law; the spirit is the unwritten
law of faith, held in the soul, which brings the death-guilty to life. The
comparison of vv.~7--9 he sums: \latin{multum ergo interest inter legem et
legem} --- there is much between law and law --- and glory is greater in
salvation than in death. Latin verified in the public Migne transcription.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Augustine, \work{De doctrina christiana} III.5.9 and \work{De spiritu et littera} 4.6, 14.23--25, 19.34 --- one Father, two readings, and his own ruling between them}
In 396 Augustine read the verse as a rule of exegesis: to take figurative
expressions literally is ``a miserable slavery of the soul,'' and ``nothing
is more fittingly called the death of the soul'' than intelligence subjected
to the flesh by the letter --- Origen's hermeneutic naturalised. In 412,
against the Pelagians, he quoted that interpretation back and demoted it
without retracting it: the verse is to be understood ``not in that way only
\dots\ but also, and indeed \textit{especially}'' (\latin{eoque vel maxime})
of the law without grace --- \latin{doctrina quippe illa, qua mandatum
accipimus continenter recteque vivendi, littera est occidens, nisi adsit
vivificans spiritus}. The letter that kills is now the Decalogue itself: the
command shows what to do and gives no power to do it, and by Romans 7 the
bare precept occasions the sin it forbids. The treatise's celebrated
formula, verified in the Latin: \latin{Lex ergo data est, ut gratia
quaereretur: gratia data est, ut lex impleretur} --- the law was given that
grace might be sought; grace was given that the law might be fulfilled.
English from the public-domain NPNF first series, vols.\ 2 and 5.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Thomas Aquinas, \work{Super II ad Corinthios}, cap.~3, lect.\ 1--2; \work{Summa theologiae} I-II q.~106 aa.\ 1--2}
Aquinas supplies the mechanics and then the astonishing corollary. In the
commentary the letter kills \latin{occasionaliter}: \latin{littera legis dat
solam cognitionem peccati \dots\ non reprimit concupiscentiam, sed magis
occasionaliter auget} --- knowledge without healing of the cause. In the
\work{Summa} he formalises Augustine's definition --- the letter is
\latin{quaelibet Scriptura extra homines existens, etiam moralium
praeceptorum qualia continentur in Evangelio} --- and draws the conclusion
in the body of the article, verified verbatim: \latin{unde etiam littera
Evangelii occideret, nisi adesset interius gratia fidei sanans} --- even the
letter of the \textit{Gospel} would kill, were the inward healing grace of
faith not present. The New Law is \latin{principaliter \dots\ ipsa gratia
Spiritus Sancti}, the written Gospel only secondarily; the letter/spirit
line no longer separates the Testaments but runs through every written norm
whatsoever. A negative worth recording: in cap.~3 of the commentary Aquinas
never cites \work{De spiritu et littera} by name --- the Augustinian doctrine
arrives in the \work{Summa}, not where a reader would first look for it.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Origen, \work{De principiis} I.1.2; \work{Contra Celsum} VII.20 --- the fountainhead; and St.\ Bernard, \work{Super Cantica} 7.5 --- the domestication}
Origen fixed the equation the young Augustine inherited: ``by letter are
meant bodily things, and by spirit intellectual things'' (\work{De
principiis} I.1.2, ANF); in \work{Contra Celsum} the verse dissolves the
alleged contradiction of Moses and Christ at the spiritual level. A bounded
negative alongside: the great hermeneutical manifesto of \work{De principiis}
IV grounds the spiritual sense on 1 Corinthians 2 and Proverbs 22, and does
\textit{not} quote this verse there. Eight centuries later the verse has
become monastic table-talk about psalmody: \latin{Mel in cera, devotio in
littera est. Alioquin littera occidit, si absque spiritus condimento
glutieris} --- honey in the wax, devotion in the letter; otherwise the letter
kills, if you swallow it without the seasoning of the spirit (Bernard,
sermon 7 on the Canticle, Latin verified in the Migne text). The verse that
named the covenants now seasons the choir office.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{The shape of the whole reception, in one paragraph.} Origen makes the
letter the literal sense; the young Augustine adopts that as exegetical rule;
Chrysostom, Theodoret and Ambrosiaster read it juridically --- law punishes,
Spirit pardons and vivifies; the older Augustine subordinates his own earlier
reading and interiorises the juridical one --- the ungraced command itself
kills; Aquinas systematises both and radicalises Augustine until the Gospel's
own text, as external letter, falls under the verse; Bernard turns it to
devotional practice. Two bounds: Theodoret's commentary on the passage (PG
82) was verified as publicly reachable but his sentences were not verified
within this guide's search, so nothing is printed as his words; and no
Gregory the Great locus for this verse was located in the corpora checked.

\subsection*{Gradual \cue{Grad.}}

\elementid{Benedícam Dóminum in omni témpore}{\latin{Graduale} --- Ps. 33, 2-3}{1555}

\begin{namedtranslation}{English: Douay--Rheims (Challoner), Ps. 33:2--3}
I will bless the Lord at all times, his praise shall be always in my mouth.
℣.~In the Lord shall my soul be praised: let the meek hear and rejoice.
\end{namedtranslation}

The chant matches the Clementine word for word, and the psalm it opens is an
alphabetical acrostic --- Cassiodorus already knows the Hebrew letters head
its verses, and Bellarmine guesses the reason: ``that it may be easier
committed to memory, and be often chanted by the faithful.'' The Gradual
takes the acrostic's first two letters, as it were, and leaves the alphabet
to the reader.

\textbf{The title's puzzle, and four received solutions.} The psalm's
superscription sets it \latin{cum immutávit vultum suum coram Achímelech} ---
when David changed his countenance before Achimelech --- but the narrative
(1 Sam.\ 21:10--15) plays the feigned-madness scene before \textit{Achis},
king of Geth. The tradition saw the discrepancy and answered it four ways,
each verified at its locus. Basil: ``Abimelech'' was the common dynastic
title of Philistine kings, as ``Pharaoh'' in Egypt, and ``Anchus'' the
king's private name from birth. Theodoret: the Abimelech of the title is the
priest of Nob --- Abiathar under a second name, as Mark 2:26 also calls him
--- and the Geth setting does not fit the psalm's words at all. Augustine:
the histories nowhere record the scene under this name, ``and yet we find
whence it is drawn'' --- \latin{mutatio nominis sacramentum est}, the changed
name is itself the mystery: Abimelech means \latin{regnum patris mei}, and
Christ ``changed his countenance before the kingdom of his father'' --- the
kingdom of David --- when he changed the sacrifice from the order of Aaron to
the order of Melchisedech and left that kingdom for the Gentiles. Cassiodorus
follows Augustine. No harmonisation is imposed here; the four answers are the
reception.

\textbf{The Vetus Latina title carried five more words, and Augustine built
on them.} His psalter's title continued \latin{et ferebátur in mánibus suis}
--- ``and he was carried in his own hands'' --- words in neither the
Clementine nor the Douay, verified absent. Augustine's astonishment is the
point: \latin{quomodo posset fieri in homine, quis intellegat? \dots\ manibus
suis nemo portatur} --- carried in another's hands a man can be; in his own,
never. ``How this could be done in David according to the letter, we do not
find; in Christ we find it: \latin{ferebatur enim Christus in manibus suis,
quando commendans ipsum corpus suum, ait: Hoc est corpus meum}'' --- Christ
was carried in his own hands when, entrusting his own body, he said: This is
my body. The eucharistic reading of the psalm the Gradual quotes hangs on an
Old-Latin clause the modern page no longer shows --- and the psalm's later
invitation, \latin{gustáte et vidéte}, is for Augustine where the riddle
opens.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Basil the Great, \work{Homilia in Psalmum 33} (PG 29, coll.\ 349--356)}
Basil hears the objection in the chant's first clause and states it at full
strength: the prophet ``seems to promise the impossible. How can the praise
of God be always in a man's mouth? When he sleeps, he will surely be silent;
and how shall the mouth of one eating or drinking produce praise?'' His
answer is the inner man's mouth: \latin{quoddam etiam esse hominis interni
spirituale os atque intelligibile, quo nutritur, dum verbum vitae recipit,
qui est panis, qui de coelo descendit} --- there is a certain spiritual and
intelligible mouth of the inner man, by which he is nourished when he
receives the word of life, the bread that came down from heaven. And the
``always'' is fixed to affliction first: it is shameful to bless in
prosperity and be silent in adversity; then rather are richer thanks owed.
On \latin{áudiant mansuéti}: since salvation came ``without battle, by the
mere change of countenance,'' let the meek hear ``that it is possible for
the quiet to raise a trophy, and for non-combatants to be declared victors''
--- and ``perhaps, prophetically, the meek are the disciples of Christ.''
Latin from Migne's parallel column, read in the registered scan of PG 29; no
public-domain English of this homily exists, and the copyrighted FC
translation was not used. A bounded negative that frames this note: Basil's
transmitted psalm homilies cover neither Ps.\ 69, nor 87, nor 103 --- of
this Sunday's four psalms he preached only on the Gradual's.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Augustine, \work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 33, two sermons (New Advent ``Psalm 34'' carries only the second)}
On the Gradual's first verse: ``So speaks Christ, so also let a Christian
speak \dots\ both when He gives them, bless; and when He takes them away,
bless. For it is He that gives, it is He that takes away: but Himself from
him that blesses Him He takes not away'' --- Job's grammar made a rule of
praise. On the verse's second clause: those who will not be praised
\textit{in the Lord} ``are not humble, but fierce, rough, lifted up, proud.
Gentle cattle would the Lord have; be the Lord's \latin{iumentum} \dots\ He
sits upon you, He guides you.'' English from the public-domain NPNF first
series, vol.~8; the first sermon, untranslated there, was read in the Latin.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Cassiodorus, \work{Expositio Psalmorum} 33; and Theodoret, \work{Interpretatio} 33 (PG 80, coll.\ 1101--1109)}
Cassiodorus answers the \latin{quomodo semper?} morally: praise is always in
the mouth of the just man ``when whatever he thinks or speaks is such that no
reproach can fasten on it \dots\ whatever we speak or carry in mind out of
patience, charity, simplicity and the other virtues is rightly counted among
God's praises'' --- and blessing is owed \latin{quando et adversa patimur, et
collata felicitate gaudemus}. On the meek he draws a pointed exclusion:
\latin{non dixit, lege docti, non ieiunántes, non psalléntes, sed mansuéti}
--- not the law-learned, not the fasters, not the psalm-singers, but the
meek are bidden rejoice. His conclusion to the whole psalm belongs beside
this Mass: the psalm's order is so complete --- hymns first, then the
people bidden approach Communion at \latin{gustáte et vidéte} --- \latin{ut
eum conscriptum putes temporibus Christianis}, ``that you would think it
written in Christian times.'' Theodoret, for his part, glosses the Gradual's
first verse from David's whole career --- ``fleeing or pursuing, in good
fortune or ill, I hymn my Master'' --- and makes the meek the singer's chorus:
he will not weave the hymn alone, but shares the singing with those who share
his meekness.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Robert Bellarmine, \work{Explanatio in Psalmos}, Ps.~XXXIII, tr.\ O'Sullivan (1866), artifact pp.~100--105}
``The word \textit{always} does not mean every moment \dots\ but it means
that he will do so in the proper time and place, to the end of his life;
nay, more, as those Psalms will be sung to the end of time, David will thus,
through others, `bless the Lord at all times.'\thinspace'' Bellarmine has,
without knowing it, described the Gradual: the Church singing David's
\latin{semper} for him is what the chant is. Read on the registered facsimile.
\end{witnessnote}

\subsection*{Alleluia \cue{All.}}

\elementid{Dómine, Deus salútis meæ}{Allelúia --- Ps. 87, 2}{1556}

\begin{namedtranslation}{English: Douay--Rheims (Challoner), Ps. 87:2}
O Lord, the God of my salvation: I have cried in the day, and in the night
before thee.
\end{namedtranslation}

The verse is the opening of the psalter's darkest psalm --- the one lament
that never turns, whose last word in the Hebrew is ``darkness'' --- and the
chant frames it in a double \latin{Allelúia}. The liturgy has taken from the
psalm exactly what the psalm has of confession: the name \latin{Deus salútis
meæ} and the cry that does not stop, day or night. Nothing else of Ps.~87 is
appointed, and the commentary keeps to what is.

The reception of the verse splits cleanly, and the split is instructive.
Augustine reads the whole psalm as the Passion prophesied --- ``the Passion
of our Lord is here prophesied'' --- decoding the title's \latin{pro
Maheleth} as ``for the chorus'' that answers Christ by imitation, the sons of
Core as sons of Calvary, and \latin{Eman} as ``his brother''; on this verse:
``He prayed both in prosperous times, that is, by day, and in calamity,
which I imagine is meant by night.'' Cassiodorus takes the same line almost
verbatim --- through the whole psalm ``the Lord Saviour speaks from the
dispensation in which he suffered''; day signifies joy and night adversity,
``and since these two times embrace the whole of human life, he declares
that he cried to the Lord continually'' --- and his conclusion turns the
dark psalm into the martyr-making chorus: hence the confessors' prisons,
hence blood more precious than purple and fine linen. Bellarmine concretises
the pair into two datable hours: ``He did so on the day of his passion, when
he cried on the cross, `My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?' and on
the night before, when he thrice cried out in the garden'' (read on the
registered 1866 facsimile). Against all three, Theodoret keeps the psalm in
history: the prophecy foretells Israel's calamities and the Babylonian
servitude, ``and at the same time teaches the pains of all human nature
after sin''; the title's Eman is a choirmaster of 1 Paralipomenon 6, not a
figure; and this verse is simply the knowledge that grounds petition: ``Thee,
Lord, I know as the God of my salvation; therefore night and day I beg that
my supplication be received.'' A guide can hold both: the chant's own
selection --- the bright first verse alone, under Alleluia --- is a fact
about the liturgy, not a ruling between Antioch and Hippo.

One bounded negative for honesty's sake: none of the checked Fathers remarks
that this psalm is formally the darkest of the psalter; that observation is
modern, and it is left to the moderns.

\subsection*{Gospel \cue{Gosp.}}

\elementid{Beáti óculi \dots\ Homo quidam descendébat}{✠ \latin{Sequéntia
sancti Evangélii secúndum Lucam} --- Luc. 10, 23-37, with \latin{Credo}}{1557}

The pericope has three movements, and the first is usually skipped. Christ
turns to the disciples --- the turning is Luke's, outside the appointed text
--- and blesses their eyes; a lawyer stands up to test him and is sent back to
the Law he knows; and the lawyer's counter-question, asked \latin{volens
iustificáre seípsum}, is answered with the story English has never stopped
retelling. The missal appoints all three movements as one lesson, so the
famous parable arrives already framed: what the blessed eyes see, and what the
prophets and kings desired to see, is the one who is about to name himself ---
in the tradition's reading --- by what he does on the Jericho road.

\subsubsection*{The blessed eyes (vv.~23--24)}

The thinner half of the reception, and honestly so: Ambrose's Lucan
commentary passes from v.~22 to v.~25 without a word for these verses, and no
Augustine treatment of them was located in the corpora checked. The
substantive witnesses are three.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Cyril of Alexandria, \work{Commentary on Luke}, Sermon 67, tr.\ R.~Payne Smith (Oxford, 1859)}
Cyril begins from the question a lector would ask: why does Christ bless the
disciples \textit{alone}, when a crowd stood there watching the same miracles?
Because the blessing is not of eyewitness: ``all the Jews saw Christ working
with divine majesty, yet it would not be right to account them all as
blessed.'' What the blessed eyes see is what faith sees: ``They saw that God
the Word, Who was in the form of God the Father, had become flesh for our
sakes.'' The spectators had the spectacle; the disciples had the recognition.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ John Chrysostom, \work{Homily 45 on Matthew} --- on the parallel saying, Mt. 13:16--17}
Chrysostom has no surviving Luke commentary, so his reading of the saying is
taken where he gives it. Two points. The disciples' blessedness lies partly in
their own act --- ``neither would they have been blessed, unless the
well-doing had been their own'' --- and what the prophets desired was not
information but presence: his coming, his miracles, his voice, his teaching.
The ancients ``beheld by faith only: but these by sight too.'' English from
the public-domain NPNF first series, vol.~10.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Bede, \work{In Lucae Evangelium Expositio} III, on Lk. 10:23--24 (PL 92, col.\ 467)}
Bede supplies what Ambrose omitted, and sharpens it socially: not the eyes of
the scribes and Pharisees, ``who saw only the Lord's body,'' but \latin{beáti
óculi parvulórum} --- the eyes of the little ones to whom the Son deigns to
reveal himself and the Father. The ``many kings'' are for him the saints who
ruled their own temptations --- \latin{reges magni, quia tentationum suarum
motibus \dots\ regendo praeesse noverunt} --- and the prophets saw indeed, but
\latin{per speculum et in aenigmate}. Verified in the retrieved Latin
transcription against the registered source-library artifact.
\end{witnessnote}

\subsubsection*{The lawyer, and the answer he was owed (vv.~25--29)}

Luke says the \latin{legisperítus} rose \latin{tentans illum} --- Douay
``tempting him'' --- and asked what one must \textit{do} to possess eternal
life. Christ's reply is a citation exam: \latin{In lege quid scriptum est?
quómodo legis?} The lawyer answers with the two commandments, and is told
\latin{Recte respondísti: hoc fac, et vives}. Only then, \latin{volens
iustificáre seípsum} --- ``willing to justify himself'' --- does he ask the
question the parable will refuse to answer: \latin{Et quis est meus próximus?}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Cyril of Alexandria, \work{Commentary on Luke}, Sermon 68}
Cyril reads the lawyer inside a documented campaign: talebearers had gone
about Judaea saying Christ taught that Moses' commandment was of no use, so
the question is a trap laid for exactly that slander, and Christ springs it by
making the lawyer answer from the Law himself. Praised, the man ``breaks forth
into pride, thinking that he had no neighbour'' --- and the parable is built
to break precisely that: ``a crown of love is being twined for him who loves
his neighbour: and he proves to be a Samaritan. Nor is he rejected on this
account.'' Priesthood and Law-learning, Cyril concludes, are vain unless
confirmed by works.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Origen, \work{Homily 34 on Luke}, in St.\ Jerome's Latin}
Before any allegory, Origen answers the lawyer's question morally: the
neighbour is whoever ``keeps the precepts and prepares himself to be neighbour
to every man who needs help'' (\latin{praeparare se, ut sit proximus omni
homini qui auxilio indiget}), and the closing command is spoken \latin{non tam
doctori legis quam nobis quoque omnibus} --- not so much to the doctor of the
Law as to all of us. The literal sense stands on its own feet in the very
homily that transmits the great allegory.
\end{witnessnote}

\subsubsection*{The parable, and what the tradition built on it (vv.~30--37)}

The road is real before it is a figure. Jericho lies some three and a half
thousand feet below Jerusalem, a day's descent through desert; St.\ Basil,
excerpted in the \work{Catena aurea} at v.~30, makes the geography the point
--- ``the man then came from the high parts to the low, to fall into the hands
of the robbers who infested the desert'' --- and St.\ Jerome, riding the same
road with Paula, noted the place ``called Adomim or the Place of Blood \dots\
because much blood was shed there in the frequent incursions of marauders,''
and says she thought there of ``the wounded man in the gospel \dots\ and of
the kindness of the Samaritan, that is, the guardian'' (\work{Epistle} 108.12,
NPNF second series, vol.~6). The danger is not a preacher's invention; it is
in Joshua's border lists as the ascent of Adummim.

The allegorical reading is older than most summaries allow, and it did not
begin as fancy. Three stages, each verified at its locus:

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Second century: St.\ Irenaeus, \work{Adversus haereses} III.17.3; Clement of Alexandria, \work{Quis dives salvetur} 28--29}
Irenaeus, arguing about the Holy Spirit, already has the Lord as the one who
compassionated the man fallen among thieves, bound his wounds, and gave ``two
royal denaria'' bearing the image of the Father and the Son --- entrusting the
man to the Spirit. Clement asks of the neighbour who showed mercy: ``Who else
can it be but the Saviour Himself?'' --- who poured on our wounded souls the
wine, ``the blood of David's vine,'' and the oil of the Father's compassion.
Both English texts from the public-domain Ante-Nicene Fathers, vols.~1--2.
The Samaritan-as-Christ reading is thus attested a full generation before
Origen wrote.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Origen, \work{Homily 34 on Luke}, in St.\ Jerome's Latin (PL 26)}
Origen reports the whole scheme as received: ``One of the elders, wishing to
interpret the parable, said\dots'' --- \latin{hominem qui descendit esse Adam;
Jerusalem, paradisum; Jericho, mundum; latrones contrarias Fortitudines;
sacerdotem, Legem; Levitem, prophetas; Samaritem, Christum \dots\ duos
denarios, Patrem et Filium \dots\ stabularium, Ecclesiae praesidem}, and the
promised return, the Saviour's second coming. Origen corrects it even as he
transmits it --- not every man descends from Jerusalem to Jericho, so the
scheme must not be forced on all --- and adds two signatures of his own: the
etymology (the Samaritan is the one \latin{qui interpretatur custos}, the
guardian, keyed to Jn. 8:48, where the Lord denies the demon and pointedly
does not deny being a Samaritan --- \latin{sciebat enim se custodem}); and the
two denarii as the knowledge of the Father and the Son, \latin{quomodo Pater
in Filio, et Filius in Patre sit}. Latin verified in the wikisource
transcription of Jerome's translation; the modern English translation (FC 94)
is in copyright and is not quoted.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Ambrose, \work{Expositio evangelii secundum Lucam} VII, §§71--84 (PL 15, coll.\ 1717--1720)}
Ambrose grounds the figure in history first --- \latin{haec simplicis historia
veritatis est: quae si altius consideretur, admiranda signat mysteria} --- and
then re-mints the inherited scheme. Jericho is \latin{figura istius mundi},
into which Adam descended from the heavenly Jerusalem, exiled not by a change
of place but of conduct: \latin{cui non loci, sed morum mutatio naturae suae
fecit exsilium}. The robbers are \latin{angeli noctis atque tenebrarum}, who
can wound only after stripping the garments of spiritual grace. The Samaritan
is read through the same etymology --- \latin{Samaritani etenim vocabulo
custos significatur} --- and identified by his descent: \latin{Qui descendit
de coelo}. Ambrose's own coinage is the neighbouring: the guardian ``became
our neighbour by taking up our compassion'' --- \latin{factus est compassionis
nostrae susceptione finitimus, et misericordiae collatione vicinus}. The two
denarii he re-reads as the two Testaments, \latin{quae imaginem in se habent
aeterni regis expressam, quorum pretio vulnera nostra curantur}; the innkeeper
becomes St.\ Paul (\latin{Stabularius itaque ille est qui dixit: Misit me
Christus evangelizare}); the return is judgment day --- \latin{Beati quibus es
debitor!} --- and the conclusion is the sentence the whole tradition kept:
\latin{Non enim cognatio facit proximum, sed misericordia: quia misericordia
secundum naturam} --- kinship does not make the neighbour; mercy does, for
mercy is according to nature.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Augustine, \work{Quaestiones Evangeliorum} II.19; \work{Sermo} 131.6; \work{De doctrina christiana} I.30}
Augustine fixes the classic table, verified in the Latin: the man is Adam
\latin{in genere humano}; Jerusalem the heavenly city of peace; Jericho ---
his own touch, from the Hebrew --- \latin{luna interpretatur et significat
mortalitatem nostram}, the moon that waxes, wanes and sets; the robbers the
devil and his angels, who stripped the man of immortality and left him
\latin{semivivum} --- half-alive because \latin{ex parte qua potest
intellegere et cognoscere Deum vivus est}; priest and Levite the
\latin{sacerdotium et ministerium Veteris Testamenti, quod non poterat
prodesse ad salutem}; \latin{Samaritanus custos interpretatur}; the binding is
\latin{cohibitio peccatorum}, the oil \latin{consolatio spei bonae}, the wine
\latin{exhortatio ad operandum ferventissimo spiritu}; the beast the flesh of
the Incarnation, and to be set on it \latin{in ipsam incarnationem Christi
credere}; the inn the Church that refreshes travellers returning to the
eternal country; the two denarii the two precepts of charity, or the promise
of this life and of the life to come; the innkeeper the Apostle. In
\work{Sermo} 131 the same table turns anti-Pelagian and pastoral at once: the
half-dead man is free will after the fall, baptism forgives all sins ---
\latin{numquid quia deleta est iniquitas, finita est infirmitas?} --- and the
Church's inn is where the cure \textit{continues}: \latin{adhuc curatur}, he
is still under treatment; let no one exalt himself. And in \work{De doctrina
christiana} I.30 Augustine draws the moral boundary of the whole figure:
since no one was neighbour to the wounded man ``except him who took pity upon
him,'' every man is to be counted our neighbour, and the Lord himself
``wished also to be called our neighbour.''
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Gregory the Great, \work{Moralia in Iob} XX (on Job 29:25); \work{Regula pastoralis} II.6; \work{Homiliae in Evangelia} II.18.2}
Gregory never preached on this pericope --- the forty Gospel homilies contain
no lection from it, a bounded negative verified across the whole series ---
but he made the parable's medicine into a rule of government: wine and oil are
applied to the half-dead man's wounds \latin{ut per vinum mordeantur vulnera,
per oleum foveantur} --- so that everyone set over the healing of wounds must
mix the bite of discipline with the softness of mercy: \latin{miscenda est
ergo lenitas cum severitate}. In the same tradition as Origen he keeps the
etymology, and gives it its finest formulation, on Jn. 8:48: charged with
having a demon and with being a Samaritan, the Lord denied the one and was
silent about the other, \latin{custos namque humani generis venerat} --- for
he had come as the guardian of the human race, and to deny the name would
have been to deny the office.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Bede, \work{In Lucae Evangelium Expositio} III (PL 92, coll.\ 467--470)}
Bede's exposition is a mosaic of Augustine's table and Ambrose's phrases ---
whole clauses recur verbatim --- and its one legislative sentence is his own,
and is this guide's rule too: the Lord framed the parable \latin{ut omnem qui
misericordiam faceret cuilibet proximum doceret}, and yet the same parable
specially designates the Son of God; but we must not so interpret it
\latin{ut moralia mutuae fraternitatis instituta sub allegoriae regulis
extenuare et auferre conemur} --- the rules of allegory must not thin out or
carry off the moral institutes of mutual brotherhood. Both senses stand, and
at v.~36 he keeps them side by side: \latin{iuxta litteram}, the Lord's
meaning is plain --- no one is more a neighbour than the one who shows mercy;
\latin{sacratiore autem intellectu}, no one is more a neighbour \latin{quam
qui vulnera nostra curavit}. His own extensions: the denarii are handed over
\latin{altera die} because the risen Lord opened the Scriptures' sense, and
the innkeeper widens from Paul to all the Spirit-taught preachers.
\end{witnessnote}

\subsubsection*{The tradition's own dissent, and its modern balance}

The chain is not unanimous, and the disagreement is worth having exactly.
Cyril's sermon on the parable, quoted above, works entirely at the moral
level and never makes the Samaritan Christ --- the major Greek commentator
declining the reading the Latin West systematised. Eleven centuries later
Calvin reports the allegory --- ``This Samaritan they imagine to be Christ,
because he is our guardian'' --- and rejects it whole: ``I acknowledge that I
have no liking for any of these interpretations; but we ought to have a
deeper reverence for Scripture than to reckon ourselves at liberty to
disguise its natural meaning'' (\work{Harmony of the Evangelists} at Lk.
10:30, tr.\ Pringle, public domain). The parable's scope, for him, is that
neighbourhood ``extends to the whole human race.'' What Calvin treats as
rivals, Bede had held together five centuries earlier; and the 1911
\work{Catholic Encyclopedia}, warning generally that parables must not be
made to establish dogmas by riddling detail, still judges of this one that
``it can be explained mystically in detail, and is therefore as much an
`allegory' as a parable.'' The modern magisterial reading completes the
circle in the other direction: \work{Salvifici doloris} 28--30 (1984) makes
the parable the charter of the ``Good Samaritan'' vocation toward the
suffering and --- fusing it with Mt. 25 --- sets Christ no longer in the
Samaritan's place but in the wounded man's: ``he himself is the one who
receives help.'' The casting has reversed, and the command has not moved.

\subsubsection*{What the \work{Catena aurea} shows about the shape of the tradition}

Aquinas's chain on this pericope (cap.~10, lect.~7--9) stacks Cyril's moral
reading and the full Latin allegory side by side without adjudicating, and
its sourcing is itself a finding. The long allegorical extracts under
Chrysostom's name are flagged in the Oxford edition's own margins as
\latin{Hom. in loc. Ed. Lat.} --- a homily existing only in the old Latin
editions, which the preface assigns to ``some very early imitator or
epitomist of Chrysostom'' --- so the one great Greek name the chain seems to
add to the allegory is not genuinely there. The genuinely Greek voices Thomas
adds are Basil's geography at v.~30 and the ``Greek expositor'' the margins
identify as Severus, who has Christ ``fully call Himself a Samaritan.'' The
chain ends morally, with genuine Chrysostom (\work{Hom. 10 on Hebrews}):
help the oppressed, ``be he Gentile or Jew.'' English quoted from the
public-domain Oxford translation (1840s).

\subsection*{Offertory \cue{Off.}}

\elementid{Precátus est Móyses}{\latin{Antiphona ad Offertorium} --- Exodi 32, 11, 13 et 14}{1558}

One of the rare offertories that tells a story, and among the most admired
pieces in the repertory --- Schuster calls it, in words and music alike, ``a
little liturgical gem.'' While the gifts go to the altar the choir sings
Moses at the mountain: the question flung at God, the plea, the appeal to the
oath sworn to the patriarchs, and the ending no drama would risk --- \latin{et
placátus factus est Dóminus} --- and the Lord was appeased.

\textbf{The text is older than the Bible beside it.} The five divergences
tabulated at the head of this commentary are one finding: the chant is not
loose quotation of the Vulgate but an Old-Latin, Septuagint-shaped
centonisation. Its \latin{Precátus est \dots\ in conspéctu Dómini} construes
the prayer as made \textit{before} the Lord, where Jerome's \latin{orabat
Dominum} takes a direct object; its \latin{Quare, Dómine, irásceris in pópulo
tuo} addresses God in the second person where Jerome has the third
(\latin{irascitur furor tuus}); its \latin{Parce iræ ánimæ tuæ} doubles anger
upon anger and carries the matter of a verse the citation does not even name;
its \latin{terram fluéntem lac et mel} keeps the accusative where Jerome
wrote \latin{lacte et melle}; and its closing clause construes \latin{de
malignitáte, quam dixit fácere} where Jerome wrote \latin{ne faceret malum
quod locutus fuerat}. Each of those is the shape the Septuagint's Greek has
at Ex.\ 32:11--14, and the chant is therefore Septuagint-shaped; but no Greek
witness was collated for this guide, so the Greek is named as the explanation
and is not quoted. What \textit{is} checkable is that this text-form
circulated in Latin before Jerome: Augustine's lemma in the
\work{Quaestiones in Heptateuchum} reads \latin{Et propitiatus est Dominus de
malitia quam dixit facere populo suo} --- the chant's construction, not the
Vulgate's. Verified in the Latin at the cited edition.

\textbf{The chant's own history, briefly and on documented witnesses.} In an
early manuscript of the Gregorian Sacramentary tradition, the Mass-set
before this Sunday's orations carries the chant cues \latin{Ant. Deus in
adiutorium. Resp. Benedicam dominum. Off. Precatus est moyses. Com. De
fructu operum} --- this Sunday's exact four chants, paired a thousand years
before the 1962 printing (Wilson's 1915 edition of the Hadrianum, p.~172,
apparatus; the old Sunday-count runs one ahead of the missal's). The
offertory once had verses, dropped with all offertory verses centuries ago:
Ott's \work{Offertoriale} prints two, the first continuing the scene with
\latin{Dixit Dominus ad Moysen: Invenisti gratiam in conspectu meo}, the
second answering with the glory in the cloud. The 1911 \work{Catholic
Encyclopedia} records the shrinkage: the offertory was once a whole psalm
with antiphon; by Durandus's day the verses were already being omitted.
Johner's 1940 commentary hears in the melody's mounting figures over
\latin{Quare} ``violent agitation'' --- the music arguing with God as the
text does. And the piece kept working after the choirbooks: Palestrina set
\latin{Precatus est Moyses} for five voices in his \work{Offertoria totius
anni} (Rome, 1593) under this very Sunday's rubric, and Lassus set it before
him --- documented settings, cited for existence from their editions.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Augustine, \work{Quaestiones in Heptateuchum} II, qq.\ 141--149 (on Ex.\ 32); \work{De civitate Dei} XV.25}
The intercession scene drew from Augustine his most careful analysis of
prayer that ``changes God's mind.'' On v.~14: \latin{malitiam hic poenam
intellegi voluit} --- the ``evil'' God is appeased of is penalty, not malice
--- \latin{malus enim Deus non est; sed malis ingerit mala, quia iustus est}.
On Moses' astonishing offer at v.~32, the logic is a lawyer's: he spoke it
\latin{securus}, safe, ``so that the conclusion would follow from what came
after: since God would not blot Moses from his book, he would forgive the
people that sin.'' Deepest of all, on \latin{Sine me} --- God's ``Let me
alone'' before Moses has said a word: by these words ``God signified how
much that people profited with him from being loved by such a man \dots\ so
that we might be advised that, when our own merits weigh us down, we can be
relieved before him by the merits of those he loves''; the sentence is
spoken \latin{ac si diceretur \dots\ dilectio tua in illos intercedit mihi}
--- as if to say, your love intercedes with me for them. God, he adds, ``was
loving them the more secretly while terrifying them aloud.'' And the
\work{City of God} supplies the rule for the whole scene: \latin{ira Dei non
perturbatio animi eius est, sed iudicium quo inrogatur poena peccato} ---
God's anger is not a disturbance of his mind but the judgment by which
penalty is imposed --- and God does not repent as a man does, his sentence
being as fixed as his foreknowledge is sure; Scripture speaks so ``to
terrify the proud and rouse the negligent.'' Latin verified at the cited
public editions.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Gregory the Great, \work{Moralia in Iob} IX.xvi.23 and XX.v.14}
Gregory turns the scene into a doctrine of intercessory boldness: ``Did not
Moses resist God's wrath, when standing up for the fallen people \dots\ by
the oblation of his own death?'' The saints ``lift up themselves against
Him, and the Divine Power arms them in alliance with Itself against Itself
\dots\ He bears the supplicant's contradiction which He inspires.'' His
reading of \latin{Sine me} converges with Augustine's from the other side:
``What is it to say to His servant, Let Me alone; but to give him boldness
to supplicate?'' --- the prohibition is the permission. In Book XX Moses is
the pattern ruler, ``mercy united with severity \dots\ he who begged for the
life of all even with his own death.'' English from the public-domain
Library of the Fathers translation.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Tertullian, \work{Adversus Marcionem} II.26; St.\ John Chrysostom, \work{Homily 16 on Romans}}
Tertullian is the witness who actually says what preachers often attribute
to the whole tradition: Moses deprecating the Father's wrath is a figure of
Christ --- the chapter presents ``Christ, prefigured in the person of Moses
as the deprecator of the Father, and the offerer of His own life for the
salvation of the people,'' and reads \latin{Sine me} as spoken precisely
``in order that by his entreaty, and by offering himself, he might hinder''
the judgment --- ``that you might by such an instance learn how much
privilege is vouchsafed with God to a faithful man.'' Chrysostom, expounding
Paul's own anathema-wish, makes Moses the model of the intercessor whose
daring is zeal for God's honour: ``as Moses seemed to be pleading for the
Jews, yet was doing everything for God's glory \dots\ so also does Paul.''
English from the public-domain ANF vol.~3 and NPNF first series vol.~11.
Johner's liturgical commentary draws the same line under this very chant:
Moses ``is but a weak type of Christ and His redemptive work,'' who did not
only pray but gave himself, and ``can show His wounds'' --- the offertory's
intercession completed at the altar it accompanies.
\end{witnessnote}

\subsection*{Secret \cue{Sec.}}

\elementid{Hóstias, quǽsumus, Dómine}{\latin{Secreta}, composed oration, with \latin{Præfatio de Ssma Trinitate}}{1559}

\begin{namedtranslation}{English: Cummiskey 1861, Secret of this formulary, printed p.~422}
Mercifully look down, O Lord, on the offerings we lay on thy holy altar; that
they may be to the honour of thy name, by obtaining pardon for us. Thro'.
\end{namedtranslation}

\textbf{A caution about that English.} The 1861 rendering reverses the
Latin's order --- honour first, pardon as the means --- and the Latin's point
is in the order it actually has. \latin{Ut, nobis indulgéntiam largiéndo,
tuo nómini dent honórem}: the gerund clause makes God's act of lavishing
pardon the very mode in which the offerings render him honour. Not pardon
in exchange for glory, but pardon \textit{as} glory: God's name is honoured
precisely in his largesse. The two ends of sacrifice that a treatise would
distinguish --- propitiation and praise --- are fused in one clause of
liturgical grammar. Schuster reads it just so: perfect adoration, and the
pardon ``to which every other favour is subordinate.''

\textbf{Transmission, on documented witnesses.} Not located in the Verona
collection; in the Gelasian it stands in the same Mass-set as this Sunday's
Collect and Postcommunion, reading \latin{largiáris} where the later books
read \latin{largiéndo} (Wilson, pp.~228--229, with the apparatus recording
the eighth-century reading); the Hadrianum has the 1962 wording exact
(Wilson 1915, p.~173). And the prayer outlived the 1962 books twice over:
the postconciliar Missal appoints it, with the same wording, as the
\latin{super oblata} of Thursday after Ash Wednesday --- its current
official English, ``that, bestowing on us your pardon, our oblations may
give honor to your name,'' keeps the gerund's theology intact.

\subsection*{Communion \cue{Comm.}}

\elementid{De fructu óperum tuórum}{\latin{Antiphona ad Communionem} --- Ps. 103, 13 et 14-15}{1560}

\begin{namedtranslation}{English: Douay--Rheims (Challoner), Ps. 103:13--15, the verses printed whole}
Thou waterest the hills from thy upper rooms: the earth shall be filled with
the fruit of thy works: Bringing forth grass for cattle, and herb for the
service of men. That thou mayst bring bread out of the earth: And that wine
may cheer the heart of man. That he may make the face cheerful with oil: and
that bread may strengthen man's heart.
\end{namedtranslation}

The antiphon's path through those verses is exact and is declared by the
missal's own citation: v.~13's second clause with its order inverted and a
vocative added, v.~14's cattle-and-herb clause omitted, then vv.~14b--15
entire. What the Church sings at the rail is the creation psalm's pantry:
bread out of the earth, wine that gladdens the heart, oil that brightens the
face, bread that strengthens the heart --- sung at the moment the faithful
receive what the day's Secret called \latin{hóstias} and the tradition calls
the Bread.

The reception of these verses runs the full spectrum, and the spectrum is
the commentary. At the literal pole, Theodoret: God supplies rain so that
the earth brings forth every kind of fruit --- ``bread to strengthen and
nourish human nature, wine to gladden and make life sweeter, oil to nourish
within and brighten without'' --- providence, and nothing else; and
Bellarmine agrees (``\thinspace`wine may cheer the heart of man,'
intelligible enough''), drawing three morals: gratitude to the source of all
supports of bodily life, wonder that the Father gave ``even the luxuries,''
and the duty of frugal meals and moderate drink. At the other pole stand the
two Latin doctors the liturgy has, in effect, sided with by singing these
verses here.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Augustine, \work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 103 (New Advent ``Psalm 104''), Sermo 3, §§12--14}
``That it may bring forth bread out of the earth. What bread? Christ. Out of
what earth? From Peter, from Paul, from the other stewards of the truth
\dots\ He is the bread who descended from heaven, that He might be brought
forth out of the earth.'' On the wine, the sober inebriation: ``How
excellent is Your cup which makes inebriate! \dots\ Be inebriated; yet
beware, from what source'' --- an ebriety seen ``in the holy love of
righteousness'' and in a mind estranged from earth toward heaven. On the
oil: Christ is Christ from the chrism, ``but He anoints over His whole
Body,'' so every comer receives the grace that gladdens the countenance.
And on the psalm's strange restriction --- bread strengthening the
\textit{heart} --- ``as it were, he has forced us to understand what bread
he was speaking of'': visible bread strengthens the stomach; there is
another bread, \latin{panis cordis}. English from the public-domain NPNF
first series, vol.~8, checked against the Latin sermon divisions.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Cassiodorus, \work{Expositio Psalmorum} 103, ad vv.\ 14--15}
The most explicitly sacramental witness this guide has met. Lest anyone take
the bread as common bread, Cassiodorus says, the psalm adds three things to
be understood spiritually: \latin{vinum laetificat cor hominis, cum sacratum
fuerit in sanguinem Domini Christi} --- wine gladdens the heart of man
\textit{when it has been consecrated into the blood of the Lord Christ} ---
``hence we are permitted to be inebriated, as it is written: and thy cup
which inebriateth, how excellent it is \dots\ \latin{ebrietas sobria,
satietas felix}''; and the face is gladdened with oil \latin{cum regale
chrisma conficitur}, when the royal chrism is confected --- that face of
which it is said \latin{Accedite ad eum et illuminamini}. The proof-text he
reaches for is Ps.~33:6 --- the Gradual's own psalm, quoted inside the
Communion's: the two chants of this Mass were already cross-linked in a
sixth-century commentary. On \latin{panis cor hóminis confírmet} he makes
the letter itself force the spirit: if you take it bodily, does bread
strengthen only the heart and not the other members? Latin from the public
Migne text; the copyrighted ACW translation was not used.
\end{witnessnote}

That the 1962 books appoint these verses as a \textit{Communion} chant is a
fact about the liturgy that sits squarely on the Augustine--Cassiodorus line;
whether any compiler chose them for that reason is not documented and is not
claimed. The pairing itself, though, is old: the same early Gregorian witness
that cues \latin{Precatus est moyses} for this Sunday cues \latin{Com. De
fructu operum} beside it.

\subsection*{Postcommunion \cue{Postcomm.}}

\elementid{Vivíficet nos, quǽsumus, Dómine}{\latin{Postcommunio}, composed oration}{1561}

\begin{namedtranslation}{English: Cummiskey 1861, Postcommunion of this formulary, printed p.~423}
May the sacred participation of these thy mysteries, O Lord, we beseech thee,
give us life; and be to us both an expiation and protection. Thro'.
\end{namedtranslation}

\textbf{A caution about that English.} The Latin's one verb of bestowal,
\latin{tríbuat}, and its equaliser \latin{páriter} both vanish in ``be to us
both''; and the 1861 English pluralises ``these thy mysteries'' where the
Latin has the singular \latin{huius \dots\ mystérii}. The argument is
conducted on the Latin.

The prayer opens on the day's most loaded verb. The Epistle's hinge was
\latin{spíritus autem vivíficat}; the last oration of the same Mass begins
\latin{Vivíficet nos} --- the lesson's verb turned into a petition over the
sacrament just received. That is a textual observation about shared
vocabulary, verified on the two printed texts; no claim is made that anyone
designed it. What the prayer then asks is a pair held level: \latin{et
páriter nobis expiatiónem tríbuat, et munímen} --- that the one holy
participation bestow, equally and at once, expiation for what is past and a
rampart for what is coming. Cleansing looks backward, \latin{munímen}
forward; \latin{páriter} refuses to rank them. Schuster's verdict on the
prayer is quoted for its exactness: in its Latin brevity, reminiscent of an
ancient inscription, it ``is an epitome of Eucharistic doctrine.''

\textbf{Transmission, on documented witnesses.} The Verona collection
carries it among the July Masses with one different word --- \latin{
participatio \textbf{tui} sancta mysterii}, participation \textit{in thee}
(Feltoe, p.~60) --- a reading worth a footnote all by itself; the Gelasian
and the Hadrianum have the 1962 form with \latin{huius} (Wilson, pp.\
228--229; Wilson 1915, p.~173, where the older \latin{tui} form also
recurs in the Masses in time of war, p.~199). The postconciliar Missal keeps
the prayer verbatim as the postcommunion of Tuesday of the third week of
Lent. First attested in the fifth-to-sixth-century collection, still asked
after Communion today: of this Sunday's three orations, none has been
allowed to retire.
