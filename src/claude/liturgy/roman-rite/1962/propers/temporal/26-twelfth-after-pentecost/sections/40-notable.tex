\section*{The Propers: Notable and Quotable}

Four verified afterlives of wording from the scriptural propers, each one a
use that moves the phrase into a register its author did not occupy. Straight
exegesis, devotional reuse, and bare quotation are excluded; they belong in
the commentary above. Two of the four are judicial and that concentration is
itself the finding: of everything these appointed texts contain, what the
law took and kept were a lawyer's evasive question and a warning against
reading by the letter.

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel, Lk. 10:29 --- the lawyer's dodge becomes the law of negligence, 1932}
\textbf{Appointed phrase.} \latin{Et quis est meus próximus?}, Douay--Rheims
``And who is my neighbour?''

\textbf{Later use and locus.} Lord Atkin's speech in \work{Donoghue v
Stevenson}, House of Lords, 26 May 1932, [1932] A.C.\ 562 at 580 --- the case
of the snail in the ginger-beer bottle, and the foundation of the modern law
of negligence: ``The rule that you are to love your neighbour becomes in law
you must not injure your neighbour; and the lawyer's question `Who is my
neighbour?' receives a restricted reply. You must take reasonable care to
avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to
injure your neighbour. Who then in law is my neighbour?'' Verified in the
Scottish Council of Law Reporting's transcription of the judgment.

\textbf{The turn.} Luke frames the question as an evasion --- the lawyer asks
it \latin{volens iustificáre seípsum} --- and the parable refuses to answer
it, replying instead with a neighbour who acted. Atkin does the opposite of
the parable on purpose: he takes the evasive question seriously, announces
that in law it ``receives a restricted reply,'' and builds the boundary the
Gospel declined to draw. The turn is double: sacred command becomes tort
doctrine, and the question Christ deflected becomes the one the judge must
answer --- while naming it, candidly, ``the lawyer's question.''

\textbf{Rights and limit.} A 1932 United Kingdom judgment, freely quotable.
Atkin's verbal dependence is on the English Bible's wording of this verse; he
cites no evangelist, and the ``restricted reply'' is his own coinage.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel, Lk. 10:30--32 --- the seminarians step over the man in the road, 1973}
\textbf{Appointed phrase.} \latin{Homo quidam descendébat ab Ierúsalem in
Iéricho}, Douay--Rheims ``A certain man went down from Jerusalem to Jericho''
--- and the priest and Levite who, seeing him, ``passed by.''

\textbf{Later use and locus.} John M.\ Darley and C.\ Daniel Batson, ```From
Jerusalem to Jericho': A Study of Situational and Dispositional Variables in
Helping Behavior,'' \work{Journal of Personality and Social Psychology} 27.1
(1973), pp.\ 100--108. Seminarians at Princeton Theological Seminary were sent
between two buildings past a shabbily dressed confederate slumped in a
doorway; some had been told to hurry, and some were on their way to give a
talk on this very parable. From the published abstract: ``Subjects in a hurry
to reach their destination were more likely to pass by without stopping.''
From the discussion, p.~107: ``on several occasions, a seminary student going
to give his talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan literally stepped over
the victim as he hurried on his way!''

\textbf{The turn.} The pericope becomes an experimental protocol, and the
experiment re-enacts it: the title is the parable's own road, the slumped
confederate is the \latin{semivívo relícto}, and the finding --- hurry, not
piety or personality, predicted who stopped --- is the priest and the Levite
measured at $p<.05$. Scripture's most famous indictment of religious
professionals was confirmed on religious professionals by their own
psychology department.

\textbf{Rights and limit.} The article is under APA copyright; only the short
sentences above are quoted, with citation. The study engages the parable's
narrative, and its title quotes the verse's route exactly; the phrase ``passed
by on the other side'' in its abstract is the King James idiom, where the
Douay of vv.\ 31--32 has simply ``passed by.''
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Epistle, 2 Cor. 3:6 --- Paul's covenant contrast becomes a canon of statutory construction, 1950}
\textbf{Appointed phrase.} \latin{Líttera enim occídit}, Douay--Rheims ``For
the letter killeth.''

\textbf{Later use and locus.} Justice Felix Frankfurter, dissenting in
\work{United States ex rel.\ Knauff v.\ Shaughnessy}, 338 U.S.\ 537 (1950), at
548 and 550, verified in the official United States Reports: ``It is true also
of Acts of Congress that `The letter killeth,''' and, of the statute before
the Court, ``Due regard for the whole body of immigration laws and policies
makes it singularly appropriate in construing the War Brides Act to be heedful
of the admonition that `The letter killeth.'\thinspace'' The case was the
summary exclusion, without a hearing, of an American soldier's German war
bride.

\textbf{The turn.} Paul's sentence contrasts two covenants --- the
ministration engraved on stones and the ministration of the Spirit --- and
says nothing about how to read documents. English usage long ago re-cut it
into a maxim of interpretation, letter against spirit, and Frankfurter
completes the migration: the Apostle's theology of the new testament is quoted
as authority for construing an Act of Congress generously. That a dissent on
behalf of an excluded immigrant wife reaches for a verse about a covenant of
mercy is the kind of fit no one planned.

\textbf{Rights and limit.} United States Reports text, public domain. The four
quoted words are common to the Douay and the King James; Frankfurter cites no
scripture reference, and the interpretive maxim he invokes is the naturalised
idiom, not Paul's argument --- which the detailed commentary shows is about
covenants, not construction.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Introit, Ps. 69:2 --- the cry for help rides a ducal fanfare, 1610}
\textbf{Appointed phrase.} \latin{Deus, in adiutórium meum inténde: Dómine, ad
adiuvándum me festína}, Douay--Rheims ``O God, come to my assistance; O Lord,
make haste to help me.''

\textbf{Later use and locus.} Claudio Monteverdi, \work{Vespro della Beata
Vergine} (Venice: Ricciardo Amadino, 1610), opening versicle and response
``Deus in adiutorium meum intende / Domine ad adiuvandum me festina.'' The
whole choir intones the response on a single repeated chord while the
orchestra plays the opening toccata of Monteverdi's 1607 opera \work{Orfeo}
--- the Gonzaga court's own instrumental flourish --- around it; the borrowing
is documented in the standard literature and stated plainly in, for example,
Jennifer More Glagov's 2016 Music of the Baroque programme note, which
identifies the versicle and response ``into which Monteverdi incorporates
music from the opening of his 1607 opera \work{Orfeo}.''

\textbf{The turn.} This verse is among the most-repeated petitions in Western
liturgy: since Cassian commended it and the Rule of Benedict appointed it, it
has opened every hour of the Office but Matins, always as a bare cry before
anything else is allowed to happen. Monteverdi opens his most ambitious sacred
publication by dressing exactly that cry in opera-house brass --- a plea for
rescue delivered as a court fanfare, sacred urgency wearing theatrical
splendour. The setting does not merely decorate the text; it makes the
Church's plainest sentence arrive like a duke.

\textbf{Rights and limit.} The 1610 print and its music are public domain; the
programme note is quoted in short compass with attribution. The register
reading --- liturgical cry inside a theatrical fanfare --- is the standard
musicological account of a documented self-borrowing, not a recorded intention
of Monteverdi's; and the versicle's Office use belongs to the Breviary, this
Introit being the one place the 1962 Missal sings the same verse at Mass.
\end{afterlife}
