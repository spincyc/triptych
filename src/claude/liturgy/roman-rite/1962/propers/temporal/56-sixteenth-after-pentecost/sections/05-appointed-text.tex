\section*{The Appointed Formulary in Full}

Everything the 1962 \latin{Missale Romanum} prints for this Mass, in the order
it prints it: the Latin of the Vatican \latin{editio typica} exactly as the
controlling facsimile has it, each element under its own printed heading,
scriptural reference and marginal number --- except the Alleluia, over which the
edition prints no separate heading and cites the versicle in line --- and
beneath it the English of an identified public-domain witness.

\textbf{Formulary.} \latin{DOMINICA DECIMA SEXTA post Pentecosten},
\latin{II classis}, printed pp.~397--398, marginal nos.\ 1592--1601. It opens
immediately below the Postcommunion of the Fifteenth Sunday (no.\ 1591) and
closes immediately above the heading \latin{DOMINICA DECIMA SEPTIMA post
Pentecosten} (no.\ 1602). The
two pages appoint no Tract, no Sequence, no second or third oration, no
commemoration, no blessing and no ritual or seasonal alternative.

\textbf{The English below.} Scriptural elements are the Douay--Rheims as
revised by Bishop Challoner (1749--1752) --- the English of the Clementine
Vulgate the missal prints --- in the Project Gutenberg e-text of that revision.
The three orations are the anonymous English of \work{The Roman Missal
translated into the English language for the use of the laity}, first revised
edition (Philadelphia: Eugene Cummiskey, 1861), from the three lines that book
supplies for this formulary, reproduced with its own nineteenth-century
spelling and its abbreviated conclusion ``Thro'.'' \textbf{That English is a
free devotional rendering and not a word-for-word translation.} The Douay, for
its part, renders the psalter and not the chant.

\elementguard
\subsection*{1. Introit \cue{Int.}}

\begin{appointedlatin}{\latin{Ant. ad Introitum} --- Ps. 85, 3 et 5 --- marginal no.\ 1592}
Miserére mihi, Dómine, quóniam ad te clamávi tota die: quia tu, Dómine, suávis
ac mitis es, et copiósus in misericórdia ómnibus invocántibus te.
\textnormal{Ps. ibid., 1} Inclína, Dómine, aurem tuam mihi, et exáudi me:
quóniam inops et pauper sum ego. ℣.~Glória Patri.
\end{appointedlatin}

\begin{namedtranslation}{English: Douay--Rheims (Challoner), Ps. 85:3, 5 and 1}
\textbf{v.~3} \textbf{Have mercy on me, O Lord, for I have cried to thee all
the day.} \textbf{v.~5} \textbf{For thou, O Lord, art sweet and mild: and
plenteous in mercy to all that call upon thee.} \textit{Ps.} \textbf{v.~1} A
prayer for David himself. \textbf{Incline thy ear, O Lord, and hear me: for I
am needy and poor.} ℣.~\latin{Glória Patri.}
\end{namedtranslation}

\englishgap{The antiphon joins v.~3 to v.~5 across a verse it does not sing,
and nothing on the page marks the join. \textbf{One of its five departures from
the psalter has no English here}: the Douay's \emph{Incline thy ear, O Lord}
does not carry the dative \latin{mihi} the chant adds. The psalm title \emph{A
prayer for David himself} runs the other way --- the Douay prints it inside
v.~1 and the chant does not sing it --- and it is not one of the five. The
doxology cue \latin{Glória Patri} is the Ordinary's text and no part of this
formulary's own.}

\elementguard
\subsection*{2. Collect \cue{Coll.}}

\begin{appointedlatin}{\latin{Oratio} --- marginal no.\ 1593}
Tua nos, quǽsumus, Dómine, grátia semper et prævéniat et sequátur: ac bonis
opéribus iúgiter præstet esse inténtos. Per Dóminum.
\end{appointedlatin}

\begin{namedtranslation}{English: Cummiskey (Philadelphia, 1861), Collect of this formulary}
May thy grace, O Lord, always prevent and follow us; and make us constantly
zealous in the practice of good works. Thro'.
\end{namedtranslation}

\englishgap{\emph{Prevent} here is the seventeenth-century sense of \emph{go
before}, answering \latin{prævéniat}; it does not mean \emph{hinder}. The
rendering turns \latin{bonis opéribus iúgiter præstet esse inténtos}, which
asks that grace make us intent on good works, into a state of zeal in
practising them, and \latin{iúgiter} and \latin{semper} arrive as one word,
\emph{always}.}

\elementguard
\subsection*{3. Epistle \cue{Ep.}}

\begin{appointedlatin}{\latin{Léctio Epístolæ beáti Pauli Apóstoli ad Ephésios.} --- Ephes. 3, 13-21 --- marginal no.\ 1594}
Fratres: Obsecro vos, ne deficiátis in tribulatiónibus meis pro vobis: quæ est
glória vestra. Huius rei grátia flecto génua mea ad Patrem Dómini nostri Iesu
Christi, ex quo omnis patérnitas in cælis et in terra nominátur, ut det vobis
secúndum divítias glóriæ suæ, virtúte corroborári per Spíritum eius in
interiórem hóminem, Christum habitáre per fidem in córdibus vestris: in
caritáte radicáti, et fundáti, ut possítis comprehéndere cum ómnibus sanctis,
quæ sit latitúdo, et longitúdo, et sublímitas, et profúndum: scire étiam
supereminéntem sciéntiæ caritátem Christi, ut impleámini in omnem plenitúdinem
Dei. Ei autem, qui potens est ómnia fácere superabundánter quam pétimus, aut
intellégimus, secúndum virtútem, quæ operátur in nobis: ipsi glória in
Ecclésia, et in Christo Iesu, in omnes generatiónes sǽculi sæculórum. Amen.
\end{appointedlatin}

\begin{namedtranslation}{English: Douay--Rheims (Challoner), Eph. 3:13--21}
\textbf{v.~13} Wherefore I pray you not to faint at my tribulations for you,
which is your glory. \textbf{v.~14} For this cause I bow my knees to the Father
of our Lord Jesus Christ, \textbf{v.~15} Of whom all paternity in heaven and
earth is named: \textbf{v.~16} That he would grant you, according to the riches
of his glory, to be strengthened by his Spirit with might unto the inward man:
\textbf{v.~17} That Christ may dwell by faith in your hearts: that, being
rooted and founded in charity, \textbf{v.~18} You may be able to comprehend,
with all the saints, what is the breadth and length and height and depth,
\textbf{v.~19} To know also the charity of Christ, which surpasseth all
knowledge: that you may be filled unto all the fulness of God. \textbf{v.~20}
Now to him who is able to do all things more abundantly than we desire or
understand, according to the power that worketh in us: \textbf{v.~21} To him be
glory in the church and in Christ Jesus, unto all generations, world without
end. Amen.
\end{namedtranslation}

\englishgap{\latin{Fratres: Obsecro vos} is a liturgical formula and stands in
no Bible: where the missal addresses the brethren, the Douay renders Paul's own
\latin{Propter quod peto}, \emph{Wherefore I pray you}, and the connective that
points back to a verse this Mass does not read is gone from both. At v.~20 the
missal prints \latin{intellégimus} against the Clementine's
\latin{intelligimus}, a received spelling of one word and not another word; the
Douay's \emph{understand} answers either.}

\elementguard
\subsection*{4. Gradual \cue{Grad.}}

\begin{appointedlatin}{\latin{Graduale} --- Ps. 101, 16-17 --- marginal no.\ 1595}
Timébunt gentes nomen tuum, Dómine, et omnes reges terræ glóriam tuam.
℣.~Quóniam ædificávit Dóminus Sion, et vidébitur in maiestáte sua.
\end{appointedlatin}

\begin{namedtranslation}{English: Douay--Rheims (Challoner), Ps. 101:16--17}
\textbf{v.~16} And the Gentiles shall fear thy name, O Lord, and all the kings
of the earth thy glory. \textbf{v.~17} For the Lord hath built up Sion: and he
shall be seen in his glory.
\end{namedtranslation}

\englishgap{\textbf{The Douay does not answer this chant at its decisive word.}
\emph{He shall be seen in his glory} renders the psalter's \latin{in gloria
sua}; the Gradual sings \latin{in maiestáte sua}, and no public-domain English
witness of the appointed text carries that noun. The Douay also keeps the
connective \emph{And} which the chant drops, and its \emph{For} renders
\latin{quia} where the chant reads \latin{Quóniam}.}

\elementguard
\subsection*{5. Alleluia \cue{All.}}

\begin{appointedlatin}{No separate heading --- ℣.~Ps. 97, 1, cited in line --- marginal no.\ 1596}
Allelúia, allelúia. ℣.~\textnormal{Ps. 97, 1} Cantáte Dómino cánticum novum:
quia mirabília fecit Dóminus. Allelúia.
\end{appointedlatin}

\begin{namedtranslation}{English: Douay--Rheims (Challoner), Ps. 97:1}
\textbf{v.~1} A psalm for David himself. \textbf{Sing ye to the Lord a new
canticle: because he hath done wonderful things.} His right hand hath wrought
for him salvation, and his arm is holy.
\end{namedtranslation}

\englishgap{The chant supplies \latin{Dóminus} as the subject of \latin{fecit},
which the psalter leaves implicit and the Douay renders \emph{he}; that added
noun has no counterpart in this English. The psalm title is not sung, and
neither is the second half of the verse, where the Lord's arm stands.}

\subsection*{6. Gospel \cue{Gosp.}}

\begin{appointedlatin}{✠ \latin{Sequéntia sancti Evangélii secúndum Lucam.} --- Luc. 14, 1-11 --- marginal no.\ 1597}
In illo témpore: Cum intráret Iesus in domum cuiúsdam príncipis pharisæórum
sábbato manducáre panem, et ipsi observábant eum. Et ecce homo quidam
hydrópicus erat ante illum. Et respóndens Iesus dixit ad legisperítos et
pharisǽos, dicens: Si licet sábbato curáre? At illi tacuérunt. Ipse vero
apprehénsum sanávit eum, ac dimísit. Et respóndens ad illos, dixit: Cuius
vestrum ásinus, aut bos in púteum cadet, et non contínuo éxtrahet illum die
sábbati? Et non póterant ad hæc respondére illi. Dicébat autem et ad invitátos
parábolam, inténdens quómodo primos accúbitus elígerent, dicens ad illos: Cum
invitátus fúeris ad núptias, non discúmbas in primo loco, ne forte honorátior
te sit invitátus ab illo, et véniens is qui te et illum vocávit, dicat tibi: Da
huic locum: et tunc incípias cum rubóre novíssimum locum tenére. Sed cum
vocátus fúeris, vade, recúmbe in novíssimo loco: ut, cum vénerit qui te
invitávit, dicat tibi: Amíce, ascénde supérius. Tunc erit tibi glória coram
simul discumbéntibus: quia omnis, qui se exáltat, humiliábitur: et qui se
humíliat, exaltábitur.
\end{appointedlatin}

\begin{namedtranslation}{English: Douay--Rheims (Challoner), Luke 14:1--11}
\textbf{v.~1} And it came to pass, when Jesus went into the house of one of the
chief of the Pharisees, on the sabbath day, to eat bread, that they watched him.
\textbf{v.~2} And behold, there was a certain man before him that had the
dropsy. \textbf{v.~3} And Jesus answering, spoke to the lawyers and Pharisees,
saying: Is it lawful to heal on the sabbath day? \textbf{v.~4} But they held
their peace. But he taking him, healed him and sent him away. \textbf{v.~5} And
answering them, he said: Which of you shall have an ass or an ox fall into a pit
and will not immediately draw him out, on the sabbath day? \textbf{v.~6} And
they could not answer him to these things. \textbf{v.~7} And he spoke a parable
also to them that were invited, marking how they chose the first seats at the
table, saying to them: \textbf{v.~8} When thou art invited to a wedding, sit not
down in the first place, lest perhaps one more honourable than thou be invited
by him: \textbf{v.~9} And he that invited thee and him, come and say to thee:
Give this man place. And then thou begin with shame to take the lowest place.
\textbf{v.~10} But when thou art invited, go, sit down in the lowest place; that
when he who invited thee cometh, he may say to thee: Friend, go up higher. Then
shalt thou have glory before them that sit at table with thee. \textbf{v.~11}
Because every one that exalteth himself shall be humbled: and he that humbleth
himself shall be exalted.
\end{namedtranslation}

\englishgap{\latin{In illo témpore} stands in no Bible either: the Douay gives
Luke's own \emph{And it came to pass}, which the liturgical incipit replaces.
At vv.~8--9 the missal runs two verses into one sentence, lowercasing the
\latin{Et} and setting a comma where the Clementine ends a sentence, so the
Douay's full stop after \emph{invited by him} has no counterpart on the missal's
page. The missal reads \latin{éxtrahet} at v.~5, which the Douay answers with
\emph{draw him out}.}

\rubric{Credo.} is printed immediately after \latin{exaltábitur.}, at the head
of the right-hand column and set apart from the pericope. It is the rubric
appointing the Creed and is no part of the Gospel text.

\subsection*{7. Offertory \cue{Off.}}

\begin{appointedlatin}{\latin{Ant. ad Offertorium} --- Ps. 39, 14 et 15 --- marginal no.\ 1598}
Dómine, in auxílium meum réspice: confundántur et revereántur, qui quærunt
ánimam meam, ut áuferant eam: Dómine, in auxílium meum réspice.
\end{appointedlatin}

\begin{namedtranslation}{English: Douay--Rheims (Challoner), Ps. 39:14--15}
\textbf{v.~14} Be pleased, O Lord, to deliver me: \textbf{look down, O Lord, to
help me.} \textbf{v.~15} \textbf{Let them be confounded and ashamed together,
that seek after my soul to take it away.} Let them be turned backward and be
ashamed that desire evils to me.
\end{namedtranslation}

\englishgap{\textbf{The second decisive word of this Mass has no English
either.} \emph{Look down, O Lord, to help me} renders the psalter's \latin{ad
adjuvandum me respice}; the antiphon sings \latin{in auxílium meum réspice},
which is verbatim Ps.~70:12 --- a verse of this Mass's own Communion psalm --- and
no public-domain English of the appointed text answers it. The Douay also carries
\emph{together}, the \latin{simul} the chant drops. The repetition of the first
clause is printed in full on the page and is not a cue, and the psalm carries
no refrain answering it.}

\elementguard
\subsection*{8. Secret \cue{Sec.}}

\begin{appointedlatin}{\latin{Secreta} --- marginal no.\ 1599}
Munda nos, quǽsumus, Dómine, sacrifícii præséntis efféctu: et pérfice
miserátus in nobis; ut eius mereámur esse partícipes. Per Dóminum.
\end{appointedlatin}

\begin{namedtranslation}{English: Cummiskey (Philadelphia, 1861), Secret of this formulary}
Cleanse us, O Lord, we beseech thee, by the efficacy of this sacrifice: and by
thy mercy make us worthy to partake thereof. Thro'.
\end{namedtranslation}

\englishgap{\latin{Pérfice miserátus in nobis} is an imperative addressed to
God, and \latin{mereámur} hangs on it in a purpose clause, so what the Latin
asks God to complete is one thing and the sharing that follows from it another.
Cummiskey folds the two together as \emph{by thy mercy make us worthy to
partake thereof}: the verb the Latin gives God, and the clause it governs, do
not survive into this English. The rendering compresses \latin{sacrifícii
præséntis efféctu} into \emph{the efficacy of this sacrifice}.}

\rubric{Præfatio de \Sanctissima{} Trinitate.} stands between the Secret and
the Communion antiphon, in the place the missal gives the preface direction,
and it is a rubric of this formulary.

\elementguard
\subsection*{9. Communion \cue{Comm.}}

\begin{appointedlatin}{\latin{Antiphona ad Communionem} --- Ps. 70, 16-17 et 18 --- marginal no.\ 1600}
\textnormal{Ps. 70, 16-17 et 18} Dómine, memorábor iustítiæ tuæ solíus: Deus,
docuísti me a iuventúte mea: et usque in senéctam et sénium, Deus, ne
derelínquas me.
\end{appointedlatin}

\begin{namedtranslation}{English: Douay--Rheims (Challoner), Ps. 70:16--18}
\textbf{v.~16} I will enter into the powers of the Lord: \textbf{O Lord, I will
be mindful of thy justice alone.} \textbf{v.~17} \textbf{Thou hast taught me, O
God, from my youth:} and till now I will declare thy wonderful works.
\textbf{v.~18} \textbf{And unto old age and grey hairs: O God, forsake me not,}
Until I shew forth thy arm to all the generation that is to come: Thy power,
\end{namedtranslation}

\englishgap{The missal's citation \latin{Ps. 70, 16-17 et 18} describes what the
antiphon takes: the second half of v.~16, the first half of v.~17 and the first
part of v.~18, which no verse range would express. There is no gap of wording
here and this is the only appointed chant of which that is true: every word of
the antiphon is the Clementine's, and its whole distance from the psalter is
where it starts and stops.}

\subsection*{10. Postcommunion \cue{Postcomm.}}

\begin{appointedlatin}{\latin{Postcommunio} --- marginal no.\ 1601}
Purífica, quǽsumus, Dómine, mentes nostras benígnus, et rénova cæléstibus
sacraméntis: ut consequénter et córporum præsens páriter et futúrum capiámus
auxílium. Per Dóminum.
\end{appointedlatin}

\begin{namedtranslation}{English: Cummiskey (Philadelphia, 1861), Postcommunion of this formulary}
Mercifully, O Lord, we beseech thee, purify our souls, and renew them by these
holy mysteries; that we may receive help thereby, both while we are in these
mortal bodies and hereafter. Thro'.
\end{namedtranslation}

\englishgap{In \latin{córporum præsens páriter et futúrum \dots\ auxílium} the
two adjectives agree with \latin{auxílium} and qualify the help asked for, not
the ones who receive it, and \latin{córporum} says whose help it is. Cummiskey
has \emph{both while we are in these mortal bodies and hereafter}, which keeps
the two times, attaches them to the worshippers and loses the noun they belong
to. \latin{Auxílium}, which the Offertory has already sung, is the word the two
elements share, and the English carries it as \emph{help} in one place and as
\emph{to help me} in the other.}
