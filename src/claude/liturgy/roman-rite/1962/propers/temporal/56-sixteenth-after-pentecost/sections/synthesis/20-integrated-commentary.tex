\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

\subsection*{1. Four expositors of one pericope, and the one whose vocabulary is this Mass's}

Four commentators expound Luke~14:1--11 directly, and they divide over what its
first half is even about. \textbf{Cyril of Alexandria spends a whole sermon on
the sabbath}, the first of two that break the pericope where it breaks
internally: the law was a shadow set to wait upon the truth, to keep the day
reasonably is to rest from sin and not from mercy, the lawyers say nothing out of
malice --- and he presses it to sarcasm, that on such reasoning a man would do
better to lay his own child joyfully in the grave than to break the sabbath. His second sermon reads the seating parable as a plain virtue of the
modest mind, where an honour seized is a theft with the goods still to be given
back. \textbf{Ambrose leaves
the controversy alone.} He gives the whole pericope one paragraph: the sick man
is one \latin{in quo fluxus carnis exuberans, animae gravabat officia, spiritus
exstinguebat ardorem}, and from there the lesson is humility, forbidden the
appetite for the higher place and taught \latin{ut humanitas persuasionis
coercitionis asperitatem excluderet}, and then hospitality, which is the Lord's
kind only when it is spent on the poor. Augustine, at \work{Quaestiones
euangeliorum} II q.~29, sets the dropsical man beside the beast in the well,
both of them weighed down by a humour, and then beside the covetous rich.
\textbf{Bede argues the sequence itself}, from the body's sickness to the
heart's --- \latin{per huius aegritudinem corporis, in illis exprimeretur
aegritudo cordis} --- and takes the ox and the ass for the wise and the slow, or
else for the two peoples, every one of them found in the pit of concupiscence
and hauled out \latin{iustificati gratis per gratiam ipsius}.

\textbf{What earns Bede his place at this formulary is a vocabulary the other
three do not have.} His name for the first place at table is a glorying in one's
own merits --- \latin{non se, de meritis gloriando, quasi caeteris sublimior
extollat} --- which is the word the Collect and the Communion of this Mass are
already using, and it belongs with his argument that the closing sentence is a rule
about God and not about men. He is also the one who declines
to close the parable's promise. \textbf{At \latin{Tunc erit tibi glória} he holds
two readings at once and will not choose}: the last things,
\latin{ne nunc quaerere incipias quod tibi servatur in fine}, and every day,
\latin{Potest autem et in hac vita intelligi \dots\ quia quotidie Dominus suas
nuptias intrat}.

\textbf{Not one of the four reads this Gospel together with any other element of
this Mass.} Guéranger does: he carries the closing axiom straight into the
doctrine of grace by way of Ecclus.~3:20--21 --- ``The greater thou art, the more
humble thyself in all things, and thou shalt find grace before God'' --- and
\textbf{among the witnesses on this formulary he alone ties one of its elements
outright to another}. He also names Ambrose the homilist the Church has chosen
for this Sunday, which is his own statement and rests on him alone, and reads the
wedding through Matt.~22:2 rather than through Luke. Benedict's use of the
sentence is of another kind. He sets it at the head of \work{Regula}~7, quoting
it as the voice of Scripture and naming no chapter --- but \textbf{the words are
not this pericope's alone}, standing at Luke~18:14 and at Matt.~23:12 besides, so
what the Rule attests is the reception of the sentence and not, demonstrably, of
the Gospel this Mass reads.

\textbf{Cyril's commentary survives entire only in Syriac}, and what is read here is R.~Payne Smith's 1859 English of that
Syriac, two removes from the Greek he preached; his lemma at 14:5 reads ``whose
son of yours, or whose ox,'' on which his whole appeal to a father's endangered
child turns, where the missal sings \latin{ásinus}. Bede tells Acca, in the
letter prefixed to the work, that he drew it from Ambrose, Augustine, Gregory and
Jerome and set each author's initial in the margin beside the borrowed words;
\textbf{no such initials survive in the Migne transmission}, and his
dropsy-and-miser comparison stands almost word for word in Augustine's
\work{Quaestiones}. And of the eight authorities the \work{Catena aurea} names at
this pericope, four are the four above at their own works, while the remaining
four --- Theophylact, Gregory's \work{Moralia}, Basil on the order of places at
table, and a Chrysostom excerpt --- \textbf{reach the page only in the Catena's
medieval Latin of the Greek Fathers}, which is not their Greek.

Two relations belong to the appointed words themselves. \latin{Amíce} stands in
five verses of the Clementine, and in only two of them does a host at a wedding
feast use it of his guest: this Gospel's v.~10, and Matt.~22:12, where the man so
addressed is thrown out. And the closing logion keeps company with Matt.~23:12,
Luke~1:52, Jas.~4:10 and 1~Pet.~5:6, and with the sapiential pair Prov.~25:6--7
and Ecclus.~3:20--21, of which Prov.~25:7's \latin{Ascende huc} comes nearest to
\latin{Amíce, ascénde supérius}, and comes nearest in the same situation.
\textbf{The pericope halves one continuous sabbath meal}: the healing and the
parable of places are read, the instruction to invite the poor and the parable of
the great supper are not, and the bread that opens the scene at v.~1 returns at
v.~15, outside what is appointed.

\subsection*{2. Four measures of a love said to surpass knowledge, and the three answers standing over the verse}

The lesson begins one verse before the sentence it completes and stops where the
letter's doctrinal half stops. Eph.~3:1 opens \latin{Huius rei gratia} and breaks
off into a digression; v.~14 takes up the same two words again, and the
liturgical incipit erases even that trace of the join by putting
\latin{Fratres: Obsecro vos} where the Clementine has \latin{Propter quod peto}.
At the far end the exhortation begins at 4:1 with \latin{Obsecro itaque vos}, the
very verb the incipit set at the front --- \textbf{two Latin texts falling
together, and not one derived from the other}. Every petition inside the reading is addressed to God for a gift, and the nouns
those petitions turn on are words the letter has used already. In the calendar registry named in the
References the doxology of vv.~20--21 stands at this Mass and at no other, while
the two remaining appointments of Ephesians~3 both cut v.~13 out.

Chrysostom's Homily~7 runs from 3:8 to the doxology, so the whole appointed
pericope lies inside a single homily. At v.~13 he answers the difficulty in the
Apostle's own words, making the tribulations the readers' own glory, since it was
God's love for them that let his servants be afflicted on their account; at vv.~14--15 he takes the
bent knees for the mark of a prayer meant in earnest, and \latin{omnis
patérnitas} for the reckoning of the tribes by their Creator where they had been
reckoned ``according to the number of Angels.'' \textbf{The four measures he does
not read as the Cross at all}: what they measure is the immensity of God's love
``and how it extends every where,'' drawn for the eye by the dimensions of a
solid body, ``pointing as it were to a man.'' What is read here is Gross Alexander's
English in the \work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers}, with no Greek text behind
it, so nothing above turns on Chrysostom's own wording.

\textbf{Augustine reads the measures as the four members of the Cross in three
works, and distributes the virtues differently in every one.}
\work{Epistula}~55.14.25 and \work{In Iohannis euangelium tractatus}~118.5 each
name Eph.~3:18 as the text under exposition, so neither attribution is an
inferred echo; between them they disagree on two of the four and exchange height
for length; and \work{Sermo}~165 gives a third set --- breadth to the charity
that alone works well, length to perseverance, height to the \latin{sursum
cor} and to loving God \latin{gratis}, depth to the judgments of God that cannot
be searched. Aquinas lays out three readings and adopts none: the Dionysian,
which takes the dimensions of God metaphorically and rests on Job~11:8--9; the
reading from charity; and the Cross, whose hidden depth he names
\textbf{predestination} --- and \textbf{Augustine's name appears nowhere in his
cross-reading}. Guéranger measures neither the love nor the Cross but the
indwelling: ``God alone \dots\ can strengthen in us the inward man enough to make
us understand, as the saints do, the dimensions \dots\ of the great mystery of
Christ dwelling in man.'' \textbf{So the cruciform reading is a development
inside one author and one position among three, and not the reading of the
verse.}

The last of Augustine's three ties the lesson to the day's own prayers. Headed
\latin{deque gratia et libera voluntate, contra Pelagianos}, \work{Sermo}~165
divides at \S1.1 precisely where the Collect divides grace ---
\latin{Peto enim a vobis, propter arbitrium voluntatis: rogo det vobis, propter
auxilium maiestatis}, and \latin{auxilium} is the word this Mass's Offertory and
Postcommunion both use --- and it ends at \S7.9 on \latin{unde gratiam tuam
meritum meum praecedat? Non}. The sermon itself runs only to 3:18 and stops three verses before
the doxology; and where it opens by remarking that the day's Apostle, psalm and
Gospel were of one mind, the day is Augustine's own in Africa, whose psalm was
Ps.~56:2, and the remark settles nothing about this Roman formulary.

Two words of the appointed Latin control what may be built upon it. At \S2.2
Augustine stops to defend \latin{altitudo}, the reading his African text carries
where the missal and the Clementine alike print \latin{sublímitas} ---
\textbf{so what he says of \latin{altitudo} he has not said of the missal's
noun}; and the missal's \latin{intellégimus} beside the Clementine's
\latin{intelligimus} is one word received two ways and not two words. Outside the
pericope two Pauline places carry the reading's own vocabulary:
2~Cor.~4:16--17 gathers \latin{non deficimus}, the renewal within, tribulation
and a weight of glory into one movement, and Col.~1:24 states most nearly what
Eph.~3:13 trades between an apostle's affliction and his hearers' glory.

\subsection*{3. What the chants did to the psalter, and what that does to every Father quoted at them}

Fifteen divergences stand between the appointed text and the Clementine Vulgate,
the Latin behind the English printed with these propers. The Introit departs at
five places --- \latin{Miserére mihi} for \latin{Miserere mei}, \latin{quia} for
\latin{Quoniam}, \latin{suávis ac mitis es} for \latin{suavis et mitis},
\latin{copiósus in misericórdia} for \latin{multæ misericordiæ}, and the dative
added at the psalm verse. The Gradual departs at three, of which
\latin{maiestáte} for \latin{gloria} is the substantive one. The Alleluia names
\latin{Dóminus}, whom the psalter leaves implicit, and stops before the Lord's
arm. The Offertory sings \latin{in auxílium meum réspice} for \latin{ad
adjuvandum me respice}, drops \latin{simul}, and prints its opening clause a
second time as a refrain the psalm has not. \textbf{The Communion departs at no word
at all}: every syllable of it is the Clementine's, and its whole distance from
the psalter lies in where it begins and ends. The two readings depart only in liturgical formulae
--- \latin{Fratres: Obsecro vos} and \latin{In illo témpore}, neither of which
stands in any Bible --- with the missal running Luke~14:8--9 into one sentence.

\textbf{The consequence for reception is two rules and not one.} At four of the
five chants the sixth-century lemma of Cassiodorus sides with the missal and
against the Clementine --- \latin{suavis ac mitis es \dots\ copiosus in misericordia},
\latin{videbitur in maiestate sua}, \latin{quia mirabilia fecit Dominus},
\latin{Domine, in auxilium meum respice} --- and against the Introit's clause and
the Offertory's, Migne's apparatus records no manuscript variant at all; at the
Communion his text and the Clementine agree with the antiphon alike.
\textbf{None of that is a critical text.} Migne's transcription stands behind
every one of the four agreements, so each of them hangs on a single word of a
single printing. Theodoret's Greek supplies the Alleluia's subject as the chant
does, and turns against the chant at two of the same four: at Ps.~101:17 his lemma is a
future whose noun is the Clementine's, and at Ps.~39:14 he has the Septuagint,
which Migne's facing Latin puts back into the Clementine's words.

\textbf{Augustine's lemma differs at three places where the difference carries
what he says.} At the Gradual it reads \latin{in gloria sua}, so no sentence of
his is about \latin{maiestáte}. At the Alleluia's unsung half-verse it reads
\latin{Sanavit ei dextera eius} --- ``healed,'' and ``for him'' --- and on those
two words he builds a distinction, \latin{Multi enim sanantur sibi, non ei},
against the Clementine's \latin{Salvavit sibi} and a third form again in
Cassiodorus, \latin{salvavit eum}. At the Offertory his clause is a third form
too, \latin{in adiuvandum mihi respice}. Jerome, ruling on that same clause at
\work{Epistula}~106 against a reported \latin{festina}, carries a fourth Latin
form, \latin{in adiutorium meum respice}, and \textbf{settles the verb the chant
sings while leaving its noun untouched}; the reading he corrects is what Ps.~69:2
has, the verse this psalm doubles.

\textbf{One departure moves toward another verse instead of away from one.} The
added dative turns Ps.~85:1 into the petition-form \latin{inclina aurem tuam
mihi}, which that psalter carries once and once only, at Ps.~16:6. The three
places that come nearest --- Ps.~30:3, Ps.~70:2 and Ps.~101:3, two of them the
psalms this Mass sings at the Communion and at the Gradual --- all read
\latin{inclina ad me aurem tuam}, and not one of them has \latin{mihi}. \textbf{What the Introit and
Ps.~16:6 hold in common is that four-word petition and the \latin{et exáudi}
standing beside it, and nothing wider}: Ps.~16:6 has no vocative, asks a hearing
for \latin{verba mea}, and opens on a clause the psalm verse does not carry. \latin{Oratio}
opens four psalms in the whole Clementine, of which this Mass appoints two,
Ps.~85 and Ps.~101, and a third, Ps.~16, is the psalm whose v.~6 that dative
reproduces --- and neither appointed title is sung.

Where the chants stop sets two of them beside a further text each. \latin{Canticum
novum} occurs at nine places in the Clementine and four of them carry the
Alleluia's full incipit; \textbf{two of those four touch other elements of this
formulary}. Ps.~95:1 carries the title \latin{quando domus aedificabatur post
captivitatem}, a building after captivity where the Gradual has
\latin{ædificávit Dóminus Sion}; Ps.~149:1 runs on into \latin{laus eius in
ecclesia sanctorum}, and the Epistle has both of those nouns, at
\latin{cum ómnibus sanctis} and at \latin{glória in Ecclésia}; and a
third of the nine, Ps.~39:4, belongs to the Offertory's own psalm. Cassiodorus makes the first
of those joins himself, two verses past the Gradual's cut, where he quotes the
Alleluia outright: \latin{Hic laudabit Dominum, novo scilicet cantico, sicut in
alio psalmo dictum est: Cantate Domino canticum novum}. And the Offertory's
petition is verbatim Ps.~70:12, a verse of this Mass's Communion psalm, under
another name for God.

\subsection*{4. Where the four psalm commentators agree, and where their divisions fall}

Augustine expounds all five appointed psalms, and at each of them three others
stand beside him: Cassiodorus in Latin, Theodoret in Migne's Greek with its
facing Latin, and Bellarmine in O'Sullivan's 1866 English. Aquinas reaches one of
the five, his \work{Postilla super Psalmos} breaking off inside Psalm~54, and
Jerome adjudicates the wording at two. \textbf{Where they agree they agree about
the man who prays; where they divide they divide about the referent.}

Their agreements are what make the divisions real ones. \textbf{All four take the Introit's \latin{inops et pauper} for the
confession of a man with nothing of his own, and not one of them for a statement
about money}: Cassiodorus and Bellarmine volunteer that the rich are not shut
out, and Augustine arrives at the same place by asking who the poor man is and
answering that a rich man may be \latin{in Deo pauperes}. \textbf{All four
measure the Communion's youth-to-old-age as the reach of God's teaching, and none
of them as a life's ordinary length.} And \textbf{no one of the five witnesses at
the Offertory hears \latin{confundántur et revereántur} as a wish that the
enemies be destroyed}: Augustine and Bellarmine find a prophecy already fulfilled
at the Resurrection, Cassiodorus a prayer for the persecutors' conversion,
Theodoret a petition that historical enemies be routed, and Aquinas lets the
penitential and the punitive confusion stand together.

The divisions run at every psalm, and every one of them is a division about who
speaks or what is meant. \textbf{Who speaks.} At the Gradual Augustine gives the
psalm to Christ; Cassiodorus records that reading and declines it, since Christ
is nowhere said to have been \latin{anxius}, and hands the psalm instead to an
afflicted penitent left nameless on purpose, \latin{ut cum uni datur, omnes sibi
pauperes Christi cognoscerent attributum}; Theodoret gives it to the people in
the Babylonian captivity. At the Offertory Theodoret supplies a speaker no Latin
offers --- the Church herself, who lays what befalls her to her own sins and
holds the slack along with the perfect, \latin{quoniam unum est corpus, tanquam
ex una persona}. \textbf{What is seen.} Augustine sets the Judgment against the
first coming, where the builder appeared \latin{in infirmitate sua} and, as the
\work{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers} render that exposition, ``had no form nor
comeliness''; Cassiodorus and Bellarmine keep the Judgment with him;
Theodoret takes \latin{vidébitur} of Jerusalem rebuilt; and Guéranger takes it of
the majesty God shows now, in the Church.

\textbf{What the wonders are.} For Augustine, a whole world raised out of
everlasting death by the Lord's holy arm; for Cassiodorus, regeneration and the
Incarnation, the Gospel healings named and then put aside as things the saints
did too, \latin{Sed ista fecerunt et sancti eius}, so that the new thing is the
Resurrection; for Bellarmine, an enumeration gathering both; for Theodoret,
simply whatever exceeds nature and expectation. \textbf{How long a day is.}
Augustine's \latin{tota die} is the whole duration of the Body, \latin{corpus
Christi tota die clamat, sibi decedentibus et succedentibus membris};
Cassiodorus's is one human life crying by its works; Bellarmine's is
perseverance in prayer; Theodoret's is Symmachus's ``on each day'' and nothing
beyond it. \textbf{And what \latin{solíus} does.} For
Augustine it shuts out every righteousness the singer might call his own; for
Bellarmine, human counsel and his own strength; for Theodoret it names the just
sentence God passed in the speaker's favour --- and for Cassiodorus it excludes
nothing of the singer's at all, carrying the remembering forward to the Judgment
instead.

\textbf{The exposition standing over the Communion is an exposition of exactly
its three verses.} \work{Enarratio in Ps.~70} sermo~II \S\S1, 2 and 4 are
Ps.~70:16, 17 and 18, and \S3 of the same discourse reaches for Ps.~85:11, the
psalm of this Mass's own Introit. At v.~16 Augustine fastens on the single word
--- \latin{O solius! Quid addidit, solius? \dots\ Justitia tua sola me liberat}
--- and at v.~18 he marks the Greek pair standing under the Latin doublet
\latin{senéctam et sénium} and draws two senses from it: for one man, that
nothing of his is his own merit to the end; for the Church, \latin{vox est enim
Ecclesiae}, whose youth was the apostles' age. \textbf{So the grace-alone sense
of this antiphon is Augustine's, with Bellarmine beside him in another key, and
not the tradition's single voice} --- and two things hold it there. His most
quotable formula on the doctrine, \latin{sua dona coronabit, non merita tua},
falls at \S5 on v.~19, one verse past where the antiphon stops. And in
Theodoret's Greek the word for \emph{alone} is a genitive that may hang on
\emph{thy} as readily as on \emph{justice}, where the Latin fastens it to the
justice, so Augustine's whole reading of the clause rests on the Latin.

\textbf{Twice a single commentator makes two elements of this Mass name one
thing.} Cassiodorus glosses the Communion's Judgment with the sheep-and-goats
formula he had already used at Ps.~101:17 over the Gradual's \latin{videbitur in
maiestate sua}, so in his hands the Gradual's second half and the Communion's
first clause are one event; and Aquinas, at the Offertory's \latin{cor meum
dereliquit me}, reads the distraction of fervour by venial sins and cites
2~Kings (2~Samuel)~7:27, the verse Augustine reaches for at this Mass's Introit
for the same fugitive heart. \textbf{Two readings stand outside that consensus.} Bellarmine carries the Communion's petition on to
the finishing of the psalter --- ``until I shall have finished the book of
Psalms, through which I will shew forth thy arm to all posterity'' --- which is a
startling thing over a Communion antiphon, and the clause bearing it lies outside
the appointed text. And Guéranger, who is not of the exegetical tradition at all,
keeps the Offertory's enemies as enemies, hearing the antiphon as the Church's
prayer against the efforts of hell, and turns the Communion into her
thank-offering, a promise to keep the justice that is God's law.

\subsection*{5. Three prayers no Father expounds, and the one witness who reads them}

\textbf{No Father expounds the Collect}, and there is nothing unusual in that:
the orations of the Roman sacramentaries have no commentator who works through a
Sunday's own prayers, and the single witness who reads this one as this Mass's
own is a nineteenth-century liturgist writing to feed devotion rather than to
argue. Above the Latin and its English Guéranger puts what the two verbs divide:
``unless grace prevent, that is, anticipate, us, we cannot have so much as the
thought of doing what is holy; and again, unless it follow up the inspirations it
has given us \dots\ we shall never be able to pass from the simple thought to the
act of any virtue whatsoever.'' \textbf{That is testimony that the prayer is read
so, and not authority for the distinction itself}, which belongs to Augustine and
to Trent --- and two of the words in the exposition are his own rather than the
prayer's: \emph{prevent} stands here and not in his English of the Collect, which
gives \latin{prævéniat} as ``ever go before us,'' and ``persevering continuity of
this most precious aid'' is a phrase of Guéranger's that renders nothing in
\latin{iúgiter}.

Where the doctrine itself stands is outside any commentary on the prayer.
Augustine states it at \work{Enchiridion}~32, and again at \work{De gratia et
libero arbitrio} 17.33 with the same pair put as \latin{operatur} and
\latin{cooperatur}. \textbf{Its scriptural substratum is Ps.~58:11}, which stands
verbatim in the Clementine, and the clause that follows answers Ps.~22:6, which
the Clementine gives as \latin{misericordia tua subsequetur me} where Augustine
has \latin{eius}. Cassiodorus reaches the same half of the
doctrine at the Offertory's own psalm, making God's very looking our protection: \latin{aliter enim
liberari non possumus, nisi nos Divinitas propitiata respiciat}.

\textbf{Neither the Secret nor the Postcommunion has a patristic or saintly
reception.} The Latin corpora behind the reception of this Mass's other
elements hold neither \latin{sacrificii praesentis} nor \latin{mereamur esse
participes} nor \latin{Munda nos}. \textbf{The Veronense has none of the three
orations}, its one near miss --- \latin{tua nos semper gratia praeveniens
laetificatur} --- being a different prayer altogether.

\textbf{What the two prayers leave open, one witness closes, and only he.} The
Secret asks that the effect of the sacrifice now being offered cleanse us, and
the Postcommunion asks for renewal by the sacrament just received; how offered
sacrifice stands to received sacrament neither text says. Guéranger gives the
direction: the Sacrifice is ``the most direct and efficacious of all the
immediate preparations that we can make for the Communion of the Body and Blood,
which that Sacrifice produces on the altar,'' so that the two orations are two
moments of one movement. He reads \latin{præsens páriter et futúrum} temporally
as well, of the body ``both in this and in the next life.'' \textbf{One thing in
the Latin he does not take up}: whether the Secret's \latin{eius} belongs to the
sacrifice or to its effect.

In the Collect \latin{bonis opéribus} and in the Postcommunion \latin{rénova
\dots\ mentes nostras} have their vocabulary in common with Eph.~2:10 and
Eph.~4:23, verses of the appointed letter this Mass does not read; those are verbal echoes and no more, and
\textbf{the three orations of this Mass quote no Scripture at all.}
