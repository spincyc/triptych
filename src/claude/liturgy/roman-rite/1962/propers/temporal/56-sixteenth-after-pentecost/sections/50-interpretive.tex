\section*{The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities}

\begin{studybox}{Exploratory editorial and AI proposals}
Everything below is exploratory editorial and AI proposal. None of it is
sourced historical intent, attributed teaching, or documented reception, and
none of it should be quoted as any of those. Each proposal joins at least two
precisely named appointed elements and ends on the strongest thing that stands
against it. \textbf{No connection here is claimed to be unknown, unprecedented,
first, or authored by a model}; where a proposal and a checked witness conflict,
the witness wins. The precedent search behind each of these, its corpus and its
bounds, is recorded in \texttt{research/scope.md} and summarised in the terminal
appendix.
\end{studybox}

\begin{proposal}{P1. Two systems of measurement in one formulary}
\pfield{Anchors}{Epistle \cue{Ep.}, Eph.~3:18--19, \latin{quæ sit latitúdo, et
longitúdo, et sublímitas, et profúndum: scire étiam supereminéntem sciéntiæ
caritátem Christi}; Gospel \cue{Gosp.}, Luke~14:8--11, \latin{non discúmbas in
primo loco}, \latin{recúmbe in novíssimo loco}, \latin{Amíce, ascénde supérius},
\latin{qui se exáltat, humiliábitur}}
\pfield{Mechanism}{The reading asks the hearer to take four measures of a
charity it says in the next breath surpasses knowledge. The Gospel, in the same
Mass, ranks three seats and a comparative --- first place, lowest place, higher
--- and settles the ranking by an act that is not the guest's. \textbf{One
magnitude is offered for measurement and declared immeasurable; the other is
small, countable, and where the hearer is told to stand.}}
\pfield{Fruit}{A way of preaching the Epistle's dimensions that does not turn
them into a devotional flourish: the Mass answers an immeasurable magnitude with
a measurable one and makes the measurable one the place to be.}
\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses}{Read separately, the
Epistle's four measures are an ornament of Pauline prayer and the Gospel's
places are table manners. Read together they are two measuring systems in one
formulary, and the second is the one the hearer is given something to do about.}
\pfield{Controlling limit}{\textbf{The join is lexical at one end and narrative
at the other}, four spatial nouns against three seat positions, which is weaker
than a shared verb would be and is stated as such. \textbf{And the Epistle says
in terms that its magnitude surpasses knowledge}, so the Gospel's table must not
become a measure of it: the proposal fails at once if the seating is made a
scale for the charity rather than a different order of thing. And none of the six readings of the
measures standing over that verse --- Chrysostom's, Augustine's three,
Aquinas's and Guéranger's --- reaches the Gospel at all.}
\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{P2. The swollen body and the cleansed mind}
\pfield{Anchors}{Gospel \cue{Gosp.}, Luke~14:2 \latin{Et ecce homo quidam
hydrópicus erat ante illum} and 14:4 \latin{Ipse vero apprehénsum sanávit eum};
Secret \cue{Sec.}, \latin{Munda nos \dots\ sacrifícii præséntis efféctu};
Postcommunion \cue{Postcomm.}, \latin{Purífica \dots\ mentes nostras benígnus,
et rénova cæléstibus sacraméntis}}
\pfield{Mechanism}{The Mass has an outward healing in its Gospel and asks for an
inward cleansing and renewal in the two orations that follow the offering and
the communion. \textbf{The disease the Gospel heals is one of superfluity} ---
dropsy is a body holding what it cannot carry --- \textbf{and what the two
orations ask to have taken away is not named at all.} The formulary moves from a
body relieved of what it could not hold to a mind asked for the same relief.}
\pfield{Fruit}{The healing stops being the mere occasion of the sabbath argument
and becomes the Mass's own figure for what the two orations ask; and the
orations stop being generic, because the Gospel has already said what kind of
excess is meant.}
\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses}{The healing is ordinarily
read as the setting of the controversy and then dropped, and the orations are
ordinarily read as conventional. Neither reading has a reason to put them side
by side, and the movement from outward to inward is only visible when they are.}
\pfield{Controlling limit}{\textbf{No checked witness joins this healing to the
doctrine of grace, and the two who read the dropsy figuratively both join it to
something else}: Ambrose makes the dropsical man one in whom the flux of the
flesh overwhelmed the soul's offices, and Bede reads the disease of the body as
expressing the disease of the heart and then names avarice. \textbf{So a reading
that makes the healing a figure of prevenient grace is this section's and not
theirs}, and if a later sweep documents the join it belongs in the commentary
instead. The verbal side is thin: \latin{munda}, \latin{purífica} and
\latin{rénova} share no root with \latin{hydrópicus} or \latin{sanávit}, and the
join is one of sense throughout.}
\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{P3. Three acts of looking, and only one of them is asked for}
\pfield{Anchors}{Gospel \cue{Gosp.}, Luke~14:1 \latin{et ipsi observábant eum}
and 14:7 \latin{inténdens quómodo primos accúbitus elígerent}; Offertory
\cue{Off.}, \latin{Dómine, in auxílium meum réspice \dots\ Dómine, in auxílium
meum réspice}}
\pfield{Mechanism}{Two human acts of looking stand in the Gospel --- the
Pharisees watching Christ, and Christ marking how the guests chose their places
--- and one divine act of looking is petitioned at the Offertory, and petitioned
twice, the antiphon returning to its opening clause after the intervening plea.
\textbf{The asymmetry is the point: the human looking is appraisal, of a guest by
a host or of a guest by a guest, and the divine looking is asked for.}}
\pfield{Fruit}{It gives the Offertory a place in the day's argument that its own
words do not supply, and it recovers \latin{observábant} from being scene-setting:
the Mass has already shown what it is to be watched before it asks to be looked
at.}
\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses}{\latin{Observábant} is
almost always read as narrative furniture, and the Offertory as a psalm of
distress with no relation to the Gospel. Set side by side across two ritual
moments they are one contrast between being under appraisal and asking to be
regarded.}
\pfield{Controlling limit}{\textbf{Whether the printed repetition carries any
weight cannot be answered here.} No chant book was opened: no
\work{Graduale}, no \work{Antiphonale}, no Hesbert, no manuscript of the six
antiphonaries whose AMS~188 results above are a modern database's index of
Hesbert's numbering. So whether an antiphon that returns to its first clause is
peculiar to this piece or an ordinary convention of the genre is unknown, and if
it is ordinary the doubling carries none of the emphasis this proposal puts on
it. \textbf{And the
looking verbs are three different words} --- \latin{observábant},
\latin{inténdens}, \latin{réspice} --- so the series is a series of acts and not
of a lexeme. Aquinas's \latin{Respectus Dei est auxilium nostrum} at this
Offertory's own psalm supports the divine half and says nothing about the
Gospel.}
\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{P4. What is built and what is conferred}
\pfield{Anchors}{Gradual \cue{Grad.}, \latin{Timébunt gentes nomen tuum,
Dómine, et omnes reges terræ glóriam tuam. ℣.~Quóniam ædificávit Dóminus Sion};
Epistle \cue{Ep.}, \latin{in caritáte radicáti, et fundáti}, \latin{quæ est
glória vestra}, \latin{secúndum divítias glóriæ suæ}, \latin{ipsi glória in
Ecclésia}; Gospel \cue{Gosp.}, \latin{Tunc erit tibi glória coram simul
discumbéntibus}}
\pfield{Mechanism}{One building vocabulary runs from a city God has built to
hearers rooted and founded in charity. And \latin{glória} is appointed in three
elements with three different owners: God's own, which the kings of the earth
will fear; the Ephesians', which is the Apostle's chains; and \textbf{a guest's,
which is the only one in the formulary that a host confers}.}
\pfield{Fruit}{It stops the word reading as one word said three times. The Mass
sets a glory that is seen, a glory that is given and a glory that is assigned at
a table, and the last of the three is the only one anybody in the Gospel could
have gone looking for.}
\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses}{Taken singly each
\latin{glória} looks like a different word that happens to be the same word.
Taken across the three, the formulary has arranged them, and the building
vocabulary supplies the ground the arrangement stands on.}
\pfield{Controlling limit}{\textbf{The count is a count over the appointed text
and not over the psalter.} The Gradual replaces the psalm's second
\latin{gloria} with \latin{maiestáte}, so the chant says \latin{glóriam tuam}
once where the psalm says it twice, and a reader checking the Bible will not
find the distribution this proposal is built on. Augustine's citation of
Eph.~2:20 at the Gradual's own v.~16 is a striking convergence and is not
evidence: it is a verse of Ephesians this Mass does not appoint, and nothing
shows that anyone joined the two before.}
\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{P5. What both psalm chants stop just short of saying}
\pfield{Anchors}{Alleluia \cue{All.}, \latin{Cantáte Dómino cánticum novum:
quia mirabília fecit Dóminus}, stopping before \latin{Salvavit sibi dextera
ejus, et brachium sanctum ejus}; Communion \cue{Comm.}, \latin{\dots\ Deus, ne
derelínquas me}, stopping before \latin{donec annuntiem brachium tuum
generationi omni quae ventura est}; Epistle \cue{Ep.}, whose doxology ends
\latin{in omnes generatiónes sǽculi sæculórum}}
\pfield{Mechanism}{Both psalm chants cut immediately before the Lord's
\emph{arm}, and the Communion cuts before its announcement to a \emph{coming
generation}. The Epistle's doxology, which the Mass does read, ends on
generations. The Communion also stops one clause short of \latin{pronuntiabo
mirabilia tua} --- the very word the Alleluia sings.}
\pfield{Fruit}{The formulary sings the wonders and does not sing the arm, and
the one place it says ``generations'' is a reading and not a chant. That
distributes across the Mass a thing usually noticed only at one element, and it
gives the Epistle's doxology a function beyond closing the lesson.}
\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses}{Each cut looks arbitrary on
its own, and an element-by-element reading has no occasion to compare two
antiphons' stopping points. Together they show the same word twice left just
outside the sung text.}
\pfield{Controlling limit}{\textbf{The strongest limit is that these extents are
inherited chant tradition and nothing establishes that anything was cut in order
to leave it out.} Duller explanations stand available and untested --- the
metrical length of an antiphon, the ordinary bounds of the genre --- and whether
these extents are stable across the tradition is beyond the AMS~188 evidence
above, which is a modern database's index of Hesbert's numbering and not a chant
book, a printing of Hesbert or a manuscript of the six antiphonaries.
\textbf{The material past each cut belongs to the source psalms and never to the
appointed chants}, and a reader must not be left with the impression that the
Mass suppressed it. \textbf{And the material past the
Alleluia's cut is not one text}: the Clementine reads \latin{Salvavit sibi
dextera ejus}, Augustine's lemma \latin{Sanavit ei} and Cassiodorus's
\latin{salvavit eum}, so a proposal that quotes the continuation is quoting one
psalter and not the tradition.}
\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{P6. Poverty and plenitude, distributed by element}
\pfield{Anchors}{Introit \cue{Int.}, the psalm verse \latin{quóniam inops et
pauper sum ego} and the antiphon's \latin{copiósus in misericórdia}; Epistle
\cue{Ep.}, \latin{secúndum divítias glóriæ suæ}, \latin{ut impleámini in omnem
plenitúdinem Dei}, \latin{qui potens est ómnia fácere superabundánter quam
pétimus, aut intellégimus}; Gospel \cue{Gosp.}, \latin{recúmbe in novíssimo
loco}}
\pfield{Mechanism}{\textbf{The Mass's one confession of poverty is sung, and
abundance is predicated twice and of two different subjects.} \latin{Inops et
pauper} stands in the Introit's psalm verse; the antiphon over it sings God's
mercy as \latin{copiósus in misericórdia}, and the Epistle reads what God gives
and does as riches, fullness and superabundance; and the Gospel's remedy for the
poor man's problem is a place rather than a possession. \textbf{What is
distributed is God's abundance in both registers against the singer's poverty in
one.}}
\pfield{Fruit}{The Introit's formula stops being a conventional opening. Set
against the Epistle it is the Mass's own statement of the condition the Epistle
answers, and the Gospel then declines to answer it with anything the guest can
hold.}
\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses}{\latin{Inops et pauper}
reads as a psalm's habitual self-description until the Epistle piles up riches
in the same Mass; and the distribution of the two magnitudes across chant and
reading is invisible at any single element. \latin{Superabundánter} occurs
nowhere else in the whole Clementine.}
\pfield{Controlling limit}{\textbf{The strongest form of this rests on the
Introit's psalm verse rather than on its antiphon.} The verse is appointed text:
it is printed inside marginal no.~1592 under the formulary's own rubric
\latin{Ps. ibid., 1}, which is what settles the boundary question here --- but a
psalm verse is not sung in the same way an antiphon is, and a reader who counts
only antiphons will not find the poverty. \textbf{And this formulary's poverty
language is mostly in the context of the appointed texts and not in them}: only
Ps.~85:1 is actually sung, \latin{Oratio pauperis} is Ps.~101's unappointed
title, \latin{mendicus sum et pauper} is Ps.~39's unappointed v.~18, and the
Gospel's \latin{pauperes, debiles, claudos, et cæcos} falls two verses past the
end of the pericope. A theme built from that context is built from around the
appointed text, and the difference has to stay visible. \textbf{And the poverty
word the proposal turns on is not the word a second Latin witness lemmatizes}:
Cassiodorus reads \latin{quoniam egenus et pauper sum ego} at Ps.~85:1, so a
commentator quoted in support may be expounding \latin{egenus} where the chant
sings \latin{inops}.}
\end{proposal}
