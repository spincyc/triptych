\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\label{triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

This Mass says one thing three times over in three registers that share almost
no vocabulary --- a doctrine of grace in the three orations, a christological
pattern in the chants, and a parable of place in the Gospel. Augustine reads the
Communion's psalm in the Collect's terms, Bellarmine beside him and Cassiodorus
restating him; Theodoret does not. \textbf{The Epistle is not an interlude
between the other two registers but their centre}, and where the tradition most
visibly develops and divides. Four commentators stand at each of the Mass's five
psalms and differ at every one.

\subsection*{1. Grace before and after, said in three prayers and sung in the first person}

The Collect asks one grace to do two things --- \latin{et prævéniat et
sequátur} --- then names what they are for: \latin{ac bonis opéribus iúgiter
præstet esse inténtos}. The Secret puts \latin{mereámur} inside a purpose clause
under \latin{pérfice miserátus in nobis}, so what is merited is the completing.
The Postcommunion asks help \latin{præsens páriter et futúrum}. \textbf{Three
spans of three kinds stand across the four}: before and after, present and
future, and --- in the Communion --- youth to old age.

Augustine states the Collect's distinction outside any commentary on it, at
\work{Enchiridion} 32: \latin{nolentem praevenit, ut velit, volentem
subsequitur, ne frustra velit}. \textbf{The load-bearing witness is his
exposition of the Communion's own three verses.} \work{Enarratio in Ps.~70}
sermo~II §§1, 2 and 4 are exactly Ps.~70:16, 17 and 18, the extent the antiphon
sings: \latin{Justitia tua sola me liberat}; \latin{docuisti me nihil in me
praecessisse}; and at the last verse \latin{gratia tua semper perseveret mecum}.
\textbf{His \latin{nihil in me praecessisse} is the Collect's \latin{prævéniat}
said as a confession, and his \latin{semper} stands where the Collect's does.}
Sermo~II §3 cites Ps.~85:11, the psalm of this Mass's own Introit, for the same
point. Cassiodorus reaches it at the Communion's psalm, opening his in~Ps.~LXX
on a charity \latin{quae nullis meritis praecedentibus gratis semper
impenditur}, which is what Augustine's \latin{docuisti me nihil in me
praecessisse} says there. \textbf{But he names \latin{doctor Augustinus} inside these five
psalms} (in~Ps.~LXXXV \S18), so there the two Latins are one judgment restated
and not two.

\textbf{At the antiphon's decisive word the four part.} Augustine takes
\latin{iustítiæ tuæ solíus} to exclude any righteousness of the singer's own,
Bellarmine human counsel and his own strength, Cassiodorus to refer the
remembering forward to the Judgment, Theodoret to name God's just sentence in
the speaker's favour. \textbf{The grace-alone sense here is Augustine's, with
Bellarmine beside him, and not the tradition's one voice.} Guéranger, a fifth
voice from outside the exegetical tradition, makes the antiphon the Church's
promise to keep God's justice, which is his law.

Trent, Session~VI, cap.~XVI puts the doctrine in three verbs where the Collect
has two: \latin{virtus \dots\ bona eorum opera semper antecedit et comitatur et
subsequitur}, and shares \latin{iugiter} and \latin{semper} with it at the
doctrinal point. \textbf{The middle verb is Trent's and not the Collect's}, and
cap.~X quotes not this Collect but the Thirteenth Sunday's, altered. Guéranger,
above this Collect, states it in the prayer's own two verbs: without the grace
that anticipates, not the thought; without the grace that follows, not the act.
\textbf{The Postcommunion carries the doctrine into the chants}: its
\latin{auxílium} is the Offertory's own word and stands nowhere else in this
Mass, and its \latin{rénova} asks as a petition the newness the Alleluia sings
in \latin{cánticum novum}.

\subsection*{2. The humbling and the exalting are God's acts before they are a rule of conduct}

\latin{Omnis, qui se exáltat, humiliábitur: et qui se humíliat, exaltábitur}
closes the Gospel and names no agent for either verb. \textbf{Bede supplies one,
arguing from the sentence itself}: it is plainly false that everyone who exalts
himself before men is humbled by men, so the parable must be read
\latin{typice}, and the humbling and the exalting are the Lord's (PL~92, 513A).
He reads the first place at table as a glorying in one's own merits (512A), the
matter the Collect and the Communion state doctrinally; and Guéranger joins the
same sentence to grace through Ecclus.~3:20--21.

The chants carry the pattern without stating it. At the Introit's own psalm
verse Augustine makes the motion a rule about God: he draws near the humbled and
goes far from the exalted, unless he has himself humbled and then raised him;
his boasting Pharisee there is Luke~18's, not Luke~14:1's chief Pharisee.
Cassiodorus says the exchange at the Gradual's own psalm, and Theodoret reaches
Philippians~2 there at the appointed v.~16. The Epistle's \latin{flecto génua
mea ad Patrem} is that motion inside the reading, and Phil.~2:8--11 holds both
of this Mass's motions in one sentence.

At the Gradual Augustine reads the descent and the return together --- Sion is
being built now, \latin{Hoc agitur nunc}, and he who was seen at the first
coming \latin{in infirmitate sua} will be seen at the Judgment. \textbf{His
lemma says \latin{in gloria sua} where the chant sings \latin{in maiestáte sua},
and his verb is a future where the chant prints a perfect}, so no sentence built
on \latin{maiestáte} is his. Neither difference is the missal's: the future is
the Greek tradition's, which Theodoret expounds, and the chant's noun is
Cassiodorus's. \textbf{Theodoret does not hold the two appearings at all},
taking \latin{vidébitur} of Jerusalem rebuilt. \textbf{Benedict makes that
Gospel sentence the head of \work{Regula}~7}, naming no chapter; it stands at
Luke~18:14 and Matt.~23:12 too.

\subsection*{3. The Epistle is the formulary's centre, and the tradition divides inside it}

The lesson opens by resuming a sentence the Mass does not read. Eph.~3:1 begins
\latin{Huius rei gratia} and breaks off; vv.~2--13 are the digression; v.~14
resumes with those same words --- so \textbf{the resumption is heard in church
and the thing resumed is not}, and the liturgical incipit removes even the
connective, substituting \latin{Fratres: Obsecro vos} for \latin{Propter quod
peto}. It closes at the end of the letter's doctrinal half, the exhortation
opening at 4:1.

\textbf{At vv.~18--19 the witnesses read the four measures at least three ways.}
Chrysostom, in Homily~7 on Ephesians, takes them for the immensity of God's
love, sketched by the visible dimensions of a solid body --- \textbf{he does not
read them as the Cross}. Augustine reads them as the four members of the Cross
in three works, assigning the virtues differently in each:
\work{Epistula}~55.14.25, \work{In Iohannis euangelium tractatus}~118.5, and
\work{Sermo}~165.3.3--5.5. \textbf{So the cross-reading is a development inside
one author and not one fixed patristic position.} Aquinas, at \work{Super
Ephesios} cap.~3 lect.~5, sets out three readings without choosing and names the
hidden depth \textbf{predestination} at the Cross. And Guéranger takes them of
neither, but of the indwelling: Christ dwelling in man and filling him with
God's plenitude.

The last of Augustine's three, \work{Sermo}~165, ties the reading to the Collect
too. Headed \latin{deque gratia et libera voluntate, contra Pelagianos}, it
divides at \S1.1 the hinge where the Collect divides grace: what the Apostle
asks of his hearers belongs to their will, what he asks God to give to God's
help --- \latin{auxilium}, the word the Offertory and the Postcommunion both use
--- and at \S7.9 it closes on no merit preceding grace. \textbf{That sermon
stops three verses short of the doxology}, and its remark that the day's
Apostle, psalm and Gospel agreed is about Augustine's own African order, whose
psalm was Ps.~56:2. The Alleluia supplies the other tie: there Augustine moves
from the new song to the new man to an inward healing, and Cassiodorus bids
Christians sing it \latin{novae regenerationis sacramenta sumentes}, the
sacramental term the Postcommunion uses --- the renewal the Epistle asks for
within and the Postcommunion asks for in \latin{rénova}.

\subsection*{4. Four chants of five depart from the psalter, and the fifth departs at no word}

The Introit departs from the Gallican psalter at five places, the Gradual at
three, the Alleluia at one and a stop, the Offertory at two and a printed
refrain --- \textbf{and the Communion at none}, the antiphon made entirely by
where it starts and stops. The Offertory's \latin{in auxílium meum réspice} is
verbatim Ps.~70:12, a verse of this Mass's own Communion psalm.

\textbf{The consequence for the Fathers is not one rule but two.} At four of the
five chants Cassiodorus's lemma stands with the missal against the psalter ---
\latin{videbitur in maiestate sua} among them --- and at the Alleluia
Theodoret's Greek names the Lord as the chant does. \textbf{Augustine's lemma differs at
three places where the difference carries his argument}: \latin{in gloria sua}
at the Gradual, \latin{Sanavit ei dextera eius} at the Alleluia's unsung
half-verse, and \latin{in adiuvandum mihi respice} at the Offertory, where
Jerome, ruling against a reported \latin{festina}, has a fourth form again.

One departure runs the other way and ties the chants to the Gospel.
\latin{Mitis} as a whole word stands in exactly two verses of the Clementine
Vulgate, Ps.~85:5 and Matt.~11:29, where Christ pairs it with \latin{humilis
corde} and \latin{iugum meum suave est} follows at once in v.~30:
\textbf{across those two verses the antiphon's \latin{suávis} and \latin{mitis}
are joined}, and the humility the Gospel ends on is in the Mass's first sung
line.

\textbf{No New Testament book quotes any verse this Mass appoints}, Hebrews
reaching Ps.~101 and Ps.~39 only at verses the chants do not sing --- \textbf{so
every christological reading of these chants rests on the psalms' wider context,
on typology or on the commentators above, and on no apostolic citation of the
words sung.}

One grammatical fact runs under all of it. \textbf{All three antiphons speak in
the first person singular and address God directly, all three orations in the
first person plural, and the Gradual and the Alleluia carry no first-person form
at all.} One voice confesses and the other asks, and the Gospel stands between
them telling a guest what to do with a seat.
