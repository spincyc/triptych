\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

Each appointed element is named by its Latin incipit, its printed heading and
reference, and the missal's marginal number. Its complete text, Latin and
English together, has just been printed in \textit{The Appointed Formulary in
Full}; what stands here is the evidence. Latin is quoted again only where an
argument turns on the wording, and Scripture is read in its whole literary
context before any witness is allowed to speak about it.

\textbf{Where the English comes from.} Scriptural elements take the
Douay--Rheims as revised by Bishop Challoner (1749--1752), the English of the
Clementine Vulgate the missal prints, in the Project Gutenberg e-text of that
revision. The three orations take the anonymous English of \work{The Roman
Missal translated into the English language for the use of the laity}, first
revised edition (Philadelphia: Eugene Cummiskey, 1861), from the three lines
that book supplies for this formulary. Both are public domain in the United
States; neither is an official liturgical translation. This guide has
composed, translated, adapted and paraphrased nothing, and where a checked
witness has no public-domain English at the locus used, he is summarised and
not quoted.

\subsection*{The text: what two 1962 books actually print}

The Latin was collated at 300 and 600 dpi against the CMAA facsimile of the
Vatican \latin{editio typica} (printed pp.~393--394, marginal nos.\

\latin{editio iuxta typicam} of the same year (printed pp.~388--389). These
are two editions of the 1962 revision, not two photographs of one page, and
they differ. The typical edition governs everything printed in this guide;
nothing is blended.

Two of these are worth more than a footnote. Benziger's \latin{misirére} at
Luke 17:13 was read twice at magnification and the first vowel is an \textit{i};
it is an apparent misprint in that edition, and the typical edition's
\latin{miserére} is the Vulgate's own word. And the Secret's conclusion runs
the other way from the pattern a reader of these guides might expect: here it
is Benziger that prints the long form and the typical edition that breaks off
at \latin{Per Dóminum nostrum}. Neither is a different prayer. Both show that
``the 1962 Latin'' is not one artefact, and that a guide which does not name
its witness has not said what it checked. Three words of the Gospel's left
column are touched by ink loss in the CMAA scan --- the \textit{m} of
\latin{magna}, the first \textit{c} of \latin{cécidit}, the \textit{ti} of
\latin{grátias} --- and all three were confirmed letter for letter against the
Benziger images. The damage is in the scan, not in the text.

\subsubsection*{Twelve places where the appointed text is not the Bible's text}

The more consequential collation is against the Clementine Vulgate, the Bible
whose English this guide quotes. Twelve divergences were verified directly and
are textual observations, not inferences. Five of them fall in the two chants
drawn from Psalm 73, which is why this Sunday's Introit and Gradual cannot be
read out of an English Bible without misquoting the missal.

\begin{divergencetable}

\end{divergencetable}

Three negative results belong with the table. The Epistle matches the
Clementine word for word; the Gospel matches it word for word after the
liturgical incipit; and the Introit's psalm verse, Psalm 73:1, matches it
exactly, question mark included. The Gradual's \latin{memor esto oppróbrii
servórum tuórum} is the one place where this guide reports a fact it cannot
explain: the clause is not the Clementine's Psalm 73:22b, and the wording that
answers it stands at Psalm 88:51, but the missal's printed reference says
Psalm 73 and this guide does not correct the book it studies. No
\latin{Psalterium Romanum} witness was collated here, so no derivation from
any particular older psalter is asserted for any of these readings. What can
be said is narrower and firmer: five of them --- \latin{derelinquas},
\latin{quaerentium te}, \latin{opprobrii servorum tuorum}, \latin{et
progenie}, \latin{tempora mea} --- are already in print in the Pustet Missal
of Ratisbon of 1862, which this repository tracks as public-domain text.

\elementguard
\subsection*{Introit \cue{Int.}}

\elementid{Réspice, Dómine, in testaméntum tuum}{\latin{Antiphona ad Introitum} --- Ps. 73, 20, 19 et 23, with \latin{Ps. ibid.,} 1}{1562}

\textbf{The whole psalm, first.} Psalm 73 is a communal lament of unusual
violence. Its inscription is \latin{Intellectus Asaph}; its opening line is the
question the Mass will spend the rest of the morning answering --- \latin{Ut
quid, Deus, repulisti in finem?}, ``why hast thou cast us off unto the end?'';
and its body is a description of a sanctuary already sacked. Enemies have
roared in the midst of God's solemnity, set up their ensigns for signs, cut
down the carved work with axe and hatchet, burned the sanctuary to the ground
and defiled the dwelling place of God's name (vv.~4--7). There is no prophet
any more, and no one who knows how long (v.~9). Only then, from v.~12, does the
psalm turn to recital: God is king before the ages, he divided the sea, he
broke the heads of the dragons, he made the springs and the rivers, the day and
the night, summer and spring. The petition the Introit sings comes after that
recital and rests on it. \latin{Respice in testamentum tuum} --- look upon thy
covenant --- is a plea addressed to a God whose past acts are being cited
against his present silence.

\textbf{What the antiphon does to the psalm.} It is a centonisation, and the
missal's own printed reference does not fully describe it. Verses 20, 19 and 23
are named; the clause \latin{exsúrge, Dómine, et iúdica causam tuam} is v.~22a
and is not named at all. The antiphon adds the vocative \latin{Dómine} to
v.~20, which has no addressee in the psalter. It drops the first half of v.~19,
where the soul that confesses is not to be delivered up to beasts, and keeps
only the second half. It sings \latin{ne derelínquas}, forsake not, where the
psalter has \latin{ne obliviscaris}, forget not --- and the Gradual, singing
the same verse a few minutes later, will sing the psalter's verb. And at the
close it turns the psalm's \latin{voces inimicorum tuorum}, the voices of thy
enemies, into \latin{voces quæréntium te}, the voices of them that seek thee.
That last change is the largest. In the psalter God is asked to remember what
his enemies are shouting, so that he will act against them; in the chant he is
asked not to forget what his seekers are saying, so that he will act for them.
The prayer has changed its grammatical direction while keeping its words.

\textbf{And what it leaves standing.} The psalm verse is the Clementine's
v.~1 exactly, question mark included. The Church does not soften the
accusation before singing it. The Mass of this Sunday opens with a
congregation asking God why he has cast them off and why his anger smokes
against the sheep of his own pasture, and the answer, when it comes, will come
from Paul rather than from the psalm.

\subsubsection*{Whose psalm the Fathers thought this was, and which army sacked
the temple}

The tension the page-2 dossier reports --- an Asaph title on a psalm that
mourns a sanctuary already destroyed --- is not a modern discovery. Every
witness checked for this guide had to decide what the psalm is about, and they
did not agree.

\begin{receptiontable}{Witness}{Which destruction}{On what grounds}
Augustine, \work{Enarr. in Ps.} 73 & Rome, as past history & At v.~5 he identifies the ensigns: ``Habebant signa quae ibi ponerent, vexilla sua, aquilas suas, dracones suos, \latin{signa Romana}; aut etiam statuas suas, quas primo in templo posuerunt,'' and ties the sack to Jn. 11:48 --- ``venient Romani, et tollent nobis, et locum, et gentem \dots\ et ubi occiderunt Christum, ibi occisi sunt,'' the city falling at Passover\\
Cassiodorus, \work{Expositio Psalmorum} & Rome, as prophecy & The psalm ``futuras clades civitatis Ierusalem miseranda lamentatione deplorat,'' and the wonder is that it should ``sic dolere ventura, quasi iam probentur esse suscepta''; \latin{in finem} means the captivity ``quam Ierusalem temporibus Vespasiani atque Titi principum a Romanis legitur pertulisse''\\
Theodoret, \work{Interpretatio in Psalmos} & Rome, as prophecy, argued by elimination & Some, he says, fitted the psalm to what Antiochus Epiphanes did; they attended neither to the history nor to the psalm's prophecy. Nor does it fit the Babylonian siege --- for the psalm says there is no longer a prophet, and then they had many: Jeremiah, Uriah son of Shemaiah, Ezekiel, Daniel. If it fits neither the Babylonians nor the Macedonians, it foretells the ruin brought by the Romans. At v.~4 he names Titus and the Passover siege\\
Jerome, \work{Commentarioli in Psalmos} & \textbf{Babylon} & His whole note on this psalm is on the title and v.~5: ``Sic Babylonii, in adrogantiam victoriae tumore sublati, in templo tuo sua signa posuerunt,'' with Symmachus's rendering cited. He has \textbf{nothing} on vv.~1, 19, 20, 22 or 23\\
\work{Glossa ordinaria} on the Psalter & Rome, as prophecy & Keeps the patristic line and compresses it: \latin{in finem} means ``interitum et captivitatem \dots\ que a Tito et Vespasiano''; the ensigns of v.~5 are \latin{vexilla romana} and, under Augustine's name, \latin{aquilas}; v.~4 is glossed ``tunc eversa est Hierusalem quando sollemnitas agebatur paschalis in qua crucifixerunt Dominum''; and the psalm is spoken \latin{nondum eversa}\\
Bellarmine, \work{Explanatio in Psalmos} & \textbf{Antiochus IV and the Maccabees} & He lists the candidates --- Nebuchadnezzar, Antiochus, Titus and Vespasian, Diocletian, Antichrist --- and chooses Antiochus, naming Augustine, Theodoret and Euthymius as holding the other view. His decisive reason is \emph{liturgical}: the words of this psalm do not suit unbelieving Jews, ``who were not the sheep of God's pasture \dots\ neque Ecclesia frequentaret hunc Psalmum, si contineret voces infidelium Iudaeorum''\\
\end{receptiontable}

Four of the six read the psalm of the Roman destruction, and three of those
four read it as prophecy. That is worth stating carefully, because it is the
one place in this formulary where a chant's own reception carries a heavy
supersessionist charge: Theodoret adds that after that ruin the people obtained
no restoration, ``paying the penalty of impiety against the Saviour,'' and
Augustine's exposition has sentences in the same key. Both are attributed to
them by name here. What is equally true, and is part of the same record, is
where each ends. Augustine's \work{Enarratio} closes on Romans 9:27,
\latin{reliquiae salvae fiant} --- let the remnant be saved.

And the dissent at the far end of the series is the most interesting thing in
it, because of the ground on which it is made. Bellarmine rejects the reading
of Augustine and Theodoret partly \emph{because the Roman Rite sings this
psalm}: a church that put these words in her own mouth cannot have understood
them as the voice of unbelievers, since the psalm calls its speakers the sheep
of God's pasture and the souls that confess to him. A Counter-Reformation
cardinal is arguing from the liturgy against a patristic consensus, about the
psalm of this Sunday's own Introit. He is honest about the cost: at v.~7 he
concedes that the burning of the sanctuary ``seems to be the only passage
forbidding the application of this Psalm to the persecution of Antiochus,''
since the temple was burnt by Nebuchadnezzar and by Titus and not by Antiochus,
``still the gates of the temple were burned by him.''

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Augustine, \work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 73 --- and the Epistle
quoted at the Introit}
Augustine's first move is to decide whose voice sings. \latin{Asaph}, he says,
``latine Congregatio, graece Synagoga dicitur'': this is the voice of the
Synagogue \textit{come to understanding} --- \latin{intellectus Asaph} --- the
believing remnant rather than the Church simply. And the verse he grounds that
on is this Sunday's own Epistle, four verses past where the lesson stops:
``quia et nos semen Abrahae sumus; Gentibus quippe loquebatur Apostolus, cum
diceret: \latin{Ergo semen Abrahae estis, secundum promissionem haeredes}''
(Gal. 3:29). The exposition of the Introit's psalm opens by quoting the
sentence that closes the Epistle's chapter. Neither text was chosen for the
other, so far as anything here shows; but the man who wrote most about both
already read them together.

On v.~1 his lemma differs twice from the chant --- \latin{animus} for
\latin{furor}, \latin{oves gregis tui} for \latin{oves pascuae tuae} --- and
his gloss turns the accusation into a question with an answer: ``Non
reprehendit, sed quaerit \dots\ Quid est, in finem? Forte usque in finem
saeculi. An repulisti nos \textit{in Christum}, qui finis est omni
credenti?'' (Rom 10:4). On v.~19 the beasts are the devil and his angels, the
confession is Pentecost's, and the poverty is the poverty of grace against
merit: ``Divites eramus, fortes eramus \dots\ iam modo non constituimus
iustitiam nostram, sed agnoscimus gratiam tuam; pauperes sumus, exaudi mendicos
tuos.''
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{\latin{Testamentum} is the word the whole formulary turns on, and the
two Latin Fathers who expound this psalm read it the same way.} It is the noun the Introit pleads,
the noun the Gradual pleads again, and the noun the Epistle will take up and
argue about. Jerome, commenting on the Epistle, supplies the philology: where
the Latin Bible writes \latin{testamentum}, anyone who compares the Hebrew
volumes ``inveniet ubi testamentum scriptum est, non testamentum sonare, sed
pactum, quod Hebraeo sermone dicitur BERITH'' --- the word does not sound
\textit{will} but \textit{pact}. And at the psalm itself, Augustine and
Cassiodorus independently make v.~20 the hinge of the whole psalm and identify
the covenant as the \emph{new} one, both citing Jeremiah 31:31.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Augustine at Ps. 73:20, and Cassiodorus after him}
Augustine: ``Respice in testamentum tuum. Redde quod promisisti: tabulas
tenemus, haereditatem exspectamus. Respice in testamentum tuum; \textit{non
illud vetus}: non propter terram Chanaan rogo; non propter inimicorum
temporalem subiectionem \dots\ Respice in testamentum tuum, quo promisisti
regnum coelorum.'' The public-domain English of the Oxford translators renders
it: ``Fulfil that which You have promised: the tables we have, for the
inheritance we are looking. Have regard unto Your Testament, not that old one
\dots\ wherein You have promised the kingdom of Heaven.'' He then quotes
Jeremiah's new covenant in full, and closes the verse on Romans 9:27.

Cassiodorus repeats the structure and adds the sentence that best explains why
this psalm opens this Mass: ``Respice, dixit, ut digneris implere quae
cognosceris ante promisisse,'' citing the same Jeremiah verse, and then ---
``quia non erant bona, quae delinquentis populi commemorare potuisset, meminit
promissionis Dominicae quae semper impletur.'' Having no merits to plead, the
psalmist pleads the promise. That is exactly what the day is about to ask the
Collect for and exactly what the Epistle is about to argue cannot be revoked.
\end{witnessnote}

The \work{Glossa ordinaria} hardens that reading into the schoolroom gloss. Its
interlinear note on \latin{Respice in testamentum tuum} reads ``non vetus de
terrenis sed tuum quod promisisti regnum celorum,'' and its marginal note,
under Cassiodorus's name, compresses his argument to eight words that are worth
the whole verse: ``Imple que promisisti ut testamentum novum quod est de
eternis constituas. \textbf{Meminit promissionis quia merita deficiunt}'' ---
he remembers the promise because the merits fail. A medieval reader met the
covenant plea already answered.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{And the greatest of the later commentators reverses it}
Bellarmine reads the same clause and gives the opposite answer. God is asked to
regard ``pactum, siue testamentum quod fecisti cum Patribus nostris, vt esses
nobis in Deum, nosque protegeres, atque defenderes \emph{in terra, quam nobis
dedisti}'' --- and the public-domain English of O'Sullivan renders it: ``If you
will not regard us, regard, at least, thy covenant or testament which you made
with our fathers, that you would be a God to us, and that you would protect and
defend us in the land you gave us.'' He makes no connection at this verse to
Christ, to the New Covenant, or to the kingdom of heaven, and he does not name
Abraham here. The covenant pleaded is the old one, and what it promised is
territorial.

That is a flat contradiction of Augustine, Cassiodorus and the Glossa at the
same clause, and this guide prints it as one. It is also of a piece with his
reading of the psalm's occasion: a Maccabean lament pleads a Sinai covenant
about a land, and a psalm about AD 70 pleads the covenant that replaced it.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{And the witness who says nothing --- which is also a finding}
Theodoret does not gloss the word at all. His printed lemma at v.~20 is not
\latin{epiblepson eis ten diatheken sou} but \latin{epiblepson eis ten
diatheken ton doulon sou} --- ``look upon the covenant \emph{of thy servants}''
--- and Migne's own \latin{Variae Lectiones} flag the intruded words as absent
from both his codices, from the Hebrew and from the Septuagint. His entire note
expounds the verse's second half, about those who loved the darkness of
ignorance. He says nothing whatever about what the covenant is: no Abraham, no
Sinai, no new covenant. Migne's facing Latin does not even render the word with
the Introit's noun; it prints \latin{foedus}. The sharpest single contrast in
this dossier is therefore that the two Latin Fathers make v.~20 the hinge of
the psalm and the Antiochene passes it without comment --- with Bellarmine, a
thousand years later, making it the hinge again and turning it the other way.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{One divergence turns out not to be a divergence of the chant's making.}
The Introit closes \latin{et ne obliviscáris voces quæréntium te}, the voices
of them that seek thee, where the Clementine has \latin{voces inimicorum
tuorum}, the voices of thy enemies. That looks like the boldest liberty in the
formulary --- the prayer changing direction while keeping its words --- and the
psalm commentaries show that it is not a liberty at all. Augustine's lemma
reads \latin{Ne obliviscaris vocem deprecantium te}, the voices of them that
beseech thee, and he glosses them as those ``ingemiscentium, et exspectantium
iam quod promisisti de Novo Testamento.'' Cassiodorus's lemma is the chant's
exactly --- \latin{Ne obliviscaris voces quaerentium te} --- glossed ``ut
inveniant quem quaerunt, et mereantur cernere quem expetunt.'' And Theodoret's
Greek lemma is ``the voice of thy suppliants,'' with Migne's apparatus setting
out the whole textual situation in one line: the Hebrew has \textit{thy
enemies}, Aquila and Symmachus follow it, and the Septuagint has the
suppliants. The Clementine's reading is the Hebrew; the chant's is the
Septuagint, and it is what the Old Latin, Augustine, Cassiodorus and Theodoret
all had in front of them. The electronic edition of the \work{Glossa
ordinaria} on the Psalter states the same thing in the terms a chant historian
would use: it records \latin{inimicorum} as the Gallican reading and
\latin{querentium} as the Roman, citing Jerome's letter to Sunnia and Fretela.
This guide reports that as the Glossa's apparatus and not as its own finding,
because no \latin{Psalterium Romanum} witness was collated for this leaf; but it
is the nearest thing in the dossier to an identification of the psalter the
Roman chant sings.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{And the one commentator who knew all this voted against the chant}
Bellarmine's Latin note on the verse --- one of the passages his
nineteenth-century English translator deliberately omitted --- records the
whole situation and then decides: ``Illud, inimicorum, in graecis libris est,
seruorum, siue supplicum tuorum; \& sic etiam legit S.\ Augustinus. Sed in
hebraeo est, inimicorum \dots\ proinde necesse est in textibus graecis
irrepsisse errorem.'' He knows the reading this Introit sings, attributes it to
Augustine and to the Greek books, and judges it a corruption --- of
\latin{echthron}, enemies, into \latin{oiketon}, servants. Migne's apparatus at
Theodoret independently records \latin{oiketon} as the variant of one codex
against \latin{hiketon}, suppliants. So the Roman Rite goes on singing, in this
Introit, a reading its own greatest controversialist thought secondary. Worth
saying, and worth saying calmly: the guide reports the judgment and prints the
chant.
\end{witnessnote}

This guide accordingly does not describe the reading
as an alteration anywhere, and the proposal built on it in \textit{Interpretive
Possibilities} carries the correction as its controlling limit.

That the liturgy heard the chant as a question and the Epistle as its answer is
documented, not deduced. Guéranger, reporting Abbot Rupert, reads the
repetition of \latin{Réspice \dots\ in testaméntum tuum} at the Gradual as ``the
cry of the ancient people, who, sighing at the weakness of the powerless Law of
Sinai, besought God to fulfil the covenant He had promised to Abraham's
faith,'' and he gives the locus: \work{De divinis officiis} xii.13. Rupert's own
text was not opened for this guide, and he is cited only as excerpted.
Guéranger also reads the Introit's three petitions in order --- the poor, the
beasts, the cause --- as the situation of ``the just men of the old law,'' and
binds the Introit to the Gospel by a single word: ``Ingratitude is the
necessary outcome of forgetfulness, and to-day's Gospel justly condemns it.''

\elementguard
\subsection*{Collect \cue{Coll.}}

\elementid{Omnípotens sempitérne Deus, da nobis fidei, spei et caritátis augméntum}{\latin{Oratio}}{1563}

\textbf{The oldest prayer in the formulary, and one of the oldest in the
book.} It stands already in the Verona (``Leonine'') sacramentary, in the July
series, under the heading \latin{Item alia}, in the compressed scribal
abbreviations that collection uses: \latin{Omp semp Ds da nobis fidei spei et
caritatis augmentum et ut mereamur adsequi quod promittis fac nos amare quod
praecipis per} (Feltoe's edition, printed p.~76). Two things about that
witness matter. The spelling is \latin{adsequi}, which the Gregorian
supplement will still have and the 1962 book will not. And it carries no
Sunday assignment at all: the Verona collection is a series of votive and
daily Mass formularies, not a temporal cycle, so the prayer entered the Roman
books before there was a Thirteenth Sunday after Pentecost to put it on.

\textbf{Where it travelled.} In the Vatican Gelasian it appears twice. In Book
III, among the sixteen unnumbered Sunday Masses, it opens the ninth of them
(Wilson's edition, printed p.~229) --- and that same Mass carries this
Sunday's Postcommunion, so the Collect and the Postcommunion have travelled
together since long before either was attached to a numbered Sunday. In Book
III again, among the \latin{Orationes pro Caritate} (p.~247), a variant
recension gives it a different opening: \latin{Deus, qui iustitiam tuae legis
in cordibus credentium digito tuo scribis, da nobis fidei et spei caritatisque
augmentum}. Wilson's apparatus records that Pamelius, Gerbert and the Leofric
Missal read the received opening; the \latin{Deus qui} form is the Vatican
manuscript's own. In the Gregorian supplement preserved in Vat.\ Reg.\ 337 the
prayer heads a numbered section --- \latin{CXXII.\ DOMINICA .XIIII.\ POST
PENTECOSTEN} --- and is followed there by this Sunday's Secret and this
Sunday's Postcommunion (Wilson's 1915 edition, printed p.~173). That these
numbered Sunday sections belong to the supplement rather than to the
Hadrianum proper is explicit in Wilson's introduction; the attribution of the
supplement itself is a question this guide does not settle, since the
scholarship that revised Wilson's attribution is not public-domain and is not
claimed here.

\textbf{The Sunday number is the least stable thing about it.}

\begin{receptiontable}{Witness}{Where the prayer stands}{Note}
Verona (Leonine) & July series, \latin{Item alia} & votive; no Sunday at all\\
Vatican Gelasian & 9th of sixteen unnumbered Sunday Masses & with this Sunday's Postcommunion\\
Rheinau, S.\ Gallen Gelasians & \latin{Hebd. XV post Pentecosten} & per Wilson's note\\
Gregorian supplement (Reg.\ 337) & \latin{Dominica XIIII post Pentecosten} & with Secret and Postcommunion\\
Menard's Gregorian & Dom.\ XV & per Wilson's note\\
Sarum & Fourteenth Sunday after Trinity & with Lk. 17:11--19\\
1570--1962 Roman & Dom.\ XIII post Pentecosten & as printed here\\
\end{receptiontable}

The formulary is stable and its number is not. A guide that spoke of ``the
Collect of the Thirteenth Sunday'' as though the ordinal were part of the
prayer's identity would be describing a filing decision of the sixteenth
century.

\textbf{The Church has quoted this prayer back at herself, once, decisively.}
The Council of Trent, in the sixth session, chapter 10, teaching on the
increase of received justification, argues that justification grows and cites
the Church's own praying as evidence: \latin{Hoc vero iustitiae incrementum
petit sancta ecclesia, quum orat: Da nobis, Domine, fidei, spei et caritatis
augmentum}. The 1863 Tauchnitz edition footnotes the quotation
\latin{Dominica XIII.\ post Pentecosten}. Waterworth's public-domain English
of 1848 renders it: ``And this increase of justification holy Church begs,
when she prays, `Give unto us, O Lord, increase of faith, hope, and
charity.'\,'' The precise claim, and it is worth stating precisely, is that
this Sunday's Collect is the decree's one liturgical citation in its chapter
on increase. The council alters the address --- \latin{Domine} for the
prayer's \latin{Omnipotens sempiterne Deus} --- and takes the three virtues
and the noun \latin{augmentum} exactly.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{An honest negative beside it}
Chapter XI of the same session quotes Augustine, but not the sentence usually
attached to this prayer in devotional literature. What Trent cites is
\work{De natura et gratia} 43, \latin{Deus impossibilia non iubet}; the
\latin{da quod iubes} of the \work{Confessions} is nowhere in the decree, in
either language. The two prayers are cousins in logic and are not connected in
this text.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{The grammar of the second half is the prayer's argument.} The
punctuation of the typical edition is load-bearing: the purpose clause
\latin{ut mereámur ássequi quod promíttis} is suspended between \latin{et} and
the imperative \latin{fac nos amáre quod prǽcipis}, so that the attaining of
what God promises is made to hang on the loving of what he commands. The
prayer does not ask to obey; it asks to \textit{love} what is commanded, and
it asks God to do the making. That is the petitionary logic Augustine
canonised against the Pelagians, and it is worth saying carefully what the
relationship is. In \work{De dono perseverantiae} he argues from the Church's
prayers to the doctrine --- ``the Church would not pray that faith should be
given to unbelievers, unless it believed that God converts to Himself both the
averse and adverse wills of men'' --- but he does not quote this collect, and
he could not have: he died in 430 and the collect's earliest witness is later.
The relation is one of logic, not of citation, and this guide states it as
such.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Thomas Aquinas, \work{Summa theologiae} II--II q.~24 aa.~4, 6, 7;
I--II q.~114 a.~8}
Aquinas supplies the theology of the noun without ever, so far as this search
reached, quoting the prayer. Charity in this life can increase, and increases
by the soul's approach to God (q.~24 a.~4); not every act of charity increases
charity in act, but every act of charity disposes to its increase (a.~6);
and there is no fixed terminus of the increase in this life (a.~7). In I--II
q.~114 a.~8 the increase of grace is said to fall under condign merit, and the
term of grace's movement is eternal life --- which is, in different words, what
the Postcommunion of this same Mass will ask for. \textbf{Bounded negative:}
\latin{fac nos amare quod praecipis} occurs in neither of those files, and an
exact-phrase search of the Corpus Thomisticum returned no page. The bound is
worth stating because a positive control turned up in the same search: at
I--II q.~113 a.~9 Aquinas quotes the \textit{Tenth} Sunday's collect,
\latin{Deus, qui omnipotentiam tuam parcendo maxime et miserando manifestas}.
He demonstrably quotes this series of collects. He does not, where this guide
looked, quote this one.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{The Anglican afterlife, verified at every step.} The prayer crossed
into English in 1549 as the collect of ``The xiiii Sonday'' after Trinity:
``ALMIGHTYE and everlastyng God, geve unto us the increase of faythe, hope, and
charitie; and that we may obteine that whiche thou doest promise; make us to
love that whiche thou doest commaunde.'' It kept its Roman company: the same
Sunday carried the Sarum epistle and this Sunday's Roman Gospel, Luke
17:11--19. The 1877 Oxford parallel edition records the text as unchanged
through 1552, 1559, 1604, 1637 (which adds ``Amen'') and 1662, and Procter and
Frere print the Latin original under ``Fourteenth.'' The Sarum antecedent is
verified at the Fourteenth Sunday after Trinity in Pearson's edition. One
correction belongs here, because the misidentification is easy to make and
common: the derived collect is Trinity \textit{fourteen}, not thirteen. The
Prayer Book's Trinity 13 collect translates the Roman Twelfth Sunday's
\latin{Omnipotens et misericors Deus, de cuius munere}.

\subsection*{Epistle \cue{Ep.}}

\elementid{Abrahæ dictæ sunt promissiónes}{\latin{Léctio Epístolæ beáti Pauli Apóstoli ad Gálatas} --- Gal. 3, 16-22}{1564}

\subsubsection*{The complete argument the lesson is cut from}

Galatians is a letter of alarm. Paul has heard that converts from paganism are
being pressed by other missionaries to add circumcision and the observance of
the law to the faith they received, and the letter's whole third chapter is
one sustained proof that the addition would undo the thing added to. The
chapter opens by appealing to the Galatians' own experience (v.~2: did they
receive the Spirit by the works of the law, or by the hearing of faith?), then
turns to Abraham, who ``believed God, and it was reputed to him unto justice''
(v.~6, quoting Gen 15:6), and to the promise that in him all nations should be
blessed (v.~8, Gen 12:3). Verses 10--14 argue that the law brings a curse and
that Christ became a curse to redeem from it. Then, at v.~15, Paul introduces
the human comparison the appointed lesson depends on: \latin{secundum hominem
dico} --- I speak after the manner of men --- a man's ratified will is not
annulled or added to. The lesson begins at v.~16 and runs to v.~22.

Two boundaries decide what may honestly be said. The opening address
\latin{Fratres:} is the missal's, not Paul's: v.~16 begins directly
\latin{Abrahæ dictæ sunt promissiónes}, and Paul's own ``Brethren'' belongs to
v.~15, outside the lesson --- so the liturgical incipit borrows the address
without borrowing the verse that carries the will-comparison. And the lesson
stops at v.~22. The famous image of the law as \latin{pædagógus noster in
Christo}, the tutor leading to Christ, begins at v.~24 and is \textit{outside}
the appointed Epistle. Almost every ancient commentator on this passage runs
straight on into it, and every witness below whose argument does so is flagged,
because a guide that quotes their pedagogue material as exegesis of this
Sunday's lesson has quietly extended the lesson.

\subsubsection*{The seed}

Paul's argument turns on a singular noun: the promises were spoken to Abraham
and to his seed, \latin{et sémini eius}; God did not say \textit{and to thy
seeds}, as of many, but as of one, \latin{et sémini tuo, qui est Christus}.
The move is grammatical and has always looked vulnerable, since the Hebrew
collective noun for seed is ordinarily singular in form whatever its
reference. The Fathers who defend it defend it in two different ways, and the
difference is not cosmetic.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Jerome, \work{Commentarii in Epistulam ad Galatas}, lib.\ II}
Jerome concedes the character of the argument and then defends its substance.
Paul argues with the Galatians, he says, not with the reasoning he used to the
Romans but with simpler arguments, ``et pene de trivio'' --- almost off the
street --- which is why he prefaces \latin{secundum hominem dico}. But the
claim is materially true, and Jerome stakes his own reading on it: ``Omnes
Scripturas sensu ac memoria peragrans, numquam plurali numero semina scripta
reperi.'' Traversing all the Scriptures in sense and memory, he never found
\latin{semina} written in the plural. The nations were blessed not in Isaac or
in the patriarchs but \latin{in Christo Jesu}.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Augustine, \work{Expositio epistulae ad Galatas} §§23--25}
Augustine's seed is not quite Jerome's. ``Et ideo semen Abraham \dots\ Christum
dicit Apostolus, hoc est omnes Christianos fide imitantes Abraham; quod ad
singularitatem redigit commendando, quod non dictum est: Et seminibus, sed:
Semini tuo, quia et una est fides.'' The seed is Christ \textit{with his
members}, and the reason the singular holds is that the faith is one --- an
early instance of the \latin{totus Christus} reading. Two sections later the
same seed ``populum significat.'' Jerome's seed is Christ singly; Augustine's
is Christ and all who imitate Abraham's believing. Both are Christological
readings, and they are not the same reading.
\end{witnessnote}

Theodoret of Cyrus holds the two together with a distinction: Christ is the
seed properly, and Moses, Samuel and Elijah are Abraham's seed by nature, but
they are not the thing that brought the fountain of blessing to the nations.
Ambrosiaster puts the same point forensically --- the promise is firm
\latin{in Christo \dots\ non in multis, sed in uno}, and the judaizers are
\latin{quasi reos falsati testamenti}, defendants charged with forging the
testament. Chrysostom simply accepts the singular without philological
defence and hurries on to the consequence. Aquinas, characteristically,
grounds the singular in what Christ alone can do: \latin{quasi in uno, quod
est Christus, quia ipse solus est per quem et in quo omnes poterunt
benedici}.

\subsubsection*{The testament, and the four hundred and thirty years}

\latin{Testamentum confirmátum a Deo}: the noun is the same one the Introit
and the Gradual have already sung four times between them, and the Latin
commentators press its legal sense hard. Ambrosiaster makes the will-image
into a theology of inheritance: ``Testimonium ergo promissionis Abrahae
testamentum appellatur; ut post mortem ejus haeredes essent \dots\ ut post
mortem Christi credentes, haeredes essent fidei Abrahae per testamentum
sanguinis Christi.'' Aquinas states the underlying principle in a sentence
worth the whole lesson: ``nihil humanum tantam firmitatem habet sicut ultima
voluntas hominis'' --- nothing human has such firmness as a man's last will ---
and calls the promises ``quasi testamentum Dei, quia est quaedam ordinatio de
haereditate danda,'' confirmed by oath. Theodoret adds the observation that
matters most for this Sunday's chants: the ancient Scripture itself calls the
promise made to Abraham God's \latin{diatheke}. The Introit is not importing a
word into the psalm; the covenant vocabulary is native to both texts, and
Jerome's \latin{berith} note explains why one Latin noun serves for both.

The four hundred and thirty years are a different matter, and here the
tradition divides between those who use the number and those who cannot
account for it.

\begin{receptiontable}{Witness}{What he does with the 430 years}{Result}
Jerome & An open crux, and he says so: whether the count from promise to law is 430 against Genesis 15's 400 is ``non \dots\ parva res \dots\ et a multis quaesita, nescio an ab aliquo sit inventa'' --- sought by many, and he does not know that anyone has found it. He falls back on the \latin{simplex sensus}: later law cannot destroy prior promises & unsolved\\
Augustine & Silent. Verified: a string search of the full Latin of the \work{Expositio} for \latin{quadringent*} returns nothing. He replaces the chronological argument with a logical one --- ``Si enim lex iustificat, non est iustificatus Abraham'' & absent\\
Chrysostom & Uses it as bare posteriority: the law ``came four hundred and thirty years after'' and therefore cannot disannul & used, not explained\\
Marius Victorinus & Cannot be consulted: the section is lost (see below) & lacuna\\
Aquinas & Solves it arithmetically. Counting from the first promise to Abraham in Genesis 12 to the exodus gives 430; counting from the birth of Isaac gives only 405, and he lays out the patriarchal arithmetic & solved\\
\end{receptiontable}

That Aquinas answers, in the thirteenth century, the question Jerome had left
open in the fourth is one of the small pleasures of reading this lesson's
reception in order. He does not say he is answering Jerome. He simply does the
sum.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Marius Victorinus, \work{In Epistulam Pauli ad Galatas} --- a finding,
not an omission}
The earliest surviving Latin commentary on Galatians has no recoverable
comment on the heart of this lesson. In the transmitted text, book I jumps from
the exposition of 3:10 directly to the lemma \latin{VERS.\ 20}: the comments on
3:11--19, which would have contained both the seed argument and the four
hundred and thirty years, are lost in a lacuna. This was verified by listing
every \latin{VERS.} lemma in the text, and the tail of the v.~10 section
already discusses the mediator, which shows the disturbance falling exactly
here. What survives of him on this passage is his comment on v.~20, which is
emphatic --- ``solus enim Christus mediator, qui jungit illa inter quae
mediator est'' --- and distinctively Neoplatonic, the mediator recalling the
earthly members \latin{ad superiorem Ecclesiam}. And at v.~22 he writes a
formula worth noticing four decades before Augustine's \work{Expositio}: through
Moses all is shut under sin ``ut fides sola Jesu Christi sufficiat ad
justificationem liberationemque nostram.''
\end{witnessnote}

\subsubsection*{The mediator: the disagreement this guide does not resolve}

\latin{Ordináta per Angelos in manu mediatóris. Mediátor autem uníus non est:
Deus autem unus est.} Two questions sit in those two sentences --- who the
angels are, and who the mediator is --- and the checked witnesses answer the
second in two incompatible ways. This is the one place in the formulary where
the tradition genuinely splits, and the split is preserved here rather than
tidied.

\begin{receptiontable}{Witness}{The mediator is\dots}{The angels are\dots}
Jerome & Christ --- specifically the pre-incarnate Word. The law was given \latin{in manu mediatoris Christi Jesu, quia omnia per ipsum facta sunt}; before he took a human body he is called \latin{mediator} without addition, and only after the Incarnation is he \latin{mediator Dei et hominum homo Christus Jesus} & real theophany-angels, in whom the Mediator speaks; \latin{manum mediatoris} is his power\\
Augustine & Christ precisely \textit{as man}: ``Mediator ergo inter Deum et Deum esse non posset, quia unus est Deus,'' and without the emptying that took the form of a servant ``non esset mediator'' & the ministers of the whole dispensation of the Old Testament\\
Chrysostom & Christ, and the Giver of the law himself: ``By Mediator here he means Christ, and shows that He was before it, and Himself the Giver of it'' & either the priests, called angels, or the angels themselves --- he allows both\\
Ambrosiaster & Christ as \textit{arbiter of two peoples}: ``mediator, hoc est, arbiter non est unius, sed duorum,'' cutting away from the gentiles their many gods and from the Jews the works of the law & \textbf{human messengers}: ``Angelos dicit Dei nuntios, id est, Moysen, et Jesum filium Nave, et caeteros prophetas usque ad Joannem Baptistam''\\
Theodoret & \textbf{Moses}, explicitly. The law was laid down with angels serving and Moses ministering at its enactment, ``for Him he called mediator''; Moses mediated between the people and God & angels, ministering\\
Victorinus & Christ alone, joining the members to the heavenly Church & (lost in the lacuna)\\
Aquinas & Christ --- \latin{in manu mediatoris, id est, in potestate Christi} --- \textbf{and Moses as his figure}: ``Iste mediator significatus est per Moysen, in cuius manu est lex data,'' citing Deut. 5:5 & both readings offered as alternatives: ``per Angelos, id est, per nuntios Dei, scilicet Moysen et Aaron \dots\ Vel per Angelos, id est, ministerio Angelorum''\\
\end{receptiontable}

The Latin tradition solidified on Christ. The Antiochene reading is Moses, and
Theodoret is its clearest witness. What Aquinas does is not a compromise but a
bridge he builds deliberately: he keeps the mediator Christ, in whose power the
law stands, and makes Moses --- in whose literal hand the law was given ---
the figure that signifies him. He offers Ambrosiaster's human-messenger reading
and the literal angelic reading side by side as alternatives, and he gives
three expositions of \latin{mediator unius non est}, the third of which absorbs
Ambrosiaster's two-peoples arbitration through Ephesians 2:14. He knows the
tradition disagrees, and he writes so as to keep both sides usable.

Liturgical reception, when it arrives, sides with the Antiochenes without
appearing to notice that it is taking a side. Guéranger describes the law as
``made known to its subjects through the mediation of Moses and the angels''
and contrasts it with ``the covenant made by Abraham directly with God.'' The
medieval \work{Glossa ordinaria} had already handed the disagreement on
unresolved: at v.~19 its marginal gloss reads ``id est Moysen et alios ministros
Dei, vel ministerio angelorum'' --- exactly the pair Aquinas inherits ---
while its interlinear gloss on \latin{in manu mediatoris} says flatly ``qui
Deus et homo inter Deum et hominem.''

\subsubsection*{Why the law, then}

Paul's own answer is two words long: \latin{propter transgressiónes}. The
witnesses expand it in five directions that are worth setting beside each
other, because the differences are real.

\begin{pairtable}{Witness}{Purpose of the law}
Jerome & Custody, not subversion: the law ``inter repromissionem et completionem ejus media subrepsit,'' given \latin{in custodiam eam repromissionis, non in subversionem} --- Israel held by legal bonds, ``et, ut ita dicam, in carcerem''\\
Augustine & Given \textit{to the proud}: ``Superbienti ergo populo lex posita est \dots\ ut transgressione humiliaretur, ut quaereret gratiam'' --- the law shows sin so that the humbled may learn that salvation is not in his own hand ``sed in manu mediatoris.'' The wordplay on \latin{in manu} is deliberate\\
Chrysostom & A bridle: the law was placed on them ``as a bridle, guiding, regulating, and checking''\\
Theodoret & For the care of the race --- \latin{epimeleia} --- from which the seed was to sprout according to the flesh; and he directs that \latin{Quid ígitur lex?} be read in an objector's voice\\
Aquinas & Four ends: to repress transgressions; to tame concupiscence by wearying ceremonies (Acts 15:10); to convict of impotence; and to instruct by figure in the grace to come (Heb 10:1)\\
\end{pairtable}

There is no contradiction in that column, but there is a real difference of
temperature. Jerome's law guards; Augustine's law humiliates; Chrysostom's law
restrains; Theodoret's law nurses a people along; Aquinas's law does four
things at once. Only Augustine makes pride the occasion, and only Augustine
makes the answer to it a pun that the Epistle itself supplies.

\subsubsection*{\latin{Conclúsit Scriptúra ómnia sub peccáto}}

The lesson ends on a verb of imprisonment, and every checked witness takes
care that it should not be heard as making Scripture the author of the
imprisonment. Jerome's image is judicial: Scripture shuts up the guilty as a
judge does, so that the sovereign may afterwards pardon. Theodoret's is
lexical and blunt --- the word ``shut up'' stands in place of ``convicted'' ---
and he offers faith's salvation as an \latin{alexipharmakon}, an antidote.
Chrysostom's is medical: the law was given ``to probe their wounds, that they
might long for a physician,'' and ``the word shut up means convinced.''
Ambrosiaster reads it against Romans 11:32 --- ``Manifestatis enim peccatis
suis, conclusi sunt, ut se excusare non possent, sed quaererent
misericordiam'' --- and seals it with Isaiah 59:20--21, where the promise is
itself called \latin{testamentum}, which brings the lesson back to the word
the Introit sang. Aquinas turns the medical image into a syllogism: as
knowledge of one's disease and awareness of one's helplessness together drive
a man to look for a doctor, so knowledge of sin and of one's own impotence
drive him to look for Christ. And Augustine had already fixed the destination
of the whole movement in the phrase this lesson's last clause supplies:
\latin{credéntibus}, to them that believe --- which is precisely what the
Collect asked an increase of, an hour earlier in the same Mass.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{The \work{Glossa ordinaria} --- what the middle ages inherited}
Checked in the electronic edition of the \work{Glossae Scripturae Sacrae},
keyed to the Rusch facsimile. Its value here is not fresh exegesis: it is a
mosaic of Augustine and Ambrosiaster with Chrysostomic matter, and it is
classified in this guide's audit as reuse rather than direct witness. But it
shows what a thirteenth-century reader met on the page. On v.~17 the marginal
gloss records that the law ``post multum tempus a promissione facta Abrahe
\dots\ data est'' and the interlinear draws the consequence sharply: if
blessing were by the law, ``frustra semini promisit et frustra semen ipsum
venit \dots\ et Deus mendax.'' On v.~22, ``lex data est ut gratia quereretur''
--- Augustine almost verbatim --- and ``Data est ut egrum de morbo convincet
qui sibi sanus videbatur.''
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Two boundaries, and one dispute that is not this one}
Every ancient witness above except Ambrosiaster and Augustine runs on past
v.~22 into the pedagogue; Ambrosiaster begins a fresh lemma at v.~23 and
Augustine's treatment is essentially self-contained within the lesson's
bounds. Separately: the celebrated Jerome--Augustine controversy over
Galatians concerns \textbf{Gal 2:11--14}, Paul's rebuke of Peter at Antioch,
and nothing in that correspondence engages 3:16--22. It is background on how
differently the two men read the letter, and this guide makes no other use of
it.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Where the supersessionist sentences actually are}
Several witnesses on this lesson write sentences about Israel that are harsher
than Paul's argument requires, and this guide attributes each to its author
rather than to ``the Fathers.'' Jerome allegorises the twins of Genesis 38 and
speaks of Israel's hand polluted with the blood of the prophets and the
Saviour. Ambrosiaster writes that the blessing ``transitum fecit ad gentes''
and that the Jews were blinded \latin{aemulatione quadam}. Guéranger, writing
in the nineteenth century, is harsher than any of them --- ``his now dead
Law,'' ``the superannuated \dots\ pretensions of the Synagogue.'' None of these
is the appointed lesson's own claim. Paul's conclusion in this pericope is that
the law was not against the promises of God, \latin{absit}, and that Scripture
shut up all --- \latin{ómnia}, not one people --- under sin.
\end{witnessnote}

\elementguard
\subsection*{Gradual \cue{Grad.}}

\elementid{Réspice, Dómine, in testaméntum tuum}{\latin{Graduale} --- Ps. 73, 20, 19 et 22}{1565}

\textbf{The one chant of the day that repeats another.} The Gradual sings the
Introit's two opening verses over again. That is unusual enough to have been
noticed in the liturgical commentary tradition, and Guéranger, citing Abbot
Rupert, gives it a reason: the repetition is ``the cry of the ancient people,
who, sighing at the weakness of the powerless Law of Sinai, besought God to
fulfil the covenant He had promised to Abraham's faith.'' On that reading the
Gradual is not a reprise but a second asking, made \textit{after} the Epistle
has been read --- the covenant plea renewed in the light of the argument that
the covenant cannot be annulled.

\textbf{One verb changes, and it changes toward the Bible.} Where the Introit
sang \latin{ne derelínquas}, forsake not, the Gradual sings \latin{ne
obliviscáris}, forget not --- which is the Clementine's own verb at Psalm
73:19, and therefore the one clause in either chant where the missal and the
Douay agree exactly. The pair is a fact of the printed page, verified in both
1962 witnesses and already in print in 1862 and 1570. What no checked witness
does is comment on the pair; nothing in the corpus searched for this guide
notices that the two chants differ, and the likeliest explanation remains
ordinary psalter transmission. The observation is recorded as a textual
observation and nothing is built on it in this section; a proposal that does
build on it stands, labelled as a proposal, in \textit{Interpretive
Possibilities}.

\textbf{The verse the missal cites and the wording it sings.} The Gradual's
℣ is \latin{Exsúrge, Dómine, et iúdica causam tuam: memor esto oppróbrii
servórum tuórum}, and the printed reference is Ps.\ 73:22. The first clause
corresponds to Psalm 73:22a with \latin{Dómine} for \latin{Deus} and an added
\latin{et}. The second clause does not correspond to Psalm 73:22b at all. The
Clementine's v.~22b is \latin{memor esto improperiorum tuorum, eorum quae ab
insipiente sunt tota die} --- remember the reproaches with which the foolish
man reproaches thee --- whereas the chant's clause is verbally identical,
minus the vocative, with Psalm 88:51, \latin{Memor esto, Domine, opprobrii
servorum tuorum}. The reproach has changed owner: in the psalm God is asked to
remember the insults offered \textit{to him}, and in the chant he is asked to
remember the reproach borne \textit{by his servants}. This guide records the
fact and does not correct the missal's reference; the Douay wording that
answers the chant's clause is quoted in the appointed-text apparatus above only
as apparatus, never as the verse the missal cites.

\textbf{A note on the divine title, because it has a history.} Both chants sing
\latin{exsúrge, Dómine} where the Clementine and the Douay have ``Arise, O
God.'' That liturgical form, not the Bible's, is the one that appears at the
head of the most famous document ever to quote this verse; the story is told
in \textit{Notable and Quotable}, and the textual point belongs here: the
divergence is verified, and it is what makes the identification possible.
Cassiodorus's lemma at this verse is the chant's on both counts ---
\latin{Exsurge, Domine, iudica causam tuam} --- so the wording the bull opens
with is the wording a sixth-century commentator had in front of him.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Theodoret defends the chant's pronoun, and Augustine did not have it}
The Gradual's clause is \latin{iúdica causam \emph{tuam}}, judge \emph{thy}
cause, and one witness makes that pronoun the point of his whole note.
Theodoret: very aptly, he says, the psalmist changed the pronoun --- he did not
say \textit{my} case but \textit{thy} case; ``for I,'' says the speaker,
``suffered these things justly, but they dared many blasphemies against
thee.'' The suppliant concedes that his own punishment is deserved and
re-founds the plea on God's injured honour, which is why the petition can be
pressed at all after a psalm that has just admitted the sanctuary is in ashes.
Cassiodorus makes the complementary move on the same words: God ``iudicat enim
causam suam, dum errantes facit manifesta cognoscere, ut conversi praedicent
quod stultis cogitationibus abnuebant'' --- he judges his own cause by
converting, not by destroying.

The caution belongs here too. \textbf{Augustine's lemma is not the chant's.} He
reads \latin{Exsurge, Domine; iudica causam \emph{meam}}, and the Oxford
translators render it ``Arise, O Lord, judge \textit{my} cause'' --- so his
exposition, which turns on the speaker being mocked for believing what he
cannot show and answers with the Resurrection (``Non enim venturus est ad
iudicium, nisi qui exsurrexit a mortuis''), is an exposition of a possessive
this Sunday does not sing. A guide that quoted him for the Gradual without
saying so would have attributed to him a comment on a word he did not have.

Bellarmine noticed the same thing eleven centuries later and settled it. The
Hebrew, he says, reads literally \latin{Litiga litem tuam}; since God cannot
plead before any tribunal but his own, the Septuagint and the Vulgate rightly
have \latin{iudica causam tuam}; ``S.\ Augustinus legit, Iudica causam meam:
sed nescio quid secutus sit: nam hebraeus, \& graecus codex habet secundam
personam, \& sic inuenitur in latinis libris.'' A Greek of the fifth century
and a Latin of the seventeenth independently defend the pronoun this Gradual
sings, and the second names Augustine's variant and rejects it.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{And the plainest statement of the covenant reading is the most
recent.} Schuster, on this Gradual, writes that it ``recalls to God the
promises made to the Patriarchs, and draws his attention to their merits,''
and puts the prayer into direct speech: ``O Lord, we have not deserved thy
grace, but grant it to us, as an inheritance due to us because of thy promises
to our Fathers the Patriarchs of old \dots\ We have no personal merits which
can avail us, but their merits are also ours \dots\ Our cause is their cause,
therefore it is thine.'' That is the same move Cassiodorus made thirteen
centuries earlier at v.~20 --- having no goods of the people's to recall, plead
the promise --- and it is the Epistle's argument arriving at the chant from the
other side. Schuster also records that this Gradual is shared with the Thursday
after the Fourth Sunday in Lent.

\elementguard
\subsection*{Alleluia \cue{All.}}

\elementid{Dómine, refúgium factus es nobis}{Alleluia, no separate heading --- Ps. 89, 1}{1566}

\textbf{The one psalm in the Psalter that bears Moses' name.} The inscription
is \latin{Oratio Moysi, hominis Dei}, and the placement is pointed whether or
not it was chosen for the point: on the Sunday whose Epistle argues that the
law given through Moses cannot annul a covenant older than it, the Alleluia
sings the prayer the Psalter attributes to Moses himself, and the clause it
takes is not about law at all. It is about refuge. A title associating a
prayer with a figure is received tradition and not a demonstration of
authorship, and this guide keeps the distinction; what is not in doubt is that
the Psalter presents this prayer as Moses' and that the missal takes its one
line from it.

\textbf{The clause and its whole psalm.} Psalm 89's burden is the contrast
between God's eternity and human brevity --- a thousand years in his sight are
as yesterday which is past, men are as grass which in the morning shall grow up
and in the evening fall and wither --- and the psalm ends by asking that the
work of our hands be directed upon us. The Alleluia takes only the opening
line, and it takes exactly the clause that carries the contrast: God has been
our refuge \latin{a generatióne et progénie}, from generation and progeny. The
Clementine reads \latin{a generatione in generationem} and the Douay
accordingly ``from generation to generation.'' The sense is near and the
wording is not, and no rendering of the chant's own form is supplied anywhere
in this guide.

\textbf{What it answers.} The Introit had asked God not to forsake his poor
\latin{in finem} and had demanded to know why he had cast them off
\latin{in finem}. The Alleluia's answer is not an argument but a fact claimed
about the past: across every generation there has ever been, the refuge held.
It is placed between the Epistle's proof that a covenant survives the interval
and the Gospel's story of what happens in one.

\subsubsection*{Two generations, or all of them}

The chant's phrase is the verse's crux, and the checked witnesses divide over
whether it counts generations or names two of them.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Augustine, \work{Enarrationes in Psalmos} 89}
Augustine begins by taking the title seriously and then denying its plain
sense. Moses ``minister Testamenti Veteris, et propheta Testamenti Novi''; but
``non enim credendum est ab ipso omnino Moyse istum psalmum fuisse
conscriptum, qui ullis eius litteris inditus non est \dots\ sed alicuius
significationis gratia tam magni meriti servi Dei nomen adhibitum est.'' The
Oxford English: ``it is not to be believed that this Psalm was entirely the
composition of that Moses \dots\ but the name of the great servant of God is
used for the sake of some intimation.'' A fifth-century bishop states, in
public, the distinction between traditional ascription and authorship that the
page-2 dossier of this guide has to state again.

Then the reading that makes this chant belong to this Sunday. His lemma is
\latin{in generatione et generatione}, and he offers two senses: ``sive in omni
generatione, \emph{sive in duabus generationibus, vetere et nova}; quia \dots\
minister fuit ille Testamenti pertinentis ad generationem veterem, et Propheta
Testamenti pertinentis ad generationem novam. Cuius Testamenti sponsor,
sponsusque coniugii \dots\ ait ipse Iesus: \latin{Si crederetis Moysi,
crederetis et mihi}'' (Jn. 5:46). On the second reading the Alleluia is not a
general comfort at all: it says that the refuge held across \emph{both
covenants} --- which is the Epistle's thesis sung in one line, on the Sunday
that argues it.

He also supplies the reason a refuge can be ``made'' without God changing:
``Tu ergo qui semper es \dots\ Ecce quae aeternitas facta est nobis refugium,
ut in ea mansuri, ad eam de hac temporis mutabilitate fugiamus.'' The refuge is
God's eternity, and what one flees to it from is \latin{temporis mutabilitas}
--- the mutability of time. That is the axis the Offertory will turn on twenty
minutes later.
\end{witnessnote}

Cassiodorus's lemma is the chant's exactly --- \latin{refugium factus es nobis,
a generatione et progenie}, against the Clementine's \latin{a generatione in
generationem} --- and he is the one witness who glosses the two nouns
separately: ``A generatione, significat ex quo generari coeptum est genus
humanum. Progeniem vero dixit usque ad finem saeculi, cum progenies sibi
humani generis per aetates singulas reparata succedit; ac si diceret,
\latin{semper}.'' From the first begetting to the end of the world: as if he
had said, always. He reads the psalm first through Moses' own biography ---
the ark among the Hebrew children, Pharaoh, the Red Sea, the murmuring people
--- and then insists on the grammar: ``nobis posuit, non mihi,'' so that the
refuge is the whole Church's and not one man's. On the title he takes over
Augustine's sentence and adds a point of his own: Moses is named because the
psalm \emph{joins} both covenants, ``quod duo sacramenta vi sui nominis
indicaret.''

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Jerome, \work{Tractatus de psalmo LXXXVIIII} --- and the Introit
quoted at the Alleluia}
Jerome asks what a refuge is for, and answers by naming three pursuers: ``aut
ab aestu nimio quaerit et calore, aut bestia illum persequitur, aut hostes
illum persequuntur, ut interficiant.'' Then he applies all three --- and the
verse he reaches for to prove the second is Psalm 73:19, the verse the Introit
has already sung: ``et nos bestia persequitur, de qua dicimus \latin{Ne tradas
bestiis animam confitentem tibi}.'' Morin's apparatus identifies the citation.
The Alleluia's psalm and the Introit's psalm are joined, in a fourth-century
homily, at exactly the verse the Introit takes; nothing about this Sunday's
arrangement is presupposed by that, and it is the closest thing in the whole
dossier to a witness reading the day's chants together.

On the phrase itself he gives both of Augustine's senses: ``In generatione et
generatione: et in praeterito, et in praesenti, et in futuro. Aliter \dots\ et
in vetere populo Iudaeorum, et in nostro Xpistianorum.'' In his second homily
on the same psalm he adds that the Hebrews ascribe this psalm and the ten
following to Moses because they carry no title, that \latin{hominis Dei} is a
title of dignity rather than of servitude, and a sentence worth keeping beside
the Introit's grief: ``Refugium numquam dicitur, nisi fuerit persecutio.'' No
public-domain English exists for these homilies, so they are quoted in Latin.
\end{witnessnote}

Theodoret reads the title differently again, and the difference is
grammatical: his lemma is a dative --- a prayer \emph{for} Moses, not \emph{of}
Moses --- which is what allows him to report two views without deciding, some
writers ascribing the prayer to Moses and some to David, who fitted it to
Moses. He then finds the psalm to carry a double oracle, one about the
calamities of the Jews and one about the corruptibility of human nature at
large. And when he expands ``in generation and generation'' he does it twice,
first as a list of rescues in Israel's history and then as a list for the whole
race: Enoch translated, Noah preserved as a spark for the race when all had
embraced a bestial life, and then --- the one place at any of these three
psalms where he reaches for the covenant --- God ``choosing the patriarch
Abraham, promised the blessing to all the nations.'' It is polemical in
purpose, against those who said the Lord came to the Gentiles from a change of
mind. Its interest here is that the Abrahamic promise-to-the-nations from which
this Sunday's Epistle argues turns up in the Greek tradition at the
\textit{Alleluia's} verse, and not at the Introit's.

The medieval Psalter gloss transmits Augustine's reading whole: the title's
interlinear note says Moses ``fuit minister Veteris Testamenti et propheta Novi
Testamenti \dots\ \emph{non quod Moyses eum conscripserit},'' and at v.~1 the
gloss under Augustine's name is his alternative verbatim --- ``id est in omni
generatione vel veteri cuius ille minister et nova cuius ille propheta.''

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Bellarmine denies the Mosaic authorship on a new ground, and gives the
Alleluia the Collect's three virtues}
His Latin argues the authorship out. Jerome's letter to Cyprian held that Moses
wrote this psalm and the ten following; Augustine denied it because it is not
in the Pentateuch with his other canticles; and Bellarmine adds a reason of his
own --- ``quoniam in hoc Psalmo definitur vita hominis, vt plurimum, annis
septuaginta, \& in robustioribus, annis octoginta: at Mosis tempore plurimi
centesimum annum transcendebant.'' Moses died at a hundred and twenty and was
eighty at his call; a psalm that fixes a man's span at seventy is not his
arithmetic. The title stands, he concludes, because David brings Moses on to
speak. None of that is in the public-domain English, which prints only the
Douay heading and calls the author David throughout.

What the English does carry is the note on the verse itself, and it is worth
having: he that seeks new favours ``can make use of no argument so convincing
as the shewing himself grateful for the past'' --- which is the Gospel's
argument arriving at the Alleluia --- and then the Hebrew: the word for refuge
``signifies a well-fortified house, placed on an eminence, the tenants of which
are quite secure from their enemies, from beasts, from flood, and from
storms.'' Those who take refuge in God, he says, ``dwell as they would in a
city fortified by faith, hope, and charity.'' The Collect of this Mass asked
for an increase of exactly those three, an hour earlier; Bellarmine is not
citing it, and the guide does not say he is.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{Two negatives worth recording.} Guéranger prints this Alleluia and
passes on without a word of comment on it. Schuster comments, and his comment
is about the psalm's constancy rather than its covenants: ``The help of man
fails either from inconstancy or from incapability. God, on the contrary, is
always constant in his friendship.''

\subsection*{Gospel \cue{Gosp.}}

\elementid{Dum iret Iesus in Ierúsalem}{\latin{✠ Sequéntia sancti Evangélii secúndum Lucam} --- Luc. 17, 11-19}{1567}

\subsubsection*{The complete episode and where it stands}

The pericope is a whole episode, opened by v.~11 and closed by v.~19; the
question about when the kingdom of God comes, at v.~20, is outside it. Luke
places the episode on the long journey to Jerusalem that structures the middle
of his Gospel, and locates it with a phrase that has puzzled readers since
antiquity: Jesus passed \latin{per médiam Samaríam et Galilǽam}, through the
midst of Samaria and Galilee. Someone travelling south from Galilee to
Jerusalem would meet Galilee first; Luke names Samaria first. Immediately
before, at 17:5--10, stand the apostles' request ``increase our faith'' and the
parable of the unprofitable servant --- and the request is worth registering,
because this Sunday's Collect has already asked for an increase of faith and
this Gospel will end by naming faith as what saved.

The liturgical incipit is the missal's own: \latin{In illo témpore: Dum iret
Iesus in Ierúsalem} supplies the subject that Luke's sentence carries over
from the preceding narrative, where the Clementine reads \latin{Et factum est,
dum iret in Ierúsalem}. After that clause the appointed Latin matches the
Clementine word for word.

\subsubsection*{What Augustine made of it, and what the Middle Ages did with what he made}

Augustine is the origin of nearly everything the Latin West said about this
pericope for eight hundred years, and his reading turns on a medical
observation. Leprosy, he says, is a defect of \textit{colour}, not of health
or limb --- which is why Luke says the ten were \latin{mundati}, cleansed,
rather than \latin{sanati}, healed. The lepers therefore signify not sinners
in general but those who lack the knowledge of the true faith and profess
variegated doctrines of error: ``Leprosi ergo non absurde intellegi possunt
qui scientiam verae fidei non habentes varias doctrinas profitentur erroris
\dots\ Nulla porro falsa doctrina est quae non aliqua vera intermisceat.'' No
false doctrine is without some truth mixed in: that is the streaked skin.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Augustine, \work{Quaestiones Evangeliorum} II.40}
Three further moves, each of which the tradition kept. \textit{Why the
priests}: the Jewish priesthood prefigures the royal priesthood of the Church,
and doctrine and the sacraments are the Church's province --- Paul is sent to
Ananias, Cornelius to Peter; and Cornelius's household, receiving the Spirit
before baptism, is cleansed \textit{while going}, which is the seed of the
whole later reading of \latin{dum irent}. \textit{The number}: nine is
imperfection, standing outside the unity that ten signifies. \textit{The
Samaritan}: the name is interpreted \latin{custos}, keeper, and this leper
keeps the unity of the kingdom by giving back to God what he had received
(Ps 58:10, \latin{Fortitudinem meam ad te custodiam}). Augustine also writes
here the sentence that its later transmission had to decide what to do with:
``Et quia illi erant Iudaei, amisisse per superbiam declarati sunt regnum
caelorum, ubi maxime unitas custoditur.'' It is Augustine's sentence and it is
attributed to him.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Augustine, \work{Sermo} 176 --- preached on this pericope}
The same pericope in the pulpit, and the register changes completely: the
sermon is anti-Pelagian. None in Adam's mass is un-sick and none is healed
without Christ's grace; infants are carried to the physician on other people's
feet. ``Doctrina inconstans, non habens unum colorem, mentis lepram
significat'' --- but the application is now to the hearer's own account of his
amendment: ``Noli tibi tribuere, ne sis inter novem qui gratias non egerunt.''
Do not attribute it to yourself, lest you be among the nine who gave no
thanks. The thankful one is the \latin{alienigena} who signifies the Gentiles;
``numerus ille Christo decimas dedit''; and the sermon's hardest sentence is
about the preacher and his congregation, not about anyone else: ``Nostrum
nihil, nisi peccatum quod habemus.'' No public-domain English exists at this
sermon --- the standard modern translation is in copyright --- so it is quoted
here in Latin only.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Bede, \work{In Lucae Evangelium Expositio} V (PL 92, 542B--544D)}
Bede's chapter on this pericope is a proven mosaic, and saying so is not a
criticism of Bede: it is what a Latin commentator of the eighth century
understood himself to be doing. The leprosy-as-false-doctrine blocks, the
Paul--Ananias and Cornelius material, the \latin{custos} etymology and the
nine-and-ten number theology are Augustine's \work{Quaestiones} II.40, matched
line for line. ``Et bene, ut salventur, Iesum praeceptorem nominant\dots'' is
verbatim Gregory, \work{Moralia} V.11 n.~28, and the gloss on true thanksgiving
reworks \work{Moralia} II.52 n.~82.

Two things are Bede's own. The first is an editorial mercy: he \textbf{drops}
Augustine's sentence about the Jews having lost the kingdom by pride. The
second is the closing paragraph, which ties this lection back to the parable of
the unprofitable servant at 17:5--10 and draws the distinction the whole
Sunday turns on --- the ten were cleansed and the one was \textit{saved}:
``Si autem fides salvum fecit eum \dots\ ergo perfidia perdidit eos qui de
acceptis beneficiis Deo gloriam dare neglexerunt.'' What saves is faith's
working, not its bare acknowledgement. \textbf{Negative within the witness:}
Bede abbreviates the lemma and has no comment at all on the route clause.
\end{witnessnote}

Five centuries later, Aquinas's \work{Catena aurea} compiles Bede under his own
name --- and restores Augustine's dropped sentence, silently, from the
\work{Quaestiones}. The Catena transmits Augustine unsoftened. Whether that was
a decision or an accident of compiling from two exemplars, the effect is that
the harshest sentence in the pericope's Latin reception disappears in the
eighth century and returns in the thirteenth, and a guide that cites ``Bede and
Aquinas'' as one voice on this passage has flattened the one place where they
demonstrably differ.

\subsubsection*{The Greek witnesses, and the one ancient comment on the route}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Cyril of Alexandria, \work{Commentary on Luke} --- fragments only}
An honest bound first: \textbf{the Syriac of Sermons 113--116 is lost}, and what
survives for this pericope is catena fragments, printed at pp.~539--541 of
Payne Smith's 1859 Oxford translation under the lemmata 17:12 and 17:14. One
cannot cite an integral ``Sermon 113 on the ten lepers,'' and this guide does
not. What the fragments carry is worth having. On the priests: they are ``ever
envious of His glory,'' and are made witnesses of the cure precisely because
they knew the marks of leprosy and of its healing --- ``He sent them to the
priests, and with them He sent also the healing.'' And on the route clause,
Cyril is \textbf{the one ancient witness who comments on it at all}: the
thankful man was ``of foreign race, having been brought thither from Assyria:
for the phrase is not without meaning, `in the middle of Samaria and
Galilee.'\,'' Payne Smith's translation is public domain and is quoted here.
Cyril's supersessionist edge is his own and is reported as such: ``It shows
therefore that the Samaritans were grateful, but that the Jews, even when
benefited, were ungrateful.''
\end{witnessnote}

Theophylact of Ohrid can be reached here only partially. His Luke commentary
occupies PG 123, and no public scan of the relevant columns could be opened;
what is verified is the Latin of the excerpts in the \work{Catena aurea}, and
they preserve the most generous reading in the whole dossier. The lepers stood
afar off ``quasi verecundantes de immunditia'' --- as if ashamed of their
uncleanness --- ``yet were made near by praying''; going to the priests
carried an implied promise of cure; and, on the Samaritan: ``nihil impedit
quemquam placere Deo, etsi de genere profano processerit \dots\ Nullus etiam
natus ex sanctis superbiat.'' Nothing prevents anyone from pleasing God even
if he came of profane stock, and let no one born of holy people be proud.
The modern English translation of Theophylact is in copyright and is not
quoted; the public-domain access is the 1843 Oxford Catena.

Titus of Bostra, surviving only as catena fragments and unaudited here against
an independent edition, turns the route into the pericope's most generous
theology: Christ crosses between the two estranged peoples ``quasi pacificans
\dots\ ut utrosque compingat in unum novum hominem,'' applying Ephesians 2:15
to Luke's geography. It is a fragment-level claim and is flagged as one.

\subsubsection*{\latin{Dum irent}: the two words that carried a doctrine}

The most consequential thing in this pericope's reception is not an
interpretation of the Samaritan but of a subordinate clause. \latin{Et factum
est, dum irent, mundáti sunt}: they were cleansed \textit{while they were
going}, before they reached the priests they had been sent to. Augustine had
already seen the pattern in Cornelius, who received the Spirit before baptism.
What the Latin Middle Ages did with it was build the theology of contrition on
it.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Pseudo-Augustine, \work{De vera et falsa poenitentia} c.~10 n.~25}
An eleventh-century anonymous treatise transmitted among Augustine's works and
printed under \latin{Auctor incertus} --- the attribution is stated here
because the text's influence is enormous and its author is not Augustine. Two
moves, in tension with each other and both retained by the tradition that
inherited them. First, oral and personal confession is grounded on v.~14:
``Praecepit enim Dominus mundandis, ut ostenderent ora sacerdotibus: docens
corporali praesentia confitenda peccata, non per nuntium, non per scriptum
manifestanda,'' with the shame of confessing itself ``partem habens
remissionis.'' Second, contrition precedes the keys: ``Mundati enim sunt
leprosi, dum ibant ostendere ora sacerdotibus, antequam ad eos pervenirent
\dots\ Unde patet Deum ad cor respicere.'' This treatise fed Gratian and Peter
Lombard, and is the hinge by which this Sunday's Gospel entered scholastic
penance theology.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Bruno of Segni, \work{Commentaria in Lucam} II, cap.\ XVII, and
\work{Homilia} C (PL 165)}
Bruno reads the pericope twice, and states the \latin{dum irent} theology at its
sharpest. The ten are not heretics but \textit{all sinners} --- ``Quid enim sunt
decem viri leprosi, nisi omnes peccatores?'' --- ten being the number of
totality; excommunication is the Church's casting of lepers outside the camp.
On the priests his reading is juridical, and it is the reading Trent's
theology of the sacrament would later need: they are sent ``non ut sacerdotes
eos mundarent, sed ut mundos eos esse judicarent'' --- not that the priests
should cleanse them but that they should judge them clean. And then: ``Facile
est Domino peccata dimittere. Prius enim multoties peccatori debita remittuntur,
quam veniat ad sacerdotem \dots\ quia cor contritum et humiliatum Deus non
spernit.'' God often remits before the penitent reaches the priest at all.
Bruno also guards the reading against presumption in the other direction:
cleansing is not election --- ``Multi enim sunt baptizati, multi a peccatorum
lepra mundati, qui \dots\ in electorum numero non computabuntur.'' His homily
gives the fullest medieval engagement with the route: ``Sed quid per
Galilaeam, nisi Judaeos? Et quid per Samariam, nisi gentiles intelligamus?
\dots\ Per medium autem illorum transibat Jesus, quia hos et illos vocabat,''
and the \latin{castellum} is the Church. He ends where Bede ended: ``Fides
igitur salvat, fides justificat, fides et interius et exterius hominem sanat.''
\end{witnessnote}

Guéranger carries the same chain into the nineteenth century and states its
sacramental conclusion outright: ``The lepers are made clean only while on
their way to show themselves to the priests \dots\ That Law gave to the sons of
Aaron the power, not of curing, but of discerning leprosy,'' whereas the new
priesthood's judgments, ``by pronouncing the sentence of absolution, are to
effectually remove the leprosy of souls.'' The chain is therefore continuous
and documentable: Augustine's Cornelius, Bede, the pseudo-Augustinian treatise,
Bruno, the scholastic penance tradition, Guéranger. This Sunday's Gospel
carries, in its reception, the doctrine of contrition and the keys.

\subsubsection*{Two large negative results, and what they show}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Ambrose has no exposition of this pericope}
What exists is one transitional clause in \work{Expositio Evangelii secundum
Lucam} VIII n.~32: ``Post haec reprehenduntur ingrati: atque ita demum ad
tractatum futuri venitur judicii.'' After this the ungrateful are rebuked, and
so at last one comes to the treatment of the coming judgment. That is all.
Method: a full search of books VIII--IX of the raw text over the stems
\textit{lepr-}, \textit{samari-}, \textit{mundat-}, \textit{alienigen-},
\textit{decem}, \textit{gratias} and \textit{ingrat-}. The \work{Catena
aurea}'s single one-clause Ambrose excerpt independently confirms that this is
all the Catena's compilers could find either.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Gregory the Great preached no homily on it --- but is in its
bloodstream}
All forty of the \work{Homiliae in Evangelia}, with the preface, were searched;
every hit is incidental. Yet Gregory supplies, through the \work{Moralia in
Iob} V.11 n.~28, the reading Bede transcribed and the Catena transmits under
Bede's name. There Job's friends, who ``mingle right things with perverse,''
typify heretics reconciled by the Church's preaching: ``Quos bene in Evangelio
illa decem leprosorum mundatio designat \dots\ Leprosi itaque haereticos
exprimunt, quia dum rectis prava permiscent, colorem sanum maculis
aspergunt. Unde et bene ut salventur, clamant, Iesu praeceptor.'' A father who
never preached on a pericope can still be one of its principal voices, by way
of a commentator who read him.
\end{witnessnote}

Two further negatives belong with those. No genuine or pseudonymous
Chrysostom homily on the ten lepers was found in Latin, English or Greek-keyed
searches, and the \work{Catena aurea}'s total silence on Chrysostom at this
lection corroborates the absence. And Bede's sentence ``electi in faciem et
reprobi retrorsum cadunt'' was not found verbatim anywhere in the
\work{Moralia}, though the doctrinally parallel exposition of Genesis 49:17
was; the sentence remains Gregorian in doctrine and unlocated in wording, and
is described that way rather than attributed.

\subsubsection*{What the \work{Catena aurea} shows about the shape of the tradition}

Read as a map rather than as an authority, Aquinas's compilation on this
lection tells us which voices a thirteenth-century reader could actually
reach: Ambrose's one clause, the Greek catena tradition (Titus, Cyril),
Augustine's \work{Quaestiones}, Bede --- and Theophylact. No Chrysostom, and no
Gregory under his own name, though Gregory is present inside the Bede
excerpts. That is a remarkably thin canon for a famous pericope, and it
explains why Augustine's reading dominated: for most of the Latin Middle Ages
there was very little else.

\subsubsection*{The two Samaritan Sundays --- and who actually connects them}

The Sunday before this one carries the Good Samaritan. Two consecutive
Sundays with a Samaritan at the centre of the Gospel is the kind of pattern a
guide is tempted to build on, so the finding needs stating exactly: \textbf{no
patristic witness links them.} The pericope of the ten lepers floated between
Sundays in the medieval homiliaries --- Bruno's homily on it is headed
\latin{Dominica XII post Pentecosten} in the arrangement printed in Migne, with
an editorial note that Haymo carries the exposition at \latin{Dominica XIV}.
The one commentator who binds the two Samaritans is Guéranger, and he does it
through Rupert of Deutz. Guéranger reports that the Good Samaritan Gospel
``formerly was assigned to this present Sunday,'' and quotes the abbot: ``Every
portion of the Office of the thirteenth Sunday \dots\ bears on the history of
that Samaritan, whose name signifies keeper; it is our Lord Jesus Christ who,
by His Incarnation, comes to the rescue of man, whom the old Law was not able
to keep from harm.'' Rupert reads the whole office --- the Introit and Gradual
included --- through \latin{Samaritanus} as \latin{custos}, which is
Augustine's etymology transferred from this leper to the Good Samaritan and
thence to Christ. Rupert's own \work{De divinis officiis} was not opened for
this guide; he is cited only as Guéranger excerpts him, and that limitation is
recorded rather than smoothed over.

\subsubsection*{The last clause}

\latin{Surge, vade; quia fides tua te salvum fecit.} The nine were cleansed and
went home clean; nothing in the text takes their cleansing back. What the tenth
receives is named with a different verb, and it is named with the word the
Epistle has spent the morning arguing about: faith. Bede's distinction ---
cleansing is not salvation, and what saves is faith's working rather than its
bare acknowledgement --- is the pericope's own, drawn from its own last
sentence. Guéranger, whose treatment of the nine is frankly supersessionist in
texture and is attributed to him where cited, nonetheless ends the chapter in a
Romans 11 key, praying that the time come ``which will be so glorious for the
two peoples.'' Schuster's sentence is blunter and is likewise quoted as his
own: ``This story prefigures the future mission of the apostles, who, driven
out by the faithless people of Judea, turned away and preached with immense
success to the Samaritans and the Gentiles.'' The appointed text says
something narrower and harder than any of them. It says that one man came
back.

\subsection*{Offertory \cue{Off.}}

\elementid{In te sperávi, Dómine}{\latin{Ant. ad Offertorium} --- Ps. 30, 15-16}{1568}

\textbf{The psalm.} Psalm 30 is inscribed \latin{In finem. Psalmus David, pro
extasi}, and is a lament with an unusually strong strand of trust running
through it: the singer is besieged by fear on every side, his life is wasted
with grief, he is forgotten as one dead from the heart, and in the middle of
that he says the two lines the missal takes. The psalm's sixth verse is
\latin{In manus tuas commendo spiritum meum} --- the verse Christ dies with at
Luke 23:46, and the verse the Church says every night at Compline. The
antiphon appointed at the offering of the gifts is therefore taken from the
psalm of the commendation of the spirit, ten verses further down.

\textbf{Three divergences, one of which matters.} The antiphon drops the
psalm's opening \latin{Ego autem}, so that the trust is stated rather than
contrasted; it inverts \latin{Deus meus es tu} to \latin{Tu es Deus meus},
which the English cannot show; and it sings \latin{in mánibus tuis témpora
mea} where the Clementine reads \latin{sortes meæ}. \textit{My times} where the
Bible of the missal's own English has \textit{my lots}. This is the most
consequential single divergence in the formulary for a reader working in
English, and its direction is worth noticing: the antiphon here agrees with the
Hebrew, whose word at this point means times, against the Clementine. The
Douay cannot be quoted for it, no other registered public-domain English
carries the chant's form, and this guide supplies none. The 1862 Pustet
printing already has \latin{tempora mea}, so the reading is not a modern
artefact of the 1962 book.

\textbf{What the antiphon does at the offering.} The Mass has just heard an
argument conducted in units of four hundred and thirty years and a Gospel in
which a cure happened somewhere along a road. At the moment the gifts are
brought, the Church sings a line that hands time itself over: not my safety,
not my cause, but my times, in thy hands. The Introit's question had been about
duration --- why hast thou cast us off \latin{in finem}, unto the end --- and
the Offertory does not answer it. It surrenders the terms of it.

\subsubsection*{\latin{Sortes} or \latin{témpora}: the one divergence with a
patristic exposition on both sides}

This is the place where the reception pays for itself. Because the Clementine
reads \latin{sortes}, one would expect every Latin expositor to have expounded
\textit{lots} and the chant's \textit{times} to stand alone. That is very
nearly true, and the exception is decisive.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{St.\ Augustine expounds \latin{sortes}, and expounds them famously}
He treats this psalm four times, not three --- the pieces printed as
\latin{enarratio} I and the three sermons of \latin{enarratio} II --- and
vv.~15--16 fall in the first and the third only. \textbf{All four read
\latin{In manibus tuis sortes meae}.} In the first, which the Oxford
translators carry: ``In Thy hands are my lots. In Thy power are my lots. For I
see no desert for which out of the universal ungodliness of the human race You
have elected me particularly to salvation \dots\ yet I, from whom this is hid,
have attained by lot unto my Lord's vesture'' --- the seamless tunic of
Jn. 19:24, won by casting lots.

The third sermon, which has no public-domain English and is therefore quoted
here in Latin, is the fullest patristic exegesis of the clause, and it is an
exegesis of \emph{lots}: ``Audito nomine sortium, non debemus sortilegos
quaerere. Sors enim non aliquid mali est: sed res est in dubitatione humana
divinam indicans voluntatem'' --- the apostles casting for Matthias --- and
then: ``Sortes dixit, quantum ego existimo, gratiam qua salvi facti sumus.
Quare sortis nomine appellat gratiam Dei? \emph{Quia in sorte non est electio,
sed voluntas Dei} \dots\ sorte voluntatis suae nos salvos fecit, quia voluit,
non quia digni fuimus.'' Grace is called a lot because in a lot there is no
choosing, only God's will. It is a magnificent reading, and this Sunday does
not sing the word it depends on.

One smaller thing his lemma does share with the chant: he reads \latin{dixi: Tu
es Deus meus}, in the antiphon's word order, not the Clementine's \latin{Deus
meus es tu}.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Cassiodorus's lemma \emph{is} the antiphon, and his exposition turns on
its word}
``Dixi, tu es Deus meus; in manibus tuis \textbf{tempora mea}.'' The Offertory's
reading, word for word, including the word order. And the exposition cannot be
transposed to \latin{sortes}, because it hangs on time: for Cassiodorus the
whole psalm is the voice of Christ --- ``per universum psalmum verba sunt
Domini Salvatoris'' --- and here he speaks ``a parte scilicet humanitatis
assumptae, quae, sicut ait inferius, et tempori subiacebat et morti. Non enim,
quemadmodum inimici putabant, eorum persecutione vitam suam finiendam esse
commemorat, sed in potestate ponit Domini \latin{tempora vitae suae}.'' The
assumed humanity was subject to time and to death; the times of his life are
placed in the Father's power, not in his enemies'. Nine verses earlier in the
same psalm, on \latin{In manus tuas commendo spiritum meum}, Cassiodorus had
already noted that these are the words the Gospel text speaks from the cross.
The hands into which the Church puts its times at the Offertory are, on his
reading, the hands into which the dying Christ commends his spirit.
\end{witnessnote}

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{And a cardinal cites the missal itself as the witness for the word}
The decisive note is Bellarmine's, and it is one of the passages his English
translator cut. On this verse he writes that \latin{sortes} means the lots of
life and death, which hang not on chance but on God's hand; and then: ``In
hebraeo habetur, in manibus tuis \emph{tempora} mea; \& fortasse quod nunc
legimus in graeco, \latin{kleroi}, quod significat sortes, legendum esset,
\latin{kairoi}, quod significat tempora. nam non desunt antiquae versiones ex
graeco, quae habeant, tempora mea. \textbf{Sic certè legitur in officio Missae
non semel, \& in Psalterio Ecclesiae S.\ Petri Romae.}'' The Hebrew reads
\textit{my times}; the Greek word for lots should perhaps be read as the Greek
word for times; ancient Latin versions made from the Greek have \textit{tempora
mea}; and --- this is the sentence --- it is certainly so read \emph{more than
once in the Office of the Mass, and in the Psalter of the Church of St Peter at
Rome}. A seventeenth-century cardinal, asked where the reading comes from,
points at the Mass propers themselves and at the psalter of the Vatican
basilica. He adds that the sense is the same either way: ``non viuam, nisi
quamdiu tu volueris, neque moriar, nisi quando tu volueris.''

The published English of this commentary says none of it. At the same verse
O'Sullivan gives only ``My life does not depend on lot or chance, but depends
on your will and power \dots\ The meaning is quite plain, and needs no
explanation'' --- which is faithful to the exposition and silent about the
text, because his translation announces that it omits ``those portions which
are purely philological, or which relate to the discrepancy and reconciliation
of the texts and versions.'' The one commentator who names this Sunday's
Offertory as a textual witness is a commentator whose only public-domain
English removed the passage where he does it.
\end{witnessnote}

Two further witnesses close the question. Jerome's \work{Psalterium iuxta
Hebraeos}, his translation from the Hebrew, reads \latin{in manu tua tempora
mea} --- singular hand, but \textit{times} --- so the appointed antiphon is a
hybrid of the two traditions: the Septuagint's plural \latin{in mánibus tuis}
with the Hebrew's \latin{témpora}, which is exactly Cassiodorus's text and not
exactly Jerome's. The medieval Psalter gloss keeps both readings on the page at
once, and attributes the alternative to the right man: ``SORTES. CASSIODORUS.
Vel tempora vite. AUGUSTINUS. quia quando vis vivimus vel morimur ideo eripe.''
And Theodoret, reading in Greek, names the variant and refuses to choose
between them: ``in thy hands are my lots,'' he quotes, ``or,
as the rest of the translators have it, in thy hands are my \emph{times}'' ---
the rest being Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion against the Septuagint --- and
he then expounds the verse on both at once. \textit{Times}, or \textit{lots},
he says, means the changes of circumstance: wealth and poverty, slavery and
mastery, peace and war, and the rest of such things, which God allots to each
as he wills and changes again as he sees fit. He is the earliest of three witnesses ---
with the Glossa and Bellarmine --- who hold both of the Offertory's readings in
view, and together they are the warrant for what this guide says: that the
chant's \latin{témpora mea} is a real reading with real ancestry, and that
Augustine's \latin{sortes} is not thereby wrong. Only Augustine is committed to
\latin{sortes} alone, and only Cassiodorus to \latin{témpora} alone.

\textbf{Two negatives and one flagged cross-reference.} Jerome wrote no homily
and no \latin{commentariolus} on this psalm at all --- the psalm is skipped in
both series, verified by listing every psalm heading in the three volumes ---
so his \work{iuxta Hebraeos} rendering is a translation and not an exposition.
Guéranger prints this Offertory without comment; Schuster comments briefly and
records that the antiphon is shared with the Tuesday after the First Sunday of
Lent. And one contact with the day's Gospel occurs inside this psalm at a verse
the antiphon does not sing: expounding v.~3, \latin{esto mihi \dots\ in domum
refugii}, Augustine brings in the Good Samaritan and writes ``Samarites enim
custos interpretatur \dots\ sic intellegi volens nostrum se esse custodem.''
The etymology on which this Sunday's Gospel reception rests occurs in
Augustine's exposition of the Offertory's own psalm, attached to the word
\latin{refugium}, which is the Alleluia's word. It is v.~3, not vv.~15--16, and
it is cited here only as a cross-reference.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{The negative that governs all three chants}
No witness read for this guide connects any of the three appointed psalms to
the Gospel's ten lepers or to its thanksgiving. The Latin texts of Augustine's
six relevant expositions and Cassiodorus's three were counted for the stems
\textit{lepr-}, \textit{samarit-} and \textit{gratias ag-}, and every hit was
inspected: the leprosy hits are the Moses'-hand typology at Ps. 73:11 in both
authors --- where Augustine writes ``Albor enim in cute, lepra est, non
candor'' and Cassiodorus turns the hand restored to the bosom into a Romans 11
hope --- and the Samaritan hits are the Good Samaritan at Ps. 30:3 and John
8:48. \latin{Decem leprosi} occurs nowhere in any of the nine texts. On the
Greek side the result is cleaner still: the stem for leprosy occurs
\textbf{zero} times in the whole of Theodoret's commentary on all hundred and
fifty psalms. The chants and the Gospel share a Sunday and, in the checked
reception, nothing else --- which is exactly why the cross-proper readings this
guide offers are labelled as proposals where they are not documented.
\end{witnessnote}

\subsection*{Secret \cue{Sec.}}

\elementid{Propitiáre, Dómine, pópulo tuo, propitiáre munéribus}{\latin{Secreta}}{1569}

\textbf{The doubled imperative is the prayer, and it is a textual fact with a
history.} In the Vatican Gelasian the prayer stands in Book III, in a
penitential Mass beginning \latin{Parce, Domine, parce peccatis nostris}
(Wilson's edition, printed p.~249), and the Vatican manuscript reads
\latin{Propitiare, Domine, populi tui propitiatus muneribus} --- one imperative
and a participle. Wilson's apparatus records that \latin{populo tuo,
propitiare} is the reading of the Rheinau and S.\ Gallen manuscripts at first
hand and of Pamelius. The received doubled-imperative form is therefore the
eighth-century Frankish reading, and it is the form the Gregorian supplement
carries at Reg.\ 337 (printed p.~173) and the form the 1962 book prints. The
prayer says \latin{propitiáre} twice --- once over the people, once over the
gifts --- and what the doubling does is refuse to let the offering stand as a
transaction: God is asked to be appeased toward the offerers before he is asked
to be appeased by what they offer.

\textbf{A bounded negative.} The prayer is not in the Verona collection. All
thirteen occurrences of \latin{propitiare} in Feltoe's edition were inspected;
the nearest verbal parallel, \latin{collatis in me per gratiam tuam propitiare
muneribus}, is a different prayer in a bishop's own Mass and is not this one.
So the Secret, unlike the Collect and the Postcommunion, has no Veronese
ancestry that this search could find.

\textbf{What it asks for, and where the English falls short.} \latin{Ut, hac
oblatióne placátus, et indulgéntiam nobis tríbuas, et postuláta concédas}:
appeased by this oblation, grant both pardon and the things asked. The two
\latin{et} clauses are parallel and the second is open --- \latin{postuláta},
the things that have been asked, without saying which. On this Sunday the
things that have been asked are on record: an increase of faith, hope and
charity. The 1861 English keeps the first \latin{propitiáre} and turns the
second into ``mercifully receive,'' so the doubling disappears; the argument
above is therefore conducted on the Latin. Guéranger reads the Secret's two
petitions as continuing the Collect's promise-theme, and Schuster analyses its
order --- ``First comes the prayer of propitiation \dots\ Next we pray for the
fruits of the Eucharist.'' Both are later commentary on a prayer neither wrote,
and are cited as such.

\subsection*{Communion \cue{Comm.}}

\elementid{Panem de cælo dedísti nobis}{\latin{Ant. ad Communionem} --- Sap. 16, 20}{1570}

\textbf{The verse in its book.} Wisdom 16 belongs to the long meditation on the
Exodus with which the book closes, in which each plague on Egypt is set against
a corresponding mercy to Israel. Verse 20 is the mercy that answers the
Egyptian plague of vermin: instead of those things, God fed his people with the
food of angels and gave them bread from heaven prepared without labour, having
in it all delight and the sweetness of every taste. The next verse explains the
last clause: the substance ``deserviens uniuscujusque voluntati, ad quod quisque
volebat, convertebatur'' --- it turned to whatever each one wished. That is the
image the antiphon takes to the altar rail: not merely bread, but bread that
becomes what each person needs it to be.

\textbf{The antiphon is an adaptation, and it is worth being exact about how.}
The Clementine's clause is third-person and past: \latin{parátum panem de cælo
præstitísti illis sine labóre}, thou gavest \textit{them} bread from heaven.
The antiphon makes it a first-person-plural address --- \latin{dedísti nobis,
Dómine} --- adding a vocative the verse does not have, and replacing the verse's
verb \latin{præstitísti} with \latin{dedísti}, which is the verb of Psalm 77:24,
\latin{panem cæli dedit eis}: the manna psalm that the Wisdom verse is itself
reworking. The two closing phrases are recast as well: \latin{omne
delectaméntum in se habéntem} loses its \latin{in se}, and \latin{omnis sapóris
suavitátem} is inverted to \latin{omnem sapórem suavitátis}. Wisdom's memory
of what God did for another generation has been turned into a statement about
what he has just done for this one.

\textbf{This antiphon has been attached to this Mass since the ninth century.}
The Gregorian supplement manuscript Vat.\ Reg.\ 337 carries, in its margins at
\latin{Dominica XIIII post Pentecosten}, a set of antiphonary cues written by a
different scribe at the same time and place as the text, in the first half of
the ninth century: \latin{Ant. Respice domine in testamentum. Resp. Respice
domine. Off. In te speraui domine. Com. Panem de caelo.} Introit, Gradual,
Offertory and Communion --- the whole chant-set of the 1962 formulary, on the
same Mass as the Collect, Secret and Postcommunion, eleven hundred years ago.
The Sunday's number has moved twice since; its texts have not.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{Rabanus Maurus, \work{Commentaria in librum Sapientiae} III.9 (PL 109,
coll.\ 748--749)}
The direct Eucharistic reading of the verse, from the ninth century, under a
chapter heading that keeps the literal sense first: \latin{Praedicat Deum
dedisse populo Israeli timenti se escam delectamenti}. Rabanus expounds the
manna literally --- ``cibum manna coelitus datum suavissimum ac saluberrimum''
--- and then turns: ``Mystice autem \dots\ in coelesti cibo consolatio superna
\dots\ significatur. Aliter manna significat panem illum, qui in Evangelio ait:
Ego sum panis vitae qui de coelo descendi \dots\ Quicunque ergo ad hujus panis
participationem veraciter pervenit, procul dubio vitam aeternam sine fine
possidebit.'' The verse the missal sings at Communion was being read as John 6
by a commentator writing in the same generation as the manuscript that first
attaches it to this Sunday.
\end{witnessnote}

\textbf{The versicle's other life.} This verse is not only a Communion
antiphon. In the \latin{Rituale Romanum} it is the versicle and response of
Benediction of the Blessed Sacrament --- \latin{℣. Panem de coelo praestitisti
eis. ℟. Omne delectamentum in se habentem} --- followed by \latin{Deus qui
nobis sub Sacramento mirabili}; it is used, with the singular
\latin{praestitisti ei}, at the Communion of the sick; and the Corpus Christi
procession carries the same versicle after the \latin{Tantum ergo}, extended by
rubric to all processions with the Sacrament. The Corpus Christi office takes
Wisdom 16:20 verbatim as an antiphon at Terce. The consequence for this guide
is practical: the words a modern Catholic is likeliest to have heard are the
Benediction form, and the Benediction form is not the Communion antiphon's
form. Both descend from the same verse; the missal's Communion has adapted it
and the Rituale's versicle has not.

\begin{witnessnote}
\wlocus{An honest negative on Ambrose}
\work{De mysteriis} 8.44--49 is the classic patristic manna-and-Eucharist
passage, and it is built on Psalm 77:24--25, not on this verse: ``all those who
ate that food died in the wilderness, but that food which you receive \dots\ is
the Body of Christ.'' The wording of Wisdom 16:20, \latin{omne delectamentum},
does not occur in it. Ambrose belongs to this antiphon's theological
background and is not a witness to its text; the distinction is kept.
\end{witnessnote}

Later commentary takes the every-taste clause exactly where the antiphon
points it. Schuster: the Eucharist is bread with the sweetness of every taste
``because in it we receive not just one particular grace alone, but the author
of all grace himself, and because, since it produces its effects according to
the dispositions of those who receive it, it is wonderfully adapted to all
their needs and desires.'' Guéranger, on this Sunday, asks that we ``sing all
the more fervently in our Communion-anthem the divine sweetness of this true
Bread of heaven.'' Both are quoted as their own commentary, not as
documentation of what the antiphon's compiler intended.

\elementguard
\subsection*{Postcommunion \cue{Postcomm.}}

\elementid{Sumptis, Dómine, cæléstibus sacraméntis}{\latin{Postcommunio}}{1571}

\textbf{It has travelled with the Collect since before either had a Sunday.} In
the Vatican Gelasian this prayer closes the very Mass whose Collect opens the
day --- Book III, the ninth of the sixteen unnumbered Sunday Masses, Wilson's
printed p.~229 --- and in the Gregorian supplement the two stand together again
at \latin{Dominica XIIII}. The pairing is therefore not a Tridentine
arrangement; it is a fact about the prayers older than the number attached to
them.

\textbf{One word of the text is a real variant.} Wilson prints
\latin{proficiant} at Book III.ix from the Rheinau manuscript, notes that the
Vatican manuscript and one S.\ Gallen hand read \latin{proficiat}, and records
that a second S.\ Gallen hand, Gerbert and Pamelius read \latin{proficiamus}
--- ``which gives a clearer sense.'' The Vatican manuscript itself has
\latin{proficiamus} at the prayer's two reuses in Book II. The difference is
the difference the 1861 English also lost: \latin{proficiat} or
\latin{proficiant} makes the sacrament advance the redemption;
\latin{proficiamus} makes \textit{us} advance toward the increase. The 1962
book prints \latin{proficiámus}, and the Gregorian supplement prints it too.
The prayer as the missal has it asks the communicants to move, not the
mysteries.

\textbf{Two reuses that show what the prayer was thought to be for.} The
Gelasian's Book II fits it out with intercession clauses on two feasts: at St
Hippolytus, \latin{Sumptis, Domine, sacramentis \dots\ intercedente beato
martyre tuo Ypolito ad redemptionis aeternae proficiamus augmentum}; and at the
Nativity of the Blessed Virgin, similarly. A prayer general enough to take an
intercession clause and remain itself is a prayer about the ordinary effect of
communion, which is what it is being asked to do here. Schuster reports a
further cross-reference --- that this Postcommunion is also that of the Monday
after the Fourth Sunday in Lent --- and that statement is reported as his,
having not been independently collated in a missal scan for this guide. Like
the Secret, the prayer is not in Feltoe's Verona collection; variant searches
were negative.

\textbf{\latin{Augméntum}, again.} The day's first prayer asked for an
\latin{augméntum} of faith, hope and charity. Its last prayer asks that, having
received the heavenly mysteries, we may advance \latin{ad redemptiónis ætérnæ
\dots\ augméntum}. The genitive phrase is split around \latin{quæsumus} in the
printed text, which throws weight onto the noun at the end of the line. What
was asked at the beginning for the three capacities is asked at the end for
their whole object. The 1861 English drops the noun entirely --- ``advance our
eternal redemption'' --- so the bracket is invisible to a reader working from
the registered translation, and it is stated here on the Latin. Whether anyone
composed the two prayers to answer each other is not something this guide
knows: they were already a pair in the Gelasian, centuries before this Gospel
was fixed to this Sunday, so the bracket is a fact of the received formulary
and not a recovered intention.
