\section*{Scriptural Date and Location}

Six passages are directly appointed. Each has one dossier: a four-field
summary, the narrated event where there is one, then a short explanation.
Composed orations are not listed; their biblical echoes are not quotations.
Psalm citations give the missal's Vulgate number with the English-Bible
equivalent; the appendix adds the Masoretic series.

\begin{dossiertable}
Offertory & Ps. 30:15--16 Vulg. (Eng. 31:14--15) & Superscription \latin{In finem. Psalmus David, pro extasi.} No episode or place named & Traditional: Davidic, \textit{c.}~1000 BC\\
\cmidrule(lr){1-4}
\dossierprose{The title claims David and nothing else; no narrative anchors it. The 1910 \work{Catholic Encyclopedia} defends the titles as ancient and canonical while conceding many are obscure; the NABRE reads the psalm as a lament with a strong emphasis on trust. Its v.~6 is \latin{In manus tuas commendo spiritum meum}, the verse Christ dies with at Lk.\ 23:46 --- so the antiphon's surrender of \latin{témpora mea} is sung from a psalm the Passion made its own.}
\midrule
Introit, Gradual & Ps. 73:1, 19--20, 22--23 Vulg. (Eng. 74:1, 19--20, 22--23) & Superscription \latin{Intellectus Asaph.} The psalm laments a ravaged sanctuary; no place of writing named & Traditional: Asaphite; modern: after 587 BC\\
\cmidrule(lr){1-4}
\dossierprose{Asaph is David's chief musician, so the bare title suggests a Davidic-era horizon --- yet the psalm mourns a sanctuary already destroyed. The NABRE calls it ``a communal lament sung when the enemy invaded the temple; it would be especially appropriate at the destruction of Jerusalem in 587 B.C.'' Witnesses since antiquity have resolved the tension as prophecy or as a later Asaphite's voice; this sheet reports it unresolved.}
\midrule
Alleluia & Ps. 89:1 Vulg. (Eng. 90:1) & Superscription \latin{Oratio Moysi, hominis Dei.} No place named & Traditional: Mosaic, \textit{c.}~13th c. BC\\
\cmidrule(lr){1-4}
\dossierprose{The one psalm in the Psalter bearing Moses' name. A title associating a prayer with a figure is received tradition, not proof of composition; the NABRE reads it as a communal lament naming its distress only in general terms, contrasting God's eternity with human brevity. The chant takes exactly the clause that carries that contrast: refuge \latin{a generatióne et progénie}.}
\midrule
Communion & Wis. 16:20 & Written in Greek at Alexandria, in Egypt, by an unknown Jew of that community & \textit{c.}~100 BC; older range 221--117 BC\\
\cmidrule(lr){1-4}
\dossierprose{Solomonic attribution is the book's own literary fiction, set aside on all sides. The \work{Catholic Encyclopedia} places the author under Ptolemy IV (221--204 BC) or Ptolemy VII (145--117 BC); the NABRE says ``about a hundred years before the coming of Christ,'' written ``in Greek, in a style patterned on that of Hebrew verse'' to edify an oppressed community. The verse remembers the wilderness manna --- narrated event Ex.\ 16, in the Sinai desert --- from Hellenistic Egypt, a millennium later.}
\midrule
Gospel & Lk. 17:11--19 & Writing: place unknown; traditionally Achaia. Audience largely Gentile and non-Palestinian, through Theophilus & Traditional: by AD 62--63. Modern: AD 80--90\\
\cmidrule(lr){1-4}
\dossierevent{Event: on the final journey to Jerusalem, ``through the midst of Samaria and Galilee'' (v.~11) --- the border country in the north of present-day Israel and the West Bank; an unnamed village. Months before the Passover of the Passion: AD 29 on the \work{Catholic Encyclopedia}'s computation, commonly AD 30--33 later; not settled here.}
\cmidrule(lr){1-4}
\dossierprose{The \work{Catholic Encyclopedia} identifies Luke the physician and Pauline companion, reports Julius Africanus for Achaia, and argues the Gospel was finished before Acts, by about AD 62--63; the NABRE reports the same attribution --- ``Luke, a Syrian from Antioch'' --- but proposes AD 80--90 and leaves the place unstated.}
\midrule
Epistle & Gal. 3:16--22 & From Ephesus (NABRE) or Antioch (older South-Galatian view), to the churches of Galatia in central Asia Minor, around present-day Ankara & Third journey, AD 54--55; or AD 48--50\\
\cmidrule(lr){1-4}
\dossierprose{Pauline authorship is traditional and the mainstream modern judgment alike. Who the Galatians were is not settled: the NABRE holds them ``most likely among the descendants of Celts'' of North Galatia and dates the letter ``around A.D. 54 or 55 \dots\ most likely from Ephesus''; the \work{Catholic Encyclopedia} favours the South-Galatian theory, under which the letter goes to the churches of the first journey, perhaps from Antioch before the council, AD 48--50. Occasion on either view: converts from paganism pressed by judaizing missionaries to add circumcision and the law --- the pressure the appointed argument answers.}
\end{dossiertable}
