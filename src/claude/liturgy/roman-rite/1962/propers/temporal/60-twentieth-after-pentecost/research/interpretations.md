`liturgy/roman-rite/1962/propers/temporal/60-twentieth-after-pentecost`, written
on 2026-10-07 by the research stage of `proper-study` v9, run

## The rules this record keeps

- **The formulary is a compilation.** Every reading of the whole Mass is the
  editor's synthesis. Each Father is credited only with what he said of his own
  passage. Every cross-element connection below is marked **[synthesis]**
  unless a named witness made it, in which case the witness is named and the
  locus given.
- **Evidence classes.** [text] a textual observation in the appointed words
  (scope § 1.1); [unsung] an observation in the unsung context of an appointed
  verse (scope § 1.2); [reception] what a checked witness says of a passage;
  [compilation] what a liturgical commentator says of the Mass as his books gave
  it; [synthesis] the editor's connection.
- **Commentators whose Mass had another Gospel** (Berno, Rupert, Honorius,
  Sicard, Durandus) are cited only for the chants, Epistle and Collect they
  share with this formulary, never for a link to Jn 4:46–53 (scope § 2.1).
  Fromage (*The Liturgical Year*) and Bl. Ildefonso Schuster expound this
  formulary with this Gospel and may be cited for links they themselves make.
- **Carrying authors.** Each reading names two persons who carry it, both
  eligible under `src/sources/inventories/author-standing-v1.toml`, at least one
  a Father or canonized saint, each with a developed contribution to the
  reading's controlling claim.
- **The commemoration.** The Maternity orations are said in a low or conventual
  Mass on 11 October 2026 and omitted in a sung Mass that is not conventual. They
  are not part of the Sunday's formulary and no reading depends on them (§ 6).

## 1. `word-at-a-distance` — The word that heals from afar: faith taught to rest on Christ's word

### 1.1 Controlling claim

The Gospel turns on one exchange. A father asks Christ to come down before his
son dies; Christ rebukes a faith that waits on signs, gives a word instead of
his presence — *Vade, fílius tuus vivit* — and the father believes the word and
goes. The Fathers read the exchange as Christ teaching faith to rest on his word,
because the one who speaks is God present everywhere and heals by command what
he made by will (Gregory), and as faith growing in the man from need, through
trust in the word, to the belief of his whole house (Chrysostom; Aquinas; Cyril:
"the one command of the Saviour healeth two souls"). **[synthesis]** Read with
that Gospel, the formulary is a school of the same faith: the Introit and Collect
ask pardon and peace of a God whose judgments are true; the Epistle asks the
understanding of God's will; the Gradual and Communion pray as men who hope in a
word and wait for food *in témpore opportúno*; the Secret asks the heavenly
medicine of mysteries in which Christ is present without being seen; the
Postcommunion asks obedience to the commandments the Introit confessed broken.
The Mass places the faithful where the father stood: asking for life, given a
word, sent away to find it true.

**Exegetical question.** What faith does Christ ask of the father, and why does
he heal by a word without going down?

**The inference that answers it.** Gregory: the father sought Christ's bodily
presence *qui per spiritum nusquam deerat*; had he believed perfectly he would
have known there is no place where God is not; Christ healed *solo jussu*, by
command only, he *qui voluntate omnia creavit*, showing he is not absent where
he is invited (*Hom.* 28.1; the English is a description of the Latin, not a
translation). The word is enough because of who speaks it.

**Distinguishing consequence.** Faith is defined by its object, the word of One
who is everywhere; the absence of bodily presence and of signs is not a lack but
the condition of the faith that saves. Applied to the formulary [synthesis]: the
sacramental "medicine" of the Secret and the hope in God's word of the
Communion are the present form of the father's situation.

### 1.2 Principal witnesses and their reasoning

- **St Gregory the Great** (*Hom. in Ev.* 28, PL 76 cols. 1210–1213; scope
  § 3.6). Carrying author. Three moves. (1) The man asked salvation, so he
  believed — one does not ask salvation of one not believed a saviour — but he
  doubted, asking Christ to come down; he gave honour to bodily presence and not
  to majesty. (2) Christ would not go in body to the ruler's son but offered to
  go to the centurion's slave: our pride, which honours wealth and rank in men
  rather than the nature made in God's image, is rebuked; *Ecce de coelo venit
  qui servo in terra occurrere non despicit*. (3) *Nolite … pensare quod
  habetis, sed quid estis*; the world that is loved is passing; at the martyrs'
  tomb, *jam mundus in seipso aruit, et adhuc in cordibus nostris floret … ut
  ipse nos jam mundus mittat ad Deum*. Gregory supplies the reading's centre:
  the Lord sought in bodily presence *qui per spiritum nusquam deerat*, and
  healing by command. What Gregory means by *per spiritum* set against
  *corporalem praesentiam* is left in his Latin; the records do not settle it
  as the Holy Spirit.
- **St John Chrysostom** (*Hom. 35 in Io.*, NPNF1-14 pp. 123–125; scope
  § 3.6). Carrying author. The coming was a mark of faith, but "he believed not
  entirely or soundly", as his asking the hour shows; Christ "healeth the
  father, sick in mind, no less than the son, in order to persuade us to give
  heed to Him, not by reason of His miracles, but of His teaching"; "miracles are
  not for the faithful, but for the unbelieving"; the ruler is not Matthew's
  centurion, whose faith was perfected, which is why Christ offered to go there
  and not here; the lesson, "Not to wait for miracles", and to give thanks in a
  child's sickness as in health. Chrysostom supplies the double healing — the
  father's mind with the son's body — and the moral.
- **St Augustine** (*Tract. in Io.* 16.3–5; scope § 3.6). Contributing. The
  ruler "had not yet believed" when he asked; the Samaritans believed the word
  without signs; "we have seen no signs, none do we demand"; Thomas, rebuked as
  the ruler was, and "Blessed are they that have not seen". "The centurion
  declared himself unworthy of His presence" and "believed me able to work by a
  word", while "the ruler sought His presence by force": "Here is a ceding to
  loftiness; there, a conceding to humility."
- **St Thomas Aquinas** (*Super Io.* c. 4 l. 7; scope § 3.6). Contributing. The
  ruler's two defects of faith; *Vade* as the disposition to grace through the
  free movement of the will; *Credidit … et ibat proficiendo in fide*; *in via Dei
  non proficere, deficere est*; the faith that grows by stages to perfect day
  (Prov 4:18); the seventh hour as the sevenfold Spirit by whom sins are
  remitted. Aquinas gathers Gregory's and Chrysostom's reasons and sets them
  side by side.
- **St Cyril of Alexandria** (*Comm. in Io.* II.5; scope § 3.6). Contributing;
  no row in the standing registry, so not a carrying author. The ruler came to
  "One able to heal" but did not yet understand "that He is by Nature God";
  Christ "doth not reject our lack of apprehension; but benefiteth even the
  stumbling"; "in *Go thy way* is Faith"; "the one command of the Saviour
  healeth two souls".
- **St Augustine on Ps 144:15** and **St Hilary on Ps 118 *Zain*** (scope
  §§ 3.4, 3.9). Contributing for the chants: God gives food "as when thou
  refreshest a sick man in due season … thou delayest, not deniest"; God's every
  word calls us to hope in heavenly goods, and the soul holds his words
  *tamquam pabulum aliquod vitae* (Hilary).
- **St Hilary** and **Cassiodorus** on Ps 118:1, the Introit verse (scope
  § 3.1). Contributing for that verse only. Hilary: the way in which the
  undefiled walk is Christ, *Ego sum via*, and it must be not only entered but
  travelled to its end, since no one on the road yet holds what it leads to
  (*non solum ineunda … sed etiam pergenda*, *Aleph* 2). Cassiodorus: the
  *via* is the Lord Saviour, walked with the steps of good action. Neither
  speaks of the Gospel; the join is the editor's (§ 1.3).
- **St Robert Bellarmine** on Ps 118:49 (scope § 3.9). Contributing: God wills
  to be asked to keep his promise; "prayer becomes one of the means through which
  God decreed to fulfill his promises" — the father's petition in the Communion's
  words.
- **St Anthony of Padua** (Sunday sermon on Jn 4:46–53, Spilsbury English;
  scope § 3.6). Contributing: "he first believed, and then went, because the
  belief of the heart comes first, and afterwards the going in action"; the Lord
  "healed by his mere command, to show that he was not absent from the place he
  was invited to". Described, not quoted at length (restricted translation).
- **Fromage** (*The Liturgical Year* XI, pp. 448–451; ecclesiastical writer,
  documented reception of the compilation). "Our Jesus has no need to come down
  from heaven to earth, in order to give efficiency to the commands of his
  gracious will"; "The whole power of the God, who, with a word, cures both soul
  and body, resides in the Mysteries" (on the Secret). Fromage is the only witness
  read who joins the Gospel to the Secret; the join is his.
- **Bl. Ildefonso Schuster** (*The Sacramentary* III, pp. 176–177; Blessed).
  Contributing: Christ required the ruler "to believe in his word without
  actually beholding the cure"; the Secret asks the Eucharist as "spiritual
  medicine"; the Gradual "has a marked eucharistic significance". Schuster
  differs from Augustine on the ruler's initial faith (§ 1.6).

### 1.4 The four senses

- **Literal.** A royal official from Capharnaum comes to Jesus at Cana, asks him
  to come and heal his dying son, is rebuked for seeking signs, receives a word,
  believes it, and on the road learns that the fever left the boy at the hour
  the word was spoken; he and his household believe (Jn 4:46–53). The Fathers
  read the rebuke as addressed to faith that wanted presence and proof.
- **Allegorical.** Christ, present everywhere by his divinity and absent in body
  since the Ascension, heals by his word; the Church receives his power through
  word and sacrament without seeing him (Gregory: *per spiritum nusquam deerat*;
  [synthesis] with the Secret's *medicínam*). The seventh hour is the sevenfold
  Spirit by whom sins are remitted (Aquinas; Anthony after the Gloss).
- **Moral.** Believe the word before you see; go on the strength of it; grow from
  need to trust to the faith of a whole house (Aquinas's stages; Chrysostom:
  "not to wait for miracles"). Honour in others the image of God rather than
  rank, as Christ would go to a slave and not to a ruler's son (Gregory).
- **Anagogical.** The father's journey home, believing what he has not yet seen,
  ends in seeing: faith gives way to sight when the promise is fulfilled
  (Augustine on Ps 118:49, "fulfil Thy promise"; Hilary, every word of God calls
  us to hope of heavenly goods). The word *Fílius tuus vivit* points to the life
  of the resurrection.

### 1.5 Strongest difficulty and alternative

- **The rebuke seems cruel to a desperate father.** Chrysostom, Cyril and
  Aquinas answer that it served him: Chrysostom, it "toucheth his conscience" and heals the father's
  mind with the son's body; Cyril, Christ "benefiteth even the stumbling";
  Aquinas, *Nisi signa* is directed at one instructed in the law who sought signs
  rather than Scripture. The studies should not soften the rebuke into
  encouragement; Augustine reads it as exposing a man "of no faith at all".
- **Does the reading make signs worthless?** No witness says so: Gregory, in
  *Hom. in Ev.* 4.3 (Wikisource Latin, line 49), says visible miracles were
  given to draw hearts to faith in what is invisible and that *linguae in signum
  sunt non fidelibus, sed infidelibus*; Chrysostom, "miracles are … for the
  unbelieving". The reading's claim is about what faith
  rests on, not about whether signs occur.
- **Alternative.** The Gospel can be read with reading 3 as a figure of peoples
  rather than of one man's faith (§ 3). The two are compatible at different
  levels — Aquinas gives both the literal-moral and the allegorical — but reading
  1 does not depend on any identification of the ruler.

### 1.6 Agreements and differences within the reading

- **Did the ruler believe before the word?** Augustine: not yet. Gregory: yes,
  but doubting. Chrysostom: not entirely or soundly. Cyril: feebly, as a child.
  Schuster: he had faith, to be purified. Aquinas: two defects, growing to
  perfection. Augustine, Chrysostom, Cyril and Aquinas place a fuller faith
  after the word; Gregory does not speak of its completion. The studies keep the
  difference visible and credit only those four with the growth (scope § 4.1).
- **Why go to the centurion and not to the ruler?** Gregory: to rebuke pride
  that honours rank. Chrysostom: because the centurion's faith was perfected and
  the ruler's was not. Aquinas gives both. A difference of reason, not of fact.
- **Is the ruler the centurion?** Chrysostom and Aquinas: no; Augustine and
  Gregory compare without identifying (scope § 4.7).

## 2. `songs-of-sion-in-babylon` — The song of the exiles: confession, humility and hope by the rivers of Babylon

### 2.1 Controlling claim

Two of the formulary's chants are songs of exile: the Introit is cut from
Azariah's prayer in the Babylonian furnace, the Offertory from the captives'
lament by Babylon's rivers. The Fathers read both as the present condition of
God's people: the Church's citizens are captives in a world that flows past
like Babylon's rivers, and are to sit by them in humility, not plunge in, keeping
the memory and the song of Sion (Augustine, Hilary, Cassiodorus, Bellarmine on
Ps 136); the exiles' first word is to confess that God's judgments are true and
to accept what befalls them as just (Jerome on Dan 3:26; Augustine on Ps
144:17); the way back is the one the Introit verse blesses, the law of the
Lord, against the roads by which all seek a false beatitude (Augustine and
Hilary on Ps 118:1); and their hope in humiliation is the promise of God's word
(Augustine and Hilary on Ps 118:49–50). **[synthesis]** The formulary sets these beside
an Epistle that tells the same people, in evil days, to redeem the time and to
sing in their hearts — *cantántes, et psalléntes* — and an Alleluia whose heart
is ready to sing — *cantábo, et psallam*. The Mass answers the exiles'
question, *Quomodo cantabimus canticum Domini in terra aliena?*, with a
song of the heart that confesses, gives thanks, and waits.

**Exegetical question.** How are people who have sinned and live in exile to
pray, sing and spend their time?

**The inference that answers it.** Augustine on Ps 136: the waters of Babylon
are "all things which here are loved, and pass away"; the citizens of Jerusalem,
"understanding their captivity", neither plunge into the waters nor stop singing,
but sit by them and weep in the memory of Sion, and must "not only sing but do"
— *oportet ut non tantum ista decantemus, sed et faciamus* — ending *vobis
invicem cantate cantica Sion* (PL 37 col. 1774). Exile is the condition, memory
of Sion the orientation, the song of the heart and of deeds the response.

**Distinguishing consequence.** The reading's centre is not the Gospel but the
chants and Epistle; it governs how the faithful hold their present life
(humility, confession, thanksgiving, song) and their longing for the heavenly
Jerusalem. The Gospel enters as one more exile's crisis (§ 2.3).

### 2.2 Principal witnesses and their reasoning

- **St Augustine** (*Enarr.* 136; 144.19–21; 118 s. 15; 56.13; scope §§ 3.1,
  3.4, 3.5, 3.7, 3.9). Carrying author. On Ps 136: Babylon's rivers are passing
  loves; sit by them, humbly, weep in remembrance of Sion; the instruments are
  the Scriptures and promises, hung on barren willows because those men are
  unfit to bear them; the captors are the devil and his angels; Babylon's little
  ones are evil desires, to be dashed against the Rock, Christ; "sing one to
  another songs of Sion". On Ps 144:15–17: God feeds as a physician feeds the
  sick, at the due time; the saints first praise God's justice when scourged,
  "So did Daniel ask". On Ps 118:49–50: "remember" asks the fulfilment of a
  promise; the humiliation is tribulation, and this hope "was given to Christ's
  Body, that is, to the Church, that it might be a comfort to Her in her
  humiliation". On Ps 56:8: the prepared heart "glorieth in tribulations". On
  Ps 118:1, the Introit verse (*Enarr.* 118 s. 1.1, PL 37 col. 1502): all want
  beatitude, few want the way without which it is not reached — *si ergo vis
  esse beatus, esto immaculatus … ubi erit quisque immaculatus, nisi in via? In
  qua via, nisi in lege Domini?* — and the verse is the divine voice calling
  back those who seek a false beatitude by evil roads: *Quo itis? Peritis, et
  nescitis*.
- **St Hilary of Poitiers** (*Tract.* 136; 144; 118 *Zain*; scope §§ 3.4, 3.7,
  3.9). Carrying author. Ps 136 speaks of a spiritual captivity: Sion is the
  seat of eternal beatitude and our heavenly mother Jerusalem; we are exiles
  since Adam's sin; the rivers are the works of the age that flow by; the
  willows, which revive in water, are the saints, revived by the word and
  baptism; our instruments are our bodies, hung up in continence, by whose honest
  movements we sing what pleases God; the saint refuses Sion's songs to the
  powers that hold him captive. On the psalm's close (vv. 7–9, *Tract.* 136.12–14)
  the sons of Edom are the body's earthly vices that would empty us down to the
  foundation, Christ; the daughter of Babylon is the flesh; her little ones are
  vices still tender, to be dashed on the rock that is Christ. On Ps 118:1, the
  Introit verse (*Aleph* 1–3): first be undefiled in the way, with morals made
  firm, and only then search God's testimonies; the way is Christ and must be
  travelled, not only entered; the undefiled curb the flesh, avarice and the
  love of earthly honours; the law of the Lord is set against the law of sin.
  On Ps 118:49–50, God's every word calls to the hope of heavenly goods and
  consoles in persecution and loss; the soul is fed by God's words *tamquam
  pabulum aliquod vitae*.
- **St Jerome** (*Comm. in Dan.* 3:26–49; *Comm. in Eph.* III on 5:16–21;
  scope §§ 3.1, 3.3). Contributing. On Dan 3:26: when pressed by distress, say
  this with all the heart and confess that whatever befalls us we bear justly; on
  3:29: the innocent youths speak in the person of the people; on 3:37: the
  Church may pray these verses when, for the people's sins, she lacks teachers
  and in persecution cannot offer the sacrifice; on 3:49: the angel in the
  furnace is the divine word coming to the soul that has despaired of human help.
  On Eph 5:16, good works buy back time and turn evil days to good; on 5:19,
  *Deo non voce, sed corde cantandum*; on 5:20, thanks are owed even if wife and
  children are taken into captivity — *minora me scio sustinere quam mereor*.
- **St John Chrysostom** (*Hom. 19 in Eph.*; scope § 3.3). Contributing: "The
  time is not yours. At present ye are strangers, and sojourners, and foreigners,
  and aliens"; redeem it by yielding all but the faith; "learn to sing psalms";
  give thanks in disease and penury.
- **St Robert Bellarmine** on Ps 136 (scope § 3.7). Contributing: God's elect
  "held here below in captivity … citizens of the Jerusalem above"; "still they
  sing, in their hearts, to God"; those who sing sacred hymns only to please the
  ear sing "the song of the Lord in a strange land" — the censure Jerome makes on
  Eph 5:19 from the other side.
- **Cassiodorus** on Ps 136 (ecclesiastical writer; scope § 3.7). Contributing:
  *super flumina*, not *in fluminibus* — those already freed sit on the bank *in
  humilitate*; *Psalmodia est … consolatio flentium, cura dolentium, sanitas
  aegrotorum; hoc animae remedium*. On the close (CCSL 98, described) he reads
  the little ones as the flesh's errors while small, dashed on the rock,
  Christ, and the prayer against Edom as prophecy, not a curse. On Ps 118:1
  (CCSL 98, described) the faithful become spotless by pardon and walk the way
  that is Christ with the steps of good action.
- **St John Chrysostom** on Ps 136 (*Expositio*, PG 55 cols. 405–407; scope
  § 3.7). Contributing, and dissenting on the close: God let his people lose
  the city so that deprivation might teach them to desire it, and they, who
  had mocked the weeping prophets, wept and kept the law among their captors;
  so many will seek the Jerusalem above on the last day and find no return,
  and the present life must be ordered so as not to become strangers to it. He
  reads vv. 8–9 as the captives' own anger, which the prophet reports, and sets
  against it the Gospel's command to feed one's enemies and pray for them.
- **Sicard and Durandus** (*Mitrale* VIII.20; *Rationale* VI.137;
  ecclesiastical writers; documented reception of the shared chants only):
  *Captivitas illa corporalis, nostram significat spiritualem. A captivitate
  reversio est peccatorum remissio*; the Introit is Daniel's voice ascribing the
  captivity to their deserts by God's judgment, the Offertory our lament of it,
  the Gradual thanksgiving, the Communion a petition to receive what is promised
  in hope. **Berno** (c. 5): *Propter haec omnia* — the confession of the Introit,
  the penitents' *Super flumina*, the Gradual's hope — the priest asks
  *indulgentiam … et pacem* in the Collect. These are the commentators' joins of
  the shared elements, not the Fathers'.

### 2.4 The four senses

- **Literal.** Azariah confesses in the furnace that God's judgments on Judah
  were just (Dan 3:26–45); captives by Babylon's rivers weep for Sion (Ps 136);
  Paul tells the Ephesians to walk wisely, redeem the time in evil days, and sing
  in their hearts (Eph 5:15–21).
- **Allegorical.** Babylon is the world that flows past, Jerusalem–Sion the
  heavenly city and mother (Augustine; Hilary); the captives are the Church's
  citizens on pilgrimage; Christ is the Rock against which Babylon's little ones
  are dashed (Augustine); the angel in the furnace is the divine word (Jerome).
- **Moral.** Confess God's justice before asking his gifts; sit by the rivers,
  not in them; keep the song of Sion in the heart and in deeds; walk the way of
  the Lord's law and not the roads to a false beatitude (Augustine and Hilary on
  Ps 118:1); kill desires while they are small, against the Rock (Augustine,
  Hilary, Cassiodorus on Ps 136:9); redeem the time by good works; give thanks
  even in captivity (Augustine, Hilary, Jerome, Chrysostom).
- **Anagogical.** The memory of Sion is longing for the heavenly Jerusalem;
  "Your captivity will pass away, your happiness will come … we shall triumph
  with our King, without death" (Augustine on Ps 136, NPNF); the hope of the
  Communion is for promises kept in glory (Hilary).

### 2.5 Strongest difficulty and alternative

- **The Introit says all God did to us was just because we sinned.** Read
  literally of the Exile, it is Israel's confession; read of every suffering, it
  could be taken as saying all suffering is punishment. Jerome blocks that
  reading: the youths who pray it had not sinned and speak *ex persona populi*.
  Augustine places the confession in the order of prayer — praise God's justice,
  then ask — not as an explanation of every pain. The studies should keep both.
- **Ps 136 ends in a blessing on whoever dashes Babylon's little ones against
  the rock (v. 9, unsung).** The Offertory sings only v. 1. Five checked
  witnesses read the close (scope § 4.9). Augustine, Hilary and Cassiodorus
  take the little ones as sins or desires at their birth, to be dashed on the
  Rock, which is Christ. Bellarmine gives the literal prophecy (the Medes and
  Persians against Babylon) and then says "All this has a spiritual meaning":
  allegorically the little ones are the pagans' children brought "to the rock,
  Christ … to die the death of the old man, to rise a new man", and morally
  blessed is he who "by a happy dash on the rock, kills sin" — so he shares the
  Rock-is-Christ reading and differs from Augustine only by adding the literal
  and allegorical senses. Chrysostom alone reads no figure: the words are the
  captives' anger, which the prophet reports, and the New Covenant commands
  love of enemies instead. The real differences are therefore Chrysostom's
  refusal of a figure, Bellarmine's added literal and allegorical senses, and
  Hilary's Edom as the body's vices where Augustine and Cassiodorus have
  persecutors. A study that mentions the close reports each witness's reading
  at its locus; it does not import the verse into the Mass, does not present
  the witnesses as disagreeing about the Rock, and does not repeat
  Bellarmine's allegorical identification of Edom with the Jews except as his,
  if at all (§ 3.5's rule applies).
- **Does the reading neglect the Gospel?** It gives the Gospel a supporting
  place (§ 2.3). If a study wants the Gospel at the centre, readings 1 and 3 do
  that; the comparison should say so.

### 2.6 Agreements and differences within the reading

- **The willows and the instruments.** Augustine and Bellarmine: barren men;
  Hilary and Cassiodorus: saints. Instruments: Scriptures (Augustine,
  Cassiodorus) or bodies (Hilary). Kept as a real disagreement inside a shared
  spiritual reading (scope § 4.3).
- **Voice or heart.** Jerome subordinates the voice; Chrysostom urges singing
  psalms with attention; both oppose the theatre (scope § 4.6).
- **Redeeming the time.** Jerome: good works turn evil days to good;
  Chrysostom: yield what persecutors demand and keep the faith; Aquinas prefers a
  form of the latter (scope § 4.5).
- **Whose voice in the Introit and Offertory.** The commentators differ (scope
  § 4.4); this reading follows Augustine and Jerome (the Church, praying for and
  as the people).
- **The close of Ps 136.** Augustine, Hilary, Cassiodorus and Bellarmine read
  the little ones dashed on the Rock as sins killed young against Christ;
  Bellarmine adds literal and allegorical senses; Chrysostom reads no figure
  and opposes the Gospel's love of enemies to the captives' anger (§ 2.5;
  scope § 4.9).
- **The Introit verse.** Augustine and Bellarmine read the way as the law of
  the Lord against false roads and the law of the flesh; Hilary and Cassiodorus
  also name the way Christ. Complementary, not opposed (scope § 3.1).

## 3. `israel-and-the-nations` — The dying son and the two peoples: Israel's confession, the nations' hope

### 3.1 Controlling claim

The Fathers read the healing at Capharnaum not only as one man's faith but as a
figure of Israel and the nations. Augustine sets the episode in its sequence:
the Samaritans believed Christ's word without signs; his own country, which
figures the people of the Jews, saw signs and scarcely believed; "we have seen
no signs, none do we demand", and yet "the root remains … in the patriarchs …
in great honor" (*Tract.* 16.3–5). Aquinas reads the ruler allegorically as
Abraham or one of the Fathers of the Old Testament, the sick son as the Jewish
people, and notes that the Fathers prayed for Israel (*Super Io.* c. 4 l. 7).
Cyril reads the same sequence as the readiness of the nations, and adds Paul's
hope: "when the fulness of the Gentiles is come in, then shall all Israel be
saved". **[synthesis]** The formulary's chants give voice to that history:
Israel's own confession in exile (Introit, from Azariah's prayer) and lament by
Babylon's rivers (Offertory), beside the nations' hope in the God who feeds all
eyes in due season (Gradual) and in his word (Communion). The Church prays
Israel's confession as her own, as Azariah prayed for a people's sin he had not
committed (Jerome), gives thanks as the nations fed, and, with Aquinas's Fathers,
intercedes for the healing of the whole house.

**Exegetical question.** Whose story is the healing at Capharnaum, and whose
voices does this Mass let us hear?

**The inference that answers it.** Augustine: the evangelist's remark that a
prophet has no honour in his own country, set between Samaria's faith without
signs and Galilee's demand for them, makes the episode a figure — *prodigium*
"as if it were *porrodicium* … what betokens something to come" — of the Jews,
who saw and did not believe, and the nations, who believe having seen nothing
(*Tract.* 16.3). Aquinas, reading the ruler allegorically, makes the healing a
figure of the Fathers' intercession for a sick people and of its cure by the
word at the seventh hour, the sevenfold Spirit by whom sins are remitted.

**Distinguishing consequence.** The reading is salvation-historical: its
application is not first the individual's faith (reading 1) or the exile's
ascesis (reading 2) but the Church's humility as the grafted branch, her
appropriation of Israel's confession, and her intercession and hope for Israel.

### 3.2 Principal witnesses and their reasoning

- **St Augustine** (*Tract. in Io.* 16.3–7; scope § 3.6). Carrying author.
  The Samaritans believed the word; "Galilee of Judea was then the Lord's own
  country … let us … assume the Lord's own country to mean the people of the
  Jews"; "we, like those Samaritans … have seen no signs, none do we demand";
  Thomas and the ruler alike rebuked; the centurion, "an alien", believed Christ
  able to work by a word — the proud branches broken off, the humble wild olive
  grafted in, "nevertheless let the root remain … the holy patriarchs … in rest
  with God, in great honor"; "My mother is Sion" — let him have honour in the
  country he founded.
- **St Thomas Aquinas** (*Super Io.* c. 4 l. 7; scope § 3.6). Carrying author.
  *Allegorice … regulus ille, Abraham, vel aliquis ex patribus veteris
  testamenti … Filius ejus est populus Judaeorum*, sick in Capharnaum, "abundance",
  which drew Israel from God (Deut 32:15); *Sic et patres veteris testamenti
  rogabant pro populo Israel* (Jeremiah, 2 Macc 15:14); the seventh hour, the
  sevenfold Spirit and the remission of sins; the Galileans worse than the
  Samaritans, because the Jews for their hardness were converted to the faith
  only gradually, *paulatim* (Mic 7:1).
- **St Cyril of Alexandria** (*Comm. in Io.* II.5; no standing row; scope
  § 3.6). Contributing: the sequence shows "the extreme readiness of the aliens
  to obedience", that "Israel with reason falleth from the hope, and the fulness of
  the Gentiles is brought in", and "when the fulness of the Gentiles is come in,
  then shall all Israel be saved".
- **St Jerome** (*Comm. in Dan.* 3:29, 37; scope § 3.1). Contributing: the
  innocent youths confess *ex persona populi*; the Church may pray Dan 3:37 when
  she suffers want for the people's sins.
- **Documented reception of the compilation** (ecclesiastical writers; chants,
  Epistle and Collect only). Rupert (XII.20): the Introit is the voice of the
  prophets and the Offertory's hung harps are preachers fallen silent before
  barren hearers. Berno: the penitent people sing *Super flumina*, the cantor
  confesses with *Omnia quae fecisti*; in the Communion the Prophet sings "in
  the person of the faithful". Honorius *sub lege*: the exiles returned from Babylon. Fromage
  (*The Liturgical Year* XI, p. 439): the Introit and Offertory voice the
  repentance of Jacob, "the Choir of the Jews", and the Gradual and Communion
  the canticles of the Gentiles, "the Gentile-Choir" — his synthesis of Berno,
  Rupert and Durandus, none of whom says it in those terms (scope § 2.1).
  **Bound.** Both Rupert's voices and Fromage's choirs are built on the
  wedding-feast Gospel (Mt 22:1–14): Rupert's grieving Fathers grieve for
  those called to the nuptials who would not come, and Fromage opens from "the
  Gospel of last Sunday" — in the 1962 Missal the Nineteenth Sunday's — places
  his Gentiles "within the hall of the marriage-feast", and calls the Mass "a
  foretaste of that happy day" on that Gospel's frame (scope § 2.1). The
  reading uses only what each says of a shared chant as quoted here, and
  neither the parable nor any paraphrase of it (the called who would not come,
  the marriage-feast, the wedding garment, the banquet hall) enters the
  reading, its senses, the concise study or the homily.

### 3.4 The four senses

- **Literal.** A royal official asks for his son's life at Cana
  after the Samaritans have believed without signs and the Galileans have welcomed
  Jesus for what they saw at Jerusalem (Jn 4:39–54); Azariah confesses for
  exiled Israel (Dan 3); the captives lament by Babylon's rivers (Ps 136).
- **Allegorical.** The ruler's country figures Israel, the Samaritans the nations
  (Augustine); the ruler figures the Fathers interceding, the son the people,
  healed by the word at the hour of the Spirit (Aquinas); the household's faith,
  the Church (Berno).
- **Moral.** The Gentile Church is the grafted branch and must be humble before
  the root (Augustine); she prays Israel's confession as her own and intercedes
  as the Fathers did (Jerome; Aquinas).
- **Anagogical.** When the fullness of the nations has come in, all Israel will
  be saved (Cyril, after Rom 11:25–26). Fromage's "foretaste of that happy day"
  is not used here: he frames it on the wedding-feast Gospel (§ 3.2).

### 3.5 Strongest difficulty and alternative

- **The reading can be heard as setting Israel against the Church.** The sources
  say more and less than that: Augustine insists the root, the patriarchs,
  remains in honour, and that Christ took flesh from Sion; Aquinas's ruler is
  Abraham interceding; Cyril ends in the salvation of all Israel; Jerome makes
  Israel's confession the prayer of the innocent and the Church's own. The studies
  must present the Fathers' figure in their own terms and keep these elements
  beside it; they must not add what the witnesses do not say, and must not use
  the figure to describe any present people.
- **The identifications conflict.** Augustine's ruler belongs to the people that
  needed signs; Aquinas's figures the patriarch who intercedes; Cyril's stands
  among the obedient aliens (scope § 4.2). The reading's controlling claim is
  only what they share — that the episode figures the relation of Israel and the
  nations to Christ's word — and each identification is credited to its author.
- **The chant voices are the commentators' and not the Fathers'.** The Jewish
  and Gentile choirs are Fromage's synthesis; the commentators he cites, whose
  Mass had another Gospel, assign the voices differently (scope § 4.4). Both
  Rupert's voices and Fromage's choirs are read out of the wedding-feast Gospel
  (scope § 2.1). The reading uses them only as documented reception of the
  shared chants, in the bounded terms of § 3.2, and never with the parable.
- **Alternative.** Reading 2 hears the same chants as the Church's own exile,
  not Israel's (Augustine on Ps 136). Both are patristic; they differ on whose
  exile the chants first voice.

### 3.6 Agreements and differences within the reading

- Augustine and Aquinas agree that the episode figures Israel and the faith of the
  nations, and that Israel's patriarchs keep their honour; they disagree on what
  the ruler figures (scope § 4.2).
- Cyril shares Augustine's reading of the sequence and adds the eschatological
  salvation of all Israel; Augustine's *Tractate* does not say this, and the
  studies must not credit it to him.
- Jerome's *ex persona populi* is said of Dan 3:29, not of the Mass; its use for
  the Church's praying the Introit is [synthesis].

## 4. Comparison

### 4.1 What the three share

All three take the Gospel's word *Fílius tuus vivit* and the Communion's hope in
God's word as the formulary's hinge, and read the Introit's confession as the
right beginning of prayer. All rest on the Fathers' agreement that the healing
was by word without presence (scope § 4.8); the Gospel's own close, *crédidit
ipse et domus eius tota*, is text, and how the ruler's faith grew toward it is
expounded by Augustine, Chrysostom, Cyril and Aquinas but not by Gregory (scope
§ 4.1).

### 4.2 Where they differ

| | `word-at-a-distance` | `songs-of-sion-in-babylon` | `israel-and-the-nations` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Centre | the Gospel | the chants and Epistle | the Gospel as figure, with the chants as voices |
| Whose situation | one believer's | the Church's and each soul's present exile | two peoples' history |
| What the Introit is | the confession that opens petition | the exile's confession of God's justice | Israel's confession, prayed by the Church |
| What the Offertory is | faith in absence | the Church's lament and memory of Sion | Israel's lament |
| Main application | believe the word; grow in faith | humility, thanksgiving, song of the heart, longing | humility of the grafted branch; intercession for Israel |
| Carrying authors | Gregory; Chrysostom | Augustine; Hilary | Augustine; Aquinas |

The readings answer different questions and arrive at different applications;
they are not one theme under three titles. Reading 1's consequence is about the
object of faith; reading 2's about the conduct of life in exile; reading 3's
about the Church's place between Israel and the nations.

### 4.3 How they relate

Complementary rather than rival. Aquinas holds the literal-moral reading of the
ruler's faith (reading 1) and the allegory of the Fathers and the people
(reading 3) together, at different senses. Augustine's reading of Ps 136
(reading 2) and the commentators' assignment of the Offertory to Israel
(reading 3) differ on whose exile the chant first voices; Augustine's own
*Tractate* 16 supplies reading 3's figure of the Gospel. One real tension: a
study that adopts Fromage's two choirs for the Introit and Offertory cannot also
say, with Augustine, that those chants are first the Church's own voice; it must
say which it follows and attribute each. Whichever it follows, Fromage's choirs
and Rupert's voices are cited without the wedding-feast Gospel on which both
were built (§ 3.2; scope § 2.1).

### 4.4 The appointed texts' most demanding features, and where each is met

- The Introit's *Omnia, quæ fecísti nobis … in vero iudício fecísti, quia
  peccávimus*: met by reading 2 (Jerome's innocent who confess for the people;
  Augustine's praise of justice before petition) and reading 3 (Israel's
  confession prayed by the Church). Reading 1 uses it only as the order of
  prayer.
- The rebuke *Nisi signa et prodígia vidéritis, non créditis* to a father whose
  son is dying: met by reading 1 (§ 1.5) and reading 3 (Augustine's two
  countries).
- Ps 136's context of bitterness (v. 9): met by reading 2 (§ 2.5), which
  reports the five checked readings of the close and their real differences.

### 4.5 The Introit verse, Ps 118:1, in the three readings

The verse *Beáti immaculáti in via: qui ámbulant in lege Dómini* has four
checked direct witnesses (scope § 3.1). Each reading states how it uses them.

- **Reading 1** uses Hilary and Cassiodorus: the way is Christ, and Hilary's
  way must be travelled and not only entered. The connection to the father who
  believed the word *et ibat* is [synthesis]; neither Father speaks of the
  Gospel (§ 1.3).
- **Reading 2** uses the verse most fully. Augustine's first sermon makes it
  God's voice recalling those who seek beatitude by evil roads to the one way,
  the law of the Lord; Hilary and Bellarmine set that law against the law of
  sin and the flesh. The verse names the road home of the exiles' confession,
  bears on the Epistle's *caute ambulétis … sed ut sapiéntes*, and is answered
  by the Postcommunion's *tuis semper obœdíre mandátis* — each join
  [synthesis] (§ 2.3).
- **Reading 3** has one reception to use, Cassiodorus's identification of the
  psalm's speaker as the single chorus of all the saints from the world's
  beginning to its end; otherwise the verse contributes only as text, because
  no checked witness reads it of Israel and the nations, and the reading does
  not invent one (§ 3.3).
### 4.6 Notes for the concise study's page-1 overview

The four overview rows summarise what the three readings share and should not
erase their differences. Defensible rows, each grounded in the readings above:

- **Literal:** a father asks Christ to come down; Christ sends a word, the man
  believes it, and his son lives at that hour; the chants are songs of Israel's
  exile and confession, the Epistle a call to walk wisely and sing in evil days.
- **Allegorical:** Christ, present everywhere though absent in body, heals by his
  word (Gregory); the exiles are the Church on pilgrimage to the heavenly
  Jerusalem (Augustine, Hilary); the healing figures the relation of Israel and
  the nations to his word (Augustine, Aquinas).
- **Moral:** believe the word before you see; confess God's justice and give
  thanks in adversity; sing in the heart; keep humility before the root.
- **Anagogical:** the promise kept in glory — the word that says *vivit* and the
  memory of Sion end in the heavenly city.

### 4.7 Notes for the homily

Reading 1 is the most intelligible single argument for an adult parish assembly:
the father's journey home on the strength of a word, with Gregory's
*Corporalem ergo praesentiam Domini quaerebat, qui per spiritum nusquam deerat*
(*Hom.* 28.1) — no public-domain English translation of *Hom.* 28 is
registered, so the homily describes it, saying that the man looked for the
Lord's bodily presence when the Lord was absent nowhere, and does not resolve
*per spiritum* into a capitalised "Spirit" without a source — Chrysostom's
"He healeth the father, sick in mind, no less than the son", and the Communion's
*Meménto verbi tui … in quo mihi spem dedísti*. Reading 2's Offertory and
Epistle (singing in the heart in evil days) can serve it as the second movement.
Reading 3 needs more care than a ten-minute homily can give and is better left to
the studies. The homily must not claim that the Fathers joined the Gospel to
the chants; that join is the editor's.

## 6. The commemoration in a low Mass

In a low or conventual Mass on 11 October 2026 the Maternity's Collect, Secret
and Postcommunion follow the Sunday's under their own conclusions. They add the
Word's taking flesh in Mary's womb at the angel's word, her intercession, *pacem*
beside the Collect's *pacem*, and *cæléstis remédii … consórtes* beside the
Secret's *medicínam* [text]. No reading depends on them; a study that mentions
them says that they belong to this date's low Mass and not to the Sunday's
formulary or to a sung parish Mass, and marks any connection [synthesis].
