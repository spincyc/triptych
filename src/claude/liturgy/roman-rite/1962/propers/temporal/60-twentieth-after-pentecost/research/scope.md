## 1. The formulary and the facts the research rests on

Ten required elements, no alternatives: Introit *Omnia, quae fecisti nobis*
(Dan. 3, 31, 29 et 35; verse Ps 118:1), Collect *Largire*, Epistle Eph 5:15–21,
Gradual Ps 144:15–16, Alleluia Ps 107:2, Gospel Jn 4:46–53, Offertory Ps 136:1,
Secret *Caelestem nobis*, Communion Ps 118:49–50, Postcommunion *Ut sacris*. The
Trinity Preface is appointed by rubric. On 11 October 2026 a low or conventual
Mass adds the commemoration of the Maternity of the Blessed Virgin Mary (three
orations); a sung Mass that is not conventual does not (`research/context.md`).
Every Latin form was re-read in this stage on page images (`propers/verified.md`).

### 1.1 Threads inside the appointed text

Textual observations only. Each is a repetition or opposition of words inside
the printed formulary; none asserts that the compilers joined the texts for it.

- **Disobedience confessed, obedience asked.** The Introit confesses *mandátis
  tuis non obœdívimus*; the Postcommunion asks *fac nos … tuis semper obœdíre
  mandátis*. The same noun and verb, negated at the entrance and prayed for at
  the end.
- **Walking.** The Introit verse blesses those *qui ámbulant in lege Dómini*;
  the Epistle opens *Vidéte quómodo caute ambulétis*; in the Gospel the father,
  having believed the word, *ibat* (Jn 4:50, *Crédidit … et ibat*).
- **Singing.** The Epistle's *cantántes, et psalléntes in córdibus vestris* and
  the Alleluia's *cantábo, et psallam tibi* use the same two verbs. The
  Offertory's exiles sit and weep (*sédimus, et flévimus*) where the verse that
  follows it in the psalm, unsung here, asks *Quomodo cantabimus canticum Domini
  in terra aliena?* (Ps 136:4).
- **The heart.** *Parátum cor meum* (Alleluia), *in córdibus vestris*
  (Epistle), *vítia nostri cordis* (Secret).
- **Hope and the word.** The Gradual's eyes *in te sperant*; the Communion asks
  God to remember *verbi tui … in quo mihi spem dedísti*; in the Gospel *Crédidit
  homo sermóni, quem dixit ei Iesus*.
- **Time and the hour.** *rediméntes tempus* (Epistle), *in témpore opportúno*
  (Gradual), and the Gospel's *horam … heri hora séptima … illa hora*.
- **Memory.** *dum recordarémur tui, Sion* (Offertory); *Meménto verbi tui*
  (Communion).
- **Mercy, pardon, peace.** *secúndum multitúdinem misericórdiæ tuæ*
  (Introit); *indulgéntiam … et pacem: ut … mundéntur offénsis* (Collect).
- **Sickness and medicine.** *infirmabátur … incipiébat enim mori … relíquit eum
  febris* (Gospel); *Cæléstem … medicínam: et vítia nostri cordis expúrgent*
  (Secret). In a Mass with the commemoration the Maternity Postcommunion adds
  *cæléstis remédii … consórtes*.
- **Glory.** *da glóriam nómini tuo* (Introit); *psallam tibi, glória mea*
  (Alleluia).
- **Humility.** *hæc me consoláta est in humilitáte mea* (Communion).

### 1.2 Threads in the unsung context of the appointed verses

The complete contexts were read in the tracked Clementine verse texts: Dan
3:1–51 and 91–97 (the Prayer of Azariah is vv. 24–45); Eph 5:1–33; Jn 2:1–11
and 4:1–54; Pss 56 and 59 (whose verses Ps 107 repeats), 107 entire, 118:1–8
and 49–56, 136 entire, 144 entire. Observations from the verses the Missal does
not sing, with the same status as § 1.1:

- **Azariah's prayer.** Dan 3:27–28 call God just in all he has done (*quia
  justus es in omnibus, quæ fecisti nobis … judicia enim vera fecisti*); v. 37
  *sumusque humiles in universa terra hodie propter peccata nostra*; v. 38 no
  prince, prophet, holocaust, sacrifice or place; vv. 39–40 *sed in animo
  contrito, et spiritu humilitatis suscipiamur … sic fiat sacrificium nostrum in
  conspectu tuo hodie, ut placeat tibi*; vv. 42–43 *fac nobiscum … secundum
  multitudinem misericordiæ tuæ … et da gloriam nomini tuo*. The prayer is said
  by three young men in the furnace who, by the narrative, had not been carried
  off for their own sins.
- **Dan 3:39–40 in the Order of Mass.** The 1962 *Ordo Missae* gives the
  celebrant, after placing the chalice, *In spíritu humilitátis et in ánimo
  contríto suscipiámur a te, Dómine: et sic fiat sacrifícium nostrum in
  conspéctu tuo hódie, ut pláceat tibi, Dómine Deus* (no. 1033, printed p. 221,
  PDF p. 302, read on the page image). The same prayer from which the Introit is
  cut is said at the offertory of every Mass.
- **Ps 118:50 completed.** The Communion stops before the verse's close, *quia
  eloquium tuum vivificavit me*. The Gospel's word is *Fílius tuus vivit*.
- **Ps 136 entire.** Vv. 3–4 the captors' demand for a song of Sion and the
  refusal; vv. 5–6 *Si oblitus fuero tui, Jerusalem* — the second-person address
  to the city which the Offertory's *tui, Sion* also carries.
- **Ps 107.** Vv. 2–6 repeat Ps 56:8–12 and vv. 7–14 Ps 59:7–14. *Exsurge,
  gloria mea* is v. 3 and *psallam tibi* stands in v. 4 (*psallam tibi in
  nationibus*); the Alleluia's *psallam tibi, glória mea* uses words that stand
  in vv. 2–4 of the same psalm.
- **Ps 144.** V. 14 *Allevat Dominus omnes qui corruunt*; v. 17 *Justus Dominus
  in omnibus viis suis*; v. 18 *Prope est Dominus omnibus invocantibus eum*.
- **Eph 5.** V. 14, just before the pericope, *Surge qui dormis … et illuminabit
  te Christus*; v. 18 sets wine against the Spirit; vv. 22–33 follow with the
  *sacramentum … magnum … in Christo et in Ecclesia*.
- **Jn 4.** The Samaritans believe *propter sermonem ejus* without a sign and
  confess him *Salvator mundi* (vv. 39–42); v. 44 *propheta in sua patria
  honorem non habet*; v. 45 the Galileans receive him having seen what he did at
  Jerusalem; v. 46, whose first half the liturgy omits, places the episode at
  Cana *ubi fecit aquam vinum*, the sign of Jn 2:1–11 at a wedding where wine ran
  out; v. 54 calls this the second sign.

Consequences, which bind the studies:

- Berno, Rupert, Honorius, Sicard and Durandus are cited only for what they say
  of the shared chants, Epistle and (Berno) Collect. None of them is cited for a
  connection between those elements and Jn 4:46–53; their Mass set the chants
  beside the wedding feast. The readings, the concise study and the homily do
  not mention their other Gospel; at most one clause of the expansive study's
  scope appendix may.
- Fromage (*The Liturgical Year*) and Schuster expound this formulary with this
  Gospel. Fromage is an ecclesiastical writer and cannot be one of a reading's
  two carrying authors; Schuster is Blessed and may be one beside a Father or
  canonized saint (`src/sources/inventories/author-standing-v1.toml`).
- Fromage's chapter (p. 439) reports that "the ancient Liturgists tell us" the
  Mass consists partly of the words of the Prophets giving Jacob his repentance
  and partly of the Gentiles' canticles, a Jewish choir taking the Introit and
  Offertory and a Gentile choir the Gradual and Communion, citing "Berno Aug. v;
  Rup. de div. Off. xii. 20; Durand. Ration vi. 137". Read at those loci, none of
  the three says this in these terms (§ 4.4); the two-choir scheme is
  Fromage's own synthesis of them.
- **The wedding-feast Gospel is the source of both the chant voices and the
  two choirs.** Rupert's assignment of the voices (XII.20, PL 170 cols.
  327–328) is explicitly a reading of the wedding-feast parable of his own
  Mass: the Office consists partly of the voices of those who entered the
  nuptials and partly of the voices of the Fathers grieving for those who,
  called to the nuptials the king made for his son, would not come and killed
  his servants (Mt 22:3–7); the prophets foresaw that those murderers would
  perish and their city be overthrown, *quorum illa vox est, qua in introitu ex
  verbis Danielis decantatur*; in the Offertory the harps hung on Babylon's
  willows are the preachers silenced by barren hearers, *qui vocati ad
  nuptias … venire noluerunt*; the Epistle warns those gathered from the
  roads not to be found without the wedding garment; the Gradual and
  Communion are the feast's banquet. Fromage opens this formulary (p. 438)
  from "the Gospel of last Sunday", the nuptials and "the rejection of the
  Jews, and the vocation of the Gentiles" — in the 1962 Missal the Nineteenth
  Sunday's Gospel, Mt 22:1–14 (the finding aid
  `src/sources/calendars/roman-1962/propers.yaml`; not re-read on the 1962
  page image in this stage) — and builds on it his Jewish and Gentile choirs,
  the Gentiles "already within the hall of the marriage-feast", and the Mass
  as "a foretaste of that happy day" (pp. 438–439). Both readings therefore
  carry another Gospel, of another Mass and (for Fromage) another Sunday, into
  the chants. Consequence: neither may be cited for the voice of a shared
  chant in terms that come from that Gospel. A study may say only what each
  says of a shared element without the parable — Rupert: that the Introit is
  the voice of the prophets and the Offertory's hung harps the silence of
  preachers before barren hearers; Fromage: that the Introit and Offertory
  voice Israel's repentance and the Gradual and Communion the Gentiles'
  thanksgiving — and no paraphrase such as "those who were called and would
  not come", "the marriage-feast" or "the wedding garment" may enter a reading,
  the concise study or the homily.

### 2.2 Where the commentators place this Gospel

Read as reception of the Gospel, to be cited at its locus without narrating the
Sunday on which they read it:

- Rupert, XII.21 (PL 170 col. 328): the fever that had almost extinguished the
  ruler's son signifies the shaking (*concussio*) of a noble, fighting soul in
  temptation, as Job's was.
- Berno, c. 5 (PL 142 col. 1071): *domus ejus intelligitur familia ejus* — the
  Church gathered from the Gentiles.
- Durandus, VI.138 (Lyon 1612 p. 411): the ruler stands for kings and prelates,
  who should be humble; *credebat enim ille Regulus quod Dominus poterat sanare
  filium suum, sed non credebat esse Deum: quia credebat quod non posset sanare,
  nisi praesens*.
- St Anthony of Padua, Sunday sermon on Jn 4:46–53 (English, Spilsbury,
  restricted compilation): his Mass for this Gospel names the Introit *Si
  iniquitates* (Ps 129) and the Epistle Eph 6:10–17, which he concords with the
  Gospel; he is cited for what he says of the Gospel (§ 3.6) and of nothing else
  here. Rupert (XII.21) and Durandus (VI.138) likewise read this Gospel in a Mass
  whose Introit is *In voluntate tua* and whose Epistle is Eph 6:10–17.

## 3. Passage-by-passage reception matrix

One row per appointed passage or composed text. "Direct" means the witness
expounds the appointed passage; "reuse" means he uses it for another purpose.
Loci are given to the edition and bytes read. Languages: Latin, English
translations of Greek (NPNF, LFC), and Greek (Swete) for § 1.3 only.

### 3.1 Introit — Dan 3 cento and Ps 118:1 (no. 1689)

- **Direct ancient exegesis checked.**
  - St Jerome, *Commentarii in Danielem*, on Dan 3:24–90 (PL 25 cols. 639–642;
    tracked Wikisource Latin, physical line 20). He notes that the Hebrew text
    ends at v. 23 and that what follows to the end of the Song of the Three
    Children is not in the Hebrew, and comments briefly from Theodotion's
    version: on v. 26, *Quando diversis premimur angustiis, ex toto cordis hoc
    loquamur affectu, et quidquid nobis acciderit, juste nos sustinere
    fateamur*; on v. 29, that the three youths had not sinned and were not of an
    age to be punished for their own faults, *ergo … ex persona populi
    loquuntur*; on v. 37, that these verses are to be used *si quando Ecclesiae
    propter peccata populi … sustinent penuriam, et quando in persecutionibus non
    offertur sacrificium et oblatio*, rejecting a reading of the place as souls
    fallen from the heavenly Jerusalem (*sed haec non recipit Ecclesia Dei*); on
    v. 49, the angel who descends into the furnace is the divine word (*sermo
    divinus*) coming to a soul that has despaired of human help and turned wholly
    to the Lord. Introductory note (line 2): the Churches read Daniel in
    Theodotion's version. Direct exegesis.
  - St Augustine, *Enarr. in Ps.* 144.21 (PL 37 col. 1882, read on the page
    image, PDF p. 434): on Ps 144:17, *omnes sancti in tribulationibus
    constituti, prius justitiam ejus laudaverunt, et sic beneficia petiverunt.
    Prius dixerunt: Justum est quod facis. Sic rogavit Daniel, sic alii sancti:
    Justa sunt judicia tua, bene passi sumus, recte passi sumus* — Migne's margin
    cites Dan 3:27–31 and 9:5–19. Reuse of the Daniel prayer inside Augustine's
    exposition of the Gradual's psalm. In *Enarr.* 91.3 (Latin, tracked part 10,
    line 35) he cites Daniel's prayer of Dan 9 for the same point ("confess God's
    truth by night").
- **Ps 118:1, the verse (*Beáti immaculáti in via: qui ámbulant in lege
  Dómini*). Direct ancient exegesis checked** (iteration 1).
  - St Augustine, *Enarr. in Ps.* 118, *prooemium* and *sermo* 1.1–2 (PL 37
    cols. 1501–1502, read on the page image, PDF p. 244; English NPNF1-8,
    tracked CCEL text, physical lines 56727–56760, abridged, with the
    *prooemium* translated in footnote 5128 at lines 56831–56860). The preface:
    he put this psalm off longest because the plainer it seems the deeper it
    is, and expounds it in sermons so that the assemblies who love to sing it
    should not lack its sense. On v. 1: the psalm begins by exhorting to
    beatitude, which no one does not desire; the exhortation is needed because
    all want the end and few want the way. *Tanquam diceret: Scio quid velis,
    beatitudinem quaeris; si ergo vis esse beatus, esto immaculatus. Illud enim
    omnes, hoc autem pauci volunt, sine quo non pervenitur ad illud quod omnes
    volunt. Sed ubi erit quisque immaculatus, nisi in via? In qua via, nisi in
    lege Domini?* The lustful, the avaricious, the cruel and the ambitious all
    seek beatitude by evil roads; the verse is the divine voice calling them
    back to the way: *Quo itis? Peritis, et nescitis. … Tam magnum bonum
    quaerere per mala nolite … Viae perversae relinquite malignitatem, qui non
    potestis relinquere beatitudinis voluntatem*; not those defiled in error
    who walk in the perversity of the world are blessed, but the undefiled in
    the way. On v. 2 (1.2): to search the Lord's testimonies and seek him with
    the whole heart *hoc est immaculatum in via, in lege Domini ambulare*. In
    1.3 (NPNF, abridged) he raises the question how the saints, who confess
    that they have sin (1 Jn 1:8), can walk in the Lord's ways, which "they who
    do wickedness" do not; the abridged English breaks off before his answer,
    and the Latin of 1.3 was not read. Direct exegesis.
  - St Hilary of Poitiers, *Tract. in Ps.* 118, *Aleph* 1–3 (PL 9 cols.
    503–505, read on the page images of the registered PL 9 scan, PDF pp.
    268–269; tracked OCR layer, physical lines 40250–40436). The edition prints
    the *Aleph* octonary as the psalm-text heading at col. 503; Hilary's lemma
    at § 1 (col. 504) is the Missal's verse. § 1, *Luminis consequendi quae sit
    praeparatio*: the order of the sayings is not to be neglected — first
    *Beati immaculati in via*, then *Beati qui scrutantur testimonia*; one
    first enters the way of truth with morals made firm in the pursuit of
    innocence, and only then searches God's testimonies with a cleansed mind,
    as Hos 10:12 puts sowing and reaping before illumination. § 2, *Viam Domini
    non sufficit inire. Via Christus*: the way is not a chance, uncertain or
    wandering one but the way *in qua in lege Domini ambulatur*; many think
    they have entered a useful way and are not undefiled in it, because they do
    not walk in the Lord's law; the way must be not only entered but travelled
    (*non solum ineunda … sed etiam pergenda*), since no one on the road holds
    what it leads to (Phil 3:12–13); and the Lord teaches which way it is,
    *Ego sum via* (Jn 14:6). He who keeps to his precepts — curbing the vices of
    the flesh, taming the mind's wantonness, conquering the hunger of avarice,
    shunning the glory of earthly honours — *hic est immaculatus in via, et in
    lege Domini ambulans*. § 3: *in lege Domini* is not idle, for there is also
    a law of sin (Rom 7:23). Direct exegesis.
  - Cassiodorus, *Expositio Psalmorum*, Ps 118 preface and 118.1–2 (CCSL 98,
    pp. 1058–1060, artifact PDF pp. 417–419, restricted scan: described, not
    quoted). The whole psalm is spoken by the universal chorus of the saints,
    from the beginning of the world to its end, introduced as one speaker for
    the Church's unity. The plural *immaculati* refers to the faithful, not to
    Christ, who alone is spotless by nature; the saints become spotless when
    they receive pardon (Ps 50:9). *In via* is the Lord Saviour himself (Jn
    14:6), walked not with bodily feet but with the steps of good action; *qui
    ambulant in lege Domini* are those who either do not sin or by heavenly
    grace make worthy satisfaction; *Domini* distinguishes this law from the
    law of sin (Rom 7:23). Direct exegesis; ecclesiastical writer.
- **Later saintly and liturgical reception.** Berno, Rupert, Honorius, Sicard,
  Durandus, Fromage and Schuster on the Introit: § 4.4. Schuster (p. 175) calls
  the Introit "the sorrowful confession of guilt which leads back the sinner to
  the way of reconciliation" and reads its hope as the hope "which underlies the
  act of contrition". St Robert Bellarmine on Ps 118:1–3 (O'Sullivan, tracked
  text, physical lines 4127–4139): the prophet argues from happiness, man's
  last end; "Blessed are they who, in their journey through life, are not
  soiled by the mud or dirt of sin", and those are they "who walk in the law of
  the Lord"; "blessed" is eternal happiness and, as far as it can be had, a
  happy life here; "Christ himself informs us that the straight road to eternal
  happiness is the observance of his law. 'If thou wilt enter into life keep
  the commandments'"; "undefiled" means without mortal sin, not without venial
  sin, else none would qualify; "in the way" means through life, "most aptly
  called a way by reason of the constant changes in it"; the law of the Lord,
  a straight and clear path, is "opposed to the law of the flesh … full of the
  dust of pride, the mud of luxury, and the dirty water of avarice"; on v. 3 he
  takes up Augustine's question and answers that the saints who walk the way
  habitually and step off it by venial sin "quickly get on it again through
  penance and confession".
- **What the four say of the verse's two clauses.** *Immaculati in via*:
  Augustine, the condition without which the beatitude all want is not reached;
  Hilary, the first stage, morals made firm before illumination, on a way that
  is Christ and must be travelled to its end; Cassiodorus, the faithful made
  spotless by pardon, on the way that is Christ; Bellarmine, free of mortal sin
  on the journey of life, restored by penance. *Ambulant in lege Domini*:
  Augustine, the only way to beatitude, against evil roads to a false one;
  Hilary and Cassiodorus, the law of the Lord as against the law of sin
  (Rom 7:23); Bellarmine, the observance of the commandments, against the law
  of the flesh. Hilary and Cassiodorus identify the way with Christ; Augustine
  and Bellarmine do not in these places. None of them speaks of the Introit or
  of the Mass.
- **Discover leads** (`commentary-work-index discover`, Daniel 3:31, 3:29, 3:35,
  3:42–43, uncapped): Jerome held and read; Cornelius a Lapide held only for
  Paul and the Pentateuch; Hugh of Saint-Cher held, restricted, not opened;
  Hippolytus, Theodoret, Chrysostom (*Interpretatio in Danielem*), Albert,
  Lyra, Denis, the *Glossa* — none registered. For Ps 118:1 (uncapped):
  Augustine, Hilary, Cassiodorus and Bellarmine held and read; Theodoret (PG 80)
  and Athanasius (PG 27), held in remote Greek or Greek–Latin layers, and Hugh
  (restricted) — not opened, four direct witnesses having been read; Ambrose,
  Denis, Lyra, Peter Lombard, Prosper, Albert, Jerome's *Commentarioli*,
  Alcuin, Euthymius, Walafrid — none registered.
- **Use.** Jerome is the direct witness for the Introit's own words; Augustine
  joins the Daniel prayer to Ps 144 in his own exposition. Augustine, Hilary,
  Cassiodorus and Bellarmine are the direct witnesses for the verse.

### 3.2 Collect — *Largire, quaesumus, Domine* (no. 1690)

- No patristic exegesis is expected of a composed oration. Documented
  liturgical reception: Berno (PL 142 col. 1070) — after the penitent people's
  *Super flumina*, the cantor's *Omnia quae fecisti* and the responsory's
  *Oculi omnium*, *Propter haec omnia rogat sacerdos pium judicem, ut fidelibus
  suis largiatur indulgentiam placatus et pacem, ut pariter ab omnibus
  mundentur offensis et secura sibi mente deserviant*. Schuster (p. 175): peace
  comes after mercy, *Non est pax impio*, with *Pax nostra* and *Beati mundo
  corde*. Sicard (PL 213 col. 401) and Durandus (Lyon 1612 p. 410): *Captivitas illa corporalis
  nostram significat spiritualem. A captivitate reversio est peccatorum
  remissio* — said of the month's prophetic readings and this Sunday's office,
  not of the Collect by name.
- Sacramentary sources were not sought (§ 5.2): no claim depends on them.

### 3.3 Epistle — Eph 5:15–21 (no. 1691)

- **Direct ancient exegesis checked.**
  - St Jerome, *Commentarii in Epistolam ad Ephesios* III (PL 26 cols. 527–530;
    read on page images, PDF pp. 271–272). On v. 16 the wise man buys back the
    time that human malice had sold, *dies malos in bonos vertimus*, with Joseph
    and Job as men whom no change of times changed; a second explanation
    addresses the Ephesians under daily persecution: keep one course whatever the
    judge. On v. 18, one cannot be filled with wine and the Spirit, as one cannot
    serve two masters. On v. 19 the distinction of psalm, hymn and canticle, and
    *canere igitur et psallere, et laudare Dominum magis animo quam voce debemus
    … Audiant haec adolescentuli: audiant hi quibus psallendi in ecclesia
    officium est, Deo non voce, sed corde cantandum: nec in tragoedorum modum
    guttur et fauces dulci medicamine colliniendas, ut in ecclesia theatrales
    moduli audiantur*; a singer of poor voice with good works *dulcis apud Deum
    cantor est*. On v. 20 thanksgiving in adversity — *si domus corruerit, si
    amantissima uxor et filii vel captivitate … intercepti sint* — is the
    Christian's proper virtue, saying *Benedictus Deus, minora me scio sustinere
    quam mereor: haec ad mea peccata parva sunt*. On v. 21, bishops and priests
    are to be subject to those under them.
  - St John Chrysostom, *Hom. 19 in Eph.* (NPNF1-13, tracked CCEL text lines
    13166–13725). "The time is not yours. At present ye are strangers, and
    sojourners, and foreigners, and aliens"; redeem it by giving up what
    persecutors demand, "only preserve the principal thing, I mean the faith";
    the days are evil because of what men do in them; "learn to sing psalms …
    they who sing psalms are filled with the Holy Spirit"; "with your hearts"
    means with attention; give thanks in disease and penury, as at the
    physician's knife and cautery.
  - St Thomas Aquinas, *Super Eph.* c. 5 lect. 6–7 (Paris: Dessain 1857, vol. 2,
    pp. 344–345; tracked OCR lines 36200–36420). Two expositions of *redimentes
    tempus*: forgo some of one's right, as one buys off a vexation, because
    since Adam snares lie ready (*prima expositio est melior*); or make up time
    lost in sin by more good works. Prudence takes the will of God as its first
    principle (*Fiat voluntas tua*).
- **Liturgical reception.** Rupert, Sicard, Durandus, Honorius and Berno read
  the Epistle with the wedding-feast Gospel of their Mass (§ 2.1) and are cited
  only for what they say of the Epistle itself: Honorius *sub lege*, that the
  returned exiles redeemed the time from enemies with money and arms; Fromage,
  the circumspection of "those evil days" of the last times and Christ who
  "bought time … at a great price"; Schuster, autumn, and evil days as old age
  and death.
- **Discover leads:** Jerome's commentary is "cataloged-only" under its own work
  record but held in PL 26 (container entry partial) and was read there;
  Chrysostom held (NPNF1-13) and read; Aquinas held and read; Theodoret, held
  only inside a remote PG 82 layer, not opened; Theophylact of Ohrid, held
  inside a remote PG 124 Latin OCR layer — recorded in
  `author-standing-v1.toml` as an ecclesiastical writer, not as a writer outside
  Catholic communion (the maintainer's settlement of 2026-09-23 under decision
  D3) — not opened in this research: three direct witnesses to the pericope,
  two of them Fathers, had been read, and the lead remains open; Ambrosiaster
  held only for 1 Cor;
  Cornelius a Lapide held (Pauline epistles), not opened; Hugh of Saint-Cher
  restricted, not opened; Lyra, Peter Lombard, Denis, Haymo, Bruno, Estius —
  none registered.

### 3.4 Gradual — Ps 144:15–16 (no. 1692)

- **Direct ancient exegesis checked.**
  - St Augustine, *Enarr. in Ps.* 144.19–21 (PL 37 cols. 1881–1882, page image
    PDF p. 434; English NPNF1-8, tracked lines 66690–66760). V. 15: *Prorsus
    tanquam reficis aegrotum in opportunitate, quando debet accipere, tunc das*
    — God gives as one feeds a sick man, at the time he ought to receive; if a
    just request is not granted, let him not faint: *differs, non aufers*; Job
    and Paul as men who asked and waited. V. 17: the saints praise God's justice
    before asking his gifts, as Daniel did (§ 3.1).
  - St Hilary of Poitiers, *Tract. in Ps.* 144 (PL 9 cols. 863–864; tracked OCR
    lines 68740–68830; col. 863 re-read on the page image, PDF p. 448). Those
    cast down look to God with all their eyes; all the supports of life are
    his, food in its season and the filling of souls with spiritual gifts. On
    v. 18 (unsung): God is near because by the power of his nature he is wholly
    everywhere (*ubique semper est … totus semper et ubique*).
  - St John Chrysostom, *Expositio in Ps.* 144 on vv. 15–16 (PG 55 cols.
    470–471; Greek read in the registered remote OCR layer, physical lines
    63680–64015, its column figures confirmed on the Internet Archive leaf
    images n460 and n463; the Latin column of that layer is not legible). He
    reads the verses of God's providence over all: the food given *ἐν εὐκαιρίᾳ*
    is the Gospel's sun rising on evil and good (Mt 5:45); not rains, earth or
    air but God's command brings fruits to ripeness; "in due season" means
    either that each thing is apportioned to its season across the year, so
    that the farmer rests and the produce does not spoil, or that he gives food
    to those who need it; the "hand" is his working and supplying power,
    against those who worshipped air and sun as the givers of fruit; he fills
    every living thing according to what is useful and pleasing to each. A
    literal, providential reading; nothing eucharistic.
  - Cassiodorus, *Expositio Psalmorum* 144.15–16 (CCSL 98 p. 1295, artifact PDF
    p. 654, restricted scan, read on the page image: described, not quoted).
    These verses open the "place" of God's justice: those who do not cease to
    hope in him receive from him the gifts of fullness; the food is whatever
    sustains each thing, bodily or spiritual, all of it the Creator's dispensed
    bounty; *in tempore opportuno* is added lest anyone think that what is asked
    must always be given — many ask and are happily deferred, so let us pray
    that he give what he knows to be expedient (Rom 8:26); the "animal" filled
    with blessing is better taken as the rational creature. Ecclesiastical
    writer.
- **Later saintly reception.** St Robert Bellarmine, *Explanation of the Psalms*
  (O'Sullivan English; tracked text line 5073): God gives "in due season … as the
  physician will sometimes prescribe total abstinence"; the open hand.
  Schuster (p. 175): the verse "has a marked eucharistic significance".
  Durandus (VI.137): the Gradual asks the food of the eternal nuptials;
  Berno: the responsory "already aspiring to God's banquet". The nuptial and
  banquet terms come from the wedding-feast Gospel of their Mass (§ 2.1) and
  are not carried into this formulary's readings.
- **Discover leads:** Augustine, Hilary, Chrysostom (PG 55) and Cassiodorus
  (read in CCSL 98; the PL 70 OCR layer's Ps 144 is not legible) held and read;
  Theodoret (PG 80) and Hugh (restricted) — held, not opened; others none
  registered.

### 3.5 Alleluia — Ps 107:2 (no. 1693)

- **Direct ancient exegesis.** St Augustine gives no exposition of Ps 107
  because it repeats Pss 56 and 59 (NPNF1-8 lines 54215–54266), and reads its
  titles "in the depth of prophecy". On Ps 56:8–9 (NPNF1-8 lines 23610–23640):
  "the patience of good men with preparation of heart accepteth the will of God:
  and glorieth in tribulations"; Paul in chains was "singing and playing"; *Rise
  up, my glory* is the glorified Jesus after his Passion, the psaltery his
  miracles and the harp his sufferings. The Latin of *Enarr.* 107.1 (PL 37
  col. 1430, page image PDF p. 208) was read for its psalter text (§ 1.3).
- Cassiodorus, *Expositio Psalmorum* 107.1–4 (CCSL 98 pp. 986–987, artifact PDF
  pp. 345–346, restricted scan, read on the text layer and the page image of
  p. 987: described, not quoted). The psalm is woven from the ends of Pss 56
  and 59 but led to another sense by its new title; Christ the King speaks
  throughout, now as man, now in the power of the Word. On v. 2 the readiness of
  heart is the holiness of the incarnation, with no discord of sin to hinder his
  praise of the Father; we say the verse rightly when we trust that our sins
  are forgiven by his gift; in him word and deed agreed, a psaltery with no
  string missing. On v. 3 the "glory" is shown by psaltery and cithara — upright
  conduct and bodily affliction, his deeds and his glorious Passion — and
  *exsurgam diluculo* is the Resurrection at dawn, said also of any faithful
  who rises early to praise the Lord in church. On v. 4 the confession among
  the peoples and the psalm among the nations are the Church of the Jews first
  and then of the Gentiles. Direct exegesis; ecclesiastical writer.
- **Later saintly reception.** Bellarmine on Ps 56:8 (tracked line 2055):
  "ready to live, ready to die … ready to take anything cheerfully from your
  hand"; on Ps 107, that it repeats 56 and 59. Schuster (p. 176) reads *glória
  mea* as God addressed: "God is called the glory of the soul".
- **Discover leads:** Augustine and Cassiodorus (CCSL 98; the PL 70 OCR layer
  is not legible at this psalm) held and read; Theodoret, Athanasius (PG 27),
  Hugh — held, not opened; others none registered.

### 3.6 Gospel — Jn 4:46–53 (no. 1694)

- **Direct ancient exegesis checked.**
  - St Augustine, *Tract. in Io.* 16 (NPNF1-7, tracked CCEL text lines
    8462–8696). §§ 1–3: the passage needs commending more than expounding; the
    Samaritans believed his word without signs, the Galileans did not; the ruler
    shows "a man lukewarm, or cold in faith, or of no faith at all", who "had not
    yet believed" when he asked, since *et credidit ipse* comes after the hour is
    known; *prodigium* "as if it were porrodicium … what betokens something to
    come"; "let us assume the Lord's own country to mean the people of the Jews …
    We have heard the gospel … we have seen no signs, none do we demand". § 4:
    Thomas rebuked as the ruler was; "Blessed are they that have not seen". § 5:
    "the centurion declared himself unworthy of His presence" and, "an alien,
    believed me able to work by a word", while "the ruler sought His presence
    by force": "Here is a ceding to loftiness; there, a conceding to
    humility"; the broken branches and the wild olive; "the root remains … in the
    patriarchs … in rest with God, in great honor". § 6: many from east and west.
    § 7: *My mother is Sion* (Ps 86) — let him have honour in the country he
    founded.
  - St Gregory the Great, *Hom. in Ev.* 28 (Wikisource Latin, tracked, lines
    349–357; PL 76 cols. 1210–1213, read on page images of the restricted DCO
    facsimile, PDF pp. 68–70). Preached at the basilica of SS. Nereus and
    Achilleus on their feast. § 1: the man who asked salvation for his son
    believed, yet doubted in part: *Corporalem ergo praesentiam Domini quaerebat,
    qui per spiritum nusquam deerat … Si enim perfecte credidisset, procul dubio
    sciret quia non esset locus ubi non esset Deus … solo jussu salutem reddidit
    qui voluntate omnia creavit*. § 2: he would not go in body to the ruler's son
    but offered to go to the centurion's servant, which rebukes our pride that
    honours wealth and rank in men rather than the nature made in God's image:
    *Ecce de coelo venit qui servo in terra occurrere non despicit*. § 3: *Nolite
    ergo in vobismetipsis pensare quod habetis, sed quid estis*; the martyrs at
    whose tomb they stand trampled a flourishing world; *Ecce jam mundus in
    seipso aruit, et adhuc in cordibus nostris floret … ut ipse nos jam mundus
    mittat ad Deum*. Elsewhere (*Hom. in Ev.* 4.3, Wikisource Latin line 49) Gregory says
    visible miracles were given to draw hearts to faith in the invisible, and
    *linguae in signum sunt non fidelibus, sed infidelibus*.
  - St John Chrysostom, *Hom. 35 in Io.* (NPNF1-14 printed pp. 123–125, PDF
    pp. 143–145; read in the text layer and on the page image of p. 124). The
    nobleman is not Matthew's centurion, by rank and by faith; his coming was a
    mark of faith, yet "if this ruler also believed, yet he believed not entirely
    or soundly", as his asking the hour shows; "He healeth the father, sick in
    mind, no less than the son"; "miracles are not for the faithful, but for the
    unbelieving"; Christ went to the centurion because his faith was perfected;
    "What now are we taught … Not to wait for miracles"; thanks are owed in a
    child's sickness as in health.
  - St Cyril of Alexandria, *Comm. in Io.* II.5 (Pusey, LFC 1874, printed
    pp. 232–236; remote OCR layer, read). The nobleman "cometh as to One able to
    heal, but he understandeth not yet that He is by Nature God"; "Feeble indeed
    unto understanding is the nobleman … a child in his petition"; "The one
    command of the Saviour healeth two souls. For in the nobleman it worketh
    unwonted faith, the child it rescueth from bodily death"; the sequence of
    chapters shows "the extreme readiness of the aliens to obedience" against the
    Jews of Jerusalem, so that Israel "falleth from the hope, and the fulness of
    the Gentiles is brought in", and "when the fulness of the Gentiles is come
    in, then shall all Israel be saved".
- **Medieval and later saintly reception.**
  - St Thomas Aquinas, *Super Io.* c. 4 lect. 7 (Venice 1745, tomus 3,
    pp. 521–524; tracked OCR lines 67318–67725). Literal: the ruler is an
    official of Herod or of the Emperor, not the centurion (four differences).
    *Allegorice autem regulus ille, Abraham, vel aliquis ex patribus veteris
    testamenti dicitur … Filius ejus est populus Judaeorum*, sick with depraved
    pleasures and teachings, in Capharnaum, "abundance", the cause of departing
    from God (Deut 32:15); *Sic et patres veteris testamenti rogabant pro populo
    Israel*, as Jeremiah in 2 Macc 15:14. *Moraliter* the king is reason, a
    "little king" when diminished, whose son, the affections, falls sick. The
    ruler's faith had two defects — he did not believe Christ's divine power,
    *cum Deus ubique sit praesens* (Jer 23:24), and, after Chrysostom, he
    doubted; Gregory's and Chrysostom's reasons for going to the servant and not
    the son; *Vade*: dispose yourself to grace by the movement of free will;
    *Credidit … et ibat proficiendo in fide … in via Dei non proficere, deficere
    est*; the seventh hour signifies the seven gifts of the Spirit, through whom
    sins are remitted, and the rest of the seventh day; his faith grew by stages
    to perfection (Prov 4:18).
  - St Anthony of Padua, Sunday sermon on Jn 4:46–53 (English translation by
    S. R. P. Spilsbury, copyright; restricted compilation, read). Any faithful
    person is a "ruler" over himself; the son is the soul; Capharnaum, "field of
    fatness" or "farm of consolation", is where the soul falls sick; "The Gloss
    says … as if otherwise he could not save, unless he were present. Therefore
    the Lord healed by his mere command"; after the Gloss, Christ would not go to
    the ruler's son "lest he seem to honour wealth"; he believed first and then

### 3.7 Offertory — Ps 136:1 (no. 1695)

- **Direct ancient exegesis checked.**
  - St Augustine, *Enarr. in Ps.* 136 (Latin PL 37 cols. 1761–1774, the
    opening and close read on the page images of PDF pp. 374 and 380; English NPNF1-8, tracked lines
    63899–64101, abridged). "The waters of Babylon are all things which here are
    loved, and pass away"; the citizens of Jerusalem, "understanding their
    captivity", sit by the waters, not in them, "humbling ourselves"; weep "in the
    remembrance of Sion"; their instruments are "God's Scriptures, God's
    commands, God's promises, meditation on the life to come", hung on the
    willows, which are "unfruitful trees … men greedy, covetous, barren in good
    works", because they are unworthy to carry them; the captors who demand a song
    are the devil and his angels; Babylon's little ones are evil desires, dashed
    against the Rock, Christ. Latin opening (§§ 1–2): *Si cives sumus de
    Jerusalem … in ista Babylonia non cives habitamus sed captivi detinemur;
    oportet ut non tantum ista decantemus, sed et faciamus*. Close (§ 22, PL 37
    col. 1774): *Fratres, organa in operando non cessent; vobis invicem cantate
    cantica Sion*.
  - St Hilary of Poitiers, *Tract. in Ps.* 136 (PL 9 cols. 777–784, the whole
    tractate; tracked OCR lines 62139–62723: §§ 1–11 at lines 62139–62575 read
    in the first pass, §§ 12–14 at lines 62576–62723 in iteration 1; cols.
    777–779 and 782–784 read on the page images, PDF pp. 405–408). The psalm
    treats a spiritual rather than a bodily captivity; Sion is the seat of
    eternal beatitude and *mater coelestis nobis Jerusalem*, Babylon confusion;
    man is exiled in Adam's sin; he questions those who read everything
    literally (which rivers? which willows in the city?); the rivers are the
    works of the age and the body, which flow by; the willows, which revive in
    water, are the saints and faithful (Isa 44:4), revived by the word of God
    and the water of baptism; the instruments are human bodies, by whose honest
    movements *quae Deo placita sunt psallimus*, hung up in continence; the
    saint refuses to share the songs of Sion with the powers that hold him
    captive. He notes that with "the Hebrew elders" the psalm has no title.
    On vv. 7–9 (§§ 12–14, cols. 782–784): § 12, *Cupiditatum strages.
    Fundamentum nostrum Christus* — Edom is interpreted "earthly"; the sons of
    Edom are the vices of our earthly body, which rule the confusions of our
    senses (the Babylonians) to empty us of the fullness we received (Jn 1:16)
    down to the foundation, which is Christ (1 Cor 3:11), so that no memory of
    God and no hope remain. § 13, *Filia Babylonis caro*: he asks those who
    think the spiritual sense of the psalms idle how Babylon's proud daughter is
    now called wretched; the daughter of Babylon is the flesh of all, and
    blessed is he who repays her by subjecting his flesh to the rule of soul and
    spirit. § 14, *Cupiditates in ortu suo premendae*: the body's vices must not
    be let grow strong but killed at their birth, for grown desires are hard to
    destroy; blessed is he who checks the flesh's indecent appetites at their
    beginning and dashes them, to faith and the fear of God; *Petra autem,
    secundum Apostolum, Christus est (I Cor. x, 4). In quem beatus est, qui
    filiae Babylonis parvulos, id est, tenera adhuc corporis vitia, allidet et
    conteret.*
  - Cassiodorus, *Expositio Psalmorum* on Ps 136 (PL 70; remote OCR layer
    lines 75150–75300; the opening and close also read in CCSL 98, pp. 1231 and
    1234–1236, artifact PDF pp. 590 and 593–595, restricted scan). Not *in
    fluminibus* but *super flumina*: those already freed sit on the bank *in
    humilitate*; the willows are holy and faithful men (with Hilary), on whom
    *organa nostra*, the Scriptures, are hung when grace is shared;
    *Psalmodia est enim consolatio flentium, cura dolentium, sanitas aegrotorum;
    hoc animae remedium*. On vv. 7–9 (CCSL 98, described): Edom, "bloody" or
    "earthly", refers to carnal vices; its sons are the persecutors of the
    Christian people who never repented, and the prayer that God remember them
    is said in the spirit of prophecy, not as a wish to curse; their emptying
    "to the foundation" failed, because the foundation of the faithful is
    Christ and the martyrs were crowned; the daughter of Babylon is our flesh,
    to be repaid by fasting and tribulation; the little ones are the flesh's
    errors while still small, to be held and dashed at once on the rock, the
    Lord Saviour (1 Cor 10:4), as Ps 100:8 kills sinners in the morning.
    Ecclesiastical writer.
  - St John Chrysostom, *Expositio in Ps.* 136 (PG 55 cols. 405–407; Greek read
    in the registered remote OCR layer, physical lines 53981–54460, its column
    figures confirmed on the Internet Archive leaf images n396–n399; the
    layer's Latin column is not legible). He expounds the psalm of the
    historical captives. Their longing for the city was great: while they held
    its goods they scorned them, and God cast them out to lead them to a
    greater desire, as he often does, so that deprivation may teach us to seek
    what we enjoyed without perceiving it. They sat by the rivers as captives
    camped outside walls; God let them carry their instruments as reminders of
    their former worship; affliction made them strong, so that those who had
    mocked the weeping prophets now wept unbidden and kept the law among
    barbarians who begged to hear their songs, refusing because profane ears
    should not hear those mysteries; they wept for the loss of their worship,
    not for their slavery. On vv. 5–6 he applies it: as they sought the city
    once they had lost it, so many of us will seek the Jerusalem above on that
    day, but for us there will be no return (Isa 66:24; Mk 9:44); therefore order the
    present life so as not to become captives and strangers to that
    metropolis. On vv. 8–9: though the words are full of wrath and punishment,
    they are the captives' passion; the prophets often speak not their own mind
    but the sufferings of others, and here he sets out the desire of the
    captives of the Exile, whose anger reached even to infants; the New
    Covenant's commands are otherwise (*ἀλλ᾽ οὐ τὰ τῆς Καινῆς τοιαῦτα*): we are
    to give our enemies drink and food and to pray for those who abuse us,
    because our justice must exceed that of the scribes and Pharisees (Mt
    5:20). He gives the close no figurative sense. Direct exegesis; Father. The
    English here is a description, not a translation.
  - St Jerome, *Comm. in Dan.* 1:7 (tracked line 6): the captors changed
    the youths' names so they should not keep Jewish names *in terra
    captivitatis*, citing *Quomodo cantabimus canticum Domini in terra aliena*.
    Reuse.
- **Later saintly reception.** Bellarmine (tracked lines 4827–4846): literally
  the captives along Babylon's rivers; "in a spiritual sense … God's elect, who
  are held here below in captivity … citizens of the Jerusalem above"; the
  willows are "unproductive trees" (with Augustine); "though the citizens of the
  celestial Jerusalem cannot rejoice and sing with the Babylonians … still they
  sing, in their hearts, to God"; they also "sing the song of the Lord in a
  strange land, who sing sacred hymns … to please the ear". On vv. 7–9
  (physical line 4843) he gives three readings. Literally, David foretells the
  destruction of the Idumeans, who urged the Babylonians on, and of Babylon,
  whose conquerors, the Medes and Persians, would "dash thy little ones against
  the rock"; "remember" means remember to punish. Then: "All this has a
  spiritual meaning. First, in an allegorical sense, looking upon the Idumeans
  as the Jews, and the Babylonians as the pagans", who sought to tear the
  Church up from its foundations; idolatry was supplanted "not by violence or
  force of arms, but by the preaching of God's word", and the pagans' children,
  converted in great numbers, are the little ones brought "to the rock, Christ,
  to get a fortunate dash against it, and die the death of the old man, to rise
  a new man". "Secondly, … in a moral point of view, we may look upon the
  Idumeans as representing the carnal, and the Babylonians as the evil
  spirits, and it is more in the spirit of the Psalm; for … the captivity of
  Babylon was a type of the captivity of mankind"; at the judgment Christ, the
  King of Jerusalem, will bind the evil spirit; and "blessed is he, who, by a
  happy dash on the rock, kills sin" in those not yet deeply stained by it.
  Schuster (p. 177): the waters are the passions; the just man sits sadly
  beside them.
- **Discover leads:** Augustine, Hilary, Cassiodorus, Chrysostom (PG 55) and
  Bellarmine held and read; Theodoret (PG 80), Athanasius (PG 27), Hugh — held,
  not opened; Lyra, Peter Lombard, Prosper, Albert, Jerome's *Commentarioli*,
  Euthymius, Walafrid, Denis — none registered.

### 3.9 Communion — Ps 118:49–50 (no. 1697)

- **Direct ancient exegesis checked.**
  - St Augustine, *Enarr. in Ps.* 118 s. 15.1–2 (PL 37 cols. 1540–1542, the
    opening read on the page image of PDF p. 263; English NPNF1-8, tracked lines
    57590–57640). Lemma *Memento verbi tui servo tuo, in quo spem dedisti mihi. Haec me consolata est in humilitate mea, quoniam verbum tuum vivificavit me*.
    God does not forget; "O remember" displays the desire of one who asks for
    what was promised; "wherein Thou hast given me hope", that is, in thy word;
    the humiliation is tribulation that pride deserved or patience proves; "This

## 4. Material disagreements preserved

### 4.1 Did the ruler believe before the sign?

- Augustine: he "had not yet believed" when he asked; lukewarm, cold, or of no
  faith (*Tract.* 16.3).
- Gregory: he believed, else he would not have asked salvation of one he did
  not believe a saviour, but he doubted, seeking bodily presence (*Hom.* 28.1).
- Chrysostom: coming was a mark of faith, but he believed "not entirely or
  soundly" (*Hom.* 35.2).
- Cyril: he came to one "able to heal" but not yet known as God by nature;
  "feeble … a child in his petition".
- Aquinas: two defects — not believing the divine power that is everywhere, and
  (after Chrysostom) doubting; his faith grew by four stages.
- Schuster: "the ruler … had faith in Jesus"; Christ purified it of material
  and self-interested elements.

Augustine and Gregory answer differently and say so in their own terms; the
others stand between. On what followed, the six do not say the same thing.
Augustine, Chrysostom, Cyril and Aquinas place a fuller faith after the word:
Augustine reads *et credidit ipse* as the ruler's first believing; Chrysostom
and Cyril make the one command heal the father's mind with the son; Aquinas
traces faith growing by stages to perfection. Gregory says that the ruler
believed and doubted and does not speak of his faith's completion. On *Nisi
signa*: Chrysostom ("thus too He the more drew him on to belief"), Cyril
(Christ "benefiteth even the stumbling"), Schuster (Christ "desiring first of
all to purify his faith") and, in part, Aquinas (the rebuke is directed at one
instructed in the law who sought signs rather than Scripture) present the
rebuke as serving the ruler's faith. Augustine reads it as exposing a man
"lukewarm, or cold in faith, or of no faith at all" and turns it against the
country that needs signs (*Tract.* 16.3, 16.5); Gregory does not treat the
rebuke as serving his faith. No study may credit Augustine or Gregory with
that view.

### 4.2 Who is the ruler in figure, and what is his house?

- Augustine: Christ's own country, Galilee, figures the Jews, who saw signs and
  scarcely believed; the Samaritans, who believed without signs, figure the
  nations; the ruler belongs to the country that needed signs. The root, the
  patriarchs, "remains … in great honor".
- Cyril: the ruler stands with the Samaritans among "the aliens" whose readiness
  to obey contrasts with the Jews of Jerusalem in Jn 5; "when the fulness of the
  Gentiles is come in, then shall all Israel be saved".
- Aquinas: allegorically the ruler is Abraham or one of the Old Testament
  Fathers, who adhere to the great King by faith, and his sick son is the Jewish
  people; the Fathers prayed for Israel. Morally, reason is the king.
- Berno: the house that believed is the Church gathered from the Gentiles.
- Anthony: any faithful person who rules himself; the son, the soul.
- Durandus: kings and prelates, who must be humble.
- Fromage: the dying son is a world near its end; the father is the Church's
  pastors.

Augustine's and Aquinas's identifications of the ruler cannot both stand in one
reading: for Augustine he belongs to the people needing signs, for Aquinas he
figures the patriarch interceding for that people. `research/interpretations.md`
keeps them as a narrower disagreement inside reading 3 and does not merge them.

### 4.3 The willows and the instruments of Ps 136

- Augustine and Bellarmine: the willows are barren men of Babylon, unworthy of
  the instruments, which are the Scriptures, commands and promises.
- Hilary: the willows are the saints and faithful, revived by water and the
  word; the instruments are human bodies, hung up in continence.
- Cassiodorus: the willows are holy men (with Hilary); the instruments are the
  Scriptures (with Augustine), hung on them when grace is shared.

A real disagreement of identification within a shared spiritual reading.

### 4.4 Whose voice sings the Introit and the Offertory?

- Rupert: the voices of the Fathers grieving for those who were called and would
  not come; the Offertory's hung harps are preachers silenced by barren
  hearers. His whole assignment reads the wedding-feast parable of his Mass
  (Mt 22:1–14) into the chants (§ 2.1); only the prophets' voice in the
  Introit and the silenced preachers of the Offertory may be cited, without the
  parable.
- Berno: the penitent people sing *Super flumina*; the cantor confesses with
  *Omnia quae fecisti* to placate the just Judge.
- Honorius (*sub lege*): the exiles returned from Babylon who saw their city
  destroyed; the Offertory is their exultation at returning.
- Sicard and Durandus: the Introit is Daniel's voice recalling the past captivity
  and ascribing it to their deserts by God's judgment; in the Offertory "we
  lament that captivity"; the Gradual gives thanks; the Communion asks to receive
  in reality the nuptials promised in hope — the last, again, the wedding-feast
  Gospel of their Mass (§ 2.1); only "what was promised in hope" may be used.
- Fromage: the Jewish people, repentant, in the Introit and Offertory; the
  Gentiles in the Gradual and Communion. He builds the two choirs on "the Gospel
  of last Sunday", the wedding feast (§ 2.1); they may be cited only as his
  assignment of the shared chants, without the marriage-feast frame.
- Augustine (on Ps 136, not of the Mass): the citizens of Jerusalem now
  captive, the Church; Jerome (on Dan 3:29): the innocent youths speaking in the
  person of the people.

### 4.5 What is it to redeem the time?

- Jerome: by good works, buying back time sold by malice, turning evil days to
  good; or holding one course under changing persecutors.
- Chrysostom: by yielding what persecutors demand and keeping the faith; "the
  time is not yours".
- Aquinas: both; he prefers the first (forgoing some right because snares lie
  ready since Adam), and gives the second as making up time lost in sin.
- Honorius *sub lege*: the returned exiles ransomed themselves from enemies.

### 4.6 Voice or heart in singing

Jerome: *Deo non voce, sed corde cantandum*, against theatrical singing in
church. Chrysostom: "learn to sing psalms", and "with your hearts" means with
attention. Bellarmine on Ps 136:4 censures the same theatrical singing. The
difference is of emphasis: none forbids the voice; Jerome subordinates it.

### 4.7 Is the ruler the centurion of Mt 8?

Chrysostom and Aquinas: no, and Aquinas gives four differences. Augustine and
Gregory compare the two episodes without identifying them.

### 4.8 Where the checked witnesses do not disagree

All the Fathers read on the Gospel agree that the healing is by word without
bodily presence; how they treat the ruler's faith before and after the word is
not uniform (§ 4.1). Augustine, Hilary and Cassiodorus, with Bellarmine, read
Ps 136 spiritually of present exile and the heavenly Jerusalem; Chrysostom
expounds it of the historical captives and draws from it the danger of being
shut out of the Jerusalem above (§ 3.7). Every witness read on Ps 118:49
(Augustine, Hilary, Cassiodorus, Bellarmine, Durandus) takes "remember" as
asking God to fulfil a promise, not to cure a lapse of memory.

### 4.9 The close of Ps 136 (vv. 7–9, unsung)

The Offertory sings v. 1 only; the psalm's close is part of its complete
context (§ 1.2). Five checked witnesses read it, at § 3.7's loci.

- **Augustine** (NPNF1-8, *Enarr.* 136.10–12): the children of Edom are carnal
  men, servants of the devil, who cried to raze the Church to the foundation;
  the daughter of Babylon is the city that succeeds the old Babylon; her little
  ones are evil desires at their birth, to be dashed while small "against the
  Rock; and that Rock is Christ".
- **Hilary** (*Tract.* 136.12–14): Edom, "earthly", the body's vices that would
  empty us down to the foundation, Christ; the daughter of Babylon, the flesh;
  her little ones, the body's vices while still tender, dashed on the rock that
  is Christ (1 Cor 10:4).
- **Cassiodorus** (CCSL 98 pp. 1234–1236): Edom's sons, carnal men who
  persecuted the Christian people, the prayer against them said in the spirit
  of prophecy and not of cursing; the daughter of Babylon, our flesh; the
  little ones, the flesh's errors while small, dashed on the rock, Christ.
- **Bellarmine** (physical line 4843): first the literal prophecy — the
  Idumeans and Babylon punished, the Medes and Persians dashing Babylon's
  children — then "All this has a spiritual meaning": allegorically the
  Idumeans are the Jews and the Babylonians the pagans, whose children,
  converted, are brought "to the rock, Christ, to get a fortunate dash against
  it, and die the death of the old man, to rise a new man"; morally the
  Idumeans are the carnal and the Babylonians the evil spirits, the captivity
  of Babylon "a type of the captivity of mankind", and blessed is he who "by a
  happy dash on the rock, kills sin".
- **Chrysostom** (PG 55 cols. 405–407): the words are the captives' own
  passion, which the prophet sets out and does not make his own; the New
  Covenant commands love of enemies. He reads no figure in the close.

What is shared and what differs. Augustine, Hilary, Cassiodorus and
Bellarmine (in his moral sense) agree that the little ones dashed on the rock
are sins or desires killed while young against Christ, the Rock. Bellarmine
alone adds a literal prophecy of the Medes and Persians and an allegory in
which the little ones are pagans' children converted to Christ; and his
allegory identifies the Idumeans with the Jews, an identification that is his
and may be reported only as his, never used of any people. Augustine,
Cassiodorus and Bellarmine's allegory take Edom as persecutors of the Church;
Hilary takes Edom as the body's earthly vices. Chrysostom stands apart: he
gives the close no figurative sense, reads it as the voice of the captives'
anger, and sets the Gospel's command against it.

### 7.4 The psalms: attribution, occasion and composition kept apart

Checked against the psalms' own Vulgate titles in the tracked Clementine and
against the record:

- **Ps 144 (Gradual).** Title *Laudatio ipsi David*. The record gives the
  traditional attribution to David (disputed), David's reign in the usual
  chronology as its reference point, no occasion, and the Psalter's composition
  boundary. The reign is the attribution's reference point, not an occasion or a
  composition date.
- **Ps 107 (Alleluia).** Title *Canticum Psalmi, ipsi David*. Same answer as Ps
  144. Its verses repeat Pss 56:8–12 and 59:7–14, whose titles name occasions —
  Ps 56 *cum fugeret a facie Saul in speluncam*, Ps 59 the campaign against Syria
  and Edom — but those occasions belong to the source psalms and not to Ps 107,
  whose title names none; Augustine reads Ps 107's title prophetically, not
  historically (§ 3.5). The occasion stays unresolved.
- **Ps 118 (Introit verse, Communion).** Title *Alleluja* only; the Clementine
  names no author. The record's Davidic attribution (disputed) is the corpus's
  tradition-level claim, not the psalm's title. No occasion; unresolved.
- **Ps 136 (Offertory).** Title *Psalmus David, Jeremiæ*. The record gives a
  setting — the Babylonian captives' lament "For the time of Jeremias, and the
  captivity of Babylon" (Haydock) — and the seventy years as a duration, with
  the destruction of Jerusalem as historical background, and no traditional
  attribution claim. Hilary reports that with "the Hebrew elders" the psalm has
  no title (§ 3.7). The setting is a setting, not a composition date.

### 7.5 Limits the page-2 dossier must preserve

- The Introit has no element-wide date; its explanatory row gives Daniel and the
  psalm verse separately.
- Daniel's composition claim has no year; the deuterocanonical Prayer of
  Azariah is "long after the Exile" in the CE's own words. Jerome reports that
  the passage is not in the Hebrew and that the Churches read Daniel in
  Theodotion's version (§ 3.1); that is reception, not a date.
- "Seventy years" is a duration and never a date.
- The Gospel's event year is derived (A.U.C. 779–780) and is displayed as

- Ephesians: the traditional dates (58–63; 61) and the critical comparison
  (c. A.D. 80–100) stay in their own labels; the comparison is not the default
  answer.
- The psalms' Davidic attribution is disputed in the record, and no occasion is
  known for Pss 107, 118 or 144. Ps 136's setting (Babylonian captivity) is a
  setting, not a composition date; its historical-background anchor dates belong
