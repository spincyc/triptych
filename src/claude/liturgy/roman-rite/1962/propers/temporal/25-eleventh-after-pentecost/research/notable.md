# Notable-and-quotable record — verified afterlives and rejected leads

Element keys touched: `epistle`, `gospel`, `introit`. The published gallery
carries four entries; a fifth verified but borderline candidate is recorded
here as a spare and not published. Full audit table in `scope.md`.

## Verified and published

1. **William Morris, "born out of my due time" (Epistle, 1 Cor 15:8).**
   *The Earthly Paradise* (London: F. S. Ellis, 1868), prefatory "Apology,"
   stanza 4 (p. 2 of the first edition): "Dreamer of dreams, born out of my
   due time, / Why should I strive to set the crooked straight? …" Verified
   in the archive.org first-edition scan (item earthlyparadise00morr); the
   Apology is NOT in the Project Gutenberg Part II e-text (checked). Turn:
   Paul's self-abasing apology for his late, violent apostolic vision becomes
   a Victorian aestheticist manifesto of withdrawal — untimeliness now means
   being a medieval dreamer stranded in the industrial age, and the apostolic
   claim is inverted into a disclaimer of any power to "set the crooked
   straight." Rights: PD (1868; Morris d. 1896). Limit: dependence is on the
   KJV/Douay idiom "born out of due time" generally, not on the Mass lection;
   Morris alters it with "my."
2. **"Effeta, The Body Deodorant" (Gospel, Mark 7:34, via the baptismal
   rite).** Advertisement, Willis Pike Co. Laboratories (129 W. 31st St., New
   York), *Perth Amboy Evening News* (N.J.), June 19, 1912, ed. 2, p. 2:
   "For That Embarrassing Odor of Perspiration Get a Box of Effeta, The Body
   Deodorant. 'Be thou opened unto odor of sweetness.'" Verified in the
   Library of Congress Chronicling America page images/OCR; campaign
   corroborated in the same paper May 29–June 22, 1912 (e.g. June 5, p. 5)
   and in the *Norwich Bulletin* (Conn.), June 22, 1912, p. 6. Turn: the
   product name is the liturgical Latin form of the Gospel word, and the
   slogan splices Christ's healing command onto the rite's *in odorem
   suavitatis* — the sacramental opening of ears and nostrils becomes the
   opening of sweat pores for 25 cents a box. Rights: PD (1912 US
   newspapers). Limit: the copywriter's proximate source is the baptismal
   Ephpheta formula (itself quoting this Sunday's Gospel), not the Mass
   lection; June 19 is the issue where the scriptural slogan line OCRs
   cleanly, and the wording was read in context.
3. **Cromwell at Dunbar (Introit psalm verse, Ps 67:2).** At the moment of
   victory, 3 September 1650, as the sun rose over the sea, Cromwell
   exclaimed "Now let God arise, and his enemies shall be scattered" —
   eyewitness report of Captain John Hodgson, *Original Memoirs… Life of Sir
   Henry Slingsby, and Memoirs of Capt. Hodgson* (Edinburgh, 1806),
   pp. 147–48 (editor's footnote: "Psalm lxviii. v. 1"; a few lines later,
   "I heard him say, 'I profess they run!'" — the 1806 OCR garbles
   "scattered"; the footnote and Carlyle's transcription control the
   reading). Relay: Carlyle, *Oliver Cromwell's Letters and Speeches*
   (Tauchnitz, 1861), vol. 2, pp. 332–33, adding Rous's metrical psalter
   version; bonus locus: to Barebone's Parliament, 4 July 1653, Cromwell read
   Psalm 68 aloud, Carlyle noting "We remember it ever since Dunbar morning"
   (Tauchnitz vol. 3, pp. 171–72). Turn: the Introit's psalm verse becomes a
   battlefield war-cry — uttered not as prayer but as the signal that the
   cavalry shock has succeeded — and in 1653 the same psalm is redeployed
   politically as warrant for a revolutionary constitution. Rights: PD.
   Limits: the wording is the English psalter/KJV, not the Douay; Hodgson's
   memoir is remembered speech printed in 1806 (edition attributed to Walter
   Scott), not a contemporary dispatch; Cromwell's own Dunbar letter does not
   contain the phrase.
4. **"Ephphatha" as deaf-work institutional name (Gospel, Mark 7:34).**
   (a) The Ephphatha Guild, an organization of "between fifty and sixty"
   deaf-mutes attached to St. Luke's, Scranton, with an affiliated secular
   "Penny Club" and fundraising fairs — *The Scranton Tribune*, Nov. 14,
   1895, p. 3, "EPHPHATHA GUILD SERVICES": "takes its name from the Greek
   word 'Ephphatha' 'Be opened' used by the Savior in healing a deaf mute"
   (the derivation documented in the source itself; the paper calls the
   Aramaic word "Greek"). (b) "Ephphatha Missions for the Deaf and Blind"
   and the Ephphatha Services Board of the American Lutheran Church, with an
   annual professional "Ephphatha Conference" — Council of Organizations
   Serving the Deaf, *Council Membership Directory 1969* (ERIC ED032704).
   Turn: a one-time miracle command becomes a durable institutional surname
   across denominations and decades — dues, boards, conferences; and in the
   era of the oralism-vs-sign controversy, deaf people organized under a word
   that commands the deaf to hear and speak. Rights: 1895 newspaper PD; the
   1969 directory is a freely distributed ERIC document, cited without long
   reproduction. Limit: both are church-affiliated bodies; they qualify as
   proper names of functioning institutions, not sermon reuse.

## Verified spare (not published)

5. **Sam Nguyen, *Ephphatha: the easiest, most rational, and natural
   anti-stuttering self-reeducation method*** (Pittsburgh: Dorrance, 1998;
   ISBN 0805942548). Verified from the archive.org catalog record (metadata
   only; lending item). The word that loosed "the string of his tongue"
   becomes the trade name of a self-help method promising the same result by
   technique. In copyright; and as essentially a title use it sits near the
   profile's bare-title exclusion. Held as a spare; dropped first; not
   published.

## Rejected leads and negative results

- **"By the grace of God I am what I am" (1 Cor 15:10) secular reuse —
  bounded negative.** A Wikisource insource search returns 32 hits, every one
  scriptural, patristic, or commentary. No source documents Popeye's "I yam
  what I yam" as dependent on 1 Cor 15:10 rather than Exod 3:14. Not run.
- **"He hath done all things well" (Mark 7:37) ironic/epitaph use — bounded
  negative.** Chronicling America full-corpus search (259 hits sampled by
  date and with epitaph/tombstone/surgeon refinements): the Oct. 1912
  cluster is a syndicated Sunday-school lesson on this very pericope
  (verified in *The Denison Review*, Oct. 23, 1912, p. 9 — devotional); the
  1855 *Liberator* hit is an index row for a devotional poem; the rest are
  consolation verse and religious columns. No verified register-changing use.
- **Prov 3:9–10 commercial/ironic use — negative.** "honored the Lord with
  his substance" hits are all religious papers; "presses shall burst out"
  leads resolve to stewardship columns (verified: *Evening Star*, May 30,
  1914, Christian Endeavor column). No advertisement or irony found.
- **Ps 67:7/68:6 "setteth the solitary in families" child-welfare register —
  rejected on evidence.** The promising Raleigh *News & Observer* (Nov. 18,
  1904, p. 1) hit is a synod address; the 1901/1910 clusters are syndicated
  charity-sermon matter. Register remains devotional in everything checked.
- **F. W. Farrar, *Ephphatha; or, the Amelioration of the World* (1880) —
  rejected:** sermons, excluded by the profile despite the socially extended
  title.
- **"Be thou opened" further newspaper hits:** two potentially secular hits
  (*New York Herald*, Nov. 12, 1922, magazine p. 102; *Evening Star*, Oct. 8,
  1935, p. 8) could not be reproduced in the page OCR — unverified, unusable.
- **Unrun leads (noted only):** *Ephphatha Inc. v. State Farm Lloyds* (a
  company named Ephphatha in a modern Texas federal docket); the Camisards'
  use of Psalm 68 as battle-psalm (would duplicate Dunbar's register); the
  Church of England "Ephphatha" deaf magazine (not located on archive.org).
  Byzantine Paschal use of Ps 67:2 excluded as liturgical, not
  register-changing.

Verification method: all Chronicling America page texts were pulled through
the Library of Congress full-text (word-coordinates) service via each page's
JSON resource record; quoted wording was read in context in the OCR or scan
text, not from aggregators or quotation sites.
