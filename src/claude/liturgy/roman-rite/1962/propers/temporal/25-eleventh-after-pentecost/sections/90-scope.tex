\section*{Appendix: Scope and Qualifications}

\textbf{As of 20 August 2026.} Everything below states the boundaries of this
guide as at that date. Retrieval mechanics, checksums, query detail, discarded
leads, and the full reception, notable-and-quotable and interpretive-proposal
audits are in \texttt{propers/verified.md} and \texttt{research/scope.md}
beside this document's source; they are not repeated here.

\textbf{Two editions, one body of research.} This guide is published in two
mechanically compiled forms from a single source leaf. The \work{Full PDF},
which is the canonical edition, prints the complete appointed formulary --- the
Latin of the 1962 Vatican \latin{editio typica} with the English of the
registered public-domain witnesses --- and then the full element-by-element
commentary. The \work{Synthesis PDF}, published at the same identity with a
\texttt{-synthesis} suffix, omits both of those and puts in their place an
integrated commentary organised by cross-proper claims. Everything else ---
the map, the dossier sheet, the two-page thematic movement, the source-grounded
synthesis, the notable gallery, the interpretive proposals and this apparatus
--- is identical in both. Neither is a separate study, and the synthesis
companion owns no research, prose or records of its own. Where a paragraph
below refers to a table or a collation, the full edition prints it and
\texttt{propers/verified.md} records it in either case.

\textbf{Edition and formulary identity.} The work is the temporal formulary
printed \latin{DOMINICA UNDECIMA post Pentecosten}, \latin{II classis}, on pp.\
390--392 of the 1962 \latin{editio typica} of the \latin{Missale Romanum}
(Vatican City: Typis Polyglottis Vaticanis), with marginal numbers 1542--1551.
Green follows the general rubrics for Sundays after Pentecost and is not
printed on those pages. The formulary appoints the \latin{Credo} and the
Preface of the Most Holy Trinity, and appoints no Tract, Sequence, second
oration, blessing or ritual text. Its boundaries are the Postcommunion of the
Tenth Sunday (no.\ 1541) and the heading \latin{DOMINICA DUODECIMA post
Pentecosten} (no.\ 1552).

\textbf{Text verification.} All ten elements, their headings, references,
wording, accents, punctuation, rank, rubrics and both formulary boundaries were
visually collated on 19 August 2026 against the CMAA facsimile of the Vatican
typical edition at 300 and 600 dpi --- and at 1200 dpi for the Gospel's
unaccented opening line --- and independently against page images of the 1962
Benziger \latin{editio iuxta typicam}. The two editions differ at the nine
points tabulated in the commentary; those are edition differences, not doubtful
readings, and nothing was blended or substituted. The appointed text was
separately collated against the Clementine Vulgate, and the eleven places where
it diverges are tabulated in the commentary as verified textual observations.
One further question is deliberately not published as a divergence: at
Mk.~7:34 the missal prints \latin{suspíciens in cælum} where two consulted
Clementine transcriptions print \latin{suscípiens}; those transcriptions may not
be independent and no printed Clementine facsimile was collated for the verse,
so the point is recorded in \texttt{research/scope.md} as an unresolved lead.
The Internet Archive OCR was used only to locate the formulary and controls no
published wording.

\textbf{What this guide is not.} It is a hand-missal companion, not a
liturgical book, a critical edition, a homily, an assembly sheet for one civil
date, or a substitute for the sources cited. It does not treat occurrence,
commemorations, particular calendars, or celebration-mode branches for any
year; those belong to the edition-specific assembly workflow and require their
own evidence.

\textbf{Translation and received text.} The project has composed, translated,
paraphrased, modernised and adapted nothing. English for the scriptural
propers --- Introit, Gradual, Alleluia, Epistle, Gospel, Offertory, Communion
--- is the Douay--Rheims as revised by Bishop Challoner (1749--1752), the
English of the Clementine Vulgate the missal prints, quoted from the Project
Gutenberg e-text of that revision. English for the three orations is the
anonymous rendering in \work{The Roman Missal translated into the English
language for the use of the laity}, first revised edition (Philadelphia: Eugene
Cummiskey, 1861), taken from the three lines that book supplies for this
formulary and reproduced with its own spelling and its abbreviated conclusion.
Both are in the public domain in the United States; neither is an official
liturgical translation, and neither was composed for the 1962 books. Where a
witness consulted for reception exists only in a translation still under
copyright --- Theodoret in Hill, the Aquinas \latin{lectura} in Larcher,
Theophylact in Stade, Cassiodorus in Walsh --- that witness is summarised, or
quoted in Latin or from Migne's Latin column, and never rendered by this
project.

\textbf{Seven places where the registered English does not answer the Latin,
and is not made to.} The Introit sings \latin{unánimes} where the Clementine,
and therefore the Douay, reads \latin{unius moris}; the Introit and the
Alleluia are centonisations, so their Douay verses are printed whole with the
sung fragments marked rather than stitched into project-made joins; the Gradual
sings \latin{clamávi} against the Douay's future and closes with
\latin{ne discédas a me}, which has no Clementine counterpart at all; the
Offertory drops the \latin{Deus meus} the Douay carries; the Communion drops
\latin{ómnium} and \latin{da ei}; the missal prints \latin{Ephphetha} where the
Douay prints ``Ephpheta''; and the liturgical incipits \latin{Fratres:} and
\latin{In illo témpore: Exiens Iesus} are the missal's own and are not
translated. In each case the gap is stated where it falls. The Epistle's
English stops where the lesson stops; the remainder of its verse is described,
not printed as appointed text. Two divergences between this e-text and the 1899
American printing of the Douay fall inside verses quoted here --- ``lest if thou
be silent'' at Ps.~27:1 and ``Then was he seen'' at 1 Cor.~15:6 --- and are
disclosed in the references; every other quoted locus of this Mass agrees word
for word with the American edition.

\textbf{Search bounds.} Reception was searched in Latin, in Greek both directly
and through Migne's Latin columns, and in English, across the major reasonably
accessible patristic, medieval and later corpora relevant to each appointed
passage: Hilary's \work{Tractatus super Psalmos} in CSEL 22; Augustine's
\work{Enarrationes}, \work{De gratia et libero arbitrio}, \work{De consensu
evangelistarum}, and sermons; Jerome's \work{Commentarioli} and
\work{Tractatus} in Morin's editions and the \work{Commentarius in Marcum}
transmitted under his name; Bede on Mark and on Proverbs; Rabanus Maurus on
Proverbs; Salvian and Salonius; Gregory the Great in PL 75--76 and the
\work{Moralia}; Cassiodorus's \work{Expositio Psalmorum}; Theodoret on the
Psalms and on First Corinthians; Chrysostom on First Corinthians and on
Matthew; Theophylact on Mark; Ambrosiaster; Irenaeus and Tertullian; Aquinas's
\work{Super Psalmos}, \work{Super I ad Corinthios}, \work{Catena aurea in
Marcum} and the \work{Summa theologiae}; Peter Lombard on the Psalms;
Bellarmine; Cornelius a Lapide; Ambrose's mystagogical works; the 1872
\work{Rituale Romanum}; and Guéranger and Schuster for modern liturgical
commentary. Consequential negative results are reported in the commentary
rather than concealed --- among them that Gregory the Great nowhere expounds
this Sunday's Gospel, that Aquinas's psalms commentary ends at Psalm 54 and so
touches neither the Introit's nor the Alleluia's psalm, that Hilary's extant
tractates omit Psalms 27, 29 and 80, that Jerome's homily on Psalm 67 stops
before the verse the Introit sings, that the \work{Summa}'s treatise on grace
never cites 1 Cor.~15:10, that the Collect is absent from the Veronese
sacramentary, and that Salonius and Cyprian do not treat the Communion's
passage. No exhaustive search of Syriac originals, chant manuscripts,
untranslated homiliaries, subscription databases or current specialist
monographs is claimed, and none of these negatives is a claim about literature
outside the corpora named.

\textbf{Bounds carried by particular claims.} Modern critical apparatus for the
orations --- Corpus Orationum, Bruylants, Deshusses's numbering --- was not
publicly reachable and was not consulted; every transmission claim rests on the
named public-domain editions themselves. The postconciliar reuse of the Collect
rests on two secondary Latin transcriptions, the official 2002 locus remaining
a lead. Guéranger was verified in the 1909 Stanbrook second edition, which is
the edition cited, although the repository's registered edition of that volume
is the Duffy printing of 1900. Peter Lombard was checked on Psalms 27 and 67
only. Greek patristic use of Proverbs 3:9 was not verified, the Septuagint's
wording defeating a Vulgate-phrase search, and is recorded as a lead rather
than closed. For the Benziger witness, individual page images were read from the
Internet Archive item; acquisition of the exact registered derivative bytes is
deliberately not claimed. The gloss-attributed line about the womb of an
abortive birth, which circulates under Gregory's name, was traced only to Hugh
of St-Cher's postill, was not verified in Gregory, and is not published.

\textbf{Psalm numbering.} Three series are in use and two of them are routinely
both called ``modern,'' so the dossier sheet prints the two that differ and this
appendix supplies the third. \textit{Vulg.} is the Vulgate/Septuagint number,
which the missal cites and which the Douay--Rheims carries throughout.
\textit{Eng.} is the convention of the King James and Revised Standard
versions, which leave the psalm inscription unnumbered and so run a verse
behind the Vulgate wherever the psalm carries a title; the dossiers print it
beside the Vulgate number. Three of this Mass's four psalms show that offset;
Psalm 27, whose title the Douay counts inside v.~1, shows none.
\textit{Heb.} is the Masoretic number of the Nova Vulgata and the New American
Bible, which renumbers the psalm but keeps the Vulgate's verse division:
Ps.~67:2, 6--7, 36 Vulg.\ is Heb.\ 68:2, 6--7, 36; Ps.~27:1, 7 is Heb.\ 28:1, 7;
Ps.~80:2--3 is Heb.\ 81:2--3; Ps.~29:2--3 is Heb.\ 30:2--3. The conversions were
taken from the concordance registered with the Douay--Rheims edition used here,
not computed by this guide; the concordance marks each of the four psalms as
carrying its offset uniformly, so each conversion holds from the head of its
psalm.

\textbf{Historical bounds.} Dates and places in the page-2 dossiers distinguish
traditional attribution from current historical judgment and are orienting
rather than probative. On the psalm titles the two witnesses named there differ
in emphasis: the 1910 \work{Catholic Encyclopedia} reports the Biblical
Commission's decree of that year that the titles are older than the Septuagint
and that it cannot prudently be denied that David is the Psalter's chief
author, while adding that neither councils nor Fathers make him its sole
author; the \work{NABRE} treats the superscriptions as pre-Christian Jewish
tradition whose technical terms are now lost and holds that no psalm can be
securely dated. This guide does not settle the authorship or date of any
psalm, the date or place of Mark's composition, the compilation history of
Proverbs, or the year of the events Mark narrates. Where the two named
witnesses disagree --- as they do about Proverbs 1--9 and about Mark's date ---
both positions are printed and neither is preferred.

\textbf{Rights.} No public-domain conclusion is claimed for the 1962 typical
edition. This repository's rights record for the facsimile finds that edition's
own status unresolved, and more likely than not restored in the United States
under 17 U.S.C.\ \S104A to the end of 2057 (that record is
\texttt{missale-romanum-1962-facsimile-rights-v1.toml}, under
\texttt{src/sources/inventories/}). What permits the Latin printed here is
17 U.S.C.\ \S103(b): the copyright in a revised edition extends only to what
that edition itself contributed and ``does not affect or enlarge the scope,
duration, ownership, or subsistence of'' any copyright in the preexisting
material. This formulary is preexisting material. All ten of its appointed
elements --- in the same order and at the same scriptural references --- are
printed in the Pustet printing of Ratisbon, 1862, and all ten again in the
Venice Missal of 1570, both of which this repository tracks as public-domain
text, so the 1962 edition's copyright does not reach them; the
element-level check and its bounds are recorded in \texttt{propers/verified.md}. That
is this project's own reading of the statute and of the evidence, not a
clearance anyone granted, and it is not legal advice. No page image of either
digitisation is reproduced here, and none of the 1962 edition's own new matter
is published in this guide; the edition's rank, pagination and marginal numbers
are cited as facts about the book. The project claims no rights in the received
liturgical text. Both digitisations used to verify it are third-party artifacts
whose own rights status the repository's source records list as unresolved;
neither is redistributed here. Quotations from sources still in copyright are
avoided or kept to short compass with attribution, as noted at each point of
use. The common reuse notice on the final page states the outbound boundary and
does not relicense any of this material. Nothing here is permission to extract
and redistribute the incorporated liturgical text independently.

\textbf{Review state.} This document has had internal production review only:

produced it. It has received no independent liturgical, theological,
