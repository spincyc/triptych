\runninghead{The Way of the Beatitudes}
\properlane{beatitudes-way-of-the-saints}{The Beatitudes, the Way the Saints Walked}

On the feast of all the saints the Mass reads the eight beatitudes, and at the
Communion it sings the last three of them again. This interpretation follows
St Augustine and St John Chrysostom, who read the beatitudes as one ordered
way, beginning in humility and the fear of God and ending in persecution for
justice, with St Thomas Aquinas and St Hilary of Poitiers on what the promises
give and when. On their reading the Gospel describes how the saints became
saints, and the promises are given to the same people whose way it describes.

\subsection{The question: why the Beatitudes, and in what tense}
\label{sec:beatitudes-question}

Schuster writes that on this feast ``the Gospel can be no other than that of
the Beatitudes,'' because ``all are included in it and to each is granted a
special blessing'' (\work{The Sacramentary} V, p.~210). But the Gospel does
not praise the saints for what they now enjoy. It calls blessed the poor in
spirit, the meek, those who mourn, those who hunger and thirst, those who are
persecuted and reviled: people who lack, grieve and suffer. The promises
raise a second difficulty, of tense. The first and the eighth beatitude say
\latin{ipsorum est regnum caelorum}, ``theirs \emph{is} the kingdom''; the six
between say that the blessed \emph{shall} possess the land, be comforted, have
their fill, obtain mercy, see God, be called the children of God. If the
promises are all future, the saints the feast honours do not yet have what
the Gospel promises; if they are all present, the mourners are comforted
already, which experience does not show. A third question is the Communion:
why the Mass, having read eight beatitudes, sings three of them again.

\subsection{St Augustine: seven steps and an eighth}
\label{sec:beatitudes-augustine}

St Augustine opens his commentary by calling the Sermon on the Mount ``a
perfect standard of the Christian life'' (\work{De serm.\ Dom.}\ I.1). He
contrasts the mountain with Sinai: the lesser precepts were given ``to a
people who as yet required to be bound by fear,'' the greater to a people
``now become suitable to set free by love'' (I.2). He then reads the eight
sayings as a sequence (I.3.10). The poor in spirit are ``the humble and
God-fearing,'' for ``the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom,'' as
``pride'' is ``the beginning of all sin'' (I.3). The soul that has submitted to
God becomes meek before the Scriptures, learns from them in what evils it is
held and mourns, labours to tear itself free and so hungers for justice,
receives counsel and shows mercy, is purified in heart to contemplate the
highest good, and at the seventh step reaches wisdom, which makes peace in
the whole man. ``The eighth, as it were, returns to the starting-point,
because it shows and commends what is complete and perfect: therefore in the
first and in the eighth the kingdom of heaven is named.''

He sets the seven steps beside the sevenfold operation of the Holy Ghost in
Isaias 11, counted upward from the fear of the Lord: fear answers to the poor
in spirit, piety to the meek, knowledge to the mourners, fortitude to the
hungry, counsel to the merciful, understanding to the clean of heart, and
wisdom to the peacemakers (I.4.11). On Augustine's reading, then, each step of
the way answers to a gift of the Holy Ghost. Rabanus Maurus, compiling his
commentary on Matthew, takes Augustine's reading over: he quotes him by name
at 5:11--12, and in his comment on the Lord's Prayer he joins the gifts to the
beatitudes again, beginning with \latin{timor Dei, quo beati sunt pauperes
spiritu} (\work{Expositio in Matthaeum}, on Mt 5--6).

The rewards are one reward under eight names: ``the one reward, which is the
kingdom of heaven, is variously named according to these stages'' (I.4.12).
The meek receive an inheritance, the mourners comfort, the hungry their fill,
the clean of heart the sight of God, the peacemakers the likeness of God.
Augustine adds that ``those promises can indeed be fulfilled in this life, as
we believe them to have been fulfilled in the case of the apostles,'' while
``that all-embracing change into the angelic form, which is promised after this
life, cannot be explained in any words.'' The eighth beatitude, which returns
to the beginning, he finds figured in circumcision on the eighth day and in
the Lord's resurrection on the day after the Sabbath, which is ``the eighth,
and at the same time the first day'' (I.4.12).

\subsection{St John Chrysostom: humility, blessing and the golden chain}
\label{sec:beatitudes-chrysostom}

St John Chrysostom begins in the same place. The poor in spirit are ``the
humble and contrite in mind,'' and the Lord blesses first those ``who by
choice humble and contract themselves''; he says ``poor'' rather than
``humble'' because he means ``them who are awestruck, and tremble at the
commandments of God'' (\work{Hom.\ in Matt.}\ 15.2). Humility comes first
because pride is ``the root and fountain of all wickedness,'' and the Lord
``laid this law first as a strong and safe foundation'' (15.3). He notes the
form of the sayings: the Lord speaks ``not \dots\ by way of advice or of
commandments, but by way of blessing,'' so that ``though thou be a slave, a
beggar, in poverty, a stranger, unlearned, there is nothing to hinder thee from
being blessed'' (15.3).

Chrysostom also reads the eight as connected, and in an order of his own:
``He hath woven a sort of golden chain for us. Thus, first, he that is
`humble,' will surely also `mourn' for his own sins: he that so `mourns,' will
be both `meek,' and `righteous,' and `merciful'; \dots\ and he that hath
attained unto all these, will be moreover arrayed against dangers'' (15.9).
The two Fathers differ on the link between the steps. For Augustine it is an
ascent of knowledge and of the Spirit's gifts toward contemplation; for
Chrysostom it is a chain of dispositions, each producing the next, and its
end is readiness for persecution. They agree that humility comes first and
that persecution comes only to one already formed by the rest. Chrysostom
reads the peacemakers' reward as a share in the Son's own work: ``this became
the work of the Only Begotten, to unite the divided, and to reconcile the
alienated'' (15.7). On the eighth, he observes that the Lord ``hath set two
limitations; when it is for His sake, and when the things that are said are
false'' (15.7), the \latin{propter me} and \latin{mentientes} of the Gospel's
last verse.

\subsection{St Thomas and St Hilary: in the way and in the homeland}
\label{sec:beatitudes-thomas-hilary}

St Thomas Aquinas answers the question of tense with a distinction. The
rewards can be had \latin{perfecte \& consummate, \& hoc in patria tantum: \&
secundum inchoationem, \& imperfecte; \& sic in via}, perfectly and in their
completion only in the homeland, and in their beginning and imperfectly on the
way. So \latin{Beati pauperes spiritu, non spe tantum, sed etiam re}: the poor
in spirit are blessed not only in hope but in fact, for ``the kingdom of God is
within you'' (Lk 17:21). On the clean of heart he records that some place the
vision of God in contemplation in this life, and holds that the Lord promises
it for the homeland. The eighth beatitude \latin{designat perfectionem omnium
praecedentium}, signifies the perfection of all that precede it, because
persecution tests them, as ``the furnace trieth the potter's vessels'' (Ecclus
27:6); and \latin{persecutio non facit beatum, sed eius causa}, persecution
does not make a man blessed, but its cause, \latin{propter iustitiam}
(\work{Super Matt.}\ 5, with Chrysostom).

St Hilary of Poitiers reads the promises more strictly of the life to come.
The Lord on the mountain, \latin{in paternae majestatis positus
celsitudine}, set in the height of the Father's majesty, lays down \latin{caelestis
vitae praecepta}, the precepts of the heavenly life. The land the meek
inherit is \latin{ejus corporis, quod ipse Dominus assumpsit habitaculum}, the
body which the Lord himself took as his dwelling. The clean of heart see God
because \latin{non enim nisi spiritu perfecti, et immortalitate mutati \dots\
quod in Deo est immortale cernemus}: only when we are perfected in spirit and
changed by immortality shall we behold what in God is immortal (\work{In
Matt.}\ IV). Chrysostom reads the land differently: the meek are promised ``a
sensible prize,'' for ``with the things spiritual'' the Lord ``hath mingled
the sensible'' (15.5); and of the mourners he asks, ``Where shall they be
comforted! tell me. Both here and there'' (15.4). Yet he too concludes that
``although He give different names to the rewards, yet He brings all into His
kingdom'' (15.7).

\subsection{The way in the other texts of the Mass}
\label{sec:beatitudes-elements}

The Alleluia verse comes before the Gospel and speaks to people who labour.
Its context in Matthew names the disposition with which the Beatitudes begin:
``learn of me, because I am meek, and humble of heart'' (Mt 11:29). St Augustine,
preaching on that verse, tells his hearers, ``first think of the foundation of
humility,'' and says the building reaches ``even to the Vision of God''
(\work{Sermo} 69, 2--3), the reward of the sixth beatitude. The Gradual's
\latin{Timete Dominum, omnes Sancti eius} names the fear that Augustine
puts at the first step; and the Introit's verse, which Augustine reads as
rejoicing ``not in yourselves \dots\ but in the Lord,'' asks for the joy of
those who are not puffed up.

The Offertory's just who ``seemed to die'' have reached the eighth beatitude:
Wisdom's just man is the one the wicked resolve to ``condemn \dots\ to a most
shameful death'' (Wis 2:20). The Collect speaks of the saints' merits, and on
this interpretation those merits are the way walked. The Communion sings the
clean of heart, the peacemakers and the persecuted: in Augustine's order the
sixth and seventh steps and the eighth that returns to the first, in
Aquinas's the vision of God and the perfection of all the rest. Schuster
calls the passage ``as it were the decalogue of happiness'' (p.~211). That
the Mass repeats these three at the Communion is the Mass's arrangement; the
Fathers named here comment on the Gospel, not on the antiphon's place. The
Sunday's Collect, said after the feast's, asks for release from sins
contracted \latin{pro nostra fragilitate}; it belongs to the Sunday, and the
study notes only that the same Mass that reads the way of the saints asks
pardon for frailty.

\subsection{The strongest difficulty: present and future}
\label{sec:beatitudes-difficulty}

The difficulty of tense is the strongest because both easy answers fail the
text. If every promise is postponed to the last day, the present
\latin{est} of the first and the eighth beatitude is ignored, and so is the
feast, which honours saints who already reign. If every promise is made
present, the mourners are already comforted and the hungry already filled,
and the Gospel's ``your reward is very great in heaven'' is ignored.

The Fathers answer the difficulty in different ways. Augustine holds that the
promises can be fulfilled in this life, as in the apostles, and that the
change ``into the angelic form'' comes after it. Aquinas says the rewards are
begun on the way and completed in the homeland, and the poor in spirit have
the kingdom \latin{re}, in fact, as the kingdom of God is within them.
Chrysostom says the mourners are comforted ``both here and there.'' Hilary
holds that the vision of God belongs to those perfected and changed into
immortality. Their answers differ in emphasis but agree on one point: the
first and the eighth beatitude are present, the six between are future, and
the future promises will be given to those who already have the kingdom in
its beginning. On the feast, the present tense is true of the saints in
heaven in its completion and of the Church on earth in its beginning.

\begin{fourSenses}
\item[Literal.] Jesus, seated on a mountain, teaches his disciples that the
poor in spirit, the meek, the mourners, those who hunger for justice, the
merciful, the clean of heart, the peacemakers and the persecuted are blessed,
and tells the disciples that when they are reviled and persecuted for his sake
their reward is great in heaven.
\item[Allegorical.] The Lord gives the new law from the mountain as the old
was given from Sinai, the greater precepts to those set free by love
(Augustine); seated in the height of the Father's majesty, he lays down the
precepts of the heavenly life, and the land of the meek is the body he took
(Hilary).
\item[Moral.] The way begins in humility and the fear of God and rises through
meekness, mourning, hunger for justice, mercy and purity of heart to peace,
each disposition linked to the next, and ends in readiness to suffer for
justice' sake (Augustine, Chrysostom); persecution blesses only for its cause
(Aquinas).
\item[Anagogical.] The one reward, the kingdom of heaven, is begun on the way
and completed in the homeland, where the clean of heart, changed by
immortality, see God (Aquinas, Hilary).
\end{fourSenses}
