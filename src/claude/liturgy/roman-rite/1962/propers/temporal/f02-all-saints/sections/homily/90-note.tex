\section*{Note on Sources and Delivery}
\runninghead{Sources and Delivery}

{\footnotesize

\textbf{Audience and occasion.} An adult parish assembly, at the Mass
\latin{In festo omnium Sanctorum}, \latin{Die 1 novembris}, I classis, of the
1962 \work{Missale Romanum}, editio typica, pp.~702--703, nos.~3974--3983, for
the occurrence of Sunday 1~November 2026, when the feast of the first class
takes the place of the Twenty-third Sunday after Pentecost, the Sunday being
commemorated by its Collect, Secret and Postcommunion (pp.~416--417,
nos.~1720, 1726, 1728), each said under its own conclusion after the feast's
corresponding prayer. The speech supposes that it is preached after the
Gospel and before the Credo: it speaks of the Introit, the Collect, the
commemorated Collect, the Lesson, the Gradual, the Alleluia and the Gospel as
heard, and of the Offertory, the Secret, the Communion and the Postcommunion
as still to come. The feast prints no Preface, and the general rubrics can be
read for the Preface of the Most Holy Trinity (p.~293, no.~1082) or for the
common Preface (p.~297, no.~1086); the question is left unsettled, as the
studies leave it, and the speech uses neither Preface. The commemorated
Secret and Postcommunion are not used in the speech.

\textbf{Length and pace.} The spoken body runs to about 1,445 words, some
11.1 to 12.0 minutes at an unhurried 120 to 130 words a minute by arithmetic
alone; no one has delivered or timed it. It was read through on the page for
sense and ease of speech, not aloud.

\textbf{Relation to the study's interpretations.} The homily takes its spine
from the second of the full study's three interpretations, \textit{The
Beatitudes, the Way the Saints Walked}: St~Augustine's one way in order, from
the poor in spirit --- the humble and God-fearing --- to the persecuted, with
the eighth blessing returning to the first (\work{De serm.\ Dom.}\ I.1,
I.3.10); St~John Chrysostom's golden chain and his observation that the Lord
speaks by blessing and not by command (\work{Hom.\ in Matt.}\ 15.3, 15.9);
and St~Thomas Aquinas's distinction of the rewards begun on the way and
completed in the homeland (\work{Super Matt.}\ cap.~5, lect.~1). From the
first interpretation, \textit{One Church, Sealed and Uncounted}, it takes
St~Bede's reading of the Lesson's vision: the definite number signifying the
innumerable multitude of the whole Church, the seal as the sign of the cross,
the robes as baptism and the palms as the cross's triumph (\work{Explanatio
Apocalypsis} I, on 7:2, 7:4, 7:9), and St~Augustine's ``This is the mass of
the saints'' (\work{Sermon LXI}, 1). From the third, \textit{No Want to Them
That Fear Him}, it takes Augustine's objector against the Gradual's promise
(\work{En.\ in Ps.}\ 33 [NPNF ``Psalm XXXIV''], 13), St~Basil's perfect good
(\work{Hom.\ in Ps.}\ 33), Aquinas's God who is nourisher and physician and
who comes to the help of those who seek him in their need (\work{Super
Psalmos} 33, nn.~10--11), and Blessed Ildefonso Schuster's sentence on the
Offertory's hands (\work{The Sacramentary} V, p.~211). The disputes the
studies record are left to them, none being contradicted here: the speech
follows Bede on the Lesson without raising Victorinus's angel and number or
Cyprian's martyrs-only crowd; and it claims only Aquinas's begun-and-completed
distinction for the beatitudes' promises, deciding nothing between
Augustine's fulfillment in this life and St~Hilary's reservation of the
vision to the perfected. The speech's own connections, credited to no Father
and recorded here as the homily's, are: the Introit's rejoicing angels set
beside the Lesson's angels falling on their faces before the throne; the
Alleluia's invitation heard as the voice by which the uncounted crowd is
gathered (Chrysostom's ``all that are in anxiety'' is his comment on Mt 11:28,
not on the Lesson); ``God loses none of his own'' as the homily's wording of
the number's completeness; the altar sentence joining the Secret's offering
to the Lamb in whose sight the crowd stands; the commemorated Sunday's
Collect heard as poverty of spirit in petition, the way's first step (the
research record marks the same juxtaposition as editorial); and the
Communion's three beatitudes heard, where the Sacrament is received, as the
pledge of the way's end --- there the homily adopts the second
interpretation's own marked inference from the two orders and the
Communion's place in the Mass. William Durandus's reason for one feast of
all the saints (\work{Rationale} VII.34) opens the speech. St~Robert
Bellarmine's two senses of the Gradual's promise, Augustine's comments on the
omitted half-verse about the rich, and the commemorated Sunday's Secret and
Postcommunion are not used.

\textbf{Texts quoted, and their route.} Scripture is the Douay--Rheims,
Challoner's revision: Apoc 7:9; Mt 11:28; Ps 33:10; Mt 5:3, 10 (the
present-tense clauses); Mt 5:8--10 (the Communion). One Scripture quotation
stands apart from that list: St~Paul's ``if ye be Christ's, then are ye
Abraham's seed'' (Gal 3:29), which the speech quotes as Bede cites the verse
in his comment on the heard number, in E.~Marshall's English of the
\work{Explanatio}; the Douay reads ``if you be Christ's, then are you the
seed of Abraham.'' The scene of the vision paraphrases Apoc 7:2--4 and
11--12. The Introit antiphon (``Let us all
rejoice in the Lord \dots''), the Offertory antiphon (``The souls of the just
are in the hand of God, and the torment of malice shall not touch them: in
the sight of the unwise they seemed to die, but they are in peace''), the
Secret's ``for the honor of all the just'', the commemorated Collect's ``the
trammels of our sins, in which, through our frailty, we have become
entangled'' and the Postcommunion's ``defended by their unceasing prayers''
are in the English of the Lasance \work{New Roman Missal} of 1945, a
historical study aid and not an approved liturgical translation of the 1962
Missal; the Offertory quotation is the Lasance's English of the adapted
antiphon the 1962 Missal sings (with \latin{malitiae} for the Vulgate's
\latin{mortis}), and omits its closing \latin{alleluia}. The Collect and the
rest of the Postcommunion are described, not quoted. Bede is quoted in E.
Marshall's English; Augustine and Chrysostom in the English of the Nicene and
Post-Nicene Fathers; Schuster in his own English. Basil and Aquinas are
paraphrased from their Latin in the study's English renderings (``the perfect
good is God himself''; ``God is both nourisher and physician''), and Durandus
is paraphrased from his Latin.
