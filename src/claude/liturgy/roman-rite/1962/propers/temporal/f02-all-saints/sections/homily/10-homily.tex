The Gradual today sang a verse of the thirty-third psalm: ``Fear the Lord,
all ye his saints: for there is no want to them that fear him.'' The line
after it promises that those who seek the Lord shall lack no good.

On this feast that promise is hard to hear. We keep today the memory of all
the saints, and a great many of them were poor. Many were hungry. The martyrs
among them were arrested and put to death. The Gospel we have just heard
calls blessed those who mourn and those who suffer persecution for justice'
sake. How can nothing be lacking to the saints, when the saints who feared
God most lacked so much?

St~Augustine puts a complaint like this in the heart of one of his hearers.
The man says to himself, ``Daily I rise for the church, daily I kneel, daily
I seek the Lord, and I have nothing good.'' He has watched wicked rich men
grow old and die in their riches, although the psalm, in the half of the
verse that the Gradual leaves out, says, ``The rich have wanted, and have
suffered hunger.'' Augustine admits that, taken by the letter, the verse
seems to deceive. But he warns that whoever reasons in this way is caught in
a snare, because he is looking for food that perishes here on earth, and not
for his true reward in heaven. Then he tells the man not to understand the
verse so. The goods it promises are the goods of the spirit.

St~Basil the Great, preaching on the same psalm, sets out the difficulty as a
dilemma. Suppose the good that the psalm promises is bread and money and
safety. The apostles sought the Lord, and they lacked those things. Then
either the apostles did not seek the Lord, or the Scripture is false, and
neither can be said. St~Paul sought the Lord. He was scourged, he was stoned,
he was shipwrecked, and Basil says that nothing of the good was lacking to
him. The good the psalm promises, then, is something Paul had while he was
being beaten. Basil names it: the wholly perfect good is God himself, and no
one who seeks him is left without him.

St~Thomas Aquinas takes the verse first as a promise of the goods of the
spirit. Then he adds that God also helps those who seek him in their need.
But St~Paul writes that he and the other apostles were hungry and thirsty to
that very hour. St~Augustine had met the same objection, and Thomas gives
Augustine's answer: God then acts like a physician, who keeps food from a
sick man for the sake of his health. Here Basil and Thomas part company.
Basil leaves bread out of the promise; Thomas lets it in, under the
physician's judgment. Both hold that the man who fears God lacks nothing
that matters.

Now set the Gospel beside the Gradual. Jesus sees the crowds, goes up the
mountain, sits down, and teaches his disciples. Six of his eight promises
speak of the future: those who mourn shall be comforted, those who hunger and
thirst after justice shall have their fill, the clean of heart shall see God.
The first and the last speak in the present: ``Blessed are the poor in
spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven,'' and, ``Blessed are they that
suffer persecution for justice' sake: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.''
The kingdom already belongs to the poor in spirit, and to those persecuted for
justice' sake while they suffer it.

St~Augustine says that the poor in spirit are the humble, who are not puffed
up and who have put themselves under God. He matches the beatitudes with the
gifts of the Holy Spirit, counting the gifts upward from the lowest, so that
the fear of the Lord stands first, and he gives that fear to the humble, the
poor in spirit. If the fear of the Lord belongs to the poor in spirit, then
the man whom the Gradual tells to fear the Lord is the man whom the Gospel
calls poor in spirit. To that one man the Gradual promises that nothing is
lacking, and the Gospel that the kingdom of heaven is his now.

The promises spoken in the future also begin now. St~John Chrysostom asks
where the mourners will be comforted, and he answers, ``Both here and
there.'' The saints had the beginning of their reward while they were still
mourning, and they have its fullness in heaven.

The Offertory's words come from the Book of Wisdom, from a chapter that
answers wicked men who had said of a just man, ``Let us condemn him to a most
shameful death.'' The answer is the verse we shall hear in a few minutes:
``The souls of the just are in the hand of God, and the torment of malice
shall not touch them: in the sight of the unwise they seemed to die, but they
are in peace.'' St~Augustine says what it is to be in that hand. The just who
have died, and who wait for the resurrection, ``dwell in the help of the Most
High, and abide in the protection of the God of heaven.''

The wicked put the just man to death, and he is in the hand of God. God
himself, Basil said, is the good the Gradual promised. So the just man kept
that good when men took his life. When the Secret offers our gifts ``for the
honor of all the just,'' the just it honours are the saints of this feast.

In the Lesson St~John is shown where they stand. He sees ``a great multitude,
which no man could number, of all nations and tribes and peoples and tongues,
standing before the throne and in sight of the Lamb, clothed with white robes,
and palms in their hands.'' They cry, ``Salvation to our God, who sitteth upon
the throne, and to the Lamb.'' In that company are the poor in spirit, those
who mourned, those persecuted for justice' sake, and the just whom the
Offertory places in God's hand.

The verse that makes the promise begins with a command: ``Fear the Lord, all
ye his saints.'' St~Bernard, preaching on this feast, said that the saints in
glory no longer need that command, because they no longer fear. It is said to
us, who are still on the way. ``Meanwhile,'' he said, ``our whole beatitude is
to fear God,'' while the saints keep their feast in joy and praise. St~Basil
explains the promise by this fear: whoever is kept by fear from every
shameful act, he says, lacks nothing he needs for any virtue.

St~Bernard also asked what our praise does for the saints. They do not need
our honours, he said. We keep their memory for our own sake, because it
makes us want to be with them. So the prayers of this Mass ask for us. The
Collect asked God's mercy for us ``through the multitude of our
intercessors,'' and the Postcommunion will ask that we be ``defended by their
unceasing prayers.''

Augustine's hearer rose for church, knelt and sought the Lord every day, and
he measured what he had against the rich man's goods. Seek the Lord as
faithfully as he did, and seek him for himself. He gives himself at this
altar: Christ, God and man, hidden under the sacrament. The Communion will
sing again, ``Blessed are the clean of heart: for they shall see God,'' and
the clean of heart will one day see him face to face.

This Mass began with the words ``Let us all rejoice in the Lord,'' and its
psalm told the just to rejoice in the Lord. St~Basil, preaching on that
psalm, said that the just rejoice because they have the Lord, and not because
things go well at home. The saints rejoice for that reason before the throne.
You who fear him have the same reason to rejoice here, whether you have
little or much, because the Lord himself is the good that he has promised you.
