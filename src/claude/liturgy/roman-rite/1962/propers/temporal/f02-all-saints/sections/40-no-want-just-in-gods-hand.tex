\runninghead{No Want to Them That Fear Him}
\properlane{no-want-just-in-gods-hand}{No Want to Them That Fear Him}

The Gradual of this Mass promises that nothing is lacking to those who fear
the Lord, and its Offertory says that the just only seemed to die. Both
statements meet an obvious objection: the saints the feast honours went
hungry, were persecuted and were put to death. This interpretation follows St
Basil the Great and St Robert Bellarmine, who read the promise of the psalm,
with St Augustine and St Thomas Aquinas, who state the objection against it
and answer it, and the Fathers who read the Offertory's verses of the just in
the hand of God.

\subsection{The question: a promise that seems false}
\label{sec:no-want-question}

The Gradual sings \latin{Timete Dominum, omnes Sancti eius: quoniam nihil
deest timentibus eum}, and then, \latin{Inquirentes autem Dominum, non
deficient omni bono}. St Augustine states the difficulty as plainly as any
objector. Many will not fear God for fear of going hungry. And when the psalm
goes on, ``The rich do lack and suffer hunger, but they that seek the Lord
shall not want any good thing,'' then ``if thou receive it according to the
letter, He seemeth to deceive thee, for thou seest that many rich men that are
wicked die in their riches.'' The man who has seen such a rich man buried
says, ``the Scripture has deceived me,'' and adds, ``Daily I rise up to
Church, daily I bend the knee, daily I seek the Lord, and have nothing good''
(\work{En.\ in Ps.}\ 33 [NPNF ``Psalm XXXIV''], 13).

Two features of the Mass bear on the difficulty. The Gradual leaves out the
words about the rich, \latin{Divites eguerunt, et esurierunt}, which stand at
the head of verse~11 and on which all the commentators below build part of
their answer; the Mass sings the promise without the contrast.
The Offertory takes its verses from the chapter in which Wisdom answers the
wicked who said, ``Let us condemn him to a most shameful death'' (Wis 2:20).
The antiphon says that the just \latin{visi sunt oculis insipientium mori},
seemed to die in the eyes of the unwise; the death that seemed to be the end
was a death that the wicked inflicted.

\subsection{St Basil: the fear that lacks nothing}
\label{sec:no-want-basil}

St Basil, preaching on Psalm~33, begins from what fear does. In the Latin
version printed in Migne: \latin{Nisi timor instituerit vitam nostram,
sanctimonia in corpore comparari non potest}, unless fear has ordered our life,
holiness cannot be gained in the body. He quotes ``Pierce thou my flesh with
thy fear'' (Ps 118:120): as those transfixed with nails have members that
cannot move to act, so those whose soul is seized by divine fear escape every
disturbance that comes from the passions of sin. \latin{Timenti itaque non est
inopia}: he who is kept by fear from every unseemly act
\latin{nihil deest ad quamlibet virtutem, sed perfectus est}, lacks nothing
for any virtue, but is perfect. And he who has received perfect fear
\latin{nihil peccabit}, will sin in nothing, and will not experience want,
because fear is always with him (\work{Hom.\ in Ps.}\ 33, PG 29,
cols.~365--368).

On the next verse Basil offers a reading of the rich who lacked. Perhaps the
psalm calls rich the people of Israel, ``to whom belongeth the adoption as of children \dots\ and the promises'' (Rom 9:4), and they, \latin{ob peccatum in Dominum admissum
eguerunt}, came into want through the sin committed against the Lord. After
they killed the bread of life (Jn 6:48), famine came upon them, a famine
``not of bread, nor a thirst of water, but of hearing the word of the Lord''
(Amos 8:11). Those from the nations who learned to seek the Lord are not
diminished in any good, and \latin{Perfectum omnino bonum Deus ipse est}, the
wholly perfect good is God himself. He then puts the objection as a dilemma.
Whoever counts bodily goods as the good the psalm promises must say either
that the apostles did not seek the Lord, since they did not obtain bodily
goods, or that Scripture is false. Neither can be said. Paul sought the Lord,
\latin{et nihil ei defuit bonorum}, and nothing good was lacking to him,
though he was thrice beaten with rods, once stoned, thrice shipwrecked, and
was in hunger and thirst to the last hour (2~Cor 11:25--27).

\subsection{St Robert Bellarmine: the higher sense and the humbler}
\label{sec:no-want-bellarmine}

St Robert Bellarmine (whose English numbers these verses one lower than the
Vulgate) reads the fear of the Gradual as ``the pure and filial fear that is
the characteristic of the saints and of the perfect'': ``it is by fear we are
made perfect, not by servile fear,'' for ``the perfect lover fears vehemently
lest he may offend his beloved in any way.'' The promise then has two senses.
In ``the higher meaning,'' ``the essence of perfection is to feel no want. And,
what want can the friend of God, who owns everything, feel, when the property
of friends is common; and if the just appear sometimes to be in want, they
really are not so, because they get patience, better than any riches, to bear
it.'' The same words, ``understood in an humbler sense,'' teach the simple
``that God will supply his servants with the temporal things of the world, and
will not desert them in time of necessity'' (\work{Explanatio}, Ps~33, his
v.~9). On the next verse he gives the higher meaning again: those who seek
the Lord ``shall not be deprived of any good,'' ``because, as they cling to
the Supreme Good, they possess all that is good'' (his v.~10).

Bellarmine and Basil agree that the promise is kept in the good that the one
who fears possesses, God himself. They differ in what they make of the
rich. For Basil the rich may be Israel, impoverished of the word; for
Bellarmine, in the plain sense, they are the wicked rich reduced to poverty,
and in the higher sense the covetous, who ``always hunger and need.'' And
Bellarmine keeps a lower sense of temporal provision that Basil does not
state.

\subsection{St Augustine and St Thomas: riches and the physician}
\label{sec:no-want-augustine-thomas}

St Augustine's answer to his own objection is that the goods the psalm
promises are not those the rich man had. ``When thou art filled with spiritual
riches, canst thou be poor? And was he therefore rich, because he had a bed
of ivory; and art thou poor who hast the chamber of thy heart filled with
such jewelry of virtues, justice, truth, charity, faith, endurance?'' The rich
who lack, he says, lack bread, and he names the bread from the Gospel, ``I am
the Living Bread which came down from Heaven,'' and with it the beatitude,
``Blessed are they which do hunger and thirst after righteousness: for they
shall be filled'' (\S14). That gloss is his comment on the half-verse about
the rich, which the Gradual does not sing; what he says of the seekers' good,
``what manner of good, I have already said,'' is his comment on the half-verse
it does sing.

St Thomas Aquinas expounds \latin{non est inopia} first of spiritual goods,
since ``the fear of the Lord is his treasure'' (Isa 33:6), then of bodily
goods: it may happen that one who fears God has little, but not that he is
needy, for \latin{Inops est qui se deficientem reputat}, he is needy who
counts himself lacking, and those who fear God are content with what they
have (Phil 4:11--12); and he adds that God comes to the help of those who
seek him in their need, \latin{Item Deus quaerentibus se subvenit in
necessitate}. He then states the objection in Augustine's name from
St Paul: ``Even unto this hour we both hunger and thirst and are naked'' (1~Cor
4:11). His answer is that \latin{Deus est nutritor et medicus}, God is both
nourisher and physician: the physician withholds food from the sick man,
because it helps his health, and so God, as it helps our salvation,
\latin{quandoque inopiam immittit, quandoque divitias confert}, sometimes sends
want and sometimes gives riches (\work{Super Psalmos} 33, nn.~10--11).
For Aquinas, the want of the God-fearing is given for their health.

\subsection{The just in the hand of God}
\label{sec:no-want-offertory}

The Offertory carries the question from want to death. St John Chrysostom
quotes its first clause, ``the souls of the righteous are in the hand of
God,'' to deny that the souls of the dead wander the earth (\work{Hom.\ in
Matt.}\ 28); St Augustine quotes it to explain the reading ``My soul is
continually in thy hand'' (\work{En.\ in Ps.}\ 118, on v.~109); and St Gregory
the Great applies the next clause to the just man whose body is bereft of life
``amidst the hands of the persecutors'' (\work{Moralia} XII.v.6). Schuster,
on this antiphon, states the contrast in terms of hands: ``The persecutor
believes that the lives of the martyrs are in his hands, but they are in the
hands of God'' (\work{The Sacramentary} V, p.~211).

The 1962 antiphon differs from the Vulgate in ways that bear on this reading.
It reads \latin{tormentum malitiae}, the torment of malice, where the Vulgate
reads \latin{tormentum mortis}, the torment of death --- a wording whose
history among the Missal printings is given with the appointed texts --- and
it leaves out the verses in which the departure of the just ``was taken for
misery.'' What the 1962 form says is that the torment of malice will not
touch the just.

\subsection{The promise in the other texts of the Mass}
\label{sec:no-want-elements}

The Introit begins in joy, before the difficulty is heard: the just are
bidden to rejoice in the Lord, and Bellarmine takes the rejoicing to include
the praise that is ``the special duty of the just'' (\work{Explanatio},
Ps~32, v.~1). The Lesson shows the promise where it is kept: a multitude
that no one could number stands before the throne and in sight of the Lamb,
clothed in white robes, crying ``Salvation to our God.'' Those who feared
the Lord want nothing there; what the psalm promises and the assembly sings,
the vision shows as fact. The Alleluia verse invites those who labour and are
burdened, and St John Chrysostom notes that it calls ``all that are in
anxiety, in sorrows, in sins'' (\work{Hom.\ in Matt.}\ 38.3). The Gospel
blesses those who hunger and thirst after justice, the verse Augustine cites
inside his exposition of the Gradual's psalm. The Collect asks for the
abundance of mercy that is desired, \latin{multiplicatis intercessoribus},
and Durandus gives as the fourth cause of the feast that petitions are more
easily granted when all the saints intercede together, \latin{quia
impossibile est multitudinem non exaudiri} (\work{Rationale} VII.34). The
Secret asks that the offering be salutary to those who make it,
\latin{te miserante}, by God's mercy. The Communion sings the clean of heart,
who shall see God, the peacemakers, who shall be called the children of God,
and the persecuted, whose is the kingdom of heaven: the good that is not
lacking, named as the vision of God himself, which Bellarmine's higher sense
makes the whole of the promise, since those who cling to the Supreme Good
possess all that is good. The Postcommunion asks that the faithful peoples be
defended by the saints' unceasing prayer.

The commemoration's orations are the Sunday's. Said in this Mass, its
Collect asks to be loosed from the bonds of sin contracted through frailty, its Secret calls what has been received a gift to the
undeserving, \latin{immeritis}, and its Postcommunion asks that those who
share in divine things be not left subject to human dangers.

\subsection{The strongest difficulty: the promise read as comfort}
\label{sec:no-want-difficulty}

The strongest difficulty is the one Augustine voices: the promise read as
the assembly hears it seems false. Saints have starved and been killed, and the
same Mass that sings \latin{nihil deest} asks, in the Sunday's prayer, to be
freed from the bonds of sins. Augustine says that taken ``according to the
letter'' the verse ``seemeth to deceive thee,'' and he turns the promise to
the goods within; Basil says that whoever takes bodily goods for the psalm's
good must accuse the apostles or Scripture. Two of the witnesses do keep a
temporal sense, each in a bounded form: Bellarmine gives the words ``an
humbler sense,'' for the simple, in which God supplies his servants with the
temporal things of the world and does not desert them in necessity, and
Aquinas says that God comes to the help of those who seek him in their need.
Neither makes temporal provision the promise's meaning: Bellarmine sets his
humbler sense beside the higher one, in which the just who appear to be in
want ``really are not so,'' and Aquinas answers the hungering apostles with
the physician who withholds food for the sick man's health.

Their answers differ in what they say is promised: God himself (Basil,
Bellarmine), the virtues (Augustine), and the want that serves salvation
(Aquinas). The Gradual's omission of
\latin{Divites eguerunt} does not remove the difficulty, because
the singers still know saints who were poor. It does mean that what the Mass
sings is the promise alone, and what the Fathers say of the rich is said
about a half-verse the Mass leaves out.

\begin{fourSenses}
\item[Literal.] A psalm of David bids the Lord's saints fear him, because
those who fear him lack nothing and those who seek him lack no good; the Book
of Wisdom answers the wicked who put the just man to death, saying that the
souls of the just are in the hand of God, that they seemed to die, and that
they are in peace.
\item[Allegorical.] The rich who lacked may be Israel, which killed the bread
of life and suffered a famine of the word, while the nations who sought the
Lord lack no good (Basil); the just in God's hand are the martyrs, whose lives
were not in the persecutor's hands (Schuster).
\item[Moral.] Filial fear, which fears to offend the one it loves, orders
the life and keeps it from sin, and he who has it lacks nothing for any virtue
(Basil, Bellarmine); want is borne with patience, and God sends it, as a
physician withholds food, for the soul's health (Bellarmine, Aquinas).
\item[Anagogical.] The perfect good is God himself, and those who seek him
are not deprived of the goods laid up in eternal rest (Basil); the just are
in the hand of God and in peace.
\end{fourSenses}
