\runninghead{Detailed Commentary}
\section{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}
\zlabel{triptych:concise:commentary:start}

Three questions arise from the texts of this Mass. Who are the saints of the
feast, when the Lesson first counts them and then says that no one could
count them? What is the fear that the Gradual commands of ``all ye his
saints,'' and what does it promise them? And when do the saints have what the
Beatitudes and the Offertory promise: on the way, at death, or at the end?

\subsection{Who the saints of the feast are}

The Lesson names the saints twice. John first hears a number, a hundred and
forty-four thousand sealed from the twelve tribes; then he sees a multitude
``which no man could number,'' from every nation. The Gradual addresses
\latin{omnes Sancti eius}, all his saints, and the Alleluia calls \latin{omnes
qui laboratis}, all who labour. Three early commentators on the Apocalypse
agree that the multitude holds the elect of every nation. They part over the
number and over the angel who seals.

For St Bede the hundred and forty-four thousand are the whole Church. He
multiplies the twelve tribes by twelve and raises the product
to thousands, ``the cube of the number ten, by which is represented the
enduring life of the Church'' (on 7:4). The Gentiles stand inside the tribes,
because they are Abraham's children by faith: ``if ye be Christ's, then are ye
Abraham's seed'' (Gal 3:29). The angel who seals is Christ, and after the
Passion, Bede says, the power of the nations was broken ``that the face of the
saints might be freely marked with the seal of faith, which these had
resisted'' (on 7:3). At verse~9 he offers two accounts of the multitude. In
the first, the vision announces ``the glory of those who are to overcome the
wickedness of the last persecution''; in the second, John, having listed the
tribes ``to whom the Gospel was first preached,'' goes on to name the
Gentiles. Bede does not say in so many words that the number and the
multitude are one people seen twice. If his reading of verse~4 is put with his
second account of verse~9, however, they are one people: the Church, counted
because it is complete, and countless because it reaches every nation.

The anonymous \work{Expositio in Apocalypsim} printed among St Augustine's
works agrees on the number, \latin{omnis omnino Ecclesia est}, it is the whole
Church altogether, but makes the angel from the east the Church itself,
rising from the Lord's Passion and Resurrection (hom.~6). St Victorinus of
Pettau, whose text has come down in uncertain form, takes the angel for
Elias, sent before Antichrist to restore the churches. The multitude, for
him, is the number of the elect from all believers, Jews and nations alike,
while the hundred and forty-four thousand are those who will believe at
Elias's preaching in the last times (on 7:2--9; 12:6). Bede and the anonymous
writer find the nations under the number; Victorinus finds them in the
multitude and gives the number to a later moment of the same history.

Bede's first account of verse~9 can be read with the Gospel. If the company in white
robes with palms are the victors of the last persecution, they are those whom
the eighth beatitude calls blessed, ``they that suffer persecution for
justice' sake,'' and their reward is the one stated in the present tense,
``theirs is the kingdom of heaven.'' St Bernard, preaching on the feast,
explains why the poor in spirit of the first beatitude receive the same
promise: \latin{vere martyrii genus paupertas voluntaria est}, voluntary
poverty is truly a kind of martyrdom, and the kingdom ``is bought by poverty,
but received without delay in suffering for Christ'' (sermo~1, n.~15).

How many, then, are the saints? Augustine quotes the multitude in a sermon on
the question put to the Lord, ``Are there few that be saved?'', and on his
reply, ``Strive to enter by the strait gate'' (Lk 13:23--24). By that reply,
Augustine argues, the Lord granted that few are saved ``in comparison of the
many that shall perish''; but ``these same `few' will constitute in themselves
a great mass,'' enough ``to fill the garner of heaven,'' and the multitude of
the Apocalypse is that harvest seen in heaven (\work{Sermon} LXI). St Cyprian
had drawn another consequence from the same verses. Urging Christians toward
martyrdom, he concludes, ``let no one think it a hard or a difficult thing to
become a martyr, when he sees that the crowd of martyrs cannot be numbered''
(\work{Ad Fortunatum} 11); his conclusion concerns martyrdom only. Blessed
Ildefonso Schuster, on this Lesson, extends it to all: ``It is not then so hard
to be saved, as St John writes that he could not count the number of the
elect'' (p.~210). The Lesson's words, \latin{quam dinumerare nemo poterat},
say that no one could number the multitude; Schuster places the failure to
count in John, and neither he nor the Lesson says anything of God's count.
Augustine and Schuster both hold that the saints are many. From this Schuster
infers that salvation is not hard; Augustine, that the gate is narrow still,
though the few who pass it are many. Schuster himself attaches a condition:
the seal is the Tau that Ezechiel saw marked on the foreheads of those who
sigh and mourn (Ezech 9:4), read with Paul's ``as you are partakers of the
sufferings, so shall you be also of the consolation'' (2~Cor 1:7).

What men cannot count, Augustine says, God knows. In the
\work{Enchiridion} redeemed mankind fills ``the gap which the rebellion and
fall of the devils had left in the company of the angels,'' perhaps with
``even a more abundant population''; ``We do not know the number either of the
saints or of the devils,'' but the number of the citizens is ``present to the
thoughts of the great Creator'' (\work{Ench.}\ 29). The angels of the Introit,
glad, as Fromage says, to see the gaps in their own choirs filled, rejoice at
the completion Augustine describes. St Gregory the Great reckons
from the other side: as many of mankind ascend as there were elect angels who
remained, \latin{quantos illic contigit electos angelos remansisse}, and the
different ways of life of the elect correspond to the nine orders of angels
(\work{Hom.\ in Ev.}\ 34, n.~11). For Augustine the saints replace the angels
who fell; for Gregory they equal the angels who stood. St Thomas Aquinas
reports three such reckonings, by the angels who fell, by those who remained
and by all who were created, and sets all three aside. The number is certain to God, and it is
``better to say that, `to God alone is known the number for whom is reserved
eternal happiness,'\,'' in the words of a Secret of the Missal, one not
said on this day (\work{ST} I q.~23 a.~7). Augustine and Aquinas agree that the
multitude no man could number has a number, and that God knows it.

The Gradual tells ``all ye his saints'' to fear. St Bernard, speaking of the
verse as it was sung in the night office of the feast, says that the command
is meant for us and not for the saints in glory, who no longer fear, and in
the same sermon he calls those still in
the fight holy \latin{juxta solam adhuc praedestinationem}, ``so far only by
predestination,'' with a holiness that \latin{latet \dots\ penes Deum}, lies
hidden with God (sermo~5, nn.~2--3). He does not comment on the Mass Gradual.
If his words are read beside the Lesson, the saints whom the Gradual
addresses are the servants of God not yet sealed, whose holiness rests on a
number known to God and hidden from them. And if the multitude is the Church drawn from every nation, the
Alleluia's ``Come to me all you that labor'' is the call that gathers it,
addressed, as St John Chrysostom says, to all and not to this or that person
(\work{Hom.\ in Matt.}\ 38.3).

Bede hears the same company in the praise at the end of the Lesson. The
multitude's loud cry is ``an unceasing praise'' that the Father and the Son,
with the Holy Spirit, reign in the Church. In the angels who fall on their
faces, he says, John ``has represented the persons of the great multitude,''
though he allows that the verse may also be taken of the angels themselves;
and the sevenfold praise is the Church's, each member confessing that it has
received this from God (on 7:10--12). The Introit's angels \latin{collaudant},
join in praising, the Son of God, and its psalm says \latin{rectos decet
collaudatio}, ``praise becometh the upright,'' \latin{collaudatio} being praise
given together. Aquinas takes the prefix to mean praise given with one mind
(\work{In Ps.}\ 32), and an extract under the name of Cassiodorus in St Bruno
of Würzburg's commentary calls it ``one praise from the mouths of many,
because of the unity of the Church'' (on Ps 32:1). If the angels and the just
praise with one mind, theirs is the praise of a single city. The feast's
three prayers ask mercy through the saints' many intercessors, offer the
gifts \latin{pro cunctorum \dots\ honore iustorum}, for the honour of all the
just, and ask that the faithful be defended by the saints' unceasing prayer.
Fromage, on the Collect, asks what the whole of heaven will not obtain when it
prays, if one of its citizens can obtain much; Bernard says that our weakness
is helped by their intercession (sermo~2, n.~1).

This is the answer of the first interpretation. The sealed and the uncounted
are one Church, a single city with the angels, whose number God knows and men
do not. Its
authorities differ on the angel who seals, on whether the number is now or
still to come, on what the number completes, and on whether the size of the
multitude makes salvation easy.

\subsection{What the fear of the Lord is, and what it is promised}

The Gradual both commands and promises: \latin{Timete Dominum}, fear the Lord,
and \latin{nihil deest timentibus eum}, nothing is lacking to those who fear
him. One interpretation reads the command as the first step of the way the
Gospel describes; another asks how the promise can be true of saints who went
hungry and were killed.

Augustine puts fear on the lowest step of the Beatitudes. He matches the seven
steps that rise from the poor in spirit to the peacemakers with the seven
gifts of the Spirit in Isaias, beginning from the fear of the Lord and going
up (Isa 11:2--3). Fear goes with poverty of spirit, piety with meekness, knowledge
with mourning, fortitude with hunger for justice, counsel with mercy,
understanding with cleanness of heart, and wisdom with peace (\work{De
serm.\ Dom.}\ I.4.11). The eighth beatitude ``goes back to the starting-point,
and makes manifest the perfect man,'' as circumcision fell on the eighth day
and the Lord rose on the day after the Sabbath (I.4.12). The poor in spirit
are those who fear God, and Rabanus Maurus, who marks his sources as he
compiles, copies this reading from Augustine with the verse ``the beginning
of wisdom is the fear of the Lord.'' If the Gradual's \latin{Timete Dominum} is
the fear of the first step, the Gradual sings the beginning of the road whose
stages the Gospel names.

Chrysostom's golden chain starts at the same place. Humility leads to
mourning for sin; mourning to meekness, righteousness and mercy; mercy to a
pure heart and to peacemaking; and whoever has come so far is armed against
danger and is not shaken when evil is spoken of him (\work{Hom.\ in
Matt.}\ 15.9). On the Alleluia's verse he looks ahead to the next one, ``learn
of me, because I am meek, and humble of heart'' (Mt 11:29). Meekness and
lowliness are ``the mother of all strictness of life,'' and so, ``when
beginning those divine laws, with this He began'' (38.3), that is, with the
Beatitudes. Augustine, preaching on the same two verses, tells his hearers
much the same: ``Thou wishest to be great, begin from the least,'' and ``first
think of the foundation of humility'' (\work{Sermon} XIX [LXIX], n.~2).

Bernard's first sermon for the feast orders the beatitudes differently, as
reconciliations: in the first three the soul is reconciled with itself, in
the next two with its neighbour, in the sixth with God; in the seventh it
reconciles others, and the eighth belongs to the martyrs (sermo~1,
nn.~14--15). The three that the Communion sings again are therefore, for
Augustine, the sixth and seventh steps and the eighth that returns to the
first, and for Bernard the soul at peace with God, the peacemaker, and the
martyr.

In Augustine's order fear comes first. St Basil the Great, Aquinas and Bernard
speak of a fear the saint keeps. Basil says that unless fear has ordered a man's
life, holiness cannot be gained in the body, and quotes \latin{Confige timore
tuo carnes meas}, ``Pierce thou my flesh with thy fear'' (Ps 118:120). The man
whom fear keeps from every unseemly act \latin{nihil deest ad quamlibet
virtutem, sed perfectus est}, lacks nothing for any virtue and is perfect
(\work{Hom.\ in Ps.}\ 33, n.~6). Aquinas: \latin{nullus potest esse sanctus
nisi sit timens}, nobody is a saint without fear, and fear is still needed
by those who stay holy (\work{Super Ps.}\ 33, n.~10). Bernard
puts fear and beatitude in one sentence: \latin{Tota interim beatitudo nostra
est timere Deum}, meanwhile our whole beatitude is to fear God, while the
saints in glory keep their feast in exultation and praise (sermo~5, n.~3).

The promise is harder. Augustine voices the objection himself: by the letter
the verse seems to deceive, since a man may seek the Lord every day and have
nothing good. Basil answers first with a figure. On the half-verse the
Gradual leaves out, the rich who lacked may teach contempt of riches, or they
may be Israel, to whom the adoption and the promises belonged (Rom 9:4--5),
which killed the Bread of life and now hungers for the word; the nations who
seek the Lord lack no good, and \latin{Perfectum omnino bonum Deus ipse est},
the wholly perfect good is God himself. Then he presses a dilemma. If the
promised good is bodily, either the apostles did not seek the Lord, since they
lacked bodily goods, or Scripture is false. Neither can be said: Paul sought
the Lord and lacked nothing of the goods, though he was scourged, stoned and
shipwrecked (n.~7). Augustine reaches the same answer by the kind of good: it
is spiritual, \latin{in bonis spiritualibus}. On the half-verse the Gradual
leaves out, he says that the rich lack the bread that says, ``I am the living
bread'' (\work{Enarr.\ in Ps.}\ 33, s.~2, nn.~14--15), and sets beside them
the Gospel's fourth beatitude, those who hunger and thirst after justice
(Mt 5:6). Basil sets the same beatitude beside the psalm's ``O taste, and
see,'' the verse before the Gradual's.

Aquinas and St Robert Bellarmine keep a place for bodily need. Aquinas
expounds \latin{non est inopia} in three ways. It may be said of spiritual
goods, for Isaias calls the fear of the Lord a treasure (Isa 33:6); of bodily
want, for a man satisfied with what he has is not poor (Phil 4:11--12); and because \latin{Deus
quaerentibus se subvenit in necessitate}, God helps those who seek him in
their need. To Paul's ``Even unto this hour we both hunger and thirst''
(1~Cor 4:11) he answers with a physician who withholds food from the sick for
their health: God sends want or gives riches as salvation requires.
Bellarmine has a higher sense, in which to feel no want ``is the essence of
perfection'' and those who cling to the Supreme Good ``possess all that is
good,'' and a humbler one, in which God provides his servants with temporal
things and does not desert them in need (\work{Explanatio in Psalmos}, on
Ps 33).

All four say that the fearer lacks nothing that matters. They divide over
bread. Basil uses the apostles' hunger to exclude bodily goods from the
promise; Aquinas uses the same hunger as an example of God's medicine. On
Basil's account the Gradual promises the saints God himself and the virtues.
On Aquinas's and Bellarmine's, it promises help in need as well, and
the hunger of the saints was medicine from God.

\subsection{When and where the reward is had}

The Beatitudes do not keep one tense. The first and the eighth say
\latin{ipsorum est regnum caelorum}, theirs is the kingdom; the six between
promise in the future; and the last verse places the reward ``in heaven.''
None of these authors divides the rewards along those lines. Augustine calls
the eight rewards one reward, the kingdom of heaven named differently at each
stage, and Aquinas calls them \latin{unum praemium}, one reward; whatever
they say about time applies to all eight.

Most of them place some of the reward in this life. Augustine says outright
that ``those promises can indeed be fulfilled in this life, as we believe them
to have been fulfilled in the case of the apostles'' (I.4.12). Chrysostom,
asked where the mourners will be comforted, answers ``Both here and there''
(15.4), and of the meek inheriting the earth he says the Lord adds future
gifts to present ones without limiting the reward to the present (15.5). Of
the rest promised to the meek and lowly in the verse after the Alleluia's, he
says, ``even before the things to come, He gives thee here thy recompense''
(38.3). Aquinas puts it in two
phrases: the rewards are had \latin{in via}, on the way, \latin{secundum
inchoationem}, as a beginning, and \latin{in patria}, in the homeland,
perfectly; the peacemakers' peace \latin{hic inchoatur}, is begun here
(\work{Super Matt.}\ 5, pp.~67, 72). Rabanus, in words he marks as his own,
says that the clean of heart see the Lord \latin{hic in aenigmate per
gratiam}, here darkly by grace, and face to face hereafter (on 5:8, with
1~Cor 13:12). Bernard says that they see now ``through a glass in a dark
manner,'' and calls the lesser kingdom given now a pledge, \latin{velut in
arrham}, of the greater (sermo~1, nn.~9, 13). On the Introit's psalm Basil
contrasts the rare taste of joy granted to us with the just man's lasting
joy, ``because the Spirit dwells in him once for all'' (\work{Hom.\ in Ps.}\
32, n.~1).

St Hilary of Poitiers places the reward chiefly beyond death. The Lord on the
mountain sits \latin{in paternae scilicet majestatis positus celsitudine}, in
the height of the Father's majesty, and gives \latin{caelestis vitae
praecepta}, the precepts of the heavenly life, because only one who stood in
eternity could hand on its laws (\work{In Matt.}\ IV, n.~1). The earth of the
meek is the body the Lord assumed, with whose glory we shall be clothed;
consolation and fullness are \latin{in caelo}, in heaven; and the clean of heart see God only ``when we
are perfected in spirit and changed by immortality'' (nn.~3--7). Read with
Hilary, the Gospel describes chiefly what the saints now possess; read with
the others, it describes the life they led as well. Augustine's next
sentence, however, keeps ``that all-embracing change into the
angelic form, which is promised after this life,'' so between him and Hilary
the difference is one of emphasis.

The Offertory receives the same two-stage answer. To \latin{illi autem sunt in
pace}, ``but they are in peace,'' the \work{Glossa}, as St Bonaventure reports
it, adds \latin{Perpetuae quietis, nunc in spe, tandem in re}, a perpetual rest
now in hope, at last in fact. St Alphonsus Liguori says that the just at death
``begin to feel that peace which they shall afterwards fully enjoy in
Heaven.'' Fromage reads the antiphon with the Gospel just read: the
Beatitudes lift man above torments and above death, which \latin{n'ébranle pas
la paix des justes, mais la consomme}, does not shake the peace of the just
but brings it to completion. This is the answer of the second interpretation:
the Beatitudes describe the way the saints walked, and its reward, begun on
the way, is made perfect at its end.

The third interpretation answers the same question from the Offertory's
first words. Augustine's gloss, that the just in God's hand dwell in the
help of the Most High (Ps 90:1), is said of the just among the dead before
their resurrection (\work{Enarr.\ in Ps.}\ 87, n.~5). Bonaventure explains why
the verse says souls and not bodies: the body is handed over to the wicked,
but the souls are under God's protection, \latin{et ideo sunt securae}, and
therefore safe, as the Lord says, ``no man shall pluck them out of my hand''
(Jn 10:28). His text reads \latin{tormentum mortis}, the torment of death, and
he takes the torment that cannot touch them for eternal death, since temporal
death is ``as it were a shadow of death, a passage to life.'' What he says of
the torment is said of his word \latin{mortis}, not of the antiphon's
\latin{malitiae}, on which none of these commentators speaks.

They differ over whose souls are meant. Schuster reads the antiphon of the
martyrs: ``The persecutor believes that the lives of the martyrs are in his
hands, but they are in the hands of God'' (p.~211). Alphonsus reads it of
any just soul at its death, when its enemy is the last temptation, and asks, ``If God holds fast in his hands the souls of the just, who can
snatch them from him?'' (\work{Preparation for Death}, Ninth Consideration).
Bonaventure has both: the martyrs first, after the \work{Glossa}, \latin{quod
etiam verum est de aliis iustis}, which is true also of the other just. On
Schuster's reading the Offertory points to the martyrs among the saints of
the feast; on Alphonsus's, to every one of them.

The third interpretation reads the Offertory with the Gradual. If the good
that the fearer cannot lack is God himself, as Basil says, then the just,
whom Augustine and Bonaventure place under God's help and protection, have
that good though they went hungry and were put to death, and the promise of
no want is kept in them.

\subsection{The four senses compared}

The literal sense of each interpretation starts from its own passage: John's
vision of the sealed and the countless; Jesus seated on the mountain, telling
his disciples who is blessed; the psalmist's promise of no want, and Wisdom's
just, who seemed to die and are at peace. The allegory of the first is Christ
sealing his own with the cross, and the whole Church under the number (Bede);
of the second, Christ teaching the precepts of the heavenly life from the
Father's majesty (Hilary); of the third, Israel, which killed the Bread of
life and went hungry, and the nations who seek the Lord and lack no good
(Basil). The moral sense of the first is Bernard's: since the holiness of
those still fighting lies hidden with God, we praise those \latin{quorum jam
certa victoria est}, whose victory is already certain (sermo~5, n.~3), and
Augustine adds that the gate stays narrow; of the second, to climb from
humility and fear (Augustine, Chrysostom); of the third, to let fear order
the life until nothing is lacking for any virtue (Basil). The anagogy of the
first is a city of angels and men complete in a number God knows (Augustine,
Aquinas); of the second, one reward given perfectly in the homeland (Aquinas) to those changed
by immortality (Hilary); of the third, God himself as the perfect good
(Basil), and the souls of the just safe in his hand (Augustine,
Bonaventure).
