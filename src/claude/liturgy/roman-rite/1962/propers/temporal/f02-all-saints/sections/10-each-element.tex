\runninghead{Each Text in Its Setting}
\section{Each Text in Its Setting}
\label{sec:each-element}

Each appointed text is set here in its own place: the chapter or psalm it
comes from, the words the Missal keeps and leaves out, its history where that
is known, and what the Fathers and later commentators say of it. The three
interpretations that follow take up the larger questions.

\subsection{Introit: the Church's antiphon and the psalm of the just}
\label{sec:element-introit}

The antiphon was not written for this feast. Blessed Ildefonso Schuster notes
that \latin{Gaudeamus \dots\ sub honore} is an ancient Introit ``originally
assigned to the feast of St Agatha on February 5'' (\work{The Sacramentary}
V, p.~209), and William Durandus, in the thirteenth century, already gives
\latin{Gaudeamus omnes in Domino} to St Agatha, explaining it by her going to
prison and to her passion with joy (\work{Rationale} VII.47). On this feast the
antiphon names, in place of one martyr, \latin{Sanctorum omnium}, and keeps
the angels who rejoice at the solemnity and praise the Son of God.

The verse opens Psalm~32, a hymn: the just are bidden to rejoice and to praise
on the harp (32:1--3), the psalm recounts the word and the works of the Lord
(32:4--9) and his counsel that stands against the counsels of the nations
(32:10--11), and it calls blessed ``the nation whose God is the Lord''
(32:12). St Augustine reads the first verse as a warning: ``rejoice, O ye
righteous, not in yourselves, for that is not safe; but in the Lord. `For
praise is comely to the upright': these praise the Lord, who submit themselves
unto the Lord'' (\work{En.\ in Ps.}\ 32 [NPNF ``Psalm XXXIII''], 1). St Robert Bellarmine
takes the rejoicing to include praise, and the praise ``becometh the upright''
because it is ``the special duty of the just'' (\work{Explanatio}, Ps~32, v.~1).

\subsection{Collect: one celebration and many intercessors}
\label{sec:element-collect}

Schuster finds the Collect already in the Gelasian Sacramentary, where it
belongs to a collective feast of the Apostles within the octave of Ss Peter
and Paul (p.~210); on this feast its \latin{omnium Sanctorum} widens it. The
prayer gives two reasons together. God has given us to venerate the merits of
all the saints in one celebration; and the intercessors, being many, are the
ground of the petition. Durandus, giving the four causes for which the feast
was instituted, names both. The second is that many saints have no feast of
their own, and on this day all are honoured together. The fourth is that
petitions are more easily granted, ``because it is impossible that a multitude
should not be heard'' (\latin{quia impossibile est multitudinem non
exaudiri}), and he quotes this Collect for it, in a form that reads
\latin{multiplicatis intercessionibus}, ``with the intercessions multiplied,''
where the 1962 Missal reads \latin{intercessoribus}, ``intercessors''
(\work{Rationale} VII.34). The third interpretation below returns to this
cause.

\subsection{Commemoration Collect: the bonds of sins}
\label{sec:element-commemoration-collect}

The Sunday's Collect enters only because the feast has displaced the Sunday,
and it is said after the feast's own, as the opening sets out. It asks that
God absolve the sins of his peoples, so that by his kindness they may be freed
from the bonds that their frailty has contracted. The two prayers were not
composed together. Said one after the other, the feast's prayer asks for
mercy through the many saints, and the Sunday's asks release from the sins of
those who pray; the order is that of the calendar, and the study notes it
only as the order in which the Mass is said.

\subsection{Lesson: the sealing and the multitude}
\label{sec:element-lesson}

The seventh chapter of the Apocalypse stands between the sixth seal and the
seventh. Four angels stand at the four corners of the earth, holding the four
winds (7:1). The angel from the rising of the sun forbids them to hurt the
earth or the sea or the trees until the servants of God are sealed on their
foreheads (7:2--3). Schuster connects the seal with the sign of Ezechiel's
vision, where the Lord commands, ``mark Thau upon the foreheads of the men
that sigh, and mourn for all the abominations'' committed in Jerusalem (Ezech
9:4). He quotes it as \latin{Signa Tau super frontem virorum gementium et
dolentium} and reads it as ``a sign of membership or conformity with the
Eternal Father and his Christ'' (p.~210). John then hears the number, a hundred and forty-four
thousand, twelve thousand from each tribe. The list of tribes is not that of
Jacob's sons: Juda is put first, Dan is missing, and Manasses, Joseph's son,
is counted with Joseph. After the number John sees a multitude ``which no man
could number,'' from all nations, in white robes and with palms, and the
angels, the ancients and the four living creatures fall down before the throne.
The Lesson ends at the angels' sevenfold praise. It does not read the elder's
question in 7:13 or his answer, that these ``are they who are come out of great
tribulation and have washed their robes \dots\ in the blood of the Lamb''
(7:14).

Schuster calls the Lesson ``significant of the origin of the feast,'' because
it is the same as for the dedication of the Pantheon on 13~May (p.~210; his
printed reference to chapter~viii is an error for vii). Durandus gives the
same Lesson, \latin{Ecce ego Ioan.}, in his account of the feast (VII.34).
How the number and the multitude are related is the question of the first
interpretation below.

\subsection{Gradual: fear and want}
\label{sec:element-gradual}

Psalm~33 is an alphabetical psalm. Its title sets it at the time when David
``changed his countenance before Achimelech, who dismissed him, and he went his
way'' (33:1; compare 1~Kings 21:10--15). In the verses before the Gradual's, the angel of the Lord
encamps round about those who fear him and delivers them, and the psalm says,
``O taste, and see that the Lord is sweet'' (33:8--9). The Gradual takes up at
the next verse, ``Fear the Lord, all ye his saints,'' and it leaves out the
first half of verse~11, ``The rich have wanted, and have suffered hunger.''
Schuster notes that the same Gradual is sung on 8~August for St Cyriacus
(p.~210).

The psalm's own word for the faithful, \latin{Sancti eius}, ``his saints,'' is
the word of the feast. The promise attached to it, \latin{nihil deest
timentibus eum}, raises the difficulty that saints have gone hungry and been
put to death. St Augustine raises it himself, and St Basil the Great and St
Robert Bellarmine answer it; the third interpretation follows them.

\subsection{Alleluia: the invitation}
\label{sec:element-alleluia}

The verse is the Lord's invitation in Mt 11:28. Before it he thanks the
Father for hiding ``these things from the wise and prudent'' and revealing
them ``to little ones,'' and says that no one knows the Father but the Son and
he to whom the Son will reveal him (11:25--27). After it he says, ``Take up my
yoke upon you, and learn of me, because I am meek, and humble of heart \dots\
For my yoke is sweet and my burden light'' (11:29--30).

St John Chrysostom notes whom the invitation reaches: ``Not this or that
person, but all that are in anxiety, in sorrows, in sins.'' He notes too what
it promises: ``He said not, `I will save you,' only; but \dots\ `I will place
you in all security''' (\work{Hom.\ in Matt.}\ 38.3). St Augustine, preaching
on the same verse, asks why the Lord calls the labouring to him, ``but that ye
may labour no more?'', and tells his hearers to ``first think of the
foundation of humility'' if they would build high, the height of that building
reaching ``even to the Vision of God'' (\work{Sermo} 69, 1--3). Schuster
calls the verse ``a preparation for the Gospel'': ``Jesus invites those who
faint beneath the cross to come to him and he will give them refreshment''
(p.~210). Durandus, in his account of the feast, gives a different Alleluia,
\latin{O quam gloriosum}, so the Mass he knew agreed with this one in the Gospel
but not in this verse.

\subsection{Gospel: the Beatitudes}
\label{sec:element-gospel}

The Gospel is the opening of the Sermon on the Mount. Seeing the multitudes,
Jesus goes up into a mountain, sits, and teaches his disciples (5:1--2). The
eight beatitudes follow (5:3--10), and then he applies the last of them to the
disciples themselves: they are blessed when they are reviled and persecuted
for his sake, and their reward is very great in heaven (5:11--12a). The Missal
stops before ``For so they persecuted the prophets that were before you.''
St Augustine calls the Sermon ``a perfect standard of the Christian life''
(\work{De serm.\ Dom.}\ I.1), and the first and the eighth beatitude end with
the same promise, ``for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.''

Schuster says that on this feast ``the Gospel can be no other than that of the
Beatitudes,'' because all are included in it, and those who have least
according to the world receive the larger share; the first beatitude is
promised ``to the humble and poor in spirit'' (pp.~210--211). Durandus gives
the same Gospel, \latin{Videns Iesus turbas} (VII.34). The second
interpretation below takes up why the Mass reads the Beatitudes for the
saints, and how its present and future promises are to be read.

\subsection{Offertory: in the hand of God}
\label{sec:element-offertory}

The Book of Wisdom gives first the speech of the wicked, who reckon life short
and death the end, and resolve: ``Let us oppress the poor just man \dots\ Let us
condemn him to a most shameful death'' (Wis 2:10, 20). Chapter~3
answers them. The souls of the just are in the hand of God; to the unwise they
seemed to die, but they are in peace; ``their hope is full of immortality''
(3:4); and ``The just shall shine'' (3:7). The
antiphon keeps the first clause, the appearance of death and the peace, and
leaves out the two clauses on the departure taken for misery and destruction.

The Fathers quote the verse often. St John Chrysostom uses ``the souls of the
righteous are in the hand of God'' to deny that the souls of the dead wander
the earth (\work{Hom.\ in Matt.}\ 28). St Augustine, on ``My soul is
continually in my hands'' (Ps 118:109), explains the reading ``in thy hand''
by it, since most copies read so: ``For `the souls of the righteous are in
God's hand''' (\work{En.\ in Ps.}\ 118, on v.~109). St Gregory the Great applies
the next clause to the just man whose body is bereft of life ``amidst the hands
of the persecutors,'' for, ``according to the words of Wisdom, In the sight of
the unwise they seemed to die'' (\work{Moralia} XII.v.6, on Job 14:8--9).
Schuster notes that the antiphon is that of St Hippolytus on 13~August, and
reads it of the martyrs: ``The persecutor believes that the lives of the
martyrs are in his hands, but they are in the hands of God''; the wicked are
instruments God uses ``to perfect his works of art,'' and ``the divine artist
and his living masterpiece'' are at peace (p.~211). The third interpretation
returns to the antiphon's ``seemed to die.''

\subsection{Secret: the honour of all the just}
\label{sec:element-secret}

The Secret offers ``the gifts of our devotion'' and asks two things of them:
that they be pleasing to God \latin{pro cunctorum \dots\ honore iustorum}, for
the honour of all the just, and that by his mercy they be for our salvation.
The saints are named here by the word the Offertory has just sung,
\latin{iustorum}. Schuster quotes the prayer whole and adds nothing to it
(p.~211). The prayer asks that the offering honour the saints and benefit
those who make it; it does not ask anything for the saints themselves.

\subsection{Commemoration Secret: the sacrifice of praise}
\label{sec:element-commemoration-secret}

The Sunday's Secret offers \latin{sacrificium \dots\ laudis}, the sacrifice
of praise, ``for the increase of our service,'' and asks God to bring to
completion what he has given \latin{immeritis}, to the undeserving. Like the
Sunday's Collect it was composed for another Mass and is said here because the
Sunday is commemorated. Said after the feast's Secret, it offers the
sacrifice for the increase of the service of those present, and names what
they have received as given without merit.

\subsection{Preface}
\label{sec:element-preface}

The feast has no Preface of its own, and which Preface is said on this day is
not settled; the appointed texts give the two readings of the rubrics.
Nothing in this study is built on
the words of either Preface.

\subsection{Communion: three beatitudes again}
\label{sec:element-communion}

The Communion repeats the sixth, seventh and eighth beatitudes, the clean of
heart, the peacemakers and those persecuted for justice. Schuster says that at
this point ``the Eternal Truth teaches men how to attain'' happiness, setting
out on the Mount ``as it were the decalogue of happiness,'' and paraphrases
the three: the clean of heart shall see God, the peaceable shall be known as
true sons of God, ``the author of peace,'' and the persecuted ``will receive
endless life and joy in exchange for the life and joy they have sacrificed
here'' (p.~211). St Augustine, as the second interpretation shows, places
the vision of God and the divine sonship at the top of his seven steps and
reads the eighth beatitude as a return to the first.

\subsection{Postcommunion: veneration and protection}
\label{sec:element-postcommunion}

The Postcommunion asks that the faithful peoples ever rejoice in the
veneration of all the saints and be defended by their unceasing prayer.
Schuster dwells on its name for the Church on earth, \latin{fideles populi},
``the faithful people'': ``the people journeying towards eternity with the
eyes, and in the light of faith'' (p.~211). The prayer asks for two things
that it holds together: the joy of venerating the saints, which the Introit
began, and their continual intercession, which the Collect asked for.

\subsection{Commemoration Postcommunion: divine things and human dangers}
\label{sec:element-commemoration-postcommunion}

The Sunday's Postcommunion asks that those to whom God gives to rejoice in
sharing divine things, \latin{divina \dots\ participatione}, he not allow to
be subject to human dangers. It is the last proper prayer of the Mass, said
after the feast's Postcommunion. In this order the feast's prayer asks for the
saints' defence, and the Sunday's for protection from human dangers; the
juxtaposition is the calendar's, and the study claims no more for it.
