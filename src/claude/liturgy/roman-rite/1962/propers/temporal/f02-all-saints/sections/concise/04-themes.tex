\runninghead{Themes and Movement}
\section{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\zlabel{triptych:concise:themes:start}

The Lesson of All Saints counts the servants of God who are sealed and then
shows a multitude that no one could number. The Gospel calls blessed the poor,
the mourning and the persecuted. The Gradual promises that nothing is lacking
to those who fear the Lord, and the Offertory puts the souls of the just in
the hand of God. From what the Fathers say of these passages come three
interpretations of the feast: the saints are one city of angels and men,
whose number God knows and no one else can count; the Beatitudes are the way
the saints walked, its rewards begun in this life and perfected in the next;
and the good that those who fear God cannot lack is God himself, in whose hand
the just are kept.

\subsection{Introit and Collect: the angels' feast and the many who pray}

The Introit antiphon is a text of the Church and has no scriptural source:
``Let us all rejoice in the Lord, celebrating a feast in honor of all the
saints, in whose solemnity the angels rejoice and join in praising the Son of
God'' (Lasance). Lucien Fromage, in \work{L'Année liturgique}, says that the angels rejoice on this feast because
they see the gaps in their own choirs filled. The verse is the first of
Psalm~32: ``Rejoice in the Lord, O ye just: praise becometh the upright.''
St Augustine and St Basil both ask what the just rejoice in. For Augustine it
is the Lord and not themselves, \latin{non in vobis, non enim hoc tutum est},
since joy in oneself is not safe (\work{Enarr.\ in Ps.}\ 32, en.~1); for
Basil, not good fortune at home but having the Lord (\work{Hom.\ in Ps.}\ 32,
n.~1). St Thomas Aquinas reads the \latin{col-} of \latin{collaudatio},
praise given together, as praise given in all things, in adversity as in
prosperity, and with one mind (\work{In Ps.}\ 32).

The Collect says that God has given us to venerate the merits of all his
saints \latin{sub una celebritate}, in one celebration, and asks the abundance
of his mercy \latin{multiplicatis intercessoribus}, the intercessors being
many. William Durandus counts the easier granting of petitions among the causes for which the feast
was instituted, ``because it is impossible that a multitude should not be
heard,'' and quotes the Collect for it in a form that reads
\latin{intercessionibus}, the intercessions being many (\work{Rationale}
VII.34). The Sunday's Collect, said next under
its own conclusion, asks God to free his peoples from the bonds of the sins
their frailty has contracted.

\subsection{Lesson: the sealed and the multitude}

In the seventh chapter of the Apocalypse an angel rises from the east
``having the sign of the living God'' and forbids the four angels who have
power to hurt the earth to do so ``till we sign the servants of our God in
their foreheads.'' John hears the number of the sealed, a hundred and
forty-four thousand from the tribes of Israel, twelve thousand from each. Then
he sees ``a great multitude, which no man could number, of all nations and
tribes and peoples and tongues,'' in white robes with palms in their hands; and the angels fall on their faces before the
throne and give God a sevenfold praise. Taken as a count, the number either
contradicts the multitude or leaves the nations outside the seal.

St Bede takes the angel for ``the Lord incarnate, Who is the Angel of the
great counsel,'' who bears ``the ensign of the cross'' to seal his own in
their foreheads (on 7:2). The number, he says,
signifies ``the innumerable multitude of the whole Church,'' descended from the
patriarchs by nature or by the imitation of faith, since St Paul says, ``if ye
be Christ's, then are ye Abraham's seed'' (on 7:4; Gal 3:29). St Augustine, preaching on the
question ``Are there few that be saved?'', quotes the multitude and says,
``This is the mass of the saints'': the grain winnowed from the threshing
floor, few beside the lost and ``many in the society of the Angels.'' When the floor has
been winnowed and the grain separated from the ungodly and the false
Christians, ``the purified mass'' stands at the right hand to reign with Christ
(\work{Sermon} LXI).

\subsection{Gradual, Alleluia and Gospel: fear, rest and the eight beatitudes}

The Gradual sings Psalm~33: \latin{Timete Dominum, omnes Sancti eius: quoniam
nihil deest timentibus eum}, ``Fear the Lord, all ye his saints: for there is
no want to them that fear him''; and then that those who seek the Lord shall
lack no good. It leaves out the half-verse between, ``The rich have wanted,
and have suffered hunger.'' St Augustine puts the objection in a hearer's
mouth: taken by the letter the verse seems to deceive, for a man may rise
daily for the church, kneel daily and seek the Lord daily, and have nothing
good (\work{Enarr.\ in Ps.}\ 33, s.~2, n.~14). St Basil answers that the good which none who seek the Lord shall lack is God himself (\work{Hom.\ in Ps.}\ 33, n.~7). St Bernard, preaching on the feast, says that the same verse,
as it was sung in the night office, is not addressed to the saints in glory,
who no longer fear, but to us (sermo~5, n.~3).

The Alleluia is the Lord's invitation in Mt 11:28, ``Come to me all you that
labor and are burdened, and I will refresh you.'' St John Chrysostom says that
it calls ``not this or that person, but all that are in anxiety, in sorrows,
in sins'' (\work{Hom.\ in Matt.}\ 38.3). St Augustine asks what the Lord means by it, ``but that ye may
labour no more?'' (\work{Sermon} XIX [LXIX], n.~1). Both read it with the verse that follows, ``learn of me,
because I am meek, and humble of heart'' (Mt 11:29), and both make the
humility of that verse the place where the way of the Beatitudes begins.

Seeing the multitudes, Jesus goes up into a mountain, sits, and teaches his
disciples: blessed are the poor in spirit, the meek, those who mourn, those who
hunger and thirst after justice, the merciful, the clean of heart, the
peacemakers, and those who suffer persecution for justice' sake. Then he
applies the last of them to the disciples themselves: they are blessed when
they are reviled and persecuted for his sake, ``for your reward is very great in
heaven.'' The first and the eighth say \latin{ipsorum est regnum caelorum},
``theirs is the kingdom of heaven,'' in the present; the six between promise in
the future.

St Augustine reads the eight as steps that rise from humility. The poor in spirit are the humble who fear God, and at the seventh step, the
peacemakers, the soul reaches wisdom. The eighth ``returns to the starting-point,'' and so the first and the eighth both name the kingdom
(\work{De serm.\ Dom.}\ I.3.10). St John Chrysostom calls the
beatitudes ``a sort of golden chain,'' in which each precept makes way for the
next (\work{Hom.\ in Matt.}\ 15.9).

\subsection{Offertory to Postcommunion: the just in God's hand}

The Offertory adapts the chapter of Wisdom that answers the wicked who said of
the just man, ``Let us condemn him to a most shameful death''
(Wis 2:20): ``The souls of
the just are in the hand of God, and the torment of malice shall not touch
them: in the sight of the unwise they seemed to die, but they are in peace.
Alleluia'' (Lasance). St Augustine glosses the first clause: the just ``dwell
in the help of the Most High, and abide in the protection of the God of
heaven'' (\work{Enarr.\ in Ps.}\ 87, n.~5). The Secret then offers the gifts \latin{pro cunctorum \dots\
honore iustorum}, for the honour of all the just, and asks that the gifts be for our salvation. The Sunday's
Secret, which follows, asks God to complete what he has given
\latin{immeritis}, to the undeserving.

The feast has no Preface of its own; either of the two the rubrics admit, the
Trinity Preface or the common Preface, ends with the praise of the angels and
the \latin{Sanctus}. The Communion sings again, word
for word, the last three beatitudes of the Gospel: the clean of heart, who
shall see God; the peacemakers; the persecuted. Fromage gives the reason: the
antiphon cannot list the whole series again, so it recalls the last three, and
in doing so brings them all to the Sacrament in which they are nourished.

The Postcommunion asks that the faithful peoples ever rejoice in venerating
all the saints and be defended \latin{perpetua supplicatione}, by their
unceasing prayer. St Bernard asks what our honour does for the saints, and
answers that they need none of it: that we venerate their memory concerns us,
not them, since the memory kindles in us a desire for their company
(sermo~5, n.~5). The Sunday's
Postcommunion, the last proper prayer of the Mass, asks that those whom God
gives to rejoice in sharing divine things be not left subject to human
dangers.
