\runninghead{The Sealed and the Uncounted}
\properlane{sealed-number-uncounted-crowd}{One City of Angels and Men, Numbered by God}

The Lesson counts the servants of God who are sealed, a hundred and forty-four
thousand, and then shows a multitude that no one could count. St Bede takes
the number for the whole Church and the seal for the sign of the cross. St
Augustine calls the uncounted multitude the mass of the saints, and says that
their number is known to God and not to us. Taken with the angels of the
Introit and the many intercessors of the Collect, the Lesson shows one company,
numbered by God and beyond our count, gathered with the angels before the
throne.

\subsection{A number, and a multitude without number}
\label{sec:sealed-question}

John hears a number before he sees anything: \latin{centum quadraginta
quatuor millia signati, ex omni tribu filiorum Israel}, and the number is
then given again tribe by tribe, twelve thousand from each. Immediately after
it he sees \latin{turbam magnam, quam dinumerare nemo poterat}, ``a great
multitude, which no man could number,'' from all nations and tribes and
peoples and tongues. Read as a count, the number either contradicts the
multitude or confines the sealed to Israel and leaves the nations outside the
seal. Two further questions follow from the same verses. If the number is a
figure, is there a number at all, and who knows it? And if no man could count
the multitude, does its size mean that salvation is easily reached?

\subsection{St Bede: the seal, the number and the multitude}
\label{sec:sealed-bede}

St Bede comments on the Apocalypse verse by verse. The four angels at the
corners of the earth are the four kingdoms, or the four winds (on 7:1). The
angel from the rising of the sun is ``the Lord incarnate, Who is the Angel of
the great counsel,'' who ``has visited us, `the day-spring from on high,'
bearing the ensign of the cross, with which to seal His own in their
foreheads'' (on 7:2; Lk 1:78). The command not to hurt the earth he reads of
what followed the Passion: ``to this end was the empire of the nations broken
up, that the face of the saints might be freely marked with the seal of faith,
which these had resisted'' (on 7:3). The seal is faith, marked with the cross,
on the forehead where it is seen.

On the number he is explicit: ``By this definite number is signified the
innumerable multitude of the whole Church, which is descended from the
patriarchs either by the lineage of nature, or the imitation of faith.'' His
warrant for counting the Gentiles among the tribes is St Paul's ``if ye be
Christ's, then are ye Abraham's seed'' (Gal 3:29). Twelve multiplied by twelve
is raised to thousands, ``the cube of the number ten, by which is represented
the enduring life of the Church'' (on 7:4). The number is a figure of the
Church's completeness, and the twelve tribes include those who are Abraham's
children by faith.

At verse~9 he gives two accounts of the multitude, in this order. First, the
vision ``announces the glory of those who are to overcome the wickedness of the
last persecution.'' Then, ``that which follows, `From all nations, and tribes,
and peoples, and tongues,' may also be thus understood, that after enumerating
the tribes of Israel, to whom the Gospel was first preached, he desires to make
mention of the salvation of the Gentiles as well.'' The white robes are
baptism and the palms ``the triumph of the Cross'' (on 7:9). Bede does not say
in so many words that the number and the multitude are one people seen twice.
But if the number signifies the whole Church, as he says at verse~4, and the
multitude adds the Gentiles to the tribes, as his second account of verse~9
has it, the Lesson shows one Church twice: under a number, in its
completeness, and beyond number, in its extent.

The rest of the Lesson he reads as the Church's praise. The multitude's loud
voice is ``great devotion, an unceasing praise, that on the throne, namely, in
the Church, there reign the Father and the Son; the Holy Spirit, nevertheless,
reigning together with them'' (on 7:10). When the angels fall on their faces
before the throne, ``In all the angels he has represented the persons of the
great multitude worshipping the Lord,'' though he allows that the verse may be
taken of the angels themselves (on 7:11). The sevenfold praise of verse~12 is
the Church's: ``The Church offers the sevenfold praise of excellence unto the
Lord, and in each of its members confesses to have received this from Him''
(on 7:12).

Two other early commentators differ from Bede on the angel, and one of them
on the number. An anonymous commentary printed among Augustine's works takes
the angel from the east for the Church itself, \latin{eamdem Ecclesiam
catholicam \dots\ ab ortu solis, a passione et resurrectione Domini}, ``the
Catholic Church, from the rising of the sun, that is, from the Passion and
Resurrection of the Lord,'' and says of the number, \latin{Centum quadraginta
quatuor millia, omnis omnino Ecclesia est}, ``the hundred and forty-four
thousand are the whole Church altogether'' (\work{Expositio in Apocalypsim},
hom.~6). St Victorinus of Pettau, in a text whose transmission is uncertain,
takes the angel for Elias, who comes before Antichrist ``for the restoration
and establishment of the churches,'' and says that ``the number of those that
shall believe, of the Jews and of the nations, is a great multitude which no
man was able to number''; the multitude of verse~9 is ``the number of the
elect out of all believers'' (on 7:2--9). Elsewhere he places the 144,000
among those who believe at Elias's preaching in the last times (on 12:6), so
that for him the two companies are two moments of one history.

\subsection{St Augustine: the mass of the saints and the number God knows}
\label{sec:sealed-augustine}

St Augustine quotes the Lesson's multitude in a sermon on the question put to
the Lord, ``Are there few that be saved?'', and on his answer, ``Strive to
enter by the strait gate'' (Lk 13:23--24). The Lord, he says, ``confirmed what
He heard'': ``few then are saved in comparison of the many that shall perish.
For these same `few' will constitute in themselves a great mass.'' The mass is
the wheat on the threshing floor, which the Winnower lays up in his garner:
``So large a mass is to come out of this floor, as to fill the garner of
heaven.'' Then he names the multitude: ``the `few' are themselves the `many;'
`few' in comparison of the lost, `many in the society of the Angels.' Hearken,
dearly Beloved. The Apocalypse hath this written; `After this I beheld of all
languages, and nations, and tribes, a great multitude, which no man can number,
coming with white robes and palms.' This is the mass of the saints.'' When the
floor has been winnowed and the grain separated from the ungodly and the false
Christians, ``the purified mass'' stands at the right hand to reign with
Christ (\work{Sermon} LXI). In Augustine's sentence ``the mass of the saints''
is the winnowed grain, and the uncounted multitude is that grain seen in
heaven.

In the \work{Enchiridion} he says what the number completes. Restored mankind
fills ``the gap which the rebellion and fall of the devils had left in the
company of the angels,'' and the heavenly city ``shall perhaps reign over even
a more abundant population.'' He adds at once: ``We do not know the number
either of the saints or of the devils,'' but ``the number of the citizens'' is
``present to the thoughts of the great Creator'' (\work{Ench.}\ 29). What Bede
calls innumerable, Augustine calls unknown to us and known to the Creator.

Others say otherwise what the number completes. St Gregory the Great holds
that the heavenly city is made of angels and men, and that as many men ascend
to it as there were elect angels who remained (\latin{tantum credimus humanum
genus ascendere, quantos illic contigit electos angelos remansisse}); the
different ways of life of the elect answer to the nine orders of angels
(\work{Hom.\ in Ev.}\ 34, n.~11). Augustine fills the place of the fallen;
Gregory matches the number of those who stood. St Thomas Aquinas reports three
opinions, that as many men are saved as angels fell, as many as remained, or
as many as were created, and sets all three aside: the number of the
predestined is certain to God, and ``it is, however, better to say that, `to
God alone is known the number for whom is reserved eternal happiness,''' in
the words of a Secret of the Missal, \latin{Deus, cui soli cognitus est
numerus electorum in superna felicitate locandus} (\work{ST} I q.~23 a.~7).
That Secret stands among the Missal's prayers for various needs and is not
said on this day.

\subsection{The angels' feast and the many who pray}
\label{sec:sealed-elements}

The Introit's antiphon keeps the feast of all the saints \latin{de quorum
solemnitate gaudent Angeli, et collaudant Filium Dei}: at their solemnity the
angels rejoice and join in praising the Son of God. Fromage reads the
angels' joy on this feast as that of spirits \latin{qui voient se combler les
vides de leurs chœurs}, who see the gaps in their own choirs filled: the gap
that, in Augustine's \work{Enchiridion}, restored mankind fills. The psalm
verse that follows bids the just praise, \latin{rectos decet collaudatio}, and
Aquinas reads the prefix of \latin{collaudatio} as praise given with one mind
(see the Introit in Each Text in Its Setting). If the antiphon's angels and
the psalm's just praise with one mind, as Aquinas reads \latin{collaudatio},
theirs is the praise of one city. Either Preface that the rubrics admit ends
with the praise of the angels and the \latin{Sanctus}.

The Collect asks for mercy \latin{multiplicatis intercessoribus}, the
intercessors being many; the form Durandus quotes, \latin{multiplicatis
intercessionibus}, makes the intercessions many. In either form the petition
rests on the number of those who pray. Durandus counts the easier granting of
petitions among the four causes for which the feast was instituted,
\latin{quia impossibile est multitudinem non exaudiri}, ``because it is
impossible that a multitude should not be heard,'' and quotes this Collect for
it (\work{Rationale} VII.34). Fromage, on the same prayer, says that with so
many intercessors the whole of heaven prays, and asks what the whole of heaven
will not obtain, if the prayer of one of its citizens is powerful. The
Postcommunion asks the same help at the end of the Mass: that the faithful
peoples be defended \latin{perpetua supplicatione}, by the saints' unceasing
prayer. Between the two, the Secret offers the gifts \latin{pro cunctorum
\dots\ honore iustorum}, for the honour of all the just together.

The Gradual calls the faithful \latin{omnes Sancti eius}, all his saints, and
bids them fear. St Bernard says that the verse, as it was sung in the night
office of the feast, is addressed to us and not to the saints in glory, who no
longer fear (third interpretation below). In the same sermon he calls those
still fighting holy \latin{juxta solam adhuc praedestinationem}, ``so far only
by predestination,'' a holiness that \latin{latet \dots\ penes Deum}, ``lies
hidden with God'' (sermo~5, n.~2). He does not comment on the Mass Gradual.
Read with the Lesson, the Gradual's saints are the servants of God still to be
sealed, holy by a number that God knows and they do not.

The Alleluia's verse calls \latin{omnes qui laboratis}, all who labour, and
Chrysostom says that it calls not this or that person but all (see the
Alleluia in Each Text in Its Setting). If the multitude is the Church from
every nation, this is the call that gathers it. The Gospel blesses the poor in
spirit, the meek and the rest, and the Communion sings three of its
beatitudes again. Sicard of Cremona, who says that the Lesson and the Gospel
fit the feast together, speaks of the number of the sealed and the number of
the beatitudes. The Offertory says that the souls of the just are in God's
hand and at peace. The Sunday's three prayers, said after the feast's own,
ask pardon for sins contracted through frailty, the completion of a gift
given to the undeserving, and protection from human dangers; no commentator
on the feast speaks of them.

\subsection{Does the multitude mean that salvation is easy?}
\label{sec:sealed-difficulty}

The uncounted multitude can be taken as an argument from number to ease, and
that inference has an ancient precedent. St Cyprian, exhorting to martyrdom,
says that the ancient examples ``may be numbered,'' but that ``the Christian
martyrs cannot be numbered, as the Apocalypse testifies,'' and quotes Apoc
7:9--15. He concludes: ``let no one think it a hard or a difficult thing to
become a martyr, when he sees that the crowd of martyrs cannot be numbered''
(\work{Ad Fortunatum} 11). His conclusion concerns martyrdom only. Schuster,
on this Lesson, draws the wider one: ``It is not then so hard to be saved, as
St John writes that he could not count the number of the elect'' (p.~210).
The Lesson's words are \latin{quam dinumerare nemo poterat}, that no one could
number it; Schuster puts the failure to count in John, and neither he nor the
Lesson speaks of God's count, which Augustine and Aquinas say is known.

Augustine takes the same verse the other way. The saved are many, many in the
company of the angels, and the multitude shows it; but they are few beside the
lost, and the Lord's answer to the question how many are saved was ``Strive to
enter by the strait gate.'' Augustine and Schuster agree that the saints are
many. They differ over what follows: for Schuster, that salvation is not hard;
for Augustine, that the few who pass the narrow gate are many in themselves,
while the gate stays narrow. Schuster himself adds a condition at once: the
seal is the Tau that Ezechiel saw set on the foreheads of those who sigh and
mourn (Ezech 9:4), and he reads it with St Paul's ``as you are partakers of
the sufferings, so shall you be also of the consolation'' (2~Cor 1:7).

\begin{fourSenses}
\item[Literal.] John sees an angel from the east forbid the angels of
destruction to hurt the earth until the servants of God are sealed on their
foreheads; he hears their number, twelve thousand from each of twelve tribes;
he then sees an uncounted multitude from every nation in white robes with
palms before the throne and the Lamb, and the angels fall on their faces and
adore God.
\item[Allegorical.] The angel from the east is Christ, the Angel of great
counsel, who seals his own with the cross, and the hundred and forty-four
thousand are the whole Church, descended from the patriarchs by nature or by
faith (Bede); the anonymous \work{Expositio} takes the same number for the
whole Church altogether.
\item[Moral.] The holiness of those still fighting lies hidden with God, so
we are to praise those whose victory is already certain, \latin{quorum jam
certa victoria est} (Bernard, sermo~5, nn.~2--3), and venerating them profits
us and not them (n.~5); the narrow gate still stands, though those who pass it
are many (Augustine).
\item[Anagogical.] The heavenly city of angels and men is complete in a number
that God knows (Augustine, Gregory, Aquinas), and the winnowed mass of the
saints is laid up in the garner of heaven (Augustine).
\end{fourSenses}
