\runninghead{The Sealed and the Uncounted}
\properlane{sealed-number-uncounted-crowd}{One Church, Sealed and Uncounted}

The Lesson of this Mass counts the servants of God who are sealed, a hundred
and forty-four thousand, and then shows a multitude that no one could count.
This interpretation follows St Bede, who expounds the chapter verse by
verse, and St Augustine, who speaks to its number and its crowd in three
passing loci. Bede reads the number as the whole Church and the seal as the
sign of the cross; Augustine says the number signifies the whole body of the
saints and calls the uncounted crowd the mass of the saints, and on the
Introit's psalm he bids the just rejoice in the Lord and not in themselves.
Victorinus of Pettau, who comments on the chapter's opening and on its
crowd, agrees with them that the multitude is the elect out of all believers
and disagrees with Bede on the angel and on the number, and St Cyprian reads
the crowd as the martyrs, the reading Bede rejects. Read together, the
saints the feast honours are those God has sealed and gathered, from Israel
and from every nation.

\subsection{The question: a number, and a multitude without number}
\label{sec:sealed-question}

John hears a number before he sees anything: \latin{centum quadraginta
quatuor millia signati, ex omni tribu filiorum Israel}, and the number is
then given again tribe by tribe, twelve thousand from each. Immediately after
it he sees \latin{turbam magnam, quam dinumerare nemo poterat}, from all
nations and tribes and peoples and tongues. Read as a count, the number
contradicts the multitude, or restricts the sealed to Israel and leaves the
nations outside the seal. The list of tribes adds a second difficulty. It is
not the list of Jacob's sons in the order of their birth: Juda stands first,
Dan is missing, and Manasses is counted beside his father Joseph. A third
difficulty is the seal itself. The angel forbids the four angels to hurt the
earth \latin{quoadusque signemus servos Dei nostri in frontibus eorum}, but
the Lesson does not say what the seal is, or what it does for those who
bear it.

\subsection{St Bede: the number is the whole Church}
\label{sec:sealed-bede-number}

St Bede, commenting on the Apocalypse verse by verse, takes the angel from
the rising of the sun to be the Lord incarnate, ``the day-spring from on
high'' (Lk 1:78), ``bearing the ensign of the cross, with which to seal His
own in their foreheads'' (\work{Explanatio Apocalypsis} I, on 7:2). The
command not to hurt the earth he reads of what followed the Passion, when the
dominion of the enemy and of worldly power was broken: ``to this end was the
empire of the nations broken up, that the face of the saints might be freely
marked with the seal of faith, which these had resisted'' (on 7:3). The
angel from the east is not read as the Lord by every Father: Victorinus of
Pettau reads him as Elias, in his own subsection below.

On the number he is explicit: ``By this definite number is signified the
innumerable multitude of the whole Church, which is descended from the
patriarchs either by the lineage of nature, or the imitation of faith.'' His
warrant for counting the Gentiles among the tribes is St Paul's ``if ye be
Christ's, then are ye Abraham's seed'' (Gal 3:29). Twelve is multiplied by
twelve and raised to thousands, ``the cube of the number ten, by which is
represented the enduring life of the Church'' (on 7:4).

The order of the tribes he explains in the same way. John begins with Juda,
``from which tribe our Lord sprang,'' and leaves out Dan, from whom, ``as it is
said, Antichrist is to be born,'' citing Jacob's blessing, ``Let Dan become an
adder in the way'' (Gen 49:17). John ``has not proposed to set forth the order
of earthly generation, but according to the interpretation of the names the
powers of the Church, which from present confession and praise is hastening to
the right hand of eternal life.'' Juda means ``confession'' or ``praise,'' and
comes first, ``for before the first step of confession no one reaches the
height of good works''; Benjamin, ``the son of the right hand,'' comes last,
because after death is destroyed the elect are given the eternal inheritance
at Christ's right hand (on 7:5--8). Between them Bede reads each name as a
virtue in which the faithful make progress, from the works of Reuben to the
martyrdom of Zabulon and the unwearied prayer of Benjamin, and concludes that
``from the merits of individuals, should the most perfect beauty of the
Church, as the sum of a hundred and forty-four thousand, be made up.'' On his
reading the twelve thousand of each tribe are not twelve groups of people
but twelve kinds of holiness, each sealed ``in his own profession,'' which
together make up one Church.

\subsection{St Bede: the multitude before the throne}
\label{sec:sealed-bede-multitude}

At verse~9 Bede gives two readings of the multitude. First, the vision
``announces the glory of those who are to overcome the wickedness of the last
persecution.'' Then, ``that which follows, `From all
nations, and tribes, and peoples, and tongues,' may also be thus understood,
that after enumerating the tribes of Israel, to whom the Gospel was first
preached, he desires to make mention of the salvation of the Gentiles as
well'' (on 7:9). Bede does not say that the number and the multitude are the
same people seen twice. He says that the number signifies the whole Church
(on 7:4), and, in his second reading, that the multitude adds the Gentiles to
the tribes. Taken together, Bede's two comments give the number as the
Church's completeness and the multitude as its extent.

The robes, he writes, signify baptism, and the palms ``the triumph of the
Cross,'' and ``in Christ they have overcome the world.'' The loud voice is
``great devotion, an unceasing praise'' (on 7:10). When the angels fall down
before the throne, ``In all the angels he has represented the persons of the
great multitude worshipping the Lord,'' though Bede allows that the verse may
also be read of the angelic spirits themselves (on 7:11). The sevenfold praise
of verse~12 is the Church's: ``The Church offers the sevenfold praise of
excellence unto the Lord, and in each of its members confesses to have
received this from Him'' (on 7:12).

Two of Bede's comments on verses that the Lesson does not read bear on whom
the feast honours. St Cyprian, exhorting Fortunatus to martyrdom, quotes the
uncounted multitude in white robes, who have washed their robes and made
them white in the blood of the Lamb, and concludes that ``the Christian
martyrs cannot be numbered'' (\work{Ad Fortunatum} \S11): on his reading the
crowd before the throne is the crowd of the martyrs. On the white robes
washed in the blood of the Lamb (7:14) Bede writes, ``He speaks not of
martyrs alone. They are washed in their own blood. But the blood of Jesus,
the Son of God, cleanses the whole Church from all sin.'' The reading
Cyprian gives the vision is the one Bede here refuses. And after expounding
the end of the chapter as the life to come, he
adds that the vision of the white-robed multitude ``may also be understood of
the present time, when `we are saved in hope''' (Rom 8:24; on 7:17). The feast
was first a feast of martyrs: Schuster traces it to a collective feast of
martyrs in fourth-century Syria and to the feast of the martyrs that Boniface
IV instituted when he consecrated the Pantheon as a church (\work{The
Sacramentary} V, pp.~208--209). On Bede's reading of
7:14 the white robes are not the martyrs' alone.

\subsection{St Augustine: the whole body of the saints, rejoicing in the Lord}
\label{sec:sealed-augustine}

St Augustine nowhere expounds this chapter; what he says of its number and
its crowd he says in passing, inside other arguments. Setting out
Tichonius's fifth rule, the rule of the times, he comes to the numbers
Scripture favours: ``twelve into twelve gives one hundred and forty-four,
which last number is used in the Apocalypse to signify the whole body of the
saints,'' and that number, he adds, ``has not reference to times, but to
men'' (\work{De doctrina christiana} III.35.51). The application stands in
his account of Tichonius's rule, which the text does not separate from his
own. Writing to Vincentius that the one Church is few beside the wicked and
many in herself, he instances this vision: ``in the Apocalypse, many
thousands `which no man can number,' from every tribe and tongue, are seen
clothed in white robes, and with palms of victory'' (\work{Letter XCIII},
\S30). Preaching on the few and the many, he tells his hearers that the few
who are saved are ``many in the society of the Angels,'' and quotes the
great multitude ``coming with white robes and palms'': ``This is the mass of
the saints'' (\work{Sermon LXI}, 1). None of the three expounds the chapter,
and none speaks of the seal or of the angel from the east; they are short
applications, and on the two points the Lesson presses they stand with Bede:
the number signifies the whole body of the saints, and the uncounted crowd
is the one Church.

His word to the Mass's own verses is on its psalms. On the Introit's
verse he writes, ``rejoice, O ye righteous, not in yourselves, for that is not
safe; but in the Lord. `For praise is comely to the upright': these praise the
Lord, who submit themselves unto the Lord'' (\work{En.\ in Ps.}\ 32 [NPNF
``Psalm XXXIII''], 1). The Introit antiphon bids all rejoice in the Lord on
the feast of all the saints, and the verse, read with Augustine, says in whom
the just rejoice: not in themselves.

Later in the same psalm, outside the verse the Mass sings, Augustine reads
``Blessed is the nation whose God is the Lord'' of ``one nation \dots\
belonging to the heavenly city,'' which ``not of itself, but by the gift of
God, hath been chosen'' (32:12; \S12). The Lesson's multitude is \latin{ex
omnibus gentibus}; to hear Augustine's one nation beside it is this
interpretation's inference, not his. What his comment gives is the point this
interpretation draws from Bede as well: those who are counted are counted by
God's choice and gift.

On the Gradual, too, Augustine is a witness. Its verse sings that those who
seek the Lord shall lack no good, and he asks what good is meant. ``When thou
art filled with spiritual riches, canst thou be poor?'' The riches are ``such
jewelry of virtues, justice, truth, charity, faith, endurance,'' and of the
promise, ``what manner of good, I have already said'' (\work{En.\ in Ps.}\ 33
[NPNF ``Psalm XXXIV''], 14). Augustine names those goods as the virtues,
justice, truth, charity, faith, endurance; they are close to the virtues Bede
reads in the tribes' names.

\subsection{Victorinus of Pettau: the angel as Elias, the number in the last
times}
\label{sec:sealed-victorinus}

One other independent Latin commentary on the Apocalypse reaches this
chapter among the witnesses read: Victorinus of Pettau's, and it reads the
chapter differently from Bede. Victorinus comments on two of the Lesson's
verses. Of the angel
ascending from the east he writes, ``He speaks of Elias the prophet, who is
the precursor of the times of Antichrist,'' sent, as Malachias says, ``to
recall the Jews to the faith of the people that succeed them'' (Mal 4:5--6;
\work{Commentary on the Apocalypse}, on 7:2). Where Bede sees the Lord
incarnate bearing the cross, Victorinus sees the forerunner of the last
persecution. He says nothing of the number at the Lesson's verse; when his
commentary reaches the woman fleeing into the wilderness, he names it: ``in
the last times a hundred and forty-four thousands of men should believe on
the preaching of Elias'' (on 12:6). The definite number, on his reading,
counts the believers of the last times, not the whole Church's completeness.

On the crowd he stands with Bede. The multitude no man could number shows
``the number of the elect out of all believers, who, being cleansed by
baptism in the blood of the Lamb, have made their robes white, keeping the
grace which they have received'' (on 7:9), and the believers are ``of the
Jews and of the nations'' (on 7:2). The elect gathered out of all believers,
and the robes as baptism in the Lamb's blood, are Bede's own readings; the
keeping of the grace received adds a note Bede does not sound, that the
white robe is a gift to be kept. Victorinus also warns that the Apocalypse
does not tell its events in their order --- ``Nor must we look for order in
the Apocalypse; but we must follow the meaning of those things which are
prophesied'' --- so that on his reading the sealing and the crowd belong to
the same last times.

His witness stands under a caution. The text of the commentary is not
secure: the editors of the English edition report that ``the manuscripts
must have been much corrupted,'' that the mention of Genseric ``proves the
subsequent interpolation of his works,'' and that what survives may be
``full of the corrections of some pious disciple of St. Augustine who lived
much later,'' with the author's own identity in doubt. His reading is given
here under that caution, and with its gaps: he passes over the sealing
itself and the tribes. What he gives this interpretation is a second
developed reading of the chapter, one that agrees with Bede on whom the
crowd is and on what its robes mean, and disagrees with him on the angel and
on what the number counts.

\subsection{The sealed in the other texts of the Mass}
\label{sec:sealed-elements}

The Introit and the Collect speak of the saints as one company. The antiphon
keeps the feast \latin{sub honore Sanctorum omnium}, and the Collect thanks
God for giving us to venerate the merits of all the saints \latin{sub una
\dots\ celebritate} and asks for mercy \latin{multiplicatis intercessoribus}.
Durandus explains the one celebration by the saints' number: so many could
not each be given a feast (\work{Rationale} VII.34). The Gradual calls them
\latin{Sancti eius}, his saints, and names them by their fear of the Lord; in
the psalm's preceding verse, which the Mass does not sing, ``the angel of the
Lord shall encamp round about them that fear him: and shall deliver them''
(Ps 33:8). That angel, set beside the Lesson's angel who holds back the
four angels from the earth, is this interpretation's comparison, not the
psalm's or Bede's.

The Alleluia calls \latin{omnes}, all who labour and are burdened, and
Chrysostom remarks that the invitation reaches ``not this or that person, but
all that are in anxiety, in sorrows, in sins'' (\work{Hom.\ in Matt.}\ 38.3);
the multitude that no one could number answers to that word. The Gospel the
feast reads with this Lesson blesses the poor in spirit, the mourning and the
hungry, and those reviled and persecuted for the Son's sake, and promises
that their reward is very great in heaven. Bede's first reading of the
multitude is that the vision ``announces the glory of those who are to
overcome the wickedness of the last persecution'' (on 7:9): the persecuted
whom the Gospel blesses are the crowd the Lesson shows in glory. On this
reading the Gospel describes the sealed on earth, those who bear the cross
openly under the world's hatred, with their reward in heaven.

The Offertory sings of the just in death. The souls of the just are in the
hand of God and ``seemed to die''; Bede's seal is set on those who ``in the midst of adverse things are
faithful confessors of His Name'' (on 7:14). The Secret asks that the gifts
be pleasing ``for the honor of all the just'': the sacrifice is offered for
the honour of the whole numbered company at once. The Communion sings again
the sixth, seventh and eighth beatitudes: the clean of heart shall see God,
the peacemakers shall be called the children of God, the persecuted for
justice possess the kingdom of heaven. Those promises are the ones the
Lesson's crowd is shown to enjoy, standing before the throne and in sight of
the Lamb; on this interpretation the assembly that hears them sung over the
Sacrament is the same Church as that multitude, still on the way. The
Postcommunion asks that the \latin{fideles populi} be defended by the saints'
unceasing prayer. Schuster reads those peoples as the Church on earth,
``journeying towards eternity with the eyes, and in the light of faith''
(p.~211); on this interpretation they are the sealed who have not yet come to
stand before the throne. The Sunday's Collect, said after the feast's, asks
God to loose the sins of his \latin{populi}; its Secret asks him to bring to
completion what he has given the undeserving; and its Postcommunion asks that
those who share in divine things be not left subject to human dangers. These
prayers belong to the Sunday and say nothing of the seal; the peoples who ask
for the saints' prayer also ask to be absolved.

\subsection{The strongest difficulty: a number that limits}
\label{sec:sealed-difficulty}

The strongest objection is the plain sense of the Lesson's own words. The
number is exact, the tribes are Israel's, and John says he \latin{heard} the
number of the sealed before he \latin{saw} the multitude; a reading that takes
the number for the whole Church may seem to drop \latin{centum quadraginta
quatuor millia} in order to reach the crowd. Bede's answer is to keep the
number and to read it, giving his reasons: Paul's seed of Abraham by faith,
the arithmetic of twelve and ten, and the order of the tribes that is not the
order of birth. The tribes and the twelve
thousands remain in his reading, as the powers of the Church from confession
to the right hand.

The disagreement on these answers is older than the reception of this Mass.
On the number, Bede reads the whole Church's completeness, and Augustine the
whole body of the saints; Victorinus reads a company of the last times,
those who believe at Elias's preaching. On the crowd, Bede, Augustine and
Victorinus read the elect gathered out of all believers, from Israel and the
nations; Cyprian reads the martyrs, a crowd that cannot be numbered. On the
angel from the east, Bede reads the Lord incarnate, and Victorinus reads
Elias, the precursor of Antichrist's times. This interpretation follows Bede
on all three, with Augustine on the number and the crowd: against Victorinus
on the number, against Cyprian on the crowd, and with Victorinus's Elias
standing beside Bede's Lord, for the Fathers do not speak with one voice on
this chapter.

A different reading of the same verses is held by Lucien Fromage, who
continued Dom Prosper Guéranger's \work{Liturgical Year}. He takes the Lesson
as a second census. At his first coming the God-Man, using Caesar Augustus,
had the world enrolled, and now, Fromage writes, \latin{l'heure a sonné d'un
autre recensement, qui doit consigner au livre de vie le résultat des
opérations du salut}: the hour has struck for another census, which is to
record in the book of life the outcome of the work of salvation. He cites St
Gregory the Great's Christmas homily on the census, and adds that because
many withdrew by their own fault from the first enrolment, which comprised
all men in the redemption, a second and definitive one was needed to cut the
guilty off from it (\work{L'Année liturgique}, vol.~15, p.~94; Gregory,
\work{Hom.\ in Ev.}\ 8). Bede reads the number as the whole Church made up of
its members' virtues; Fromage reads the sealing as the last reckoning, which
enrols the saved and strikes out the rest. On Fromage's reading the
multitude is still uncounted, but the census that precedes it is also a
judgment.

Schuster draws the opposite conclusion from the size of the multitude: ``Not
only the hundred and forty-four thousand predestined sons of Abraham, but a
turba magna of every age, sex, and condition'' enter heaven, and ``It is not
then so hard to be saved, as St John writes that he could not count the number
of the elect'' (p.~210). He adds at once one condition, the seal of the Tau
that Ezechiel saw set on the foreheads of those who sigh and mourn, and he
reads it with St Paul's ``as you are partakers of the sufferings, so shall you
be also of the consolation'' (2~Cor 1:7). Schuster's 144,000 are sons of
Abraham, and his multitude adds others to them; Bede's 144,000 are already
the whole Church.

\begin{fourSenses}
\item[Literal.] John sees an angel from the east forbid the angels of
destruction to hurt the earth until the servants of God are sealed on their
foreheads; he hears their number, twelve thousand from each of twelve tribes;
he then sees an uncounted multitude from every nation in white robes with
palms before the throne and the Lamb, and the angels' sevenfold praise.
\item[Allegorical.] The angel is Christ incarnate, the seal his cross, and
the hundred and forty-four thousand the whole Church, descended from the
patriarchs by nature or by faith (Bede, with Augustine on the number as the
whole body of the saints; Victorinus reads the angel as Elias and the number
as the believers of the last times). The multitude is the victors over the
last persecution or, in Bede's second reading, the Gentiles gathered after
Israel, washed in the blood of the Lamb --- the elect out of all believers
(Bede, with Victorinus; Cyprian reads the crowd as the martyrs).
\item[Moral.] The seal is the faith confessed openly on the forehead, and the
tribes are the virtues of the faithful, from confession to perseverance in
prayer (Bede). The white robe is the grace of baptism, to be kept
(Victorinus). The just rejoice in the Lord and not in themselves, and the
goods they are promised are justice, truth, charity, faith and endurance
(Augustine).
\item[Anagogical.] The elect stand at last at the right hand of Christ, which
Benjamin's place at the end of the list signifies, and give God unceasing
praise; the vision is also true now of those who are saved in hope (Bede).
\end{fourSenses}
