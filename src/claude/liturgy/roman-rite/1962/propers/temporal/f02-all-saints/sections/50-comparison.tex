\runninghead{Three Interpretations Compared}
\section{Where the Three Interpretations Agree and Differ}
\label{sec:comparison}

The three interpretations take up the three questions set out at the
opening.

\begin{comparisontable}{}{One Church, sealed and uncounted}{The way of the Beatitudes}{No want to them that fear him}
Question &
Who are the sealed, counted at 144,000 and then shown as a multitude no one
could number? &
Why does the feast read the Beatitudes, which bless the poor and the
persecuted, and how are their present and future promises to be read? &
How can the Gradual promise that nothing is lacking to those who fear the
Lord, and the Offertory say the just only seemed to die? \\
Where its question presses hardest &
the Lesson's counted number and uncounted multitude &
the Gospel's blessings, three of them sung again at the Communion &
the Gradual's promise and the Offertory's ``seemed to die'' \\
Answer &
The number signifies the whole Church, sealed with the cross; the multitude
is the victors of the last persecution or the Gentiles added to Israel &
The beatitudes are one way, from humility and fear to persecution for
justice, whose one reward is begun on earth and completed in heaven &
What the God-fearing are promised is God himself and the virtues; want, when
it comes, is sent for their health; the just dead are in God's hand \\
Principal authors &
Bede, with Augustine on the Lesson and the psalms &
Augustine, Chrysostom; Aquinas, Hilary &
Basil, Bellarmine; Augustine, Aquinas \\
What it asks of the faithful &
To confess the faith openly and rejoice in the Lord, not in themselves &
To begin in humility and the fear of God and be ready to suffer for justice &
Filial fear, and patience in want \\
Strongest difficulty &
The exact number and Israel's tribes, read as a limit &
The tenses: two promises present, six future &
The promise read as temporal comfort, which saints' lives contradict \\
Where its witnesses differ &
Bede and Augustine read the number as the whole Church, Victorinus as the
believers of the last times at Elias's preaching; Bede, Augustine and
Victorinus read the crowd as the elect out of all believers, Cyprian as the
innumerable martyrs; Bede reads the angel from the east as the Lord
incarnate, Victorinus as Elias; in the reception, Fromage reads the sealing
as a second, definitive census, and Schuster takes the 144,000 as sons of
Abraham with others added &
Augustine's ascent of knowledge and gifts against Chrysostom's chain of
dispositions; Hilary's land as the Lord's body against Chrysostom's sensible
prize &
Basil reads the rich as Israel, Bellarmine as the wicked rich and the
covetous; a temporal sense is kept by Bellarmine, as the humbler meaning,
and by Aquinas, as God's help in necessity \\
\end{comparisontable}

All three rest on the fear of the Lord and on humility, though they come to
it from different texts. In the first, the just rejoice ``not in yourselves''
but in the Lord, and the seal is set on those who confess. In the second, the
poor in spirit are the humble and God-fearing, and Augustine's way begins
with the gift of fear. In the third, the Gradual's \latin{Timete Dominum} is
the subject itself: for Basil the one who fears lacks nothing for any virtue,
and the perfect good is God himself; for Bellarmine the filial fear of the
perfect feels no want, because the friend of God possesses all. In each
interpretation holiness begins in what God gives: the seal, the humility of
the first beatitude, the fear the Gradual commands.

They attend to different times. The first looks at the saints already
before the throne and reads back from there to the sealing on earth. The
second follows the way the saints walked, from its first step to its last.
The third stands where the assembly stands, with the promise still being
tested by hunger and death. Bede's remark that the vision of the white-robed
multitude ``may also be understood of the present time, when `we are saved in
hope'\,'' and
Aquinas's distinction between the rewards begun on the way and completed in
the homeland, give the first two a bridge to the third's present.

The three meet in the same texts and read them differently. The first and
the third both read the Lesson and the Offertory. For the first, the
multitude with palms is the Church that has overcome, and the Offertory's
just are the sealed who have passed through death. For the third, the
Offertory answers the scandal of the just man's death, and the Lesson's crowd
before the throne is the Gradual's promise shown as kept. The second reads
the same crowd as the way's end, its robes baptism and its palms the cross's
triumph. The second and the third share the Gospel's fourth beatitude, the
hunger and thirst after justice, which Augustine cites inside his exposition
of the Gradual's psalm; for the second it is a step on the way, for the third
it names the want that is blessed.

Each question is worked out where its text presses hardest. The first
explains the Lesson's number and its list of tribes with Bede's reasons: the
seed of Abraham by faith, the arithmetic of twelve and ten, and the tribes in
their strange order read as the Church's powers. The second explains why the
Gospel is the Beatitudes and how its present and future promises stand. The
third meets the objection that the Gradual's promise is false, and says what
the Mass sings of Psalm~33 and what it leaves out. The witnesses still
disagree on the number, the crowd and the angel, on the census, on the land
of the meek and on the rich who lack.
