\runninghead{Three Interpretations Compared}
\section{Where the Three Interpretations Agree and Differ}
\label{sec:comparison}

The three interpretations answer the three questions set out at the opening.
Each begins from its own passage of the Mass.

\begin{comparisontable}{}{One city, numbered by God}{The way of the Beatitudes}{No want to them that fear him}
Question &
Who are the 144,000 sealed, and who is the multitude that no one could
number? &
When are the rewards of the beatitudes given: in this life, or only in the
next? &
What does the Gradual promise to those who fear the Lord, and whose souls
does the Offertory place in God's hand? \\
Starting passage &
The Lesson, Apoc 7:2--12 &
The Gospel, Mt 5:1--12a &
The Gradual, Ps 33:10--11, with the Offertory, Wis 3:1--3 \\
What the Fathers say of it &
Bede: the number signifies the whole Church. Augustine: the multitude is the
mass of the saints, whose number God knows and we do not &
Augustine: the beatitudes are steps, and their promises can be fulfilled in
this life. Chrysostom: a golden chain; comfort here and there. Aquinas: the
rewards are begun on the way. Rabanus: the clean of heart see God here by
grace. Hilary: the rewards belong to those changed by immortality &
Basil: the good the fearer cannot lack is God himself. Augustine: the goods
are spiritual. Aquinas and Bellarmine: God also helps in bodily need \\
The interpretation's answer &
The counted and the uncounted are one Church, whose number God knows and men
do not &
The beatitudes are the way the saints walked, its rewards begun in this life
and perfected in the next &
Nothing is lacking to the God-fearing because God himself is their good, and
the just in his hand have it \\
Where they disagree &
Augustine and Gregory on what the number completes; Cyprian and Schuster
against Augustine on whether the multitude shows that salvation is easily
reached &
Hilary against the others on when the rewards are given &
Basil against Aquinas and Bellarmine on bodily goods; Schuster, Alphonsus and
Bonaventure on whose souls are in God's hand \\
Reached by inference &
The Introit, as one praise of angels and men; the Gradual's saints, as the
sealed still on earth; the Alleluia's call, as the call that gathers the
multitude &
The Gradual, as the first step; the Lesson, through the eighth beatitude &
The Offertory, as holding the good the Gradual promises \\
\end{comparisontable}

Each interpretation gives some texts of the Mass a reason that the other two
do not. The first alone accounts for the Lesson's double vision, a number
sealed and a multitude beyond counting, and with Durandus and Fromage for the
Collect's many intercessors; it says little of how a man becomes one of the
saints, beyond Augustine's narrow gate. The second gives the Gospel and the
Communion their own weight and finds in the feast a way with a first step and
an end; it reaches the Lesson only through the eighth beatitude. The third
gives the Gradual and the Offertory an argument of their own and keeps a real
dispute over bodily need; it names the Lesson and the Communion without
drawing on them.

They look at the saints from different places. The first looks at them
already before the throne, with the angels, and reads back from there to the
sealing on earth. The second follows the road the saints walked, from the
poverty of spirit at its start to the persecution at its end. The third looks
at the condition of the just in this life, where fear is still commanded and
want and death still come, and asks what the promise means there.

Two of the interpretations meet in the Offertory. For the second, the words
\latin{illi autem sunt in pace} name a peace held now in hope and at last in
fact, as Bonaventure reports the \work{Glossa}. For the third, the hand of
God is the help and protection under which the just dwell, as Augustine
glosses the first clause.

St Augustine and St Thomas Aquinas stand behind all three, and they argue
differently in each. In the first Augustine says that the few who enter by
the narrow gate are many in the company of the angels, and both he and Aquinas
say that the number of the saved is known to God. In the second Augustine
says that the promises of the beatitudes can be fulfilled in this life, and
Aquinas that their rewards are begun here. In the third Augustine puts the
promised goods in the spiritual order and the just under God's help, while
Aquinas admits also the help God gives in bodily need. The three answers can
be held together, since each answers a different question; the disagreements
that remain lie within each interpretation, among the authorities it relies
on.
