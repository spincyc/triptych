### 1.1 Threads inside the appointed text

These are textual observations, each checked in the verified Latin. Which of
them a reading may use, and under what label, is settled in
`research/interpretations.md`.

- **Calling and hearing.** The Introit's God promises *clamaverint ad me,
  exaudiam eos*; the Alleluia bids *invocate nomen eius*; the Gospel is a
  story of calling (*vocare invitatos*, *vocate ad nuptias*, *multi … vocati*).
- **Tribulation.** *de quacumque tribulatione* (Introit) and *in medio
  tribulationis* (Offertory) are the formulary's only two occurrences of the
  word; the Collect's *universa nobis adversantia* is its oration form.
- **Direction.** *Dirigatur oratio mea* (Gradual) and *utinam dirigantur viae
  meae* (Communion) share the verb *dirigere*; the Postcommunion asks to *inhaerere
  mandatis* and the Communion names *mandata*.
- **Freedom from encumbrance.** The Collect asks that we be *mente et corpore
  pariter expediti* so as to carry out *quae tua sunt, liberis mentibus*; the
  Postcommunion asks the *medicinalis operatio* to *expediat* us from our
  *perversitates*. The two orations share *expedire*. In the Gospel the invited
  go off *alius in villam suam, alius vero ad negotiationem suam*.
- **Clothing and the new man.** The Epistle commands *induite novum hominem*;
  the Gospel's guest is *non vestitum veste nuptiali*.
- **Hands.** The Epistle's thief is to labour *operando manibus suis … ut habeat
  unde tribuat necessitatem patienti*; the Gradual's verse is *elevatio manuum
  mearum*; the Gospel's guest is bound *manibus et pedibus*; the Offertory's
  God stretches out *manum tuam* and saves by *dextera tua*.
- **In the sight of God.** The Gradual's *in conspectu tuo* and the Secret's
  *quae oculis tuae maiestatis offerimus*.
- **The nations.** *annuntiate inter gentes opera eius* (Alleluia) stands
  before a Gospel in which the servants go *ad exitus viarum*.
- **Anger.** The Epistle's *Irascimini, et nolite peccare: sol non occidat super
  iracundiam vestram*; the king of the Gospel *iratus est*; the Offertory's
  *super iram inimicorum meorum*.

### 1.2 Threads in the unsung context of the appointed verses

Read in the tracked Clementine chapter files
(`src/sources/bibles/clementine-vulgate/chapters/`) on 2026-09-30: Mt 21:23–46
with 22:1–22; Eph 4:17–32; Ps 77:1–8; Ps 104:1–6; Ps 118:1–8; Ps 137:1–8;
Ps 140:1–10; Lk 14:15–24.

- **Mt 21:23–46 is the setting the liturgical opening names.** In the temple the
  chief priests and elders challenge Jesus's authority (21:23); he answers with
  the two sons (21:28–32) and the vinedressers (21:33–41), cites the rejected
  stone (21:42) and says *auferetur a vobis regnum Dei, et dabitur genti facienti
  fructus eius* (21:43); *principes sacerdotum et pharisaei* perceive that he
  speaks of them and seek to seize him (21:45–46). The Gospel's *Et respondens
  Jesus, dixit iterum in parabolis eis* (22:1) continues that exchange; the
  Pharisees' plot follows at 22:15. Chrysostom reads Mt 22 as the sequel to
  21:43 (§ 3.6).
- **Eph 4:17–32.** The pericope begins mid-sentence: 4:22 *deponere vos …
  veterem hominem* governs 4:23–24; after it, 4:29 forbids evil speech, 4:30
  reads *nolite contristare Spiritum Sanctum Dei: in quo signati estis in diem
  redemptionis*, 4:31–32 removes *amaritudo, et ira* and ends *donantes invicem
  sicut et Deus in Christo donavit vobis*.
- **Ps 77:2 is quoted in the Gospel of Matthew.** The Introit verse is Ps 77:1;
  its next verse, *Aperiam in parabolis os meum; loquar propositiones ab initio*,
  is the verse Mt 13:35 cites as fulfilled in Jesus's speaking in parables.
  Augustine (Enarr. in Ps. 77 § 1) and Bellarmine (on Ps 77:2) both take up
  *parabolae* (§ 3.1). The Gospel's opening says Jesus spoke *in parabolis*.
- **Ps 104:1** is the psalm's first verse, with its title *Alleluja*; vv. 5–6
  name *semen Abraham … filii Jacob electi eius*.
- **Ps 118:1–8** (Aleph): v. 4 and v. 5 are one command and one wish; v. 8
  *Justificationes tuas custodiam*.
- **Ps 137:3** *In quacumque die invocavero te, exaudi me* stands four verses
  before the Offertory's v. 7; v. 1 is titled *Ipsi David*.
- **Ps 140:1** *Domine, clamavi ad te: exaudi me* precedes the Gradual's v. 2;
  v. 4 reads *ad excusandas excusationes in peccatis … et non communicabo cum
  electis eorum*. The 1962 *Ordo Missae* says Ps 140:2–4 at the incensation of
  the altar at the Offertory (§ 2.4).
- **Lk 14:15–24** is the other evangelist's supper; its invited *coeperunt simul
  omnes excusare* (14:18). Gregory discusses whether it is the same lesson
  (§ 3.6).

### 1.3 A thread that is not in the appointed text

The formulary nowhere names the Eucharist. The feast of the Gospel becomes the
Lord's table in Augustine (Sermo 90 § 1) and the mysteries in Chrysostom (Hom.
69 in Matth.), and the Postcommunion's *medicinalis operatio* is the healing
work of the sacrament received in Schuster's reading (§ 3.10). Any reading that
joins the Gospel's feast to this Mass's Communion does so through those
witnesses and says so; the formulary itself does not make the link.

### 2.1 The liturgical commentators' Mass for these chants had another Gospel

`tools/tpt commentary-work-index formulary --calendar roman-1962 --mass
pentecost-19`, run on 2026-09-30, compared 40 loci and listed six. Each listed
locus was then read in its registered tracked layer (§ 3.12). In every medieval
book read, the Mass that shares this formulary's Introit, Epistle, Gradual,
Offertory and Communion carries the healing of the paralytic (Mt 9:1–8) as its
Gospel, and the wedding feast belongs to the following Mass, whose Introit is
*Omnia quae fecisti*:

- Rupert of Deutz, *De divinis officiis* XII.19 (his Gospel: Mt 9) and XII.20,
  whose office *partim ex vocibus constat eorum qui ad nuptias intraverunt* and
  whose Epistle is Eph 5:15.
- Berno of Reichenau, *Libellus*: after the *Salus populi* Mass with the
  paralytic, *Deinde occurrit illa Dominica, ubi illud Evangelium legitur, in quo
  quidam ad nuptias filii regis invitati neglexerunt venire*, with *Super
  flumina*, *Omnia quae fecisti*, *Oculi omnium* and the collect *Largire*.
- Honorius (*Gemma animae* IV.86: Mt 9), Sicard (*Mitrale* VIII.19: Mt 9),
  Durandus (*Rationale* VI.136: *in quibusdam Ecclesiis* Mt 9).

**What this changes.** No document of this leaf may say that the wedding-feast
Gospel was anciently or everywhere read with these chants, and no commentator
may be cited for a connection between a shared element and Mt 22:1–14. The
commentators are cited only for what they say of elements this formulary
shares, and the difference itself belongs here and, at most, in one clause of
the expansive study's scope appendix.

### 2.2 Schuster's report of the Roman lists, and Gregory's homily heading

Bl. Ildefonso Schuster, *The Sacramentary* III (English, London 1927),
pp. 171–173, read in the registered tracked OCR layer at physical lines
10705–10840: the station for this Mass was at SS. Cosmas and Damian *on the
Sunday preceding the feast of the martyrs Cosmas and Damian*; *the Wurzburg
Capitulary* places this Sunday *after the second Sunday following the feast of
St Cyprian*; *The parable of the king who made a marriage feast for his son
(Matt, xxii, 1-14) is omitted in the Wurzburg Capitulary, which assigns instead
for to-day the passage which is given in the Missal to the Twenty-first Sunday
after Pentecost*; and *The allegory of the wedding feast was commentated on to
the people by St Gregory the Great in the Basilica of St Clement; but we do not,
however, know on what occasion*. The heading of Gregory's homily in the tracked
Latin transcription (§ 3.6) confirms the place: *Habita ad populum in basilica
beati Clementis martyris*, with the lesson given as *MATTH. XXII, 1-13*. The
Würzburg list itself was not read in this stage (§ 5.2). These facts may appear
only in the expansive study, and there only where they replace a claim about
the pairing's age.

### 3.1 Introit — antiphon *Salus populi ego sum* and verse Ps 77:1 (no. 1679)

**The antiphon's text.** Not a continuous Vulgate text; the Missal cites
nothing for it. A literal search of the tracked Clementine chapter files
(2026-09-30; case folded, j→i) for *salus populi*, *clamaverint ad me*, *de
quacumque tribulatione*, *quacumque tribulatione*, *ero eorum dominus* returned
nothing. The nearest verbal material found: Ps 34:3 *Salus tua ego sum*; Ps 90:15
*Clamabit ad me, et ego exaudiam eum: cum ipso sum in tribulatione*; Ps 137:3
*In quacumque die invocavero te, exaudi me* (the Offertory's psalm); *exaudiam
eos* at Is 41:17 and Zach 10:6 (and, negated, Jer 11:11, Ezek 8:18); *ero illorum
Deus* at 2 Cor 6:16. These are resemblances, not a source. Two ecclesiastical
witnesses characterise the antiphon: the continuation of *The Liturgical Year*
(English, vol. XI, 1883; layer, physical line ~25521): *The Anthem which follows
is made up of several passages of Holy Writ, without being exactly that of any
one of them*; and Schuster (p. 171): *The Introit emanates perhaps from the
Itala*, with the translator's note glossing the Itala as a Latin version *of the
New Testament*. Neither names a locus, and neither claim is adopted. The Latin
provenance ledger's note calling the antiphon a psalm centonisation is likewise
not adopted (`research/context.md`). **Result:** no textual source is
established; the antiphon's words may be described as echoing the psalter's
language of salvation and hearing only in those terms.

**Direct ancient exegesis of the antiphon:** none exists, since it is not a
biblical verse. **Documented reception of the antiphon** comes only from the
liturgical commentators (§ 3.12): Honorius IV.86 puts the words in the mouth of
Zorobabel and the priests announcing the Lord's word to the captive people whom
Cyrus let go; Sicard VIII.19 the same, with *noster Cyrus*; Rupert XII.19 and
Durandus VI.136 read it through their Mass's Gospel of the paralytic, which
this formulary does not share, so those two sentences are not usable. Schuster
(pp. 171–172) reads it as the Lord's own declaration that *no one has ever
called upon the Lord for help and been disappointed*, and ties it to the
Roman station of the physician-martyrs.

**Ps 77:1 (verse).** Direct exegesis read:

- **Augustine, Enarr. in Ps. 77 §§ 1–2** (NPNF1-8, "Psalm LXXVIII", tracked
  CCEL text, physical lines 37535–37600; layer, English). The psalm narrates *the
  things which are said to have been done among the old people: but the new and
  latter people is being admonished, to beware that it be not ungrateful regarding
  the blessings of God*. He dwells on *I will open … in parables my mouth* (v. 2):
  parables *by their very names … signify that they ought to be sought out with
  deeper view*. On v. 1, *Whom may we suppose to be here speaking, but God?* Then
  the sacramental point (§ 2): *although all the Sacraments were common, grace,
  which is the virtue of the Sacraments, was not common to all … For even
  heretics have the same Baptism, and false brethren too, in the communion of the
  Catholic name.* Used: reading 2 (the history recited to a new people) and, for
  the sacrament-grace distinction, reading 1.
- **The *Expositiones in Psalmos* printed under Athanasius's name, on Ps 77**
  (PG 27; the registered IA OCR layer, fetched in iteration 1 and matched
  `969a381f…`, physical lines 27008–27027; Greek, layer only, no page image; no
  column is cited because the layer's numerals are unreliable). The
  *hypothesis*: Asaph sings the psalm *τῷ νέῳ λαῷ παραινῶν τῷ νόμῳ προσέχειν τῷ
  εὐαγγελικῷ*, accusing the old people of the Jews of repaying their benefactor
  with ingratitude. On v. 1 he asks to which people the command *Προσέχετε, λαός
  μου* is given, *ἢ τῷ ἐξ ἐθνῶν*, and to which law, *ἢ τῷ εὐαγγελικῷ*, and proves
  from Zach 2:11 that the nations are called God's people. On v. 2 the evangelist
  shows the verse fulfilled *ὅτε τὰς πολλὰς παραβολὰς εἶπεν ὁ Κύριος*. (Greek
  normalised from the layer, which reads, for example, *«λαός* and *παραδοιζαῖς*.)
  The one Greek exposition of the Introit verse read here, and the only witness
  in this record who reads *popule meus* of the people gathered from the
  nations. Used: reading 2.
- **Bellarmine, Explanatio in Ps. 77:2** (tracked O'Sullivan English, physical
  line 2771): *Mt. chap. 13, in quoting this passage, says that "the beginning"
  refers to the beginning of the world*; the enigma *particularly alluded to here
  seems to be the kingdom of Christ*. Later saintly reception for Ps 77.
- **Jerome** cites Ps 77:49, not v. 1, on Mt 22:7 (§ 3.6): the king's armies may
  be *ultores angelos, de quibus in Psalmis scribitur: Immissiones per angelos
  pessimos*.
- The registered PG 55 layer (§ 3.4) prints, after Chrysostom's expositions,
  further Greek material under his name headed Ps 77 (physical line ~102455), as
  it does for Ps 104 (~92301) and Ps 118 (~96269). Its standing in Migne's
  volume was not established and it was not read (§ 5.2). Cassiodorus,
  Theodoret and Hugh of Saint-Cher are mapped by the index and registered; not
  opened for Ps 77 (§ 5.2).

### 3.2 Collect — *Omnipotens et misericors Deus* (no. 1680)

A composed oration. Direct exegesis does not exist. Reception read:

- **Schuster, pp. 171–172**: *In the Collect we implore God to take away from us
  everything that may destroy the perfect harmony and balance which exists
  between our body and our soul*; the body *must act obediently under the rule of
  the soul, which on its part is raised up on high by grace*, so that man *pursues
  with a ready mind the things which are of God*; *this liberty of the sons of God
  … is the result of order, harmony, and the due subjection of the creature to the
  Creator*.
- **Honorius IV.86**: *In Oratione universa eis adversantia excludi rogant*
  (the returning captives). **Berno** describes the collect but joins it to his
  paralytic Gospel (*sicut paralyticus … libero gressu incedebat*); not usable
  beyond the fact that he names the oration.
- **Verbal relations inside the formulary** (textual observation): *expediti*
  (Collect) and *expediat* (Postcommunion); *quae tua sunt … exsequamur*
  (Collect) and *tuis … inhaerere mandatis* (Postcommunion).
- **English route:** Cummiskey 1861 (`propers/verified.md`). No sacramentary
  history was pursued; none changes a claim (§ 2).

### 3.3 Epistle — Eph 4:23–28 (no. 1681)

**Complete context read** (§ 1.2). **Direct ancient exegesis read:**

### 3.4 Gradual — Ps 140:2 (no. 1682)

**Complete context read** (§ 1.2). **Direct ancient exegesis read:**

- **Hilary of Poitiers, Tract. in Ps. 140 §§ 1–5** (PL 9, cols. 825–826, read in
  iteration 1 on a 200-dpi page image of the registered whole-volume scan, PDF
  p. 429, digest re-matched `eab6d2f4…`, after locating it in the tracked PL 9
  layer, lines 65734–65960; the CSEL 22 layer, lines 40250–40385, pp. 789–791 by
  its running heads, agrees in substance in its own orthography). The Maurist
  lemma in PL 9 reads *Domine, dirigatur oratio mea* and, in § 3, *Allevatio
  manuum mearum*; Zingerle's lemma in CSEL 22 (layer, line ~40291) reads
  *dirigatur oratio mea sicut incensum in conspectu tuo, domine*, the Gradual's
  order, and reports the Maurist order from one manuscript (§ 5.1). § 2 (on
  v. 2a): *Quorum pura et recta et sincera oratio est, hi dicere poterunt,
  Dirigatur oratio mea: caeterum si externarum cogitationum interventu
  depravabitur, non dirigetur*; incense rises *in altum … de humilibus*, and its
  four spices may, he suggests, answer the four kinds of prayer of 1 Tim 2:1.
  § 3, headed in the edition *Manuum elevatio et extensio quid differant*, on
  v. 2b: the lifting of hands shows *excelsi operis
  officium* (Moses' hands in the battle); *Apostolus quoque orantes jubet elevare
  manus puras (I Tim. ii, 8). Et cum ait: Sine ira et contentione; non habitum
  orandi constituit, sed praescriptum divinae operationis adjecit*; Isaiah
  rejects *si extendatis manus*, not *si elevetis*, *quia habitus orandi in
  manibus extendendis est, perfecti autem operis efficientia est in elevandis*.
  § 4, *Vespertinum sacrificium quid*: *Manuum ergo elevatio, sacrificium
  vespertinum est. Non enim sanguine et holocaustis nos, in quos consummatio
  saeculorum devenit, sacrificamus Deo*; which evening sacrifice pleases God is
  learned from Mt 25:34–40 (*Venite, benedicti … esurivi enim, et dedistis mihi
  manducare …*); *Hoc sacrificium vespertinum, id est, temporum novissimorum est.
  In hoc manus elevandae sunt: quia istius modi orationibus, jam ab initio mundi,
  benedictis Dei regni coelestis praeparata possessio est.* Hilary's
  reasoning: the evening is the last age of the world, in which blood offerings
  have ceased; the hands that are lifted, as distinct from hands merely spread
  in the posture of prayer, are hands that do the work; and the work Christ names
  as the acceptable sacrifice is feeding, clothing, welcoming and visiting him in
  the least. The earliest Latin exposition of the Gradual's verse read here, and
  a direct witness that reads its lifted hands as works of mercy (§ 4.6). Used:
  readings 1 and 3.
- **Augustine, Enarr. in Ps. 140 §§ 2–4** (NPNF1-8 "Psalm CXLI", tracked CCEL
  text, lines 65231–65270; layer, English). V. 1: *This not I alone say: whole
  Christ saith it*; *If tribulation be finished, crying is finished: but if
  tribulation remain for the Church, for the Body of Christ, even to the end of
  the world, let it not only say, "I have cried unto Thee," but also, "Listen unto
  the voice of my prayer."* V. 2 (§ 3): the verse *is wont to be understood of the
  Head Himself*: at evening the Lord on the Cross *laid down His life*; *That then
  is the "evening sacrifice," the Passion of the Lord, the Cross of the Lord, the
  offering of a salutary Victim, the whole burnt offering acceptable to God. That
  "evening sacrifice" produced, in His Resurrection, a morning offering. Prayer
  then, purely directed from a faithful heart, riseth like incense from a hallowed
  altar.* V. 3 (§ 4): the *door of restraint* is *opened, to confession of sin;
  closed, to excusing sin*.
- **John Chrysostom, Expositio in Ps. 140** (PG 55; the registered IA OCR layer,
  fetched 2026-09-30 and matched `d9b83186…`, physical lines 57140–57700; Greek,
  layer only, no page image; the layer's damaged column heads indicate cols.
  427–430). Two moves: (i) the Fathers did not ordain this psalm to be said every
  evening for the single phrase about the evening sacrifice, *ἀλλ᾽ ὥς τι φάρμακον
  σωτήριον καὶ ἁμαρτημάτων καθάρσιον* — as a saving medicine and a cleansing of the
  sins contracted through the day (the layer reads *φάρμαχον* and *χαθάρσιον*;
  the κ/χ confusion is the layer's); (ii) on v. 1 he forbids prayer against
  enemies, citing the apostles and Stephen, and requires that anger itself be

  διαλογισμοῦ, normalised here; the layer reads ἐπαίρογτας and διαογισμοῦ) and
  telling the hearer who
  has an enemy to quench his anger and so approach the Master (the editor's
  paraphrase of the Greek at lines ~57655–57670, not a translation for
  publication). Only then does he expound v. 2 and the two altars
  of the temple.
- **Cassiodorus**: the registered PL 70 OCR layer (fetched and matched
  `d777c4c6…`) is too damaged for a literal search to locate his exposition of

  The one hit was his exposition of Ps 65:15 citing Ps 140:2 as *Intret oratio
  mea sicut incensum in conspectu tuo* and reading *incensum* as prayer (layer,

**Later reception read:** Bellarmine on Ps 140:2 (tracked O'Sullivan English,
physical line 4947; iteration 1): he first asks grace to pray well (*unless the
Spirit help our infirmity*); incense's four gums are *Faith, Hope, Love,

priest, for it is through him, as being our advocate, that we must always pray;

evening by the Church, *or, perhaps, because the evening sacrifice was of more

evening*. Schuster, p. 172: *In this present life our oblation to
God is always an evening sacrifice, for it is enveloped in the twilight of
faith*; *before attaining to the morning joy of the beatific vision, it is fitting

of the preceding Parasceve*. Honorius IV.86: the people *jam pergens* invokes the
Lord with *Dirigatur oratio mea*. Sicard VIII.19 gives the Gradual to *populus
autem liberatus* (usable) *et paralyticus sanatus* (not usable). Rupert XII.19
and Durandus VI.136 give it to the healed paralytic; not usable.

**The Order of Mass** (§ 2.4): the same verse with vv. 3–4 at the incensation of a
solemn Mass.

### 3.6 Gospel — Mt 22:1–14 (no. 1684)

**Complete context read:** Mt 21:23–22:22 and Lk 14:15–24 (§ 1.2).

**Direct ancient exegesis read, in the order of the witnesses' dates:**

- **Irenaeus, Adv. haer. IV.36.5–6** (ANF 1, pp. 516–517, the registered tracked
  IA OCR layer, physical lines 66390–66530; English, layer). The parable shows
  *that there is one King and Lord, the Father of all*, who *had from the
  beginning prepared the marriage for His Son* and called *the men of the former
  dispensation* by his servants; *He called together from all the highways, that
  is, from all nations*; *The Lord, therefore, who has called us everywhere by the
  apostles, is He who called those of old by the prophets*. § 6: *we ought, after
  our calling, to be also adorned with works of righteousness, so that the Spirit
  of God may rest upon us; for this is the wedding garment*; those *called to
  God's supper, yet have not received the Holy Spirit, because of their wicked
  conduct* are cast out; *as in the former covenant, "with many of them was He not
  well pleased;" so also is it the case here, that "many are called, but few
  chosen."* Anti-Marcionite in purpose: one God calls and judges.
- **Hilary of Poitiers, In Matth. XXII.3–7** (PL 9, cols. 1042 C–1044 B; read on
  page images of the registered whole-volume scan, PDF pp. 535–536, fetched
  2026-09-30 and matched `eab6d2f4…`, after locating the passage in the tracked
  OCR layer, lines 82398–82650; § 4 to § 7 read again in iteration 1 on a
  200-dpi image of PDF p. 536, digest re-matched). § 3: *hic nuptiae, vitae coelestis et in
  resurrectione suscipiendae aeternae gloriae sacramentum est*. § 4: *invitati
  antea populus Israel est: in gloriam enim aeternitatis per legem est advocatus.
  Servi missi, qui invitatos vocarent, apostoli sunt … Qui vero iterum cum
  praeceptorum conditione mittuntur, apostolici sunt viri et successores
  apostolorum. Tauri autem saginati, gloriosa martyrum species est*; the
  fatlings are *homines spiritales*; and he closes his reading of v. 4 (col. 1043
  B): *His enim omnibus jam paratis, et in numerum complacitae Deo multitudinis
  collectis, regni coelestis gloria tamquam nuptiae nuntiantur.* § 5: the field
  is *ambitio saeculi*, the merchandise *pecuniae cupiditas*; the heavenly armies
  *flammis aeterni ignis succendent*. § 6 (col. 1043 C–D), headed by the edition
  *Gentium vocatio*: *Quoniam vero de judicii tempore et resurrectionis ista
  loquitur, sermonem eumdem ad congregationem gentium retulit. Indignis enim
  repertis his, qui primi invitati fuerant, jubet iri ad exitus viarum. Dono enim
  gratiae, vitae anterioris crimina omittuntur. Nam saepissime viam tempus saeculi
  intelligendam monuimus. Atque ideo ad exitus viarum jubentur ire; quia omnibus
  retroacta donantur.* Of v. 10: *Vocari deinde omnes sine aliqua exceptione ad
  nuptias jubet, et mali simul cum bonis veniunt*; *Vocatio quidem bonos efficere
  debuerat, quia sancta est … sed per vitium inemendatae voluntatis discrimen est
  vocatorum.* § 7, of v. 11 (col. 1044 A): because the counterfeit can deceive
  men but not God, *ingressus Deus felicis resurrectionis istius coetum, et
  hominem accumbentem sine nuptiali veste conspiciens, interrogat*; (col. 1044 B)
  *Vestitus autem nuptialis est gloria Spiritus sancti, et candor habitus
  coelestis: qui bonae interrogationis confessione susceptus, usque in coetum
  regni coelorum immaculatus et integer reservetur*; *Non est igitur paucitas in
  invitatis, sed raritas in electis*. The highways are the gathering of the
  nations and, in the same sentence, the end of the world's way at which past
  sins are forgiven.
- **Jerome, In Matth. III** (PL 26, cols. 159–161, page images PDF pp. 87–88 of
  the registered scan; the passage lies in the registered segment
  `…vallarsi-migne-pl26-1845.pl26-columns-15-218`). Col. 159: *Rex iste qui fecit
  nuptias filio suo, Deus omnipotens est. Facit autem nuptias Domino nostro Jesu
  Christo et Ecclesiae, quae tam ex Judaeis, quam ex gentibus, congregata est.*
  The first servant is Moses, or, reading *servos*, the prophets; the second
  servants prophets or apostles according to the same reading. The dinner and
  beasts are *vel per metaphoram opes regiae … vel certe dogmatum magnitudo, et
  doctrina Dei lege plenissima*. Those occupied elsewhere are *minoris criminis*
  than those who *verterunt humanitatem in crudelitatem*. Col. 160 A (re-read in
  iteration 1 on a fresh 200-dpi image): of v. 7, the king is called *homo* while
  he invites and *agebat opera clementiae*, *nunc quando ad ultionem venit, homo
  siletur, et rex tantum dicitur*; the armies are *Exercitus, seu ultores angelos,
  de quibus in Psalmis scribitur: Immissiones per angelos pessimos [Al. malos]
  (Psal. LXXVII, 49); seu Romanos … intelligamus, sub duce Vespasiano et Tito, qui
  occisis Judaeae populis, praevaricatricem succenderint civitatem*. Jerome thus
  joins the Introit's psalm, at v. 49, to Mt 22:7 himself. Col. 160 B: *Gentilium

  outside some good were found, answering from Rom 2:14 that *gentes naturaliter

  sunt Domini, et opera quae complentur ex lege et Evangelio, novique hominis
  efficiunt vestimentum.* Col. 161: whoever is found *sub nomine Christiano* *non
  habere vestem nuptialem, hoc est, vestem supercoelestis [Al. coelestis] hominis;

  five manuscripts read *impudentiae*); v. 14: *Omnes parabolas brevi sententia
  comprehendit: quod et in opere vineae, et in aedificatione domus, et in convivio
  nuptiali, non initia, sed finis quaeratur.* Vallarsi's notes b, d, e, g quote
  Origen's commentary in Latin; they are an editor's notes and are not used.
- **John Chrysostom, Hom. 69 in Matth.** (NPNF1-10, tracked CCEL text, physical

  practice*; *although to be called and to be cleansed was of grace, yet, when
  called and clothed in clean garments, to continue keeping them so, this is of
  the diligence of them that are called*; *Hear ye, as many as having partaken of
  the mysteries, and having been present at the marriage, clothe your souls with

  the ways to whose ends the servants are sent are the teachings of the
  Gentiles, from all of which people came to the wedding, that is, believed in
  Christ. A short answer to a question, not a homily: it answers what the
  highways are and says nothing, in this question, of who the first invited
  were or why they refused. The direct witness to reading 2's central
  identification that iteration 1 missed (§ 4.3).
- **Augustine on Luke's supper, recorded beside it** (read in iteration 2). The
  other evangelist's parable, which § 1.2 reads as context and Gregory compares
  with this one (§ 1 of his homily); it is not the appointed Gospel, and nothing
  Augustine says of it is transferred to Mt 22. *Quaestiones evangeliorum*
  II.30, *[Ib. xiv, 21, 23.]*, in the same transcription at physical line 83
  (col. 1343 by its numerals) and on the page image of col. 1343 (artifact PDF
  p. 21 of the same scan, retained in the same excerpt): *Quod de civitate
  adducit invitatos ad cœnam, de ipsa gente Judæorum qui crediderunt
  significat, debiles peccatis, non habentes superbiam quasi justitiæ, quæ
  mendax primates eorum a gratia prohibebat. Quod autem de sepibus et viis
  alios adduci jussit, cum adhuc locus esset, Gentes significat, propter
  diversas vias sectarum et spinas peccatorum.* Here the ways are again the
  Gentiles', through their *sectae*. **Sermo 112** (NPNF1-6 "Sermon LXII",
  whose heading prints *[XCII. Ben.]* between Sermons LXI *[CXI. Ben.]* and
  LXIII *[CXIII. Ben.]*; tracked CCEL text, physical lines 43276–43520; layer,
  English) reads the same Lucan parable otherwise: *Who are they who had been
  invited, but those who had been called by the Prophets who were sent
  before?*; *They were sent then to the people of Israel*; *They read the
  Prophets and killed Christ* (§ 1); and of v. 23, *The Gentiles came from the
  streets and lanes: let the heretics come from the hedges, here they shall
  find peace* (§ 8). On Luke, then, Augustine gives two different
  identifications of the outer ways in two works.
- **Augustine, Sermo 90** (NPNF1-6 "Sermon XL. [XC. Ben.]", tracked CCEL text,
  physical lines 37787–38198; English, layer): heading *On the words of the
  Gospel, Matt. xxii. 2, etc., about the marriage of the king's son; against the
  Donatists, on charity. Delivered at Carthage in the Restituta.* § 1: *All the
  faithful know the marriage of the king's son, and his feast, and the spreading
  of the Lord's Table is open to them all who will*; *there are two feasts of the
  Lord; one to which the good and evil come, the other to which the evil come
  not*. § 4: *The garment that was looked for is in the heart, not on the body*;
  the one man cast out stands for many; *Be that feast which now is, received
  worthily, that we may attain to the other.* § 5: not baptism (*this garment I
  see in the good, I see in the bad*), not *the Altar, or That which is received
  at the Altar*, not fasting, not miracles. § 6: *This is the wedding garment:
  "Now the end of the commandment," says the Apostle, "is charity out of a pure
  heart, and of a good conscience, and of faith unfeigned."*; *Let charity be born
  in thee … be it nourished, fostered, increased*; *"the wedding garment" is taken
  in honour of the union … of the Bridegroom to the Bride*. § 8: *faith which
  worketh by love*. § 9: the Bridegroom *hanging upon the Cross for thee, and
  praying to His Father for His enemies*, and Stephen; *as thou art about to
  approach the Table of the Lord, the Feast of the Holy Scriptures, do not say, "O
  that mine enemy might die!"* § 10: *so be "the wedding garment" put on; so be
  the image of God, after which we were created, by this our advancing, engraven
  anew in us.*
- **Augustine, Sermo 95** (NPNF1-6 "Sermon XLV", from line 39237; §§ 4–7 at
  lines ~39289–39400): the Bridegroom *became deformed because of His deformed
  spouse, that he might make her fair*; *the Bridegroom Himself, who calleth
  together to the feast, and quickeneth whom He calleth*; *Baptism is a gift of
  God, the good and bad have it. The Sacraments of the Altar the good and bad
  receive together*; the garment is charity (1 Cor 13). The sermon is headed
  on Mk 8:5, the seven loaves, and turns to the wedding feast at § 4. Neither
  sermon takes up who the refusing guests are or what the highways are: both
  were re-read for this in iteration 1, and in iteration 2 both sermons were
  searched whole (lines 37787–38310 and 39237–39464) for *Gentile*, *nation*,
  *Jew*, *Israel*, *highway*, *ways*, *streets*, *invited* and *servants*. No
  hit identifies the refusing guests or the highways. Replayed on 30 September
  2026 in the post-acceptance correction (`tools/source-library search`, a
  case-folded literal match within each physical line, run over the artifact
  and kept to those two ranges), the search returns 23 lines, of five kinds:
  the servants who invite good and bad and do not inspect the guests (Sermo 90
  §§ 3–4, lines 37847, 37861, 37870, 37871, 37880, 37881, 37893, 37898); the
  hearers and the guests as invited (Sermo 90 § 7, 38048, *be invited as one
  who is to remain*; § 9, 38122 and 38125, *thou hast now come invited
  hither*; Sermo 95 § 4, 39291 and 39293, the Master of the house and the
  Bridegroom who had invited them; § 5, 39338, *the Master of the house Who
  had invited him*, and 39347–39348, *ye have been invited through me … He
  invited you by my ministry*); the Israelites and Pharaoh's magicians as an
  instance of miracles (Sermo 90 § 5, 37954, 37955, 37957); the Jews, in the
  Stephen passage (§ 9, 38097) and the tribute-money passage (§ 10, 38184);
  and two matches inside other words, *ways* in *always* (Sermo 95 § 2, 39261)
  and *nation* in *explanation* (§ 6, 39360). *Gentile*, *highway* and
  *streets* return nothing. The iteration-2 wording named only the first kind,
  the Stephen passage and the Israelites, as though nothing else had been
  found; research review RES-011 replayed it and showed the omission.
  Augustine's answer to the question of the highways is in the *Quaestiones*
  (above), not in these sermons (§ 4.3).
- **Gregory the Great, Hom. in Evang. 38** (Latin; the registered tracked
  Wikisource transcription of the Migne text, CC BY-SA 3.0, physical lines
  527–547; layer, not collated with the PL 76 page images, whose registered
  routes are restricted or remote). Heading *Habita ad populum in basilica beati
  Clementis martyris*. § 1: Matthew's *nuptiae* are the *praesens Ecclesia*,
  Luke's supper *aeternum et ultimum convivium*. § 3: the King is the Father, who
  made the wedding *quando hunc in utero Virginis humanae naturae conjunxit*, or,
  *apertius … atque securius*, *quo ei per incarnationis mysterium sanctam
  Ecclesiam sociavit*; he *Misit … servos suos ut ad istas nuptias amicos
  invitarent … et prius prophetas, et postmodum apostolos fecit*. § 4: bulls and
  fatlings are *novi ac veteris testamenti patres*, prophets and apostles who
  suffered persecution, and he glosses the king's message *Ac si apertius
  dicatur*: *Patrum praecedentium mortes aspicite, et remedia vitae vestrae
  cogitate*. § 5: *In villam quippe ire est labori terreno immoderate incumbere,
  in negotiationem vero ire est actionum saecularium lucris inhiare*; the worse
  refuse *vocantis gratiam* and persecute, and he asks *Ubi sunt enim superbi
  illi martyrum persecutores?*; the city burned is the flesh of the persecutors in
  *aeterna gehennae flamma*; the armies are angels; *Rebus ergo cognoscimus quod in
  parabolis audimus*. § 6: *Si in Scriptura sacra vias actiones accipimus, exitus
  viarum intelligimus defectus actionum, quia illi plerumque facile ad Deum
  veniunt, quos in terrenis actibus prospera nulla comitantur.* Hom. 38 names
  neither Israel nor the nations anywhere; a search of the whole homily in the
  tracked transcription (iteration 1) found *Judae-*, *gentil-*, *gentes*,
  *gentium* and *Israel* nowhere in it (*gentes* occurs only inside *degentes*,
  § 15, and *natio* only in a citation of Phil 2:15, § 7). § 7: the
  wedding of the king is *praesens Ecclesia … in qua cum bonis et mali conveniunt*;
  tolerate the bad. § 9: *Si enim vestem nuptialem baptisma vel fidem dicimus, quis
  sine baptismate et fide has nuptias intravit?*; *Quid ergo debemus intelligere
  nuptialem vestem, nisi charitatem?*; *Recte enim charitas nuptialis vestis
  vocatur, quia hanc in se conditor noster habuit, dum ad sociandae sibi Ecclesiae
  nuptias venit*; *Nos ad Dei nuptias venimus, et cordis vestem mutare
  dissimulamus.* § 10: the garment woven on two beams, love of God and neighbour.
  § 11: *Charitas autem vera est cum et in Deo diligitur amicus, et propter Deum
  diligitur inimicus*; *Nos sumus … qui in nuptiis Verbi discumbimus … qui
  Scripturae sacrae epulis pascimur*. § 12: *Amice, et non amice; amice per fidem,
  sed non amice per operationem*; *omne argumentum cessat excusationis*; hope for
  the imperfect (Ps 138:16). § 13: *Pedes enim qui visitare aegrum negligunt, manus
  quae nihil indigentibus tribuunt a bono opere jam ex voluntate ligatae sunt.*
  § 14: *Quia vocati sumus, novimus; si sumus electi, nescimus*; we *divini Verbi
  epulas sumimus*. §§ 15–16: exempla (Tharsilla, Gordiana, Aemiliana; a monk).

**Medieval and Doctoral reception read:**

- **Rabanus Maurus, Comm. in Matth.** (PL 107, the registered tracked IA OCR

  *(Greg.)*, *(Aug.)*, *(Chrys.)*: it transmits Jerome's *Vestis autem nuptialis

  after reading the unworthy invited, unmarked, as *Judaei, qui per legem et

  Judaeorum, de vocatione gentium*, the catena gives Augustine's *Quaestiones*

- **Thomas Aquinas, Super Matth. c. 22** (Venice 1745, t. III, pp. 279–282; the

### 3.7 Offertory — Ps 137:7 (no. 1685)

**Direct ancient exegesis read:**

- **Augustine, Enarr. in Ps. 137 §§ 10–11** (NPNF1-8 "Psalm CXXXVIII", tracked
  CCEL text, lines 64280–64300; layer, English): *whatsoever tribulation thou art
  in, confess, call on Him; He freeth thee, He reviveth thee*; *Love the other
  life, and thou shalt see that this life is tribulation*; *otherwise Thou wilt not
  revive me, unless I walk in the midst of tribulation*. His lemma has the past
  tense: *Thou hast stretched forth Thine hand over the wrath of mine enemies*;
  *mine enemies cannot separate me from Thee*.
- **Hilary, Tract. in Ps. 137 § 15** (CSEL 22, p. 748 by the layer's running
  head; the registered IA OCR layer, fetched 2026-09-30 and matched
  `6b39d974…`, lines 37995–38030; layer, damaged). The just man does not refuse to
  walk amid tribulations, knowing with the Apostle that tribulation works patience
  (Rom 5:3), that gold is proved and the soldier crowned by war, and that he is
  unequal to tribulations without God's help; therefore he is sure of his
  salvation through God. His lemma also reads the past: *extendisti manus tuas: et
  saluum me fecit dextera tua*. Paraphrase; the layer's Latin is too damaged to
  quote without reconstruction.
- **John Chrysostom, Expositio in Ps. 137 on vv. 7–8** (PG 55, col. 410 by the
  layer's column head; the registered IA OCR layer, digest re-matched
  `d9b83186…` in iteration 1, physical lines 54681–54712; Greek, layer only, no
  page image). On v. 7a, *Ἐὰν πορευθῶ ἐν μέσῳ θλίψεως, ζήσεις με*: *Οὐκ εἶπεν,
  ὅτι Τὴν θλίψιν ἀπελάσεις, ἀλλ᾽, Ἐν αὐτοῖς μένοντα τοῖς δεινοῖς ζήσεις με* —
  he did not say that God would drive the tribulation away, but that he would
  keep him alive while he remained in the dangers, able to bring through safe
  one who had fallen into them, which is the wonder: safety in the midst of what
  surrounds him. On v. 7b his lemma is past (*ἐξέτεινας … ἔσωσε*), and he
  reports that another version reads *Κατὰ τῆς ἀναπνοῆς τῶν ἐχθρῶν μου
  ἐκτενεῖς* and another *Καὶ σώσει με ἡ δεξιά σου*, both future; God's power
  shows on both sides, saving him and humbling the raging enemy. On v. 8 the
  repaying *can also be said of what Christ has done for us: for he repaid on
  our behalf* (*Αὐτὸς γὰρ ἀνταπέδωκεν ὑπὲρ ἡμῶν*). (Greek normalised from the
  layer, which reads, for example, *χαταστῆσαι* and *ἀνταπέδωχεν*;
  paraphrase, not a translation for publication.) Used: readings 1 and 3.

**Later reception read:** Bellarmine on Ps 137:7–8 (tracked O'Sullivan English,
physical lines 4863–4865; iteration 1): *If I shall walk in the midst of
tribulation, so as to be surrounded on all sides by it, still "thou wilt quicken
me;" you will preserve me alive, unhurt, unharmed*; *"and thy right hand hath
saved me;" your strength and power, Christ, hath saved me*; on v. 8, *"the
Lord," who is your right hand, "will repay for me;" will satisfy you, the
Father, for my sins*. His English lemma keeps the Vulgate's past tenses.
Schuster, p. 173: the Offertory, *like the Introit is
the same as that of the Lenten station at the Basilica of the Anargyri*; *it
happens that the hour in which God hears our prayer is the darkest and saddest of
our trial*. Honorius IV.86: the returning people give thanks *pro reversione*.
Durandus VI.136 and Rupert XII.19 read it with their paralytic; not usable.

**Textual note:** Augustine's and Hilary's lemmata read the past *extendisti …
fecit* with the Clementine, and Chrysostom's Greek lemma reads the past; the
Missal's futures *extendes … faciet* stand already in the 1862
(`propers/verified.md`). Chrysostom reports futures in two other Greek versions
(*ἐκτενεῖς*; *σώσει*), which shows that a future reading of v. 7b circulated in
Greek; it does not identify the source of the Latin chant's futures (§ 5.1).

### 3.8 Secret — *Haec munera, quaesumus, Domine* (no. 1686)

Composed oration. **Reception read:** Schuster, p. 173: *we pray that the holy
oblation may not only bring glory to God, but may also be a pledge of eternal
salvation to all those that share therein*; *we here ask for such dispositions as
may enable the mystical sacramental oblation to exert without hindrance its full
power in our souls*. **Verbal relations** (textual observation): *oculis tuae
maiestatis* (Secret), *in conspectu tuo* (Gradual), and in the Order of Mass *in
conspectu divinae maiestatis tuae* (no. 1032) and Ps 140:2 at the incensation
(no. 1037). The closest is the *Placeat* said at the end of every Mass, read on
the page image in iteration 1 (*Ordo Missae* no. 1137, printed p. 325, PDF
p. 406, at 200 dpi): *ut sacrificium, quod oculis tuae maiestatis indignus
obtuli, tibi sit acceptabile, mihique et omnibus, pro quibus illud obtuli, sit,
te miserante, propitiabile*. The Secret asks that the gifts *quae oculis tuae
maiestatis offerimus* be *salutaria nobis*; the *Placeat* asks that the
sacrifice offered *oculis tuae maiestatis* be *propitiabile* to the priest and
to all for whom he offered it. Unlike the incensation, the *Placeat* belongs to
every Mass, low or solemn. No commentator of § 3.12 names the Secret.

### 3.9 Communion — Ps 118:4–5 (no. 1687)

**Direct ancient exegesis read:**

- **Augustine, Enarr. in Ps. 118, Aleph §§ 4–5** (NPNF1-8 "Psalm CXIX", tracked
  CCEL text, lines 56775–56792; layer, English): *nimis* means *very much*; on
  v. 5: *Thou indeed hast charged: O that I could realize what thou hast charged.
  When thou hearest, "O that," recognise the words of one wishing; and having
  recognised the expression of a wish, lay aside the pride of presumption*; *if
  man desireth what God chargeth, God must be prayed to grant Himself what He
  enjoineth*.
- **Hilary of Poitiers, Tract. in Ps. 118, Aleph §§ 11–12** (PL 9, cols. 508 D–509
  B, read in iteration 1 on 200-dpi page images of the registered whole-volume
  scan, PDF pp. 270–271, digest re-matched, after locating it in the tracked PL 9
  layer, lines 40600–40800; the CSEL 22 layer carries it at lines 19346–19392,
  where the lemmata are letterspaced). § 11 on v. 4: *Mandatum vero est, quod per
  observantiam implendum est*, not a shadow of what is to come like the law but
  having *praesentem … operationis effectum*; *Nihil igitur dissoluto animo,
  nihil incurioso agendum est, sed sollicitos atque anxios curam mandatorum Dei
  exsequi nos oportet*. § 12, headed by the edition *Justificationes Dei plures;
  earum nexus; sine gratia non comparantur*, on v. 5: the justifications are many
  and differ with every kind of duty, *ad quorum custodiam, nisi a Deo
  dirigamur, infirmes per naturam nostram erimus. Adjuvandi igitur per gratiam
  ejus, dirigendique sumus, ut praeceptarum justificationum ordinem consequamur*;
  justice is owed to servant, son, father, brothers, the faithful, the Church's
  rulers, the angels and God's only-begotten Word, and *Religiosus in patrem, si
  oderit filium, modum justificationis amisit*. Hilary reads *utinam dirigantur*
  as Augustine does: the direction the psalmist wishes for is God's grace, without
  which nature cannot keep what is commanded. Used: readings 1 and 3.
- **The *Expositiones in Psalmos* printed under Athanasius's name, on Ps 118:4–5**
  (PG 27 layer, as in § 3.1, physical lines 37290–37340; Greek, layer only). The
  *hypothesis*: the psalm describes the saints' way of life, their contests and
  tribulations, and what they overcome by: the law, the oracles, patience and help
  from above. On vv. 4–5: *Γνοὺς, ὡς τῆς ἄνωθεν ἐπικουρίας χωρὶς οὐ δυνατόν τινα
  φύλακα γενέσθαι τοῦ νόμου, εὐχῇ τοῦτο ἑαυτῷ κατορθοῖ· τὸ, ὄφελον, ἀντὶ τοῦ,
  γένοιτο, κεῖται* — knowing that without help from above no one can keep the
  law, he secures it for himself by prayer; *utinam* stands for *may it be*.
  (Normalised; the layer reads *τῦς* and *ἐπ'ιχουρίας*.) The Greek counterpart
  of Augustine's and Hilary's reading. Used: readings 1 and 3.

**Later reception read:** Bellarmine on Ps 118:4 (line 4141): *"thou hast
commanded," not by way of advice, but by strict precept*. Schuster, p. 173:
*by the exclamation utinam, he signifies his joy and delight in the service of
God*. Honorius IV.86: *legem Dei se custodire pronuntiant, quam ut implere
valeant, postulant*. Rupert XII.19 gives it to the healed paralytic; not usable.

**No registered route:** Ambrose, *Expositio Psalmi CXVIII* (mapped by the index;
no library record); Alcuin (§ 5).

### 3.10 Postcommunion — *Tua nos, Domine, medicinalis operatio* (no. 1688)

Composed oration. **Reception read:** Schuster, pp. 173–174: *we make our
petition that the healing grace of the Eucharist may mercifully deliver us from
our perversity and make us ever to be devoted to the divine will*, and *our
infirmity is caused by the poisoning of our nature through the fatal apple of
Eden*. **Verbal and conceptual relations** (editorial observation, not
reception): Chrysostom's evening psalm as *φάρμακον σωτήριον* (§ 3.4); Gregory's
*remedia vitae vestrae*, which he sets beside the deaths of the Fathers of both
Testaments (§ 3.6); Hilary's *infirmes per naturam nostram* on Ps 118:5, which
only grace directs (§ 3.9). The Cummiskey English renders *medicinalis
operatio* as *the healing efficacy of these thy mysteries*; that is the 1861
translator's rendering and is not the Latin's wording.

### 3.11 The Trinity Preface (appointed by rubric)

Appointed by the printed direction and RGMR 494 b (`research/context.md`). Not

## 4. Material disagreements preserved

### 4.1 What is the wedding garment?

| Witness | Answer | Locus |
|---|---|---|
| Irenaeus | works of righteousness on which the Spirit rests; those without it *have not received the Holy Spirit* | Adv. haer. IV.36.6 |
| Hilary | *gloria Spiritus sancti, et candor habitus coelestis*, received in the baptismal confession and to be kept spotless to the kingdom | In Matth. XXII.7 |
| Chrysostom | *the garment is life and practice*; calling and cleansing are of grace, keeping the clean garment is of diligence | Hom. 69 in Matth. |
| Jerome | the Lord's precepts and works that make the new man's garment; its lack is the old man's cast skin | In Matth. III, cols. 160–161 |
| Augustine | charity from a pure heart, good conscience and unfeigned faith; explicitly not baptism, not the Altar, not miracles | Sermo 90 §§ 5–6; Sermo 95 § 7 |
| Gregory | charity; explicitly not baptism or faith, since no one enters without them | Hom. 38 § 9 |
| Aquinas | Christ, put on by sacrament, by charity, by good work and holy life; lacking any one is evil | Super Matth. c. 22 |

**The disagreement is real and narrower than it looks.** Hilary identifies the
garment with the gift received in the baptismal confession; Augustine and
Gregory deny that the garment is baptism, because the unworthy guest has
baptism too. The two positions agree that the guest is baptized and that what
he lacks is something the baptized can lose or never put on: Hilary's garment
must be *reservetur … immaculatus*, Augustine's is charity that *the bad have
not*. Chrysostom's grace-then-diligence and Aquinas's four ways of putting on
Christ each hold both sides. A reading may present the witnesses as
complementary on that shared point; it may not say that the Fathers agree that
the garment is baptism, or that they agree that it is not.

### 4.2 Which city did the king burn?

| Witness | Answer |
|---|---|
| Chrysostom | Jerusalem, under Vespasian and Titus, forty years after the Passion |
| Jerome | either avenging angels or the Romans under Vespasian and Titus burning *praevaricatricem civitatem* |
| Gregory | the persecutors' flesh in eternal fire; the armies are angels |
| Hilary | the heavenly armies burn the whole *congregatio* with eternal fire |
| Irenaeus | *burned down their city*, without naming it |

Historical and eschatological answers; Jerome keeps both. A reading must not
attribute the Roman reading to Gregory or Hilary.

### 4.3 Who are the servants, who refuses, and what are the highways?

**The servants.** Gregory: prophets, then apostles, sent to invite *amicos*
(§ 3). Jerome: Moses (or the prophets), then the prophets or the apostles,
depending on the reading *servum*/*servos*. Hilary: the servants sent to call are
the apostles; the second sending is apostolic men and their successors.
Chrysostom: prophets, John and the Son before the Passion; Peter and his
companions after it. Irenaeus: prophets and apostles, sent by one God.

**The refusers and the highways.** Here the witnesses give different kinds of
answer, and each is recorded as his own.

- **Israel first, then the nations:** Irenaeus (*the men of the former
  dispensation*; *from all the highways, that is, from all nations*), Hilary
  (*invitati antea populus Israel est*; the king *sermonem eumdem ad
  congregationem gentium retulit*, and the *exitus viarum* are also the end of
  the world's way, where past sins are forgiven), Jerome (the Church *tam ex
  Judaeis, quam ex gentibus*; the armies burn the city of *Judaeae populis*;
  *Gentilium populus … in exitibus viarum*, among whom some are good by nature,
  Rom 2:14) and Chrysostom (*the casting out of
  the Jews, and the calling of the Gentiles*, after 21:43). Of these, Irenaeus
  alone makes the reading a proof that the God who called by the prophets is the
  Father of Christ.
- **The highways as the teachings of the Gentiles:** Augustine, *Quaestiones
  evangeliorum* I.31 (PL 35 col. 1329, page image; § 3.6): *Ite ad exitus
  viarum … viæ intelliguntur dogmata Gentium; quia ex omnibus illis ad nuptias
  venerunt, id est, Christo crediderunt.* The ways are the Gentiles' teachings,
  and from all of them people came to the wedding, that is, believed in Christ.
  His answer points where the first group's does, to the Gentiles, but by a
  different identification: the *viae* are doctrines, not peoples or the
  world's time, and those who came are those who left them for Christ. In the
  same question the wedding is the Word incarnate, in whose assumed humanity the
  Church is joined to God, and the bulls are *principes plebium*; he says
  nothing there of who the first invited were or why they refused. **Recorded
  separately:** his Sermones 90 and 95, which preach this parable, take up
  neither the refusers nor the highways (§ 3.6). On Luke's supper, the other evangelist's
  parable, he identifies the outer ways as the Gentiles' in *Quaestiones* II.30
  (*propter diversas vias sectarum*) and, in Sermo 112, the streets and lanes
  as the Gentiles and the hedges as the heretics (§ 3.6); these are his reading
  of Lk 14 and are not transferred to Mt 22.
- **A moral answer that names no people:** Gregory. His invited are *amici*
  called by prophets and apostles; the refusers are those given to immoderate
  earthly labour and to the gains of worldly business (§ 5), and the worse
  among them persecute *vocantis gratiam* and the martyrs; the *exitus viarum*
  are *defectus actionum*, *quia illi plerumque facile ad Deum veniunt, quos in
  terrenis actibus prospera nulla comitantur* (§ 6). He names neither Israel nor
  the nations anywhere in the homily (§ 3.6). Rabanus Maurus's catena on
  Matthew sets this reading after Augustine's, under the heading *Moraliter
  autem* (PL 107 layer; § 3.6): the transmitter classes it as the moral answer
  beside Augustine's, which is the classification this record uses.

Two psalm witnesses, one Greek and one Latin, read the formulary's chants of the
call in the direction of the first group, each at his own verse: the
*Expositiones* printed under Athanasius's name take *popule meus* of Ps 77:1 as
the people from the nations and Ps 104:1 as the Spirit's command to the
apostles to proclaim to them; Augustine takes Ps 104:1 as spoken *Evangelistis in
prophetia* (§§ 3.1, 3.5). Neither comments on Mt 22 there. Augustine is thus
the one witness in this record who, in two separate works, reads both the
Alleluia's verse and the Gospel's highways toward the Gentiles; he does not
join the two. Jerome, on Mt 22:7, cites Ps 77:49 for the avenging angels
(§ 3.6).

A reading may therefore say that Irenaeus, Hilary, Jerome and Chrysostom read
the parable as the call passing from Israel to the nations, and that Augustine
takes the highways as the teachings of the Gentiles, from all of which those
who believed in Christ came to the wedding. It may not say that the Fathers, or
the witnesses, agree on one account: Gregory reads the refusal and the highways
morally, naming no people; and Augustine, who answers the question of the
highways in the *Quaestiones*, does not there say who refused, while his two
sermons on this parable take up neither point.

### 4.4 Are the nuptials the present Church or the glory to come?

Gregory: the present Church (Matthew's *nuptiae*), distinct from Luke's final
supper. Augustine, *Quaestiones* I.31: the nuptials are the Word incarnate, in
whose assumed humanity the Church is joined to God — the union itself, as in
Gregory's § 3. Hilary: the heavenly life and eternal glory to be received in the
resurrection; the king enters the assembly of the resurrection. Augustine: two
feasts, the present one to which good and bad come and the other to which the
bad do not. Chrysostom: the marriage and the mysteries now, the torments after.
A reading that treats the feast as the Church at the Lord's table follows
Augustine and Gregory; Hilary's is the anagogical answer. The difference is one
of emphasis and time, and each witness is cited for his own.

### 4.5 Where the checked witnesses do not disagree

Each of the six Fathers of § 3.6 who expounds vv. 11–13 (Irenaeus, Hilary,
Jerome, Chrysostom, Augustine, Gregory) takes the unclothed guest as one who has
come in under the call: Irenaeus's *called to God's supper*, Hilary's garment
received *bonae interrogationis confessione*, Jerome's guest found *in tempore judicii … sub nomine
Christiano* (col. 160 D), Chrysostom's hearers *having partaken of the mysteries*, Augustine's
and Gregory's guest who has baptism and faith. Each of them reads the casting out
as judgement: Irenaeus by 1 Cor 10:5, Hilary in the resurrection's assembly,
Jerome's king who comes *in die judicii visitare convivas, et
discerneret merita singulorum* (col. 160 C), Chrysostom in the torments to come, Augustine in the
feast *to which the evil come not*, Gregory on *futuro die judicii* (§ 14). On the
refusers and the highways they do not agree (§ 4.3).

None of them reads the wedding feast of this Gospel as the Eucharist in so many
words: Augustine calls the present feast *the Lord's Table* and *the Feast of
the Holy Scriptures*, Gregory calls it *Scripturae sacrae epulae* and *divini
Verbi epulae*, Chrysostom addresses *as many as having partaken of the
mysteries*. A reading that joins the Gospel to this Mass's Communion must
attribute that step to those words and no further.

### 4.6 What is the evening sacrifice of Ps 140:2, and whose are the lifted hands?

| Witness | The lifted hands | The evening sacrifice | Locus |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hilary | hands lifted, as distinct from hands spread in prayer, are *perfecti … operis efficientia*; *sine ira* (1 Tim 2:8) is *praescriptum divinae operationis* | the works of mercy of Mt 25:34–40, the sacrifice *temporum novissimorum* of those *in quos consummatio saeculorum devenit* | Tract. in Ps. 140 §§ 3–4 |
| Augustine | the prayer of the whole Christ while tribulation lasts | *is wont to be understood of the Head Himself*: the Passion and the Cross at evening, which produced in the Resurrection a morning offering | Enarr. in Ps. 140 §§ 2–3 |
| Chrysostom | on v. 1, prayer made without anger, with 1 Tim 2:8 | the psalm the Fathers appointed for every evening, *φάρμακον σωτήριον* for the day's sins | Expositio in Ps. 140 |
| Bellarmine | the gesture of prayer, with 1 Tim 2:8 | the evening incense of the temple, the evening psalm of the Church, *perhaps* a figure of the sacrifice of the Cross; prayer offered through Christ the high priest | on Ps 140:2 |
| Schuster | — | our present oblation, *enveloped in the twilight of faith*, before the morning of the beatific vision | p. 172 |

**The difference is real.** Augustine reads the verse first of the Head, and the
evening sacrifice is the Passion; Hilary reads it of the hands of the Church in
the last age, and the evening sacrifice is the works of mercy that the Judge
will name at the end. The two are not contradictory in doctrine, but they give
different answers to what the verse's sacrifice is, and a reading that uses
either must say whose answer it is. Chrysostom, Bellarmine and Schuster stand
between them: all read the verse of the Church's prayer; Bellarmine alone adds,
as a possibility, the figure of the Cross; Chrysostom brings in 1 Tim 2:8 as a
condition of prayer and Bellarmine cites it for the gesture, while Hilary takes
the same verse as a command to act. Iteration 0 recorded this question as
uncontested; that was wrong, because Hilary had not been read.
