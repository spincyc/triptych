## 1. `wedding-garment` — The garment the King looks for: the new man put on in charity

### 1.1 Controlling claim

The Epistle's command *induite novum hominem, qui secundum Deum creatus est* and
the Gospel's guest *non vestitum veste nuptiali* are one demand seen from two
sides: the Church is the wedding, entry is by God's call, and what the King looks
for in those who have come in is the new man put on — Christ, and the charity by
which Christ is put on — shown in the plain acts the Epistle names. The
Communion and Postcommunion pray for what this demands: that the one who has
been commanded may be directed to keep the commandments, and made to cleave to
them.

The join between Epistle and Gospel is **[R]** in Jerome, who calls the wedding
garment the garment of the new man and its lack the old man's cast skin (In
Matth. III, cols. 160–161), and who reads the Epistle's new man as Christ put on
(In Eph. II, col. 508). It is **[S]** as a claim about the formulary.

### 1.2 Principal witnesses and their reasoning

**Jerome.** Two loci, read on the PL 26 page images, meet in one image. On
Mt 22:11–12: *Vestis autem nuptialis, praecepta sunt Domini, et opera quae
complentur ex lege et Evangelio, novique hominis efficiunt vestimentum*; the
guest found *in tempore judicii … sub nomine Christiano* without it has not the
garment *supercoelestis hominis* but *vestem pollutam, id est, veteris hominis
exuvias*. On Eph 4:24: the new man is Christ, *Induite vos Christum Jesum*; *Iste
quippe est novus homo, quo universi credentes debemus indui atque vestiri*, and
whoever imitates Christ's *conversatio*, his meekness and humility, *indutus est
novum hominem*. Jerome's reasoning: the garment is not an object but a way of
living that makes the wearer a new man, and the new man is Christ; to lack it is
to come to the feast still dressed in what should have been shed. He adds on
Eph 4:28 that honest labour is for giving: whoever labours only so as not to need
*et a caeteris contrahit manum … Apostoli praeceptum non fecit*. And on Mt 22:14:
the question in the vineyard, the house and the feast is *non initia, sed finis*.

**Gregory the Great.** Hom. 38 §§ 9–14. He first removes two answers: the garment
is not baptism or faith, *quis sine baptismate et fide has nuptias intravit?* It
is charity, *quia hanc in se conditor noster habuit, dum ad sociandae sibi
Ecclesiae nuptias venit*: the Bridegroom came to the wedding wearing charity,
and so the guest who honours him must. The garment is woven on two beams, love
of God and of neighbour (§ 10); it is proved by love of the enemy (§ 11). The
guest is *Amice, et non amice; amice per fidem, sed non amice per operationem*
(§ 12). The binding of hands and feet (§ 13) is already begun in this life:
*manus quae nihil indigentibus tribuunt a bono opere jam ex voluntate ligatae
sunt*. And no one may presume: *Quia vocati sumus, novimus; si sumus electi,
nescimus* (§ 14). Gregory's reasoning moves from what all the guests share
(faith, baptism) to what distinguishes them (charity), and from the Bridegroom's
charity to the guest's.

**Augustine.** Sermo 90 (against the Donatists, on charity) makes the same
elimination at greater length: not baptism, *this garment I see in the good, I
see in the bad*; not *the Altar, or That which is received at the Altar*; not
fasting, church-going or miracles (§ 5). The garment is *charity out of a pure
heart, and of a good conscience, and of faith unfeigned* (1 Tim 1:5), not any
love but that one (§ 6); it is *in the heart, not on the body* (§ 4); it grows
(*be it nourished, fostered, increased*); it reaches the enemy, after the
Bridegroom *hanging upon the Cross … praying to His Father for His enemies* and
Stephen (§ 9); and in putting it on *the image of God, after which we were
created* is *engraven anew in us* (§ 10). The last sentence meets the Epistle's
*qui secundum Deum creatus est* without citing it: the join is **[S]**, the
thought of renewed image is Augustine's own. Sermo 95 § 7 repeats the
elimination (*Baptism … the good and bad have it. The Sacraments of the Altar the
good and bad receive together*) and ends at 1 Cor 13. Augustine's reason for the
elimination is anti-Donatist: the Church is mixed, and what separates the guests
is not a rite the bad also have.

**Supporting witnesses.** Chrysostom, Hom. 69: *For the garment is life and
practice*; calling and cleansing are of grace, *to continue keeping them so, this
is of the diligence of them that are called*. On the Epistle, Hom. 13 in Eph.: the
subject is one and the *clothing is twofold, that which is put off, and that which
is put on*, and the new man is created *to be a son: for this takes place from
Baptism*; Hom. 14: lying, revenge, theft are *the members of the old man*, and the
thief undoes his sin *If they labor, and charitably communicate to others*.

Aquinas on the Epistle, *Super Ephesios* c. 4, lect. 7–9 (1857 facsimile, page
images): the Holy Spirit dwelling in the mind is the cause of renewal; *sicut
uniuscujusque rei primum vetustatis principium fuit Adam … ita principium primum
novitatis et renovationis Christus est*, and so *Induimini … Dominum nostrum
Jesum Christum*; the new man *qui creatus est* may be Christ himself, formed in
the Virgin's womb by the Spirit; *sanctitas … in corde, veritas in ore, justitia
in opere*; members *se invicem diligunt, et se juvant mutuo in veritate*; the
movement of anger is allowed to frailty, *non permittitur mora*; and labour is
good *ut laborans possit vivere, et Ut habeat unde tribuat necessitatem
patienti*. Aquinas on the Gospel, *Super Matth.* c. 22: *Quae est illa vestis?
Christus*; one puts on Christ by sacrament, by charity, by good work, by holy
life; *si unum deficiat, malum* (OCR layer; `research/scope.md` § 3.6). The same
Doctor thus names Christ the new man of the Epistle and the garment of the
Gospel, in two separate commentaries; the join between them is his in neither
place, and is **[S]**.

Hilary on the Gradual and Communion psalms (Tract. in Ps. 140 §§ 3–4; in Ps. 118,
Aleph §§ 11–12; PL 9 page images): the lifted hands are the doing of the work,
and the acceptable evening sacrifice is the works of mercy of Mt 25; the
commandments are to be carried out *sollicitos atque anxios*, and *nisi a Deo
dirigamur, infirmes per naturam nostram erimus*. Bl. Ildefonso Schuster, on this
Mass's Epistle: *Christian sanctity under the symbol of a new garment … This
garment is Jesus Christ himself*. Rabanus Maurus transmits Jerome's sentence and
Gregory's in his catena.

### 1.4 The four senses

- **Literal.** Paul tells the Ephesians to be renewed in the spirit of their mind
  and to put on the new man created after God in justice and holiness of truth,
  and names what that means: truth to the neighbour, anger ended before the sun
  sets, no foothold for the devil, work that can give to the needy. In the
  parable, a man who has come into the king's wedding without a wedding garment
  is questioned, falls silent, and is bound and cast out; many are called, few
  chosen.
- **Allegorical.** The garment is Christ put on, the new and heavenly man
  (Jerome; Aquinas, who makes Christ the first principle of all newness as Adam
  was of all oldness; Schuster), and the charity the Bridegroom himself wore when
  he came to take the Church to himself (Gregory § 9). Its lack is the cast skin
  of the old man (Jerome). The guest is called *friend* by faith and is no friend
  by his works (Gregory § 12).
- **Moral.** Charity toward God and neighbour, proved toward the enemy (Gregory
  §§ 10–11; Augustine § 9); truth among members of one body, anger settled the
  same day, work that gives (Epistle; Chrysostom, Hom. 14 in Eph.; Jerome and
  Aquinas on v. 28); hands lifted in the works of mercy rather than merely spread
  in prayer (Hilary on Ps 140:2); hands that give nothing are already bound
  (Gregory § 13). What God commands is asked of God (Augustine and Hilary on
  Ps 118:5).
- **Anagogical.** The King's entry is the judgement (Jerome: *in die judicii
  visitare convivas, et discerneret merita singulorum*); what is asked is *non
  initia, sed finis*; no guest knows whether he is chosen (Gregory § 14);
  Hilary's garment is to be kept spotless *usque in coetum regni coelorum*.

### 1.5 Strongest difficulty and alternative

**Is the garment a gift or a work?** If entry is grace and the guest is
condemned for lacking a garment, the call seems to demand what it did not give.
The witnesses answer differently (`research/scope.md` § 4.1). Hilary makes the
garment the gift of the Spirit received in the baptismal confession, to be kept
unstained; Gregory and Augustine deny that it is baptism, since the unworthy
guest is baptized too, and make it charity. Chrysostom holds both: calling and
cleansing are grace, keeping the garment is the guest's diligence. The reading's
resolution comes from the Communion's verse and from three expositors of it who
agree: Augustine, that what God commands must be asked of God; Hilary, that
nature cannot keep the justifications unless God directs it, so that we must be
helped and directed by grace; and the Greek *Expositiones* under Athanasius's
name, that the psalmist secures the keeping of the law by prayer. Hilary, who
makes the garment a gift of the Spirit, says on Ps 118:5 that the commandments
are kept only as grace directs; putting his two loci together is the editor's
**[S]**. The reading therefore presents the garment as God's gift that must be
put on and kept, and it keeps Hilary's baptismal answer beside Gregory's rather
than merging them.

**How could a guest swept in from the highways have a garment?** Hilary asks
exactly this (*Numquid invitandorum habitum designaverat? … quomodo unus omnibus
poterat esse vestitus?*) and answers that the garment is inward and God alone
discerns the counterfeit; Augustine: *had it been put on externally, it could not
have been concealed even from the servants*. The difficulty confirms that the
garment is not clothing and not a rite.

**Alternative.** Irenaeus reads the garment as works of righteousness *so that
the Spirit of God may rest upon us*, which could make the reading a
pneumatological one (the garment as the Spirit); the Epistle's next verse, *nolite
contristare Spiritum Sanctum Dei, in quo signati estis* (Eph 4:30), lies outside
the pericope and is not appointed. Aquinas's first reading of v. 23 makes the
Holy Spirit dwelling in the mind the cause of the renewal, which is inside the
pericope. The reading notes the alternative and does not build on the unappointed
verse.

### 1.6 Agreements and differences within the reading

Gregory and Augustine agree that the guest has faith and the sacraments, that
the garment is what the unworthy lack though they share them, that it is in the
heart, and that it is charity. Jerome agrees that the guest is inside under the
Christian name and that what he lacks is the new man's garment, but names it
differently: the Lord's precepts and the works that make the new man. Aquinas and
Schuster name it Christ, and Aquinas and Jerome name the Epistle's new man
Christ. The reading holds these as complementary names of one inward reality put
on and shown in works, and preserves Hilary's baptismal answer as a real
difference about the garment's origin, not its nature. Hilary's reading of the
Gradual's lifted hands as works of mercy develops this reading's moral sense;
it is his exposition of Ps 140:2, not a comment on the Gospel.

## 2. `call-to-the-nations` — The invitation refused and carried to the highways

### 2.1 Controlling claim

The formulary sets the parable of the refused wedding inside the Church's memory
of God's dealings with his people: the Lord who says *Attendite, popule meus,
legem meam* and promises to hear whoever cries to him is the King who called
first Israel through prophets and apostles and, when the invited made light of
it and killed his messengers, sent his servants to the highways — the command
the Alleluia gives in prophecy, *annuntiate inter gentes opera eius*. This
assembly is the hall filled from the highways; the call comes to it as a warning
from the history it has been told.

The join of Alleluia and Gospel is **[S]**, made of steps each of which is one
author's. Augustine reads the Alleluia's verse as said *Evangelistis in
prophetia* (Enarr. in Ps. 104 § 1), and the Greek *Expositiones* printed under
Athanasius's name read it as the Spirit's command to the apostles to make the
Lord's wonders known to the people from the nations (on Ps 104:1): **[R]** for
the Alleluia as the command to preach to the nations. Irenaeus (*from all the
highways, that is, from all nations*), Hilary (*sermonem eumdem ad
congregationem gentium retulit*), Jerome (*Gentilium populus … in exitibus
viarum*) and Chrysostom (*the calling of the Gentiles*) read the highways of
Mt 22:9 as the nations, and Augustine reads them as the nations' teachings:
*viæ intelliguntur dogmata Gentium; quia ex omnibus illis ad nuptias venerunt,
id est, Christo crediderunt* (*Quaestiones evangeliorum* I.31): **[R]** for that
step. No author joins the two. Augustine takes both steps, the Alleluia's in
his exposition of Ps 104 and the highways' in the *Quaestiones*, but in two
separate works, and the join and its place in this formulary are the editor's.
Gregory reads the highways otherwise (§ 2.5).

### 2.2 Principal witnesses and their reasoning

**John Chrysostom.** Hom. 69 in Matth. reads the parable as the sequel to Mt 21:43
(*"It shall be given to a nation bringing forth the fruits thereof"*): it
*proclaims beforehand both the casting out of the Jews, and the calling of the
Gentiles*. His reasoning is historical and pastoral at once. The invitation
came before the Passion, *By all the prophets; by John again* … *by the Son
Himself again*, and again after it, *by Peter, and those with him*: *here even after He is slain, He
still urges them*. The excuses *seem to be reasonable*, but spiritual things are
to be set *at a higher price than all*. The burning foretells *the things that
took place under Vespasian and Titus*, and came *not straightway … but after forty
years, that He might show His long suffering*. The apostles went to the Jews
first and only then, driven out, to the Gentiles (Acts 13:46). And the Gentiles
are warned in turn: the garment *is life and practice*, *that not even these
should put confidence in their faith alone*.

**Irenaeus.** Adv. haer. IV.36.5–6 uses the parable against those who divide
the God of the prophets from the Father of Christ: *there is one King and Lord,
the Father of all*, who *had from the beginning prepared the marriage for His
Son*, called *the men of the former dispensation* by his servants, and *called
together from all the highways, that is, from all nations*. *The Lord, therefore,
who has called us everywhere by the apostles, is He who called those of old by
the prophets.* The same God judges: *as in the former covenant, "with many of them
was He not well pleased;" so also is it the case here, that "many are called, but
few chosen."* His reasoning makes the parable a proof of one economy.

**Hilary of Poitiers.** In Matth. XXII.3–7 makes the history explicit and then
lifts it. The nuptials are the *sacramentum* of heavenly life and of the eternal
glory to be received in the resurrection (§ 3). The invited are *populus Israel
… in gloriam … aeternitatis per legem … advocatus*; the servants sent to call are
the apostles; those sent again *cum praeceptorum conditione* are *apostolici …
viri et successores apostolorum*; the fatted bulls are the martyrs and the
fatlings spiritual men, and when all is ready and the multitude pleasing to God
is gathered, the kingdom's glory is announced as a wedding (§ 4). The field and
the merchandise are ambition and love of money (§ 5). Then the nations: because
the parable speaks of the time of judgement and resurrection, the king turns the
same word *ad congregationem gentium*; the *exitus viarum* are also the end of
the way that is the world's time, where *omnibus retroacta donantur*; the call
goes out *sine aliqua exceptione*, the bad come with the good, and the call
*bonos efficere debuerat, quia sancta est … sed per vitium inemendatae
voluntatis discrimen est vocatorum* (§ 6).

**Supporting witnesses.** Jerome, In Matth. III: the King makes the wedding for
Christ *et Ecclesiae, quae tam ex Judaeis, quam ex gentibus, congregata est*; the
Gentiles were *non … in viis, sed in exitibus viarum*, and among them some were
good by nature (Rom 2:14); the avengers of v. 7 are either angels, *de quibus in
Psalmis scribitur: Immissiones per angelos pessimos* (Ps 77:49), or the Romans
*sub duce Vespasiano et Tito*. Gregory, Hom. 38 §§ 3–5: prophets then apostles
sent to invite *amicos*; the field and trade are labour and gain pursued beyond
measure; *Rebus ergo cognoscimus quod in parabolis audimus* — he shares the
servants and the excuses with this reading, not its identification of the
refusers and the highways (§ 2.5). Augustine, *Quaestiones evangeliorum* I.31
(PL 35 col. 1329): the wedding is the Word incarnate, *quia in ipso homine
suscepto Ecclesia Deo copulata est*; the bulls are *principes plebium*; and the
ways to whose ends the servants go are the teachings of the Gentiles, *quia ex
omnibus illis ad nuptias venerunt, id est, Christo crediderunt*. His reasoning is
compressed: the ways are the Gentiles' doctrines, and people came to the wedding
out of all of them, and came by believing in Christ. He says nothing in this
question of who first refused. Augustine, Enarr. in Ps. 77 §§ 1–2: the psalm
tells what was done *among the old people* so that *the new and latter people*
may not be ungrateful — and he uses 1 Cor 10:5 as Irenaeus does; Enarr. in
Ps. 104 § 1: *Evangelizate in gentibus … Quibus hoc dicitur, nisi Evangelistis in
prophetia?*, and §§ 35–36: the seed of Abraham is *non de sola gente Hebraeorum,
sed quotquot istam gratiam suscipiunt in omni terra*. The Greek *Expositiones*
printed under Athanasius's name: Ps 77 exhorts *the new people* to the Gospel
law, and its *popule meus* is the people *from the nations*; Ps 104:1 is the
Spirit's command to the apostles to show the nations the Lord's wonders. On the
Alleluia's verse Schuster names the apostles, and after them the bishops and
pastors, whose first duty is this preaching of the Gospel (p. 172); Bellarmine
reads it as the prophet's call to God's people to *speak in all directions
among the gentiles of the wonderful works of God*, and names no apostles.
Rabanus Maurus's catena on Matthew transmits Augustine's sentence on the
highways, marked *(Aug.)*, after reading the unworthy invited as the Jews who
refused the sacrament of the Incarnation (PL 107 layer; `research/scope.md`
§ 3.6).

### 2.4 The four senses

- **Literal.** In the temple, answering the chief priests and Pharisees who
  perceived that his parables were about them (Mt 21:45), Jesus tells of a king
  whose invited guests refuse his son's wedding, maltreat and kill his servants,
  and see their city burned, while the hall is filled from the highways. Ps 77
  recites God's deeds to teach the coming generation; Ps 104 bids Israel praise
  and proclaim his works among the nations.
- **Allegorical.** The King is the Father; the wedding is the Son's union with
  the Church through the Incarnation (Gregory § 3; Augustine, *Quaestiones*
  I.31: the Word incarnate, in whose assumed humanity the Church is joined to
  God), a Church gathered from Jews and Gentiles (Jerome); the servants are
  prophets and then apostles (Gregory § 3; Chrysostom, with John and the Son
  between them), sent by the one God who called those of old by the prophets
  and calls us by the apostles (Irenaeus); for Hilary they are the apostles and
  then apostolic men, their successors (In Matth. XXII.4); the highways are
  the nations (Irenaeus; Hilary; Jerome; Chrysostom), or the
  Gentiles' teachings, from all of which those who believed in Christ came
  (Augustine, *Quaestiones* I.31); the Alleluia's command is the evangelists'
  (Augustine) and the apostles', given to them for the nations (the PG 27
  *Expositiones*).
- **Moral.** Not to make light of the call for field or trade — immoderate labour
  and greed (Gregory § 5), ambition and love of money (Hilary § 5) — and not to set
  necessary things above spiritual (Chrysostom); not to be ungrateful as a people
  that has been told the story (Augustine on Ps 77); not to trust in faith alone
  (Chrysostom). Gregory adds his own reading of the highways: those whom no
  worldly success accompanies come readily to God (§ 6).
- **Anagogical.** The feast the call prepares: when all is made ready and the
  multitude pleasing to God is gathered *in numerum*, the glory of the heavenly
  kingdom is announced as a wedding (Hilary on v. 4); the wedding itself is the
  *sacramentum* of the eternal glory received in the resurrection (Hilary on
  v. 2); and the King enters *felicis resurrectionis istius coetum* to see his
  guests (Hilary on v. 11). Of the filled hall (v. 10) Hilary says that the call
  goes to all *sine aliqua exceptione* and that the bad come with the good; that
  the hall filled from the highways is the Church of the nations awaiting that
  entry is **[S]**. Many are called, few chosen: *Non est igitur paucitas in
  invitatis, sed raritas in electis* (Hilary on v. 14); as in the former
  covenant, so here (Irenaeus).

### 2.5 Strongest difficulty and alternative

**The burned city.** Chrysostom and Jerome read Jerusalem's fall under Titus,
Jerome keeping the avenging angels of Ps 77:49 beside it; Gregory reads the
persecutors' eternal punishment and Hilary the heavenly armies' fire
(`research/scope.md` § 4.2). A study must not present the Roman reading as the
only patristic answer, and must keep the parable's addressees as the Gospel
names them, the chief priests and Pharisees (Mt 21:45; the liturgical opening).
Augustine on Ps 77 supplies the limit within the tradition: *there were even in
that people they that understood*. Chrysostom supplies the counterweight in the
same homily: the Gentiles, too, are judged by the garment.

**The highways without the nations: Gregory's answer.** Gregory, preaching this
Gospel *ad populum in basilica beati Clementis martyris*, names neither Israel
nor the nations in the whole homily (`research/scope.md` §§ 3.6, 4.3). His
invited are *amici*; his refusers are those who give themselves beyond measure
to earthly labour and to
the gains of worldly business, and the worse of them persecute the grace of the
one who calls; his *exitus viarum* are *defectus actionum*, the failing of
worldly undertakings, *quia illi plerumque facile ad Deum veniunt, quos in
terrenis actibus prospera nulla comitantur*. This is a different answer to the
reading's central identification, not a difference of detail: the reading
follows Irenaeus, Hilary, Jerome and Chrysostom, with Augustine in his own form
of the answer (below), and the study keeps
Gregory's reading beside it, as his own, where the highways are treated. It
also gives the moral sense its own form: in Gregory's words the call is most
readily answered by those whom worldly success does not accompany. Setting that
beside the call of the nations is the editor's **[S]**. Rabanus Maurus's catena
on Matthew already sets the two side by side, Augustine's sentence first and
Gregory's after it under the heading *Moraliter autem*; that heading is the
catena's, not Gregory's (`research/scope.md` §§ 3.6, 4.3).

**The highways as teachings: Augustine's form of the answer.** Augustine does
not say, with the four, that the highways are the nations. *Viæ intelliguntur
dogmata Gentium* (*Quaestiones evangeliorum* I.31): the ways are what the
Gentiles taught and held, and those who came to the wedding came out of all of
them by believing in Christ. His answer points where theirs does, and a study
may count him among those who read the highways of the Gentiles, but it must
give his words and not theirs: the call reaches people in the doctrines they
held and brings them out of those doctrines to Christ. In that question he says
nothing of who first refused, so he may not be cited for the refusal of Israel.
His two sermons on this parable, Sermones 90 and 95, take up neither the refusers
nor the highways. What he says of Luke's supper (*Quaestiones* II.30; Sermo
112) belongs to that parable and is not transferred here (`research/scope.md`
§§ 3.6, 4.3).

**Is the history the formulary's?** The medieval books set these chants with
another Gospel (`research/scope.md` § 2.1), so no study may present the Introit's
Ps 77 and the Gospel's parable as an ancient deliberate pairing. The reading
rests on the texts' own words and on each witness's reading of his own text, and
the join is the editor's. Jerome's use of Ps 77:49 on Mt 22:7 is his, is made of
another verse than the one sung, and is cited as such.

**Alternative.** Gregory's moral reading of the refusers and the highways,
set out above, is the alternative this reading keeps. It stands beside the
controlling claim as Gregory's own. The controlling reading remains that of
Irenaeus, Hilary, Jerome and Chrysostom, for whom the highways are the nations,
with Augustine's own form of the answer, for whom they are the teachings of the
Gentiles, out of which those who believed came to Christ (§§ 2.1, 2.6).

### 2.6 Agreements and differences within the reading

Chrysostom, Irenaeus and Hilary, the carrying authors, agree that the King called
Israel first by his servants and then the nations, and that the called are
judged; Jerome agrees. Irenaeus alone makes of it a proof that the God of the
prophets is the Father of Christ. They differ on the servants (prophets, John and
the Son, then apostles, for Chrysostom; prophets and apostles for Irenaeus;
apostles then their successors for Hilary; Moses or the prophets, then the
prophets or apostles, for Jerome) and on the city (historical for Chrysostom;
historical or angelic for Jerome; unnamed for Irenaeus; eschatological for
Hilary). Hilary alone reads the *exitus viarum* also as the end of the world's
time, where past sins are forgiven. Augustine agrees that the call at the
highways goes to the Gentiles and differs in what the highways are: not the
peoples themselves but their teachings, out of which believers came to Christ;
and in that question he says nothing of Israel's refusal, where the other four
begin. The reading keeps these as differences of detail beside a shared claim.
Gregory's moral reading of the refusers and the highways is a different answer
to that claim and is kept as his (§ 2.5).

## 3. `feast-that-now-is` — The feast that now is: the Church at the Lord's table and its evening sacrifice

### 3.1 Controlling claim

The wedding is the Church now, with good and bad at one table — *the Lord's
Table* and *the Feast of the Holy Scriptures* (Augustine), the *epulae* of the
Scriptures and of the divine Word (Gregory), the mysteries (Chrysostom) — and
the King comes in to see the guests. The formulary's chants and prayers are the
voice of that assembly at that table: it cries in tribulation and is heard, its
prayer rises as incense and its lifted hands join the evening sacrifice that is
the Lord's Passion (Augustine's answer; Hilary's differs, § 3.5), it offers
gifts before God's eyes and asks that they be saving, and at the Communion it
asks to keep what it is commanded and to be freed by the sacrament's healing
work. The garment is the question put to every guest before he approaches.

The identification of the present feast with the Lord's table is **[R]**
(Augustine, Sermo 90 §§ 1, 4, 9; Chrysostom, Hom. 69); the evening sacrifice as
the Passion is **[R]** (Augustine on Ps 140; Bellarmine, as a possibility); the
joining of those to this Mass's Gradual, Offertory, Secret, Communion and
Postcommunion is **[S]**.

Bl. Ildefonso Schuster, commenting on this Mass as a Mass, reads its Gradual as
the Christian's evening oblation (p. 172) and its Secret and Postcommunion of
the Eucharist (pp. 173–174), in the same direction as this reading. The banquet
of this Gospel is for him the heavenly one: *The primary aim of the
predestination of souls to the heavenly banquet is the supreme glorification of
Christ as first-born of the human family and head of the Church*, and of the
guest without the garment, *God calls our souls, but they must be in accord
with their calling, so that the grace of eternal happiness may also be their
due reward* (*The Sacramentary* III, p. 173; `research/scope.md` § 3.6). His
banquet is the one to come, the feast Augustine sets beside the present one as
the other, to which the evil do not come. The present-Church reading of the
wedding is Augustine's and Gregory's, not Schuster's. He is a supporting
witness here, cited on the Introit (p. 171), Collect (pp. 171–172), Gradual
(p. 172), Offertory, Secret and Communion (p. 173) and Postcommunion (p. 174)
(§§ 3.2, 3.3).

### 3.2 Principal witnesses and their reasoning

**Augustine.** Sermo 90 opens with the table: *All the faithful know the
marriage of the king's son, and his feast, and the spreading of the Lord's Table
is open to them all who will.* There are *two feasts of the Lord*: this one, to
which good and bad come, and the other, *to which the evil come not* (§ 1); *Be
that feast which now is, received worthily, that we may attain to the other*
(§ 4). The sermon's pastoral point is where it ends: *as thou art about to
approach the Table of the Lord, the Feast of the Holy Scriptures, do not say, "O
that mine enemy might die!"* (§ 9). Yet the sacrament is not the garment — *many
eat, and "eat and drink judgment to themselves"* (§ 5) — so the feast now is real
and its guests are still sifted. On Ps 140 Augustine gives the prayer of this
assembly: *whole Christ saith it*; *if tribulation remain for the Church, for
the Body of Christ, even to the end of the world*, it must go on praying; the
evening sacrifice *is wont to be understood of the Head Himself* — *the Passion
of the Lord, the Cross of the Lord, the offering of a salutary Victim* — which
*produced, in His Resurrection, a morning offering*, and prayer *riseth like
incense from a hallowed altar* (§§ 2–3). On Ps 137:7: *whatsoever tribulation
thou art in, confess, call on Him; He freeth thee, He reviveth thee*.

**Gregory the Great.** Hom. 38 fixes the time: Matthew's *nuptiae* are the
*praesens Ecclesia*, Luke's supper the last banquet (§ 1); the wedding hall full
of *malos et bonos* is the present Church, *in qua cum bonis et mali conveniunt*,
where the good must bear the bad (§ 7). The assembly he preaches to is already
at table: *Nos sumus … qui in nuptiis Verbi discumbimus … qui Scripturae sacrae
epulis pascimur* (§ 11); *divini Verbi epulas sumimus, sed futuro die judicii rex
intraturus est* (§ 14). Glossing the king's *Tauri mei et altilia occisa sunt*,
he sets the deaths of the Fathers of both Testaments beside the remedies for
our life: *Patrum praecedentium mortes aspicite, et remedia vitae vestrae
cogitate* (§ 4).

**John Chrysostom.** Hom. 69 addresses the guests as communicants: *Hear ye, as
many as having partaken of the mysteries, and having been present at the
marriage, clothe your souls with filthy deeds. Hear whence ye were called.* On
Ps 140 he says the Fathers ordained this psalm for every evening as a saving
medicine and a cleansing of sins (φάρμακον σωτήριον καὶ ἁμαρτημάτων καθάρσιον;
the rendering is the editor's, from the Greek OCR layer). Expounding its v. 1,
before he reaches the lifted hands of v. 2, he forbids prayer against enemies and
requires that the hands raised in prayer be raised without anger (1 Tim 2:8). On
Ps 137:7 he observes that the psalmist does not say God will drive the
tribulation away but that he will keep him alive within it.

**Bl. Ildefonso Schuster**, a supporting witness, reads this Mass itself: the
Gradual's oblation *is always an evening sacrifice, for it is enveloped in the
twilight of faith*, to be endured *with Christ* before *the morning joy of the
beatific vision* (p. 172); the Secret asks that *the holy oblation* be *a pledge
of eternal salvation to all those that share therein* (p. 173); the
Postcommunion asks *that the healing grace of the Eucharist may mercifully
deliver us from our perversity* (p. 174). He is the witness who reads the Mass
as a Mass, and for these three elements he reads it in this reading's
direction; the reading also cites him on the Introit (p. 171), Collect
(pp. 171–172), Offertory and Communion (p. 173), element by element (§ 3.3).
He does not carry the reading: the feast he finds in this Gospel is
the heavenly banquet, not the present Church (p. 173; § 3.1). Augustine,
Gregory and Chrysostom carry it.

**Supporting witnesses.** Bellarmine on Ps 140:2: prayer rises straight as
incense only with pure intention and attention; *Christ is the high priest, for
it is through him … that we must always pray*, which is why every prayer ends
*through our Lord Jesus Christ*; the evening sacrifice was perhaps preferred
*as being a figure of the sacrifice on the cross*. On Ps 137:7: *your strength
and power, Christ, hath saved me*. Jerome on Eph 4:27 and Aquinas on the same
verse both find the devil's foothold at the Lord's supper, in Judas: Jerome, *in
convivio Salvatoris*; Aquinas, *post buccellam*. Hilary on the Communion's verse:
the justifications are kept only if God directs us, *per gratiam*. The *Placeat*
of the Order of Mass asks that the sacrifice offered *oculis tuae maiestatis* be
*propitiabile* (`research/scope.md` § 3.8).

### 3.4 The four senses

- **Literal.** The king's hall is filled with guests good and bad, and the king
  comes in to see them. The psalmist asks that his evening prayer rise like
  incense and his lifted hands be accepted as the evening sacrifice; walking in
  the midst of tribulation, he trusts to be kept alive.
- **Allegorical.** The feast is the Church now, at the Lord's table and the
  Scriptures' banquet (Augustine §§ 1, 9; Gregory §§ 1, 7, 11, 14); the evening
  sacrifice is the Lord's Passion, the offering of a salutary Victim (Augustine
  on Ps 140; Bellarmine, as a possibility), and prayer is offered through Christ
  the high priest (Bellarmine); the guests who have received the mysteries are
  the ones addressed (Chrysostom).
- **Moral.** Examine the heart's garment before approaching the table; pray for
  enemies, not against them (Augustine § 9; Chrysostom on Ps 140:1); bear with the
  bad within the one Church (Gregory § 7; Augustine § 1); lift hands without anger
  (Chrysostom on Ps 140:1), and, in Hilary's sense, lift them in the works of
  mercy; ask for the grace that keeps the commandments (Augustine, Hilary).
- **Anagogical.** The other feast, to which the evil do not come (Augustine
  §§ 1, 4); the morning offering the evening sacrifice produced in the
  Resurrection (Augustine on Ps 140); the King's entry on the day of judgement
  (Gregory § 14); *the morning joy of the beatific vision* (Schuster). Hilary,
  from his different answer, gives this sense another form: the evening is
  *temporum novissimorum*, and to those who lift their hands in the works of
  mercy *jam ab initio mundi … regni coelestis praeparata possessio est*.

### 3.5 Strongest difficulty and alternative

**Augustine denies that the sacrament is the garment.** *Peradventure it is the
Altar, or That which is received at the Altar. But no* (Sermo 90 § 5). This
reading therefore must not say that receiving Communion clothes the guest; it
says, with Augustine, that the feast now includes the table and that the garment
is what the guest brings to it.

**No Father says in so many words that this Gospel's wedding feast is the
Eucharist.** Augustine's *Lord's Table*, Gregory's banquet of the Word and
Scriptures and Chrysostom's *mysteries* are the whole of the evidence
(`research/scope.md` § 4.5). The reading attributes to each exactly his words and
no more. Gregory's feast is, in his words, of the Word and the Scriptures; he does
not name the altar.

**What is the evening sacrifice? Augustine and Hilary answer differently.** The
reading's Gradual follows Augustine, who says the verse *is wont to be
understood of the Head Himself*: the evening sacrifice is the Passion, which
produced a morning offering in the Resurrection. Hilary, expounding the same
verse, distinguishes hands spread in prayer from hands lifted in the doing of
the work, reads *sine ira et contentione* as a prescription of divine work, and
asks which evening sacrifice pleases God: the answer is Christ's own in
Mt 25:34–40, the feeding, clothing, welcoming and visiting of the least; this
evening sacrifice *id est, temporum novissimorum est* (`research/scope.md`
§ 4.6). The two are not contradictory in doctrine, and a study may set them side
by side, but it must give each his own answer: the Passion is Augustine's
reading of the verse, and the works of mercy are Hilary's. Bellarmine, who offers
the Cross only as a possible figure, does not settle the question.

**The incensation is not heard at every Mass.** The Order of Mass's Ps 140:2–4
belongs to the incensation of a Mass celebrated with incense
(`research/scope.md` § 2.4). A study may say that the Gradual's verse returns in
the Order of Mass at a solemn Mass, not that this Sunday's assembly always hears
it twice. The *Placeat*'s *oculis tuae maiestatis*, by contrast, is said at every
Mass (§ 3.8).

**Alternative.** Hilary places the nuptials in the resurrection and the King's
entry in the assembly of the risen (§ 3, § 7). That anagogical reading is kept
in this reading's fourth sense and in the comparison; it is not the controlling
claim here.

### 3.6 Agreements and differences within the reading

Augustine and Gregory agree that the present feast is the Church with good and
bad; they differ in what they call its food — Augustine the Lord's Table and the
Scriptures, Gregory the Word and the Scriptures. Chrysostom agrees in addressing
communicants. Augustine (Enarr. in Ps. 140) and Chrysostom agree that Ps 140 is
the Church's prayer; Chrysostom, on its v. 1, makes praying without anger a
condition of it, as Augustine forbids praying for an enemy's death at the Table
(Sermo 90 § 9); Bellarmine cites 1 Tim 2:8 on v. 2 only for the gesture of
lifted hands. Augustine makes the evening sacrifice
the Passion, and Bellarmine offers the Cross as a possible figure; Chrysostom
alone calls the psalm a medicine; Hilary makes the evening sacrifice the works of
mercy of the last age, which is a different answer and is kept as his (§ 3.5).
Schuster reads the Secret and Postcommunion as Eucharistic; no Father comments on
those prayers.

## 4. Comparison

### 4.1 What the three share

One King who calls, by grace, and who judges; a Church that is mixed until he
enters; the Epistle's demand that the called live as the new man in one body;
the Communion's prayer that what is commanded may be kept, which Augustine,
Hilary and the Greek *Expositiones* each read as a prayer for grace. All three
read the same Gospel whole and let every element speak.

### 4.2 Where they differ

| | `wedding-garment` | `call-to-the-nations` | `feast-that-now-is` |
|---|---|---|---|
| Governing question | what the guest must wear | who is called and what refusal does | where the feast is now and how this assembly takes part |
| Weight of the Gospel | vv. 11–14 | vv. 1–10 | vv. 10–11, 14 |
| Leading psalm | the Communion (commandments) | the Introit verse and Alleluia (history, nations) | the Gradual and Offertory (prayer in tribulation, evening sacrifice) |
| What the nuptials are | the Church entered by faith, judged by charity | the union of Christ and a Church of Jews and Gentiles; its glory received in the resurrection (Hilary) | the present Church at the Lord's table and the Scriptures' banquet |
| The Gradual's lifted hands | works of mercy (Hilary), without anger (Chrysostom on v. 1) | the prayer of the last age (Hilary); the whole Christ's prayer, which goes on while tribulation remains for the Church, even to the end of the world (Augustine); a people still on the road [S] | the Passion's evening sacrifice (Augustine), or the works of mercy (Hilary) |
| Principal difficulty | gift or work (Hilary against Gregory and Augustine) | the burned city (historical against eschatological), and Gregory's moral reading of the highways | the sacrament is not the garment (Augustine); the evening sacrifice (Augustine against Hilary) |
| Carrying authors | Gregory, Augustine, Jerome | Chrysostom, Irenaeus, Hilary | Augustine, Gregory, Chrysostom |

### 4.3 How they relate

They are complementary, not rival doctrines, and they answer different
questions about the same texts; each keeps real disagreements within it. The
garment disagreement (`research/scope.md` § 4.1) runs through the first and the
third: the first develops it, the third turns it into the question put to every
communicant. The city disagreement (§ 4.2) and the question of the highways
(§ 4.3) belong to the second. The evening-sacrifice disagreement (§ 4.6) runs
through the first and the third: the first takes Hilary's works of mercy into
its moral sense, the third follows Augustine and keeps Hilary's answer beside
him. The second supplies the history in which the first and third take place:
the guests examined for the garment and the assembly at the table are the ones
gathered from the highways. The third is the one that reads the Mass's own
prayers most closely; the first reads the Epistle most closely; the second reads
the Introit verse and Alleluia most closely. Augustine and Hilary are witnesses
in all three, for different loci; Gregory in the first and third, and as the
alternative in the second; Chrysostom in all three.

### 4.4 Notes for the concise study's page-1 overview

Four rows that belong to no single reading and misstate none:

- **Literal:** Paul's new man and its four acts; the king's wedding refused,
  filled from the highways, and inspected; the psalmist's evening prayer and walk
  in tribulation.
- **Allegorical:** the Father's wedding for the Son and the Church of Jews and
  Gentiles; the garment Christ and charity; the evening sacrifice the Lord's
  Passion or the works of mercy of the last age.
- **Moral:** accept the call; put on the new man in charity, truth and work that
  gives; lift hands without anger and in works of mercy; ask God for what he
  commands.
- **Anagogical:** the King's entry at the judgement; the few chosen; the other
  feast to which no evil guest comes.

### 4.5 Notes for the homily

For an adult parish assembly the strongest single argument joins the first and
third readings: you have been called and you are at the feast; the King looks for
the garment, which is charity — the new man the Epistle describes in four plain
acts; and it is asked for, as the Communion asks, before it is worn. The second
reading supplies the story the homily can tell briefly (the invitation sent twice,
refused, carried to the highways). The homily may name Gregory (*Amice, et non
amice*; the hands that give nothing already bound), Augustine (the garment is not
the sacrament; examine the heart before approaching the Table) and Hilary (the
hands lifted in the evening sacrifice are hands that feed and visit Christ in the
least) in their own words. It must not say that the Fathers call the wedding
feast the Eucharist without the qualification in § 3.5, must not present the
highways as the nations in the name of every Father (§ 2.5), and must not use the
other Masses of `research/scope.md` § 2.
