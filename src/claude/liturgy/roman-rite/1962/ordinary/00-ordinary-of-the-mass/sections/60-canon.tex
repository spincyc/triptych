\section{The Canon of the Mass (nn.~1088--1112)}

\subsection*{What kind of text this is}

The 1962 missal treats the Canon differently from everything else it contains.
It gives it a separate title page, a separate running head, and a full-page
colour plate of the Crucifixion on the leaf facing its first words, so that a
priest who opens the book at \latincue{Te igitur} is looking at a crucifix
whether or not one stands on the altar. It sets the two consecration forms in
type larger than any other text in the volume. And it prescribes that the whole
of it be said inaudibly.

\begin{rubricnote}{Voice: \work{Rubricae generales Missalis romani} nn.~500, 511--512}
n.~500: \latincue{Post praefationem et Sanctus dicitur Canon Missae secreto, ut
in Ordine Missae.} n.~511 lists what is said in a clear voice at a read Mass;
between the \latincue{Sanctus} and \latincue{Per omnia saecula saeculorum} at
the end, the list contains exactly one item, \latincue{verba Nobis quoque
peccatoribus}, and adds \latincue{Cetera dicuntur secreto}. n.~512 defines what
``secretly'' means with unusual precision: the priest is to pronounce the
silent parts \latincue{ita \dots\ ut ipsemet se audiat, et a circumstantibus non
audiatur} --- so that he himself hears them, and the bystanders do not. The
Canon is therefore not unspoken; it is spoken to an audience of one.
\end{rubricnote}

This is the practice that the Council of Trent defended by name. Session XXII,
chapter V, states that the Church instituted certain rites, \latincue{ut
scilicet quaedam submissa voce, alia vero elatiore in missa pronunciarentur};
canon IX anathematises the assertion that the Roman rite, \latincue{quo
submissa voce pars canonis et verba consecrationis proferuntur}, is to be
condemned. Waterworth's English of the canon runs: ``If anyone saith that the
rite of the Roman Church, according to which a part of the canon and the words
of consecration are pronounced in a low tone, is to be condemned \dots\ let him
be anathema.'' Note what the council actually defends: not that silence is
better, but that the practice is not condemnable.

The same session made the strongest statement any council has made about this
text.

\begin{witnesscard}{Council of Trent, session XXII (17 September 1562), chapter IV, \latincue{De canone missae}}
\latincue{\dots\ digne reverenterque offerretur ac perciperetur, sacrum canonem
multis ante saeculis instituit, ita ab omni errore purum, ut nihil in eo
contineatur, quod non maxime sanctitatem ac pietatem quandam redoleat,
mentesque offerentium in Deum erigat. Is enim constat cum ex ipsis Domini
verbis, tum ex Apostolorum traditionibus, ac sanctorum quoque Pontificum piis
institutionibus.}\par
\vspace{0.3em}
Waterworth: ``the Catholic Church instituted, many years ago, the sacred canon,
so pure from every error, that nothing is contained therein which does not in
the highest degree savour of a certain holiness and piety, and raise up unto
God the minds of those that offer. For it is composed out of the very words of
the Lord, the traditions of the Apostles, and the pious institutions also of
holy pontiffs.'' Canon VI adds the anathema: \latincue{Si quis dixerit, canonem
missae errores continere, ideoque abrogandum esse: anathema sit.}\par
\vspace{0.3em}
Read exactly, the chapter says three things and not a fourth. It says the Canon
is free of error. It says it is composed of three kinds of material --- the
Lord's own words, apostolic traditions, and the pious institutions of holy
popes. And it dates its institution vaguely, \latincue{multis ante saeculis}.
It does not say that any part of it is of apostolic composition, and it does not
say that it has never been altered; the third of its three sources is by
definition later than the first two.
\end{witnesscard}

The historical claim usually attached to the Canon should be stated with the
same care. Fortescue reports ``a constant tradition that St Gregory was the
last to touch the essential part of the Mass, namely the Canon'', and quotes
Benedict XIV: ``No Pope has added to or changed the Canon since St.
Gregory.''\footnote{Fortescue, \work{The Mass}, p.~172, read at the page image;
the footnote there cites Benedict XIV, \work{De ss. Missae Sacrificio} II, xii,
12.} That tradition is worth keeping, and it is worth noticing that the missal
in front of us falsifies its strongest form. On 13 November 1962 the Sacred
Congregation of Rites, executing a decision John XXIII had announced to the
Council fathers that morning, decreed that after \latincue{Communicantes \dots\
Domini nostri Iesu Christi} the words \latincue{sed et beati Ioseph eiusdem
Virginis Sponsi} be added, with effect from 8 December.\footnote{\work{Acta
Apostolicae Sedis} 54 (1962), p.~873, decree \latincue{De S. Ioseph nomine
Canoni Missae inserendo}, read in the rendered text of the Vatican archive
scan.} The facsimile collated for this study prints those words. The 1861 hand
missal does not. The Canon of the Roman Mass was therefore altered within
living memory, by a pope, in the very edition this study describes --- and the
alteration is four words long.

\subsection*{\latincue{Te igitur} (n.~1088)}

\begin{appointed}{1088 $\cdot$ \latincue{Te igitur}}
Te igitur, clementissime Pater, per Iesum Christum, Filium tuum, Dominum
nostrum, supplices rogamus ac petimus, \upshape(osculatur altare et, iunctis
manibus ante pectus, dicit:)\itshape\ uti accepta habeas et benedicas
\upshape(signat ter super hostiam et calicem simul, dicens:)\itshape\ haec
\misscross\ dona, haec \misscross\ munera, haec \misscross\ sancta sacrificia
illibata, \upshape(extensis manibus prosequitur:)\itshape\ in primis, quae tibi
offerimus pro Ecclesia tua sancta catholica: quam pacificare, custodire,
adunare et regere digneris toto orbe terrarum: una cum famulo tuo Papa nostro
N. et Antistite nostro N. et omnibus orthodoxis, atque catholicae et apostolicae
fidei cultoribus.
\end{appointed}

\begin{englishwitness}{xxxiii}
``We therefore, humbly pray and beseech thee most merciful Father, thro' Jesus
Christ thy Son, our Lord, that thou wouldst vouchsafe to accept and bless these
gifts, these presents, these holy unspotted sacrifices, which in the first
place we offer thee for thy holy Catholic Church, to which vouchsafe to grant
peace; as also to preserve, unite, and govern it throughout the world: together
with thy servant N. our pope, N. our Bishop, as also all orthodox believers and
professors of the catholic and apostolic Faith.''
\end{englishwitness}

The prayer is a single Latin sentence and its architecture is worth setting out
before anything is said about its meaning. The main verb is
\latincue{rogamus ac petimus}, we ask and beseech; its subject is unexpressed
and its object is the whole of what follows. The first word of the Canon is not
the verb but its addressee, \latincue{Te}, thrown to the front for emphasis:
\emph{You} therefore, most merciful Father. Between the address and the verb
stands the mediation, \latincue{per Iesum Christum, Filium tuum, Dominum
nostrum}. The Roman Canon thus opens by putting three things in order --- to
whom, through whom, and in what posture --- before it says a word about what
is being asked.

\latincue{Igitur} is the hinge. It is an inferential particle: therefore,
accordingly. Grammatically it can only refer to what has just been said, which
is the preface and the \latincue{Sanctus}. The Canon presents itself as a
consequence.

Then the doubling begins. \latincue{Rogamus ac petimus} is one idea in two
verbs. \latincue{Accepta habeas et benedicas} is one idea in two verbs.
\latincue{Haec dona, haec munera, haec sancta sacrificia illibata} is one thing
in three phrases, each preceded by a sign of the cross. \latincue{Pacificare,
custodire, adunare et regere} is four infinitives where one would carry the
sense. This is the characteristic manner of the Roman Canon and it is not
decoration: it is the style of Roman legal and euchological Latin, in which
what is being asked is specified by piling up near-synonyms so that no
loophole remains. The 1861 translator, reaching for equivalents, produced
``these gifts, these presents, these holy unspotted sacrifices'', which is the
same manoeuvre in English.

The three signs of the cross over the gifts at \latincue{dona}, \latincue{munera}
and \latincue{sacrificia} are the first of many in this prayer. They are made
over what is still bread and wine, and they accompany words asking that it be
accepted and blessed; nothing yet asserts that it is more than bread.

The intercession that follows is ordered outward from the Church as such
(\latincue{pro Ecclesia tua sancta catholica}) to the pope by name, the local
ordinary by name, and then \latincue{omnibus orthodoxis, atque catholicae et
apostolicae fidei cultoribus} --- all the orthodox and cultivators of the
catholic and apostolic faith. \latincue{Cultor} is a farmer's word; the phrase
means those who till that faith, not merely those who hold it.

\subsection*{\latincue{Memento} of the living (n.~1089)}

\begin{appointed}{1089 $\cdot$ \latincue{Commemoratio pro vivis}}
Memento, Domine, famulorum famularumque tuarum N. et N. \upshape(iungit manus,
orat aliquantulum pro quibus orare intendit: deinde manibus extensis
prosequitur:)\itshape\ et omnium circumstantium, quorum tibi fides cognita est
et nota devotio, pro quibus tibi offerimus: vel qui tibi offerunt hoc
sacrificium laudis, pro se suisque omnibus: pro redemptione animarum suarum,
pro spe salutis et incolumitatis suae: tibique reddunt vota sua aeterno Deo,
vivo et vero.
\end{appointed}

The rubric prints a pause inside the prayer: the priest joins his hands and
prays a little while for those he intends. It is the one moment in the Canon
where the book deliberately leaves a silence with no words in it at all.

The Latin then does something that has occupied commentators for centuries.
Having said \latincue{pro quibus tibi offerimus} --- for whom we offer to you
--- it adds \latincue{vel qui tibi offerunt hoc sacrificium laudis}, or who
offer to you this sacrifice of praise. The subject changes: first the ministers
offer for the people, then the people offer. Whatever weight one puts on
\latincue{vel} (it can mean ``or'' or ``and also''), the sentence as printed
will not let the offering be attributed exclusively to either party --- exactly
as \latincue{meum ac vestrum sacrificium} would not, an hour earlier in the same
Mass.

The ends named are three and they descend from eternity into the body:
\latincue{pro redemptione animarum suarum}, for the redemption of their souls;
\latincue{pro spe salutis}, for the hope of salvation; \latincue{et
incolumitatis suae}, and of their safety, \latincue{incolumitas} being bodily
soundness. The Roman Canon asks for health in the same breath as redemption and
does not apologise for it.

\subsection*{\latincue{Communicantes} (n.~1090)}

\begin{appointed}{1090 $\cdot$ \latincue{Infra Actionem}}
Communicantes, et memoriam venerantes, in primis gloriosae semper Virginis
Mariae, Genetricis Dei et Domini nostri Iesu Christi: sed et beati Ioseph,
eiusdem Virginis Sponsi, et beatorum Apostolorum ac Martyrum tuorum, Petri et
Pauli, Andreae, Iacobi, Ioannis, Thomae, Iacobi, Philippi, Bartholomaei,
Matthaei, Simonis et Thaddaei: Lini, Cleti, Clementis, Xysti, Cornelii,
Cypriani, Laurentii, Chrysogoni, Ioannis et Pauli, Cosmae et Damiani: et omnium
Sanctorum tuorum; quorum meritis precibusque concedas, ut in omnibus
protectionis tuae muniamur auxilio. \upshape(Iungit manus.)\itshape\ Per eundem
Christum Dominum nostrum. Amen.
\end{appointed}

The heading the missal prints over this prayer is \latincue{Infra Actionem},
``within the Action'', and it is the technical name for the part of the Canon
that varies. Five proper forms are printed at nn.~1093--1097, for Christmas and
its octave, the Epiphany, the Mass of the Easter Vigil to Low Sunday, the
Ascension, and the Vigil of Pentecost to the following Saturday. Each inserts a
clause naming the day --- \latincue{et diem sacratissimum celebrantes, quo
\dots} --- and then rejoins the common text at a shared continuation printed at
p.~305. General rubric n.~501 adds that within the octaves of Christmas, Easter
and Pentecost the proper \latincue{Communicantes} and \latincue{Hanc igitur}
are said even in Masses that are not of the octave.

The list of saints has a shape. Our Lady first, with the title
\latincue{Genetricis Dei et Domini nostri Iesu Christi} --- Mother of God, the
Ephesine title, in the classicised spelling \latincue{Genetricis} that the
twentieth-century Vatican editions restored. Then Joseph. Then twelve Apostles
--- Peter, Paul, Andrew, James, John, Thomas, James, Philip, Bartholomew,
Matthew, Simon and Thaddaeus, a list that puts Paul in and leaves Matthias out.
Then twelve martyrs: five popes (Linus, Cletus, Clement, Xystus, Cornelius),
one bishop (Cyprian), a deacon (Lawrence), then Chrysogonus standing alone, and
finally two pairs, John and Paul, Cosmas and Damian. The whole is a Roman list:
these are the martyrs whose basilicas and cemeteries stood in and around the
city.

\begin{proposal}{Collation: the four words added in 1962}
The insertion of \latincue{sed et beati Ioseph, eiusdem Virginis Sponsi} is the
one place where the two witnesses collated for this study disagree about
substance rather than spelling. The 1861 Philadelphia hand missal, printed
before the change, passes directly from \latincue{Domini nostri Jesu Christi} to
\latincue{sed et beatorum Apostolorum ac Martyrum tuorum}, and its English
reads: ``communicating with, and honouring the memory in the first place of the
glorious Virgin Mary, Mother of our Lord and God Jesus Christ; as also of thy
blessed Apostles and Martyrs \dots'' The facsimile of the 1962 typical edition
prints the clause, in the common form and again in the shared continuation of
the five proper forms. Since the volume's own promulgating decree is dated 23
June 1962 and the insertion took effect on 8 December of the same year, the
printing collated here embodies the Missal as amended by the November decree; a
copy of the same typical edition impressed before that date would not carry the
clause. This is a collation result, not an inference: both readings were read
on identified page images.
\end{proposal}

The petition attached to the list --- \latincue{quorum meritis precibusque
concedas, ut in omnibus protectionis tuae muniamur auxilio} --- distinguishes
carefully between the saints' merits and their prayers, and asks for
\latincue{auxilium protectionis}, the help of protection, not for their
intercession as such. The conclusion is \latincue{Per eundem Christum Dominum
nostrum}, with \latincue{eundem} because Christ has been named inside the
prayer. That small grammatical scruple recurs throughout the Canon and is one of
the surest signs that its clauses were composed as continuous Latin rather than
assembled from independent pieces.

\subsection*{\latincue{Hanc igitur} and \latincue{Quam oblationem} (nn.~1091--1092)}

\begin{appointed}{1091--1092}
\upshape(Tenens manus expansas super oblata, dicit:)\itshape\par
Hanc igitur oblationem servitutis nostrae, sed et cunctae familiae tuae,
quaesumus, Domine, ut placatus accipias: diesque nostros in tua pace disponas,
atque ab aeterna damnatione nos eripi, et in electorum tuorum iubeas grege
numerari. \upshape(Iungit manus.)\itshape\ Per Christum Dominum nostrum.
Amen.\par
\vspace{0.4em}
Quam oblationem tu, Deus, in omnibus, quaesumus, \upshape(signat ter super
oblata,)\itshape\ bene\misscross dictam, adscrip\misscross tam, ra\misscross
tam, rationabilem, acceptabilemque facere digneris: \upshape(signat semel super
hostiam,)\itshape\ ut nobis Cor\misscross pus, \upshape(et semel super
calicem,)\itshape\ et San\misscross guis fiat dilectissimi Filii tui,
\upshape(iungit manus,)\itshape\ Domini nostri Iesu Christi.
\end{appointed}

The two prayers are printed as separate paragraphs but they are one sentence in
two parts: \latincue{hanc \dots\ oblationem} is picked up by \latincue{Quam
oblationem}, a relative pronoun agreeing with it. Nothing may be inserted
between them, and the book's own layout enforces the point. In the pages of
proper forms it reprints the ordinary \latincue{Hanc igitur} at n.~1098 and
prints the one proper form at n.~1099 --- for the Mass of the Easter Vigil to
Low Saturday and from the Vigil of Pentecost to the following Saturday, which
inserts a clause for the newly baptised --- and then reprints \latincue{Quam
oblationem} entire at n.~1100, so that whichever \latincue{Hanc igitur} is used
the next words on the page are still \latincue{Quam oblationem}.

The gesture over \latincue{Hanc igitur} --- the priest holds his hands
outspread over the offerings --- is the only such gesture in the Roman Mass.
Fortescue records that it is late, first appearing in the fifteenth century,
that the fourteenth Roman \work{Ordo} does not know it, and that in Durandus's
time a profound inclination was made instead, a custom the Dominicans and
Carmelites kept. He is equally clear that the second half of the prayer is old:
there is a tradition, which he traces through Gregory's biographer, that Gregory
the Great added \latincue{diesque nostros in tua pace disponas}.

That is a claim worth weighing rather than repeating. If it is true, the seam is
visible in the Latin: the first half asks that the offering be accepted, and the
second asks for three things about us --- our days ordered in peace, rescue
from eternal damnation, enrolment in the flock of the elect --- which have
nothing to do with the offering as such. The prayer changes subject in
mid-sentence. A reader may find that seam persuasive without having verified
the attribution, and this study does not treat the attribution as established.

\latincue{Quam oblationem} is the most technically loaded sentence in the
Canon, and it is the one for which a fourth-century witness survives.

\begin{witnesscard}{Ambrose (or the author of \work{De sacramentis}) IV, 5, 21}
\latincue{Vis scire quia verbis coelestibus consecratur? Accipe quae sunt verba.
Dicit sacerdos: Fac nobis, inquit, hanc oblationem ascriptam, ratam,
rationabilem, acceptabilem: quod figura est corporis et sanguinis Domini nostri
Jesu Christi.}\par
\vspace{0.3em}
Read at Migne, \work{Patrologia Latina} 16 (Paris, 1880), coll.~462--463. Four
of the Roman Canon's adjectives stand there in the same order ---
\latincue{ascriptam, ratam, rationabilem, acceptabilem} --- in a Milanese
homily of the late fourth century. The Maurist note printed on the same column
records that \latincue{ratam} was absent from all earlier printed editions and
from some manuscripts and is restored from several others, so even this
agreement is a text-critical result rather than a bare fact.
\end{witnesscard}

The 1962 Canon has five adjectives where \work{De sacramentis} has four: it
prefixes \latincue{benedictam}. Each of the first three carries a sign of the
cross. Their sense is bureaucratic before it is mystical: \latincue{adscriptam}
is ``entered in the register''; \latincue{ratam} is ``ratified'', a legal term
for an act confirmed; \latincue{rationabilem} answers the \latincue{logike
latreia} of Romans 12, 1, a reasonable service; \latincue{acceptabilem} is
simply acceptable. What is being asked is that this offering be admitted,
recorded, ratified and accepted --- and then, in the final clause, that it
\emph{become} something: \latincue{ut nobis Corpus et Sanguis fiat dilectissimi
Filii tui}. The verb is \latincue{fiat}, and the two nouns each take a sign of
the cross, one over the host and one over the chalice.

That is the closest thing in the Roman Canon to what Eastern anaphoras do with
an explicit invocation of the Holy Spirit. It asks for the change; it does not
name the agent of the change; and it is placed before the institution narrative
rather than after it. A study of this kind can record that arrangement and its
consequences without adjudicating the long theological argument about the
Roman Canon's want of a separate epiclesis.

\subsection*{The institution narrative (nn.~1101--1105)}

\begin{appointed}{1101--1102 $\cdot$ \latincue{Qui pridie}}
Qui pridie quam pateretur, \upshape(accipit hostiam,)\itshape\ accepit panem in
sanctas ac venerabiles manus suas, \upshape(elevat oculos ad caelum,)\itshape\
et elevatis oculis in caelum ad te Deum Patrem suum omnipotentem,
\upshape(caput inclinat,)\itshape\ tibi gratias agens, \upshape(signat super
hostiam,)\itshape\ bene\misscross dixit, fregit, deditque discipulis suis,
dicens: Accipite, et manducate ex hoc omnes.\par
\vspace{0.4em}
\upshape\centering\normalsize\bfseries Hoc est enim Corpus meum.
\end{appointed}

\begin{appointed}{1103--1105 $\cdot$ \latincue{Simili modo}}
Simili modo postquam cenatum est, \upshape(ambabus manibus accipit
calicem,)\itshape\ accipiens et hunc praeclarum calicem in sanctas ac
venerabiles manus suas: \upshape(item caput inclinat,)\itshape\ tibi gratias
agens, \upshape(sinistra tenens calicem, dextera signat super eum,)\itshape\
bene\misscross dixit, deditque discipulis suis, dicens: Accipite, et bibite ex
eo omnes.\par
\vspace{0.4em}
\upshape\centering\normalsize\bfseries Hic est enim Calix Sanguinis mei,\\
novi et aeterni testamenti:\\
mysterium fidei:\\
qui pro vobis et pro multis effundetur\\
in remissionem peccatorum.\par
\vspace{0.4em}
\upshape\itshape\raggedright(Quibus verbis prolatis, deponit calicem super
corporale, et dicens:)\par
\vspace{0.3em}
\upshape\centering\normalsize\bfseries Haec quotiescumque feceritis,\\
in mei memoriam facietis.
\end{appointed}

\begin{englishwitness}{xxxvi}
The 1861 book sets the two forms in small capitals in both columns. For the
first it prints ``TAKE AND EAT YE ALL OF THIS. FOR THIS IS MY BODY.'' The words
themselves are the Church's, not the translator's; this is quoted here as a
historical witness to how an English lay book of that date presented them, not
as a version for use.
\end{englishwitness}

Four features of this narrative should be recorded exactly.

\emph{It is a relative clause.} \latincue{Qui pridie quam pateretur} continues
the sentence begun at \latincue{Quam oblationem \dots\ Domini nostri Iesu
Christi}: the \latincue{Qui} is Christ, just named. The Roman Canon never
introduces the institution as a narrative in its own right. Grammatically, the
Last Supper is a subordinate clause inside a petition.

\emph{It has been adjusted to the rite.} The narrative says
\latincue{fregit}, he broke, but the priest does not break the host here; the
fraction happens twenty minutes later, after the Lord's Prayer. It says
\latincue{deditque discipulis suis}, and he gives to no one. What the priest
actually does at each verb is printed in the rubric: he takes the host, raises
his eyes, bows his head, signs it. The gestures follow the narrative up to
\latincue{benedixit} and then stop. The rite reports Christ's actions and
performs only those it can perform now.

\emph{The forms are not simply Scripture.} No Gospel or Pauline account has
\latincue{Hoc est enim Corpus meum} with \latincue{enim}, and none has the
chalice form as it stands here. Matthew 26, 26--28 in the Douay-Rheims reads
``Take ye and eat. This is my body \dots\ For this is my blood of the new
testament, which shall be shed for many unto remission of sins''; 1 Corinthians
11, 25 has ``This chalice is the new testament in my blood''. The Canon's
\latincue{Hic est enim Calix Sanguinis mei, novi et aeterni testamenti} fuses
the two constructions, adds \latincue{aeterni}, and inserts
\latincue{mysterium fidei}, which stands in no scriptural account of the
Supper. Trent's chapter IV had described the Canon as composed
\latincue{cum ex ipsis Domini verbis, tum ex Apostolorum traditionibus, ac
sanctorum quoque Pontificum piis institutionibus}; the consecration form is
where all three are visibly present in one sentence.

\emph{The oldest Latin witness differs.} \work{De sacramentis} IV, 5, 21--22
gives a form recognisably the same and verbally different:
\latincue{Accipite, et edite ex hoc omnes; hoc est enim corpus meum, quod pro
multis confringetur} --- ``which shall be broken for many''. The Maurist editors
note that where all editions and manuscripts read \latincue{pro multis
confringetur}, the Roman edition alone corrected it to \latincue{pro vobis
datur}, and that \latincue{et discipulis suis} is omitted in that same Roman
edition. The Milanese homilist of about 390 already had \latincue{Qui pridie
quam pateretur \dots\ accepit panem \dots\ respexit in coelum \dots\ gratias
agens, benedixit, fregit}. What he did not have was the Roman Canon: he had a
prayer with the same bones.

\begin{proposal}{Editorial reading: the typography as argument}
The 1962 book gives the two forms a treatment it gives nothing else: larger
type, centred, no interleaved rubric, each followed by a rubric describing
adoration, elevation and adoration again. Everywhere else in the Canon the
missal breaks the prayer with directions; here it refuses to. The layout is
therefore doing what a commentary would do --- marking the sentences at which
the action is accomplished --- and doing it without a word of theology. That is
an observation about this printing. Whether the same is true of every 1962
impression is a question about printings, not about the rite, and this study
has collated one.
\end{proposal}

\subsection*{\latincue{Unde et memores}, \latincue{Supra quae}, \latincue{Supplices} (nn.~1106--1108)}

\begin{appointed}{1106 $\cdot$ \latincue{Unde et memores}}
Unde et memores, Domine, nos servi tui, sed et plebs tua sancta, eiusdem Christi
Filii tui, Domini nostri, tam beatae passionis, nec non et ab inferis
resurrectionis, sed et in caelos gloriosae ascensionis: offerimus praeclarae
maiestati tuae de tuis donis ac datis \upshape(iungit manus, et signat ter super
hostiam et calicem simul, dicens:)\itshape\ hostiam \misscross\ puram, hostiam
\misscross\ sanctam, hostiam \misscross\ immaculatam, \upshape(signat semel
super hostiam, dicens:)\itshape\ Panem \misscross\ sanctum vitae aeternae,
\upshape(et semel super calicem, dicens:)\itshape\ et Calicem \misscross\
salutis perpetuae.
\end{appointed}

This is the pivot of the Canon, and its Latin is arranged to make the pivot
audible. \latincue{Unde} --- whence, and therefore. \latincue{Memores}, an
adjective with no verb of its own, governing three genitives: of the passion, of
the resurrection from the dead, of the glorious ascension into heaven. Only
after all three does the main verb arrive: \latincue{offerimus}. And the
subject is doubled once more --- \latincue{nos servi tui, sed et plebs tua
sancta}, we your servants, but also your holy people.

Then \latincue{de tuis donis ac datis}: from your own gifts and grants. The
Church offers what it has been given. And the thing offered is named in five
phrases with five crosses --- a pure victim, a holy victim, a spotless victim,
the holy Bread of eternal life, and the Chalice of everlasting salvation. The
same tripling that opened the Canon at \latincue{haec dona, haec munera, haec
sancta sacrificia illibata} closes the offering here, but the nouns have
changed: what was \latincue{dona} and \latincue{munera} before the consecration
is \latincue{Panem sanctum vitae aeternae} after it.

\begin{witnesscard}{The same prayer at Milan, c.~390}
\work{De sacramentis} IV, 6, 27 (\work{PL} 16, col.~464): \latincue{Ergo memores
gloriosissimae ejus passionis, et ab inferis resurrectionis, et in coelum
ascensionis, offerimus tibi hanc immaculatam hostiam, rationabilem hostiam,
incruentam hostiam, hunc panem sanctum, et calicem vitae aeternae: et petimus et
precamur, ut hanc oblationem suscipias in sublimi altari tuo per manus
angelorum tuorum, sicut suscipere dignatus es munera pueri tui justi Abel, et
sacrificium patriarchae nostri Abrahae, et quod tibi obtulit summus sacerdos
Melchisedech.}\par
\vspace{0.3em}
Read at the page image. The correspondence is close enough to be structural and
loose enough to forbid identification: \latincue{memores} with the same three
genitives, \latincue{offerimus} with three adjectival phrases, \latincue{panem
sanctum et calicem}, then the petition that the offering be taken up to the
sublime altar by angelic hands, then Abel, Abraham and Melchisedech in that
order. But the Milanese text has \latincue{incruentam} where Rome has
\latincue{puram} and \latincue{sanctam}, has \latincue{per manus angelorum
tuorum} plural where Rome has \latincue{per manus sancti Angeli tui} singular,
and runs \latincue{Supra quae} and \latincue{Supplices} together as one
petition. It establishes the antiquity of a pattern; it does not establish the
antiquity of the Roman wording.
\end{witnesscard}

\begin{appointed}{1107--1108 $\cdot$ \latincue{Supra quae} and \latincue{Supplices}}
Supra quae propitio ac sereno vultu respicere digneris: et accepta habere,
sicuti accepta habere dignatus es munera pueri tui iusti Abel, et sacrificium
Patriarchae nostri Abrahae: et quod tibi obtulit summus sacerdos tuus
Melchisedech, sanctum sacrificium, immaculatam hostiam.\par
\vspace{0.4em}
\upshape(Profunde inclinatus, iunctis manibus et super altare positis,
dicit:)\itshape\par
Supplices te rogamus, omnipotens Deus: iube haec perferri per manus sancti
Angeli tui in sublime altare tuum, in conspectu divinae maiestatis tuae: ut,
quotquot \upshape(osculatur altare)\itshape\ ex hac altaris participatione
sacrosanctum Filii tui \upshape(iungit manus, et signat semel super hostiam, et
semel super calicem,)\itshape\ Cor\misscross pus et San\misscross guinem
sumpserimus, \upshape(seipsum signat, dicens:)\itshape\ omni benedictione
caelesti et gratia repleamur. \upshape(Iungit manus.)\itshape\ Per eundem
Christum Dominum nostrum. Amen.
\end{appointed}

\latincue{Supra quae} asks God to look on what has just been offered
\latincue{propitio ac sereno vultu} --- with a favourable and serene
countenance --- and to hold it accepted as he held accepted three earlier
offerings. The three are chosen with care. Abel is called \latincue{puer tuus
iustus}, your just servant, and what he offered were \latincue{munera}, gifts;
Genesis 4, 4 says only that he offered of the firstlings of his flock and that
the Lord had respect to him and to his offerings. Abraham is
\latincue{Patriarcha noster}, our patriarch, and what he offered is a
\latincue{sacrificium}. Melchisedech alone is called \latincue{summus sacerdos
tuus}, your high priest, and what he offered is named twice over,
\latincue{sanctum sacrificium, immaculatam hostiam}; Genesis 14, 18 says that
he brought forth bread and wine, for he was the priest of the most high God.
The ascent is deliberate: from a shepherd's gift, to a patriarch's sacrifice, to
a priest's offering of bread and wine.

The strangeness of \latincue{Supplices} is worth stating plainly, because
smoothing it over would misrepresent the prayer. It asks that \latincue{haec}
--- these things, the consecrated gifts --- be carried up by the hands of God's
holy angel to an altar on high, in the sight of the divine majesty, so that all
who receive Christ's Body and Blood from participation in \emph{this} altar may
be filled with heavenly blessing and grace. The movement is upward and the
purpose is downward. The Canon does not explain who \latincue{sanctus Angelus
tuus} is, and the tradition has never agreed; the Milanese parallel has angels
in the plural, which is one datum and not a solution. What the Latin does say is
that the two altars are related by a single participation: the sentence begins
at the altar in heaven and ends at the altar the priest is at that moment
kissing.

\subsection*{The dead, and the sinners (nn.~1109--1110)}

\begin{appointed}{1109 $\cdot$ \latincue{Commemoratio pro defunctis}}
Memento etiam, Domine, famulorum famularumque tuarum N. et N., qui nos
praecesserunt cum signo fidei, et dormiunt in somno pacis.\par
\upshape(Iungit manus, orat aliquantulum pro iis defunctis, pro quibus orare
intendit, deinde extensis manibus prosequitur:)\itshape\par
Ipsis, Domine, et omnibus in Christo quiescentibus, locum refrigerii, lucis et
pacis, ut indulgeas, deprecamur. \upshape(Iungit manus, et caput inclinat,
dicens:)\itshape\ Per eundem Christum Dominum nostrum. Amen.
\end{appointed}

The phrase \latincue{cum signo fidei} means those who went before us marked with
the sign of faith, that is, the baptised; \latincue{dormiunt in somno pacis} is
the language of the Roman catacombs. What is asked for them is
\latincue{locus refrigerii, lucis et pacis} --- a place of refreshment, of
light and of peace --- three nouns in the same manner as the Canon's other
triples. \latincue{Refrigerium} is another word from the cemeteries: it means a
cooling, and it had a fixed sense in Roman Christian usage for the condition of
the faithful departed.

\begin{appointed}{1110 $\cdot$ \latincue{Nobis quoque peccatoribus}}
\upshape(Manu dextera percutit sibi pectus, elata aliquantulum voce
dicens:)\itshape\par
Nobis quoque peccatoribus \upshape(extensis manibus ut prius, secrete
prosequitur:)\itshape\ famulis tuis, de multitudine miserationum tuarum
sperantibus, partem aliquam et societatem donare digneris, cum tuis sanctis
Apostolis et Martyribus: cum Ioanne, Stephano, Matthia, Barnaba, Ignatio,
Alexandro, Marcellino, Petro, Felicitate, Perpetua, Agatha, Lucia, Agnete,
Caecilia, Anastasia, et omnibus Sanctis tuis: intra quorum nos consortium, non
aestimator meriti, sed veniae, quaesumus, largitor admitte. \upshape(Iungit
manus.)\itshape\ Per Christum Dominum nostrum.
\end{appointed}

These are the only words of the Canon a congregation is meant to hear.
\work{Rubricae generales} n.~511 lists \latincue{verba Nobis quoque
peccatoribus} among the things said in a clear voice, and the rubric printed
with the prayer says the priest strikes his breast with his right hand and
raises his voice somewhat --- and then continues secretly. Three words are
audible: \emph{we too, sinners}. The rite chose, out of the whole Canon, the
admission of sin as the one thing the people should hear from it.

The list here is the counterpart of the one in \latincue{Communicantes}, and it
is arranged as its mirror. There, twenty-six names: Our Lady and Joseph, then
twelve Apostles and twelve martyrs, in a list of intercessors. Here, fifteen
names --- eight men and seven women: John, Stephen, Matthias, Barnabas,
Ignatius, Alexander, Marcellinus, Peter; Felicitas, Perpetua, Agatha, Lucy,
Agnes, Cecilia, Anastasia --- in a list of company hoped for. Matthias, absent
from the earlier list, is present in this one. And the request is not for their
prayers but for \latincue{partem aliquam et societatem}, some part and
fellowship, with them.

The last clause is the finest sentence in the Canon and it depends entirely on
word order: \latincue{intra quorum nos consortium, non aestimator meriti, sed
veniae, quaesumus, largitor admitte}. Admit us into their fellowship --- not as
an assessor of merit, but as a bestower of pardon. \latincue{Aestimator} is a
valuer, one who prices a thing; \latincue{largitor} is a giver on a lavish
scale. The two nouns are set against each other across the caesura, and the
verb \latincue{admitte} is held back to the very end. The Roman Canon, having
named forty-one saints in its two lists, asks not to be judged by comparison
with them.

\subsection*{The doxology (nn.~1111--1112)}

\begin{appointed}{1111--1112 $\cdot$ \latincue{Per quem} and \latincue{Per ipsum}}
Per quem haec omnia, Domine, semper bona creas, \upshape(signat ter super
hostiam et calicem simul, dicens:)\itshape\ sancti\misscross ficas,
vivi\misscross ficas, bene\misscross dicis et praestas nobis.\par
\vspace{0.4em}
\upshape(Discooperit calicem, genuflectit, accipit hostiam \dots\ cum hostia
signat ter a labio ad labium calicis, dicens:)\itshape\par
Per ip\misscross sum, et cum ip\misscross so, et in ip\misscross so,
\upshape(cum ipsa hostia signat bis inter se et calicem, dicens:)\itshape\ est
tibi Deo Patri \misscross\ omnipotenti, in unitate Spiritus \misscross\ Sancti,
\upshape(elevans parum calicem cum hostia, dicit:)\itshape\ omnis honor, et
gloria.
\end{appointed}

\latincue{Per quem haec omnia \dots\ semper bona creas} has puzzled readers
since the Middle Ages, because \latincue{haec omnia} after the consecration
cannot be the offerings in the sense the verbs \latincue{creas} and
\latincue{sanctificas} would require. The historical explanation usually given
is that this clause is the surviving head of a blessing of other produce ---
oil, grapes, beans --- which was once inserted at this point and of which the
1962 book still preserves an instance in the blessing of holy oils on Maundy
Thursday. That explanation is a reconstruction; what is not in doubt is that
the verbs form the Canon's last quadruple: he creates, sanctifies, gives life
to, blesses and bestows.

\latincue{Per ipsum} is grammatically the simplest sentence in the Canon and
ritually the most elaborate. Its skeleton is: \latincue{omnis honor et gloria
est tibi Deo Patri omnipotenti} --- all honour and glory is to you, God the
Father almighty. Into that frame are set three prepositional phrases with the
same pronoun --- through him, and with him, and in him --- and one qualifier,
\latincue{in unitate Spiritus Sancti}. The whole Canon has been addressed to the
Father through the Son; the doxology says so in five words and then adds the
Spirit.

The rubric turns those words into a diagram. Holding the Host, the priest makes
three signs of the cross from lip to lip of the chalice at \latincue{Per ipsum,
et cum ipso, et in ipso}; two more between the chalice and himself at
\latincue{Deo Patri omnipotenti} and \latincue{in unitate Spiritus Sancti}; and
then raises Host and chalice slightly together at \latincue{omnis honor, et
gloria}. Five crosses and one elevation, made with the consecrated Body over
the consecrated Blood. The Canon that began with three signs of the cross over
bread and wine ends with five made by the Body itself.

\subsection*{The shape of the whole}

Read as one text, the Roman Canon has a symmetry that its silence conceals. It
opens with an intercession (Church, pope, bishop; the living; the saints) and
closes with an intercession (the dead; ourselves; the saints again). Between
those wings stand a request that the offering be made acceptable, the
institution narrative, the memorial offering, an appeal to three ancient
sacrifices, and a request that the offering be carried to the altar on high.
The consecration is not at the centre by accident: it has two prayers of
approach on one side and two prayers of ascent on the other.

Its style is uniform throughout: doubled verbs, tripled nouns, adjectives in
threes and fives, each series usually marked by a sign of the cross. Its
address never changes; every conclusion is \latincue{Per Christum Dominum
nostrum} or, where Christ has just been named, \latincue{Per eundem}. And its
persons are consistently plural and consistently divided: \latincue{nos servi
tui, sed et plebs tua sancta}; \latincue{pro quibus tibi offerimus: vel qui
tibi offerunt}. Whatever else the Roman Canon is, it is a text that will not
let one party in the Church offer without the other.
