# Kyrie, Preface, and Creed: second-pass evidence

Checked 2026-07-27. This note supplements the edition manifest, source audit,
and existing passage records. It distinguishes direct witnesses from critical
reconstruction. It does not authorize wording beyond what each witness proves.

## Kyrie

| Claim | Evidence | Evidential limit |
|---|---|---|
| The 1962 Ordinary has nine invocations. | *Missale Romanum* (1962), General Rubrics n. 430: `Kyrie, eleison dicitur novies`. | Edition fact only; it does not date the ninefold form. |
| Late-sixth-century Rome distinguished its practice from Greek practice. | Gregory the Great, *Registrum epistolarum* IX.12, to John of Syracuse: Greek congregations said the Kyrie together, whereas Roman clerics said it and the people responded; Rome also said `Christe eleison`. | Direct witness to Gregory's contemporary explanation, not proof that Gregory introduced the acclamation. |
| A longer-litany ancestry and Gregory's precise role remain reconstructions. | Fortescue, *The Mass* (1922), pp. 232, 234--235; repository passage records `p-232.toml` and `p-234-235.toml`. | Attribute the reconstruction to Fortescue. Do not turn it into an uncontested event or repeat John the Deacon's introduction claim as fact. |

Drafting consequence: Gregory safely supports a concise account of Roman
call-and-response and the presence of `Christe eleison`. The ninefold count
must be grounded in the 1962 rubric. Claims about origin in a displaced litany
need named scholarly attribution.

## Preface dialogue and frequency

| Claim | Evidence | Evidential limit |
|---|---|---|
| `Sursum corda` / `Habemus ad Dominum` was already a congregational dialogue in third-century North Africa. | Cyprian, *De dominica oratione* 31: the priest prepares the brethren before prayer by saying `Sursum corda`, and the people answer `Habemus ad Dominum`. The inspected text is the Migne 1844 edition reproduced by Latin Wikisource. | This is Carthaginian evidence. It does not by itself establish the entire four-part Roman dialogue or the date of its adoption at Rome. |
| The 1962 Missal supplies sixteen prefaces. | Direct enumeration in the 1962 typical edition and General Rubrics nn. 487--499; already recorded in the source audit and bindings. | Inventory count, not empirical frequency of use. |
| Earlier Roman books preserve much larger preface inventories. | Modern critical literature commonly reports 267 for the Veronense, 54 for the Old Gelasian, and 14 for the Hadrianum. | These counts remain secondary leads until checked against the named critical editions. Do not cite them as manuscript counts from this review. |

Drafting consequence: remove or source the claim that `Dignum et iustum est`
was borrowed from Roman public life. Avoid saying which preface Catholics
"heard most often" unless a calendar-weighted or use-frequency study is
provided. Rubrical availability, inventory size, and observed frequency are
three different propositions.

## Creed: textual and conciliar layers

| Layer | Evidence | Safe conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Nicaea, 325 | Eusebius of Caesarea's letter preserves the conciliar creed: its Holy Spirit article is only `[We believe] also in the Holy Spirit`, followed by an anathema. | The Mass text is not a verbatim transcription of the 325 creed. |
| Constantinopolitan recension | The Council of Chalcedon (451) receives both the creed of the 318 fathers at Nicaea and that of the 150 fathers at Constantinople, and explains the latter's fuller teaching on the Holy Spirit. | The longer recension is securely witnessed in conciliar reception at Chalcedon. Avoid claiming that extant acts of 381 themselves preserve the complete text without a critical source establishing that point. |
| Toledo III, 589 | Canon 2 is quoted by Benedict XIV, *Allatae sunt* 28, as requiring the Constantinopolitan creed to be recited in Spain and Galicia before the Lord's Prayer. Current conciliar scholarship flags a textual question over whether `ex Patre et Filio procedentem` belongs to Toledo III's creed text. | Toledo safely witnesses liturgical recitation in the Visigothic churches. Do not flatten the disputed textual transmission into “Toledo inserted the *Filioque*” without a critical edition and qualification. |
| Rome, 1014 | Berno's report, as assessed by Fortescue, *The Mass*, p. 288; repository passage record `p-288.toml`. Fortescue notes dissent from the common dating. | Say “Berno reports” and “probably 1014,” not that a secure contemporary Roman decree survives. |
| 1962 Roman form | Direct 1962 Missal control text includes `Filioque`. | State this as the received Latin liturgical recension, distinct from the textual history of the ecumenical creeds. |

Drafting consequence: present the creed as a received liturgical recension
with identifiable conciliar and Western textual layers, not as a single text
composed in full by one council. Keep the theological legitimacy of the
`Filioque`, its textual history, and its introduction into Roman liturgical use
as separate questions.
