# Part I: Cold Transfer / Pencil Test (`ST` I, q. 8, a. 1)

*Conducted strictly after reading `vertical-slice.tex` and opening `unmarked-test-passage.txt`, using only the method absorbed from the vertical slice, with zero external glossary or specification.*

## Claim

Stated with its qualifications intact, the claim of *Summa Theologiae* I, q. 8, a. 1 ("Whether God Is in All Things?") is this: God is present in all things, and innermostly—not as a part of their essence, nor as an accident inhering in them, nor as a bodily object enclosed by what contains it, but in the manner in which an immediate active cause is present by its power to that upon which it continuously operates, containing spiritually what it is in; and in the case of creatures deformed by sin (such as demons), God is in them not without qualification, but strictly "inasmuch as they are beings."

Restated in ordinary English without dropping a single clause or distinction from the body (*I answer that*) or the four replies: the article asks whether God is in everything that exists. The answer is yes, and more deeply than anything else, once we set aside misleading spatial and compositional pictures of what "being in" means. God is not inside creatures as an ingredient of their nature (part of their essence), nor as a quality attached to them (an accident), nor as a physical object trapped inside a container; rather, spiritual realities hold together what they inhabit, the way a soul holds together a living body, so God is in creatures by containing them (and only by comparison to bodies do we say creatures are "in" God). God is in things the way an active agent is present to whatever it acts on directly. Why must an agent be present where it acts? Because any agent acting directly, without an intermediate go-between, must be joined to what it acts on and touch it by its power—the principle cited from Aristotle's *Physics* VII that mover and moved must be joined together, and sharpened in Reply 3 to the rule that no agent, however powerful, acts across a distance except through a medium. Now, because God is existence itself ("very being") by His own essence, created existence ("created being") must be God's own proper effect, in the way setting things on fire is the proper effect of fire. Moreover, God does not produce this effect in creatures only at the moment they begin to exist; He causes it continuously for as long as they remain in existence, just as the sun causes light in the air for as long as the air stays illuminated. It follows that for as long as any thing has existence, God must be present to it in a way matching the kind of existence that thing has. Finally, existence itself is what is deepest and most fundamental inside every thing, because it stands as the actualizing ("formal") principle with respect to everything else found in a thing (citing the earlier discussion in Question 7, Article 1). Consequently, God must be present in all things, and present in the innermost way. The four replies secure the borders of that thesis: God's being "above all things" is superiority of nature, not spatial remoteness (Reply 1); spiritual presence contains rather than is contained (Reply 2); God's supreme power is shown by acting immediately everywhere rather than across a gap, so creatures are "distant" from God only by unlikeness in nature or grace (Reply 3); and because demons combine a created nature from God with a deformity of sin not from God, we do not say unconditionally that God is in demons, but only that He is in them inasmuch as they are beings (Reply 4).

Four phrases in the English text must be kept sharp and never flattened into casual modern speech. First, **"being"** (in "very being," "created being," "has being," and "being is innermost") must not be flattened into "a thing" or a static fact of sitting around in reality; the whole argument depends on treating being as an active, ongoing effect continuously produced in every creature. Second, **"proper effect"** does not mean a fitting or well-behaved outcome, but an effect bound directly to what the agent is by its own essence. Third, **"formal in respect of everything found in a thing"** does not mean outward shape or ceremonial stiffness; it means the actualizing principle by which everything else in a thing is real rather than unrealized possibility. Fourth, **"inasmuch as they are beings"** (Reply 4) performs the exact qualifying work of *inquantum*: it isolates one formal respect in a concrete subject so that divine presence is affirmed of created nature without implicating God in sin.

## Meaning

Before testing a single inference, we fix the senses of the five load-bearing terms and distinctions on which this article turns, checking what changes if each is taken otherwise.

First, **"being in" as operative presence of an agent** (contrasted with essence-part, accident, and bodily containment). Here, to say that God is "in" a creature means neither spatial enclosure (water in a cup), nor structural composition (hydrogen in water), nor inherence of a dependent property (whiteness in chalk). It means direct causal presence: the creature is presently receiving God's immediate active power, while God spiritually contains the creature rather than being bounded by it (Reply 2). *What changes if taken otherwise:* Take "in" spatially, and Objection 2 succeeds—an infinite, incorporeal God cannot be enclosed inside finite bodies. Take "in" as part of essence or as an accident, and Creator and creature collapse into a single composite substance (pantheism) or God becomes a dependent attribute of finite things.

Second, **immediate action and "touching by power"** (*joined together*, *no action at a distance except through a medium*). An agent acts *immediately* when it produces an effect without relying on an intervening medium or chain of go-betweens bridging a gap. To be "joined to" a patient and "touch it by its power" does not mean bodily surface-to-surface contact (contact of quantity), since God has no body; it means contact of active efficacy (*virtus*), where the agent's power bears directly upon the patient itself rather than across an empty gulf. *What changes if taken otherwise:* Restrict "joined together" to physical surface contact, and the appeal to *Physics* VII excludes an incorporeal God from touching bodies at all. At the opposite extreme, dilute "touching by power" to mean simply "causing an effect somewhere, even remotely," and Objection 3 wins: an omnipotent agent could produce effects in creatures from afar without being present in them.

Third, **"very being by His own essence" and "proper effect."** To call God "very being by His own essence" is to say that God does not merely possess existence as one attribute alongside what He is; His essence is existence itself. A *proper effect* is an effect that corresponds immediately to the agent's own essence (as igniting corresponds directly to fire's own fiery nature). Linking the two yields the claim that created existence as such belongs directly to God as His proper effect. *What changes if taken otherwise:* If "very being" is weakened to mean only "an exceptionally mighty existing being," the inference that every creature's existence is God's proper effect loses its ground. If "proper effect" is weakened to mean "an effect God might delegate entirely to deputies or natural instruments," then God need not act *immediately* in every creature, and universal immediate presence does not follow.

Fourth, **continuous preservation in being** (contrasted with mere initial origination). Causing created being is here defined as ongoing concurrent production for as long as a thing lasts—like sunlight sustaining illumination in air—not a one-time initial production after which the effect persists independently on its own, like a house standing after the carpenter departs. *What changes if taken otherwise:* Read divine causation of being merely as initial creation at the creature's origin, and the step to "as long as a thing has being, God must be present to it" collapses: God would only have had to be present when creatures first came to be, not throughout their continued existence.

Fifth, **"formal" and "innermost"** (joined with the Reply 4 qualifier **"inasmuch as they are beings"**). To call being "formal in respect of everything found in a thing" means that being is what actualizes every essence, faculty, and property a thing possesses; therefore "innermost" means ontologically most fundamental, not geometrically centered inside a body's core. Likewise, "inasmuch as they are beings" separates a demon's created nature from its moral privation. *What changes if taken otherwise:* Read "formal" as outward shape or conceptual classification, and being ceases to be the deepest reality inside a thing, collapsing the "innermostly" conclusion. Drop "inasmuch as they are beings" in Reply 4, and the article either fails to cover all beings (excluding demons) or makes God causally present in the deformity of sin.

Finally, provenance and revealed premises must be kept distinct from philosophical warrant. This article is written by St. Thomas Aquinas and cites Scripture (Ps. 112:4, 2 Cor. 6:14, Isa. 26:12), Augustine, and Aristotle (*Physics* VII). None of those citations proves a philosophical premise by bare authority; and the Scriptural premises—especially Isa. 26:12 in the *sed contra* and the revealed status of demons deformed by sin in Objection 4 and Reply 4—belong to revealed theology and must stay labelled as **revelation**, apart from the philosophical reasoning inside the body of the article.

## Warrant

Tracing the body of the article (and the operative additions from the replies) premise by premise reveals a braid of six distinct moves from our working list of warrants.

The opening disambiguation (Sentence 1: God is in things neither as essence-part nor as accident, but as an agent present to what it acts upon) operates as **definition** fixing the sense of "being in" under investigation, paired silently with unstated **prior results** (that God is simple and cannot enter into composition with creatures). On this page by itself, those exclusions are stipulated rather than argued.

The contact principle (Sentence 2: an agent must be joined to that wherein it acts immediately and touch it by its power; supported in Reply 3 by the rule that no agent acts at a distance except through a medium) appeals explicitly to a **prior result** ("hence it is proved in *Phys.* vii that the thing moved and the mover must be joined together") backed by a **metaphysical principle** banning unmediated action at a distance. Because Aristotle's argument in *Physics* VII is neither reproduced here nor checked for whether a proof about bodily movers transfers cleanly to an incorporeal agent's "contact of power," Sentence 2 stands on **provenance only** until the reader goes to inspect *Physics* VII and test that extension independently.

The antecedent of Sentence 3 ("since God is very being by His own essence") enters as an uncited **prior result** from earlier in the *Summa* (that divine essence and existence are identical); taken on this page alone without examining that earlier proof, it functions as a promissory note (**provenance only** on the page). The consequent of Sentence 3 ("created being must be His proper effect; as to ignite is the proper effect of fire") pairs a **metaphysical principle** (that an agent whose essence is a perfection produces participated instances of that perfection as its proper effect) with an appeal to **experience** (fire's proper effect is to ignite) presented as a **logical consequence** of the antecedent plus that principle. Notice that everyday observation of fire only illustrates what "proper effect" means; it does not establish that existence behaves like a physical quality such as heat or combustion.

Sentence 4 ("Now God causes this effect in things not only when they first begin to be, but as long as they are preserved in being; as light is caused in the air by the sun as long as the air remains illuminated") offers **experience** purely as an analogy (air stays lit only while the sun shines) to stand in for a substantive **metaphysical principle**—that creatures lack self-sustaining persistence and depend concurrently on God at every instant for their ongoing existence. On the page of Question 8, Article 1, no argument is given showing that created existence works like light in air rather than like heat persisting in an iron bar removed from the fire or a house standing after its builder leaves.

Sentence 5 ("Therefore as long as a thing has being, God must be present to it, according to its mode of being") is offered as a **logical consequence** of Sentences 2, 3, and 4. Strictly speaking, however, that deduction requires an extra premise that the body leaves implicit and only surfaces later in Reply 3 ("it belongs to the great power of God that He acts immediately in all things"): that God causes created being *immediately*, without an intervening instrument bridging a distance. That immediacy claim enters in Reply 3 as an unargued **metaphysical principle**.

Sentence 6 ("But being is innermost in each thing and most fundamentally inherent in all things since it is formal in respect of everything found in a thing, as was shown above (Q. 7, A. 1)") invokes an explicit **prior result** resting on a **metaphysical principle** about the primacy of act ("being is formal"). As a citation, it is again a promissory note (**provenance only** until Q. 7, A. 1 is audited). Sentence 7 ("Hence it must be that God is in all things, and innermostly") then follows as a **logical consequence** of Sentences 5 and 6, together with the implicit bridge that an agent immediately causing what is innermost in a thing is thereby present innermostly in it. Elsewhere in the article, the *sed contra* joins a **metaphysical principle** ("A thing is wherever it operates") to **revelation** (Isa. 26:12), while Reply 2 draws on an **experience**-based analogy (soul containing body) to illustrate a **metaphysical principle** about spiritual containment, and Reply 4 relies on **revelation** for the existence of sin-deformed demons paired with an *inquantum* qualification.

## Burden

To locate the burden-bearing sentence—the first proposition in the argument whose truth is supplied neither merely by vocabulary nor by a previous citation—we test candidates openly against the hostile-reader question: *could an intelligent opponent accept every definition, distinction, and illustration on the page (and even grant the cited prior results) and still deny this proposition?*

A first natural candidate is Sentence 2: *an agent must be joined to that wherein it acts immediately and touch it by its power* (together with Reply 3's ban on action at a distance without a medium). Test it with a hostile reader. An intelligent opponent might of course challenge the ban on action at a distance from physics—or she might simply grant Sentence 2 outright: "I accept your definition of 'being in' as operative presence, I grant Aristotle's *Physics* VII on bodily movers, and I even grant that whenever an incorporeal agent acts immediately on something without a medium, its active power is right there touching it. In fact, once bodily contact is dropped, 'touching by power' scarcely says more than 'acting immediately on it.'" Notice what happens when our opponent grants Sentence 2: nothing whatsoever follows about God being in all things right now unless God is actually exercising immediate, ongoing causal power in every existing creature at every moment. Sentence 2 therefore loses as the primary burden—both because it leans on a prior citation (*Phys.* VII) and borders on unpacking immediate operative presence, and because granting it concedes nothing about the present state of creatures.

A second candidate is Sentence 3's inference: *because God is very being by His own essence, created being must be His proper effect (as to ignite is the proper effect of fire)*. Here an opponent *can* already dig in—even while granting *Physics* VII and the prior result from Question 3 that God is subsistent being—by denying that "being" (*esse*) is an ongoing causal effect poured into things like ignition rather than simply the fact that concrete substances exist once produced by ordinary causes. Yet suppose a still more charitable opponent—say, a classical Deist—grants even a weaker version of Sentence 3: "Very well, God is the proper originating cause of created existence; nothing ever gets into existence except from God." Even after granting Sentence 1, Sentence 2, Sentence 3's originating claim, and Q. 7, A. 1, has the Deist conceded the article's conclusion? No.

Look at **Sentence 4** (reinforced by Reply 3's immediacy clause): *"Now God causes this effect in things not only when they first begin to be, but as long as they are preserved in being; as light is caused in the air by the sun as long as the air remains illuminated."* Run the hostile-reader test here. Can an intelligent opponent accept every fixed definition ("operative presence," "touching by power," "proper effect," "formal principle"), accept that fire ignites and sunlight disappears from air when the sun sets, and even accept that God is subsistent being who brought creatures into existence at the beginning—and still deny without self-contradiction that creatures require God's immediate, continuous causal activity at every subsequent instant simply to stay in existence? Yes, easily. Nothing in the vocabulary forces created existence to behave like light in the air rather than like a house standing after its builder leaves or a son living after his father dies; and no citation or proof is offered on this page to close that gap. Sentence 4 (understood together with Sentence 3's commitment to *created being* as an immediate proper effect of God, not merely a historical beginning) is therefore the decisive burden-bearing premise where the philosophical weight of the article sits.

## Consequence

If every premise of the body and replies is granted—operative presence as the meaning of "in," immediate contact of power without action at a distance, God as subsistent being whose proper immediate effect is created being, continuous divine causation of every creature's being for as long as it persists, and being as formal and innermost in every thing—then two exact consequences follow at licensed strength. First, for as long as any creature exists, God is immediately present to it by His sustaining causal power, proportionate to that creature's own mode of being, and because being actualizes everything else in the creature, that operative presence is deeper and more fundamental than any other aspect of the creature. Second, Objections 1 through 4 are neutralized within those exact limits: divine presence by sustaining agency is compatible with superiority of nature (Reply 1), spiritual containment rather than bodily enclosure (Reply 2), supreme immediacy of power (Reply 3), and divine holiness with respect to sinful creatures when qualified by "inasmuch as they are beings" (Reply 4).

Even if every sentence of *ST* I, q. 8, a. 1 is granted in full, however, the passage has **not yet established** any of the following specific propositions, each of which readers routinely assume is already secured by "God is in all things, and innermostly":

First, **that God actually exists, or that God is subsistent being itself.** Those claims are imported as prior results; nothing inside Question 8, Article 1 argues for God's existence or essence-existence identity.

Second, **that God dwells in creatures by sanctifying grace, personal friendship, conscious union, or sacramental presence.** Readers frequently slide from "God is in all things, and innermostly" to spiritual intimacy. Reply 3 and Reply 4 refute that conflation on the page: demons are "distant from God by unlikeness of nature or grace," yet God is still present in them inasmuch as they are beings. Universal presence by sustaining causal power belongs equally to stones, demons, and saints; presence by grace or glory is completely unestablished here.

Third, **that God sustains creatures freely by voluntary will rather than by necessary natural emanation.** Look at the two analogies driving the body of the article: fire igniting, and the sun illuminating air. Both are natural, necessary, involuntary agents. Nothing in the body of Question 8, Article 1 shows that God freely chooses to hold creatures in existence rather than radiating created being by necessity of nature.

Fourth, **that God is spatially everywhere (in every place, including empty place if any), or that creatures lack genuine secondary causal powers of their own (occasionalism).** The article addresses God's presence in *things that have being*, not an analysis of "place" or "everywhere" as such; and saying that God immediately causes *being* as His proper effect neither establishes nor rules out genuine secondary causation by creatures in producing particular changes.

## Denial

What does it cost a thinker to reject our burden-bearing premise—to deny that created beings require God's immediate, continuous causal activity at every instant simply to remain in existence (and, deeper behind it, to deny that "being" is an ongoing proper effect continuously caused like sunlight in air)? Among our five possible outcomes (*contradiction*, *conflict with experience*, *explanatory cost*, *rival framework*, *non-assent*), we test **contradiction** first and reject it with reasons. Does a thinker contradict herself if she grants that God exists, created the world at its origin, and touches by power whatever He acts on immediately, yet denies that a rock or a human being vanishes into nothingness unless continuously re-supplied with being at every moment? No. Nothing logically self-refuting follows from holding that once a substance has been brought into existence, it continues to exist on its own until some positive destructive cause ends it. Calling that denial "incoherent" or "self-contradictory" mistakes a substantive disagreement about the ontology of existence and persistence for a violation of logic.

Nor does rejection incur a bare **conflict with experience**. Everyday observation offers examples on both sides: sunlight vanishes immediately from air when the sun is blocked, yet houses endure after builders depart and offspring live after their parents die. Bare observation does not tell us which of those phenomena is the right model for existence itself.

The honest classification for a serious denier is **rival framework**—coupled with a genuine dispute over **explanatory cost**. A thinker who rejects Sentence 4 (and the accompanying ontology of Sentence 3) commits himself either to an **existential-inertia / deistic framework**, on which substances, once created or generated, possess an intrinsic tendency to remain in existence unless destroyed, making God's causation originating rather than continuously concurrent; or to a **naturalistic / deflationary framework**, on which "existence" is not a formal metaphysical act continuously poured into things as a proper effect at all, and physical systems interact through intrinsic powers (or even non-local physical relations, challenging Reply 3's ban on action at a distance). Within that clash of rival frameworks, each side presses an **explanatory cost** on the other: the Thomist presses the denier to explain what grounds the sheer continued existence of contingent beings from one moment to the next if existence is not part of their essence, while the denier presses the Thomist to justify treating "being" as a quasi-physical proper effect analogous to light and ignition. Finally, a reader who declines the entire act-and-potency framework of "formal being" without building a competing ontology stands in **non-assent**: she declines a metaphysical vocabulary without logical contradiction or conflict with observation.

### Major Defect 2: Flat Logical Error and Silent Domain Shift in Section 8's Symbolic Notation

**Severity:** Major logical defect inside the sole formal-logic section of the document.

**Verbatim offending passages (Section 8, lines 143–153):**
- Lines 143–149:
  > *"Let the quantifiers range over the members of a single causal series, and write $\mathrm{Dep}(x, y)$ to mean: the causing done by $x$ is derived from the causing done by $y$, so that $x$ causes only because $y$ causes. Now compare these two statements:*
  > *(1) $\forall x\,\exists y\,\mathrm{Dep}(x, y)$*
  > *(2) $\exists y\,\forall x\,\mathrm{Dep}(x, y)$"*
- Line 153:
  > *"line (2) says that there is some one member $y$ on which the causing of every dependent member in the series depends (taking the universal quantifier over all subordinate members, since no first cause derives its causing from itself)---a single common source at the head of the whole series. ... Even more sharply, the conclusion that cosmological arguments usually want is something like (2) (a single first source for every dependent member), whereas our passage asserts only that (1) under the strict derived-causing reading is impossible---and does so without showing that any real, cosmos-wide series of dependence satisfies (1) under that reading in the first place."*

**Exact diagnosis (Fact & Logic):**
1. **Incoherent claim about "satisfies (1)":** Line 151 defines line (1) under the strict derived-causing reading as the **endless *per se* regress** (*"Read $\mathrm{Dep}(x, y)$ strictly as derived causing---'$x$ causes only because $y$ causes'---and line (1) is what the per se prohibition denies can hold without end"*). Line 153 then says our passage *"asserts only that (1) under the strict derived-causing reading is impossible---and does so without showing that any real, cosmos-wide series of dependence **satisfies (1)** under that reading in the first place."* If (1) under the strict reading is *impossible*, then **no** real series can satisfy (1) under that reading, and Aquinas would never want to show that one does! What is unshown is that any real, cosmos-wide series **is governed by $\mathrm{Dep}(x, y)$ in the strict derived-causing sense** (that subordinate members stand in that derived-causing relation at all), NOT that a real series satisfies (1)!
2. **Self-reference / domain inconsistency in (2):** Line 143 fixes the domain of both quantifiers as *"the members of a single causal series."* Under that domain, `(2) \exists y\,\forall x\,\mathrm{Dep}(x, y)` entails $\mathrm{Dep}(y, y)$—that the first cause derives its causing from itself. Line 153 tries to patch this retroactively inside parentheses (*"taking the universal quantifier over all subordinate members, since no first cause derives its causing from itself"*), which silently changes the domain of $\forall x$ between `(1)` and `(2)` without changing the notation.

**Recommended fix:**
1. Rewrite the end of line 153 to say: *"...whereas our passage asserts only that (1) under the strict derived-causing reading is impossible---and does so without showing that any real, cosmos-wide series of dependence instantiates $\mathrm{Dep}(x, y)$ under that strict reading in the first place."*
2. For `(2)`, either write $\exists y\,\forall x\,(x \neq y \to \mathrm{Dep}(x, y))$ (if adding `x \neq y` is acceptable within the two-line budget) or explicitly fix the domain of $x$ to *subordinate* (non-first) members—noting upfront that `(1)` ranges over *all* members of the series while `(2)` asserts a single source $y$ for every other member $x$, rather than slipping in a domain rewrite mid-sentence.
