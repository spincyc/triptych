## Methodological Notice: Liturgical Compilation vs. Patristic Exegesis

The First Sunday of Advent, Year B, is a **postconciliar liturgical compilation**: its shared Missal formulary (*Dominica I Adventus*) serves all three Lectionary years, while its Year B readings (`Isa 63:16b–17, 19b; 64:2b–7` [Vg `63:16b–17; 64:1, 3b–8`], `Ps 80[79]:2ac, 3b, 15–16, 18–19`, `1 Cor 1:3–9`, `Mark 13:33–37`) were assembled in the 1969/1981 *Ordo lectionum Missae*. No Church Father or medieval Doctor preached on this exact postconciliar combination as a single unit (confirmed by our negative formulary search: 0/40 matches in `formulary-loci.yaml`).

Accordingly, every interpretation below strictly separates:
1. **Verified patristic and saintly exegesis on particular verses** (grounded in exact held witnesses documented in `research/scope.md` and `research/source-bindings.toml`), from
2. **Whole-formulary editorial synthesis**, which groups genuinely compatible patristic answers into an internally coherent reading of all twelve appointed elements without attributing modern compositional intent to ancient witnesses or reconstructing unheld modern Missal texts.

All cross-element relation tags below use exclusively the controlled nine-term taxonomy of `guidance/liturgy/postconciliar-propers.md` (lines 202–210): `officially correlated`, `responsorial`, `acclamatory`, `semi-continuous`, `seasonal or ritual`, `textual observation`, `documented reception`, `source-grounded synthesis`, and `editorial or AI proposal`.

Each of the three readings below is anchored in a **different controlling exegetical question**, turns on **different passage-level textual difficulties**, weighs real patristic disagreements differently, carries at least **two eligible Fathers or Doctors** verified in `src/sources/inventories/author-standing-v1.toml`, and produces distinct theological and pastoral consequences.

## Interpretation 1: Divine Forbearance and the Unveiling of an Already-Present Lord
*(Identifier: `hidden-presence-and-forbearing-patience`)*

### 1. Controlling Exegetical Question
Why does God appear absent, silent, or distant while human hearts grow hard and the Master of the house seems gone on a journey — is divine delay abandonment, or is it a deliberate economy of patient hiddenness preparing the unveiling (*apokalupsis*) of a Lord who is already present?

### 2. Consequential Passage-Level Textual Difficulties
1. **Isaiah 63:17 (*Quare errare nos fecisti, Domine, de viis tuis; indurasti cor nostrum ne timeremus te?*) & 64:1 → 64:3b (*Utinam dirumperes caelos, et descenderes... descendisti*):**
   - *Textual difficulty:* The prophet boldly accuses God of causing Israel to wander and hardening its heart, then leaps abruptly from an unreal wish (*Oh, that you would rend the heavens!*) straight into an accomplished past verb (*you came down!*).
   - *Principal witness inference:* **St. Jerome** (*Commentarii in Isaiam* XVII, PL 24 cols. 643–646, OCR lines 44340–44660, raw OCR locating witness) refuses to attribute moral evil to God: God's *nimia patientia* ("excessive forbearance"), refraining from immediately striking sinners so as to wait for their repentance, *looks* from below like hardening and abandonment; yet the sudden grammatical leap from optative *Utinam dirumperes* to past *Descendisti* reveals the prayer **already answered in the Incarnation** (*Verbum caro factum est*), melting the proud "mountains."
   - *What changes if accepted:* The First Reading is no longer a cry into an empty sky; it exposes how divine patience can feel like divine absence precisely while God's saving descent has already begun.
2. **1 Corinthians 1:7 (*exspectantibus revelationem [`apokalupsin`] Domini nostri Jesu Christi*):**
   - *Textual difficulty:* Why does Paul say the Corinthians wait for the *revelation* (`apokalupsis` / unveiling) of Jesus Christ rather than merely His *arrival* (`parousia`)?
   - *Principal witness inference:* **St. John Chrysostom** (*Homilies on 1 Corinthians* II.6, NPNF1-12 lines 768–775, admitted passage `2.1cor-1.7`) seizes on the exact noun *revelation*: *"implying that although He be not seen, yet He is, and is present even now, and then shall appear."*
   - *What changes if accepted:* Christian Advent waiting is not longing for an absent monarch to travel back from a distant province, but awaiting the lifting of the veil over a Lord who already inhabits and sustains His Church.
3. **Mark 13:33–34 (with omitted 13:32: *nescitis... homo qui peregre profectus reliquit domum suam*):**
   - *Textual difficulty:* How can the omniscient Son say the hour is unknown (13:32–33), and in what sense has the Lord of the house "gone abroad" and "left His house" (13:34)?
   - *Principal witness inferences:* **St. Hilary of Poitiers** (*De Trinitate* IX.67, NPNF2-9 lines 21594–21611) shows that Christ's silence is an economy (*dispensatio*) withholding secure dates *"that our minds may be kept on the stretch by the uncertainty of suspense... for the security of knowledge might breed negligence of the faith."* **St. Bede** (*In Marci Evangelium Expositio* IV.13, lines 402–405, drawing on **St. Gregory the Great**, *Hom. in Evang.* 9) resolves the departure image: Christ left His Church *only bodily* (*corporaliter*, carrying human flesh — whose native home is earth — into the "far country" of heaven at the Ascension), while **never withdrawing His divine presence and safeguard**, concealing the hour so disciples remain in *"pendulae exspectationis incerto"* (poised expectation).

### 3. Carrying Authors and Supporting Witnesses
- **Eligible carrying authors (`author-standing-v1.toml`):** **St. Jerome** (`father`), **St. John Chrysostom** (`father`), **St. Bede** (`father`), **St. Hilary of Poitiers** (`father`).
- **Supporting witnesses:** **St. Augustine** (`father`, *Enarr.* Ps 79 on lifting the veil of flesh), **St. Cyril of Jerusalem** (`father`, *Catechesis 15* §§1–2, 4 on the unobserved first descent and manifest second coming), **St. Gregory the Great** (`father`, *Hom.* 9 & 13 on ascended flesh and the *patiens redditor*), and **St. Bernard of Clairvaux** (*Sermones in Adventu* 3.4 & 5.1, supplementary spiritual witness — not a carrying author — on the hidden intermediate advent bridging past weakness and future majesty).

### 4. Whole-Formulary Synthesis Across All Twelve Elements
*(Explicitly distinguished as editorial synthesis uniting verified verse-level patristic insights; cross-element links use the exact controlled taxonomy.)*
- **Introductory Rites (`entrance-antiphon`, `collect`):** Lifting the soul in *Ad te levavi* (`Ps 25[24]:1–3`) addresses the temptation to despair during God's silence: those who endure in waiting (*qui sustinent te*) will not be put to shame (`textual observation`, `documented reception`: Augustine and Bellarmine on Ps 24:1–3). With the *Gloria* omitted (`GIRM 53`), the Church enters the quiet of expectant waiting, opening with the Collect (*Da, quaesumus, omnipotens Deus*; `seasonal or ritual`, illuminated historically by the Gelasian antecedent II.lxxxiv petition for a willing heart to go out to meet the coming Christ rather than drifting into numbness during the delay).
- **Liturgy of the Word (`first-reading`, `responsorial-psalm`, `second-reading`, `gospel-acclamation`, `gospel`):**
  - Isaiah's lament over God's hidden face (`Isa 64:7`, *Abscondisti faciem tuam*) connects by `documented reception` (Jerome, *In Isaiam* XVII, PL 24 col. 650, explicitly quoting `Ps 79:4`) and `responsorial` placement to the **Responsorial Psalm** refrain (`Ps 80[79]:4`, *ostende faciem tuam, et salvi erimus*). Augustine (*Enarr.* Ps 79, `psalm-79-coxe-3`) reads *illumine Thy face* as lifting the veil of lowly flesh so Christ is recognized in majesty — resonating with Chrysostom's *apokalupsis* in the **Second Reading** (`1 Cor 1:7`), where an already-present Lord is waited for until His unveiling (`source-grounded synthesis`).
  - The **Gospel Acclamation** (`Ps 85[84]:8`, *Ostende nobis, Domine, misericordiam tuam, et salutare tuum da nobis*) echoes the same petition to *show* what is hidden — asking, as Augustine puts it (*Enarr.* Ps 84, `psalm-84-coxe-6`), *"grant us Thy Christ"* (`acclamatory`, `textual observation`).
  - In the **Gospel** (`Mark 13:33–37`), Bede and Hilary clarify why this already-present Lord keeps His return hidden: His bodily departure to heaven leaves His divine guardianship intact, while the withheld hour keeps faith stretched in vigilant prayer (`source-grounded synthesis`).
  - *(Advent Common Psalm branch:)* If `Ps 25(24)` or `Ps 85(84)` is sung under U.S. Lectionary No. 174 (`GIRM 61`), both canonical psalms preserve this arc of patient waiting (*sustinent te*) and unveiling (*ostende nobis*).
- **Liturgy of the Eucharist and Concluding Rites (`prayer-over-the-offerings`, `preface`, `communion-antiphon`, `prayer-after-communion`, `concluding-blessing`):**
  - In the **Prayer over the Offerings** (*Suscipe, quaesumus, Domine*, bounded comparison against *Veronense* XXVIIII.372), the Church places temporal gifts before the Lord so that temporal devotion becomes the reward of eternal redemption (`seasonal or ritual`).
  - At the **Seasonal Preface** (`seasonal or ritual`: one of the two Prefaces of Advent verified in `ordo-missae-praefationes`, whose exact subtitle and modern text remain uninspected offline), the Church enters Eucharistic thanksgiving (`GIRM 364`) in the Advent horizon that St. Cyril of Jerusalem (*Cat.* 15.1–2) names as Christ's two descents — *"one, the unobserved, like rain on a fleece; and a second His open coming, which is to be"* — bridged by Bernard's hidden intermediate coming in spirit (`Sermo 5.1`, `documented reception`).
  - At the **Communion Antiphon** (`Ps 85[84]:13`, *Dominus dabit benignitatem, et terra nostra dabit fructum suum*), the hidden Lord gives Himself sacramentally, granting interior *sweetness* (Augustine, `psalm-84-coxe-12`) as pledge of the unveiling to come (`seasonal or ritual`, `source-grounded synthesis`). The **Prayer after Communion** (*Prosint nobis*, closing the Communion Rite; protected modern body uninspected offline) and the **Concluding Blessing** (ordinary blessing or optional solemn blessing / prayer over the people, `GIRM 90`; `seasonal or ritual`) dismiss the assembly back into the interval to live by faith in the unseen Lord until He appears.

### 5. Four Senses of Scripture (Under Interpretation 1)
- **Literal:** Postexilic Israel cries out from perceived divine silence (`Isa 63–64`) and pleads for God's face to shine on the ravaged vine (`Ps 80[79]`); Paul assures the newly founded Corinthian assembly of Christ's coming unveiling (`1 Cor 1:3–9`); Jesus on Mount Olivet warns His disciples not to mistake His coming departure or hidden timetable for license to sleep (`Mark 13:33–37`).
- **Allegorical:** God's tearing open of the heavens (*Descendisti*, Jerome on `Isa 64:3b`) and lifting the veil of weakness (`Ps 80[79]:4`, Augustine) signify the Incarnation and the Ascension (*homo peregre proficiscens*, Bede/Gregory), wherein Christ hides His majesty in flesh and sacrament while remaining divinely present in His Church (`1 Cor 1:7`, Chrysostom).
- **Moral:** Because divine patience can feel like absence (`Isa 63:17`, Jerome) and predictable schedules breed complacency (`Mark 13:32–33`, Hilary), the believer resists spiritual torpor through alert prayer, refusing to misread God's slowness as indifference.
- **Anagogical:** Every hidden coming in flesh, word, and Eucharist moves toward the *apokalupsis* (`1 Cor 1:7`) — the unveiled vision of the Lord's face (`Ps 80[79]:4`) when Christ returns in open glory (Cyril of Jerusalem, *Cat.* 15.1).

### 6. Limits and Trade-offs of Interpretation 1
- **Strength:** Unites Jerome's *nimia patientia*, Chrysostom's *apokalupsis*, and Hilary/Bede's economy of suspense with unusual exegetical power; directly addresses parishioners who experience God as silent or absent.
- **Limit:** Softens the sharp penitential edge of `Isa 64:5–7` (Israel's real defilement and sin) and the corporate discipline of Corinthian factions (`1 Cor 1:8–10`) if divine hiddenness is overemphasized at the expense of human culpability and concrete moral conversion. Also follows Jerome's Incarnational reading of `Isa 64:3b` rather than Aquinas's literal Exodus/Arnon reading.

## Interpretation 2: The Potter's Clay, the Vine, and the Faithfulness of God Amid Human Frailty
*(Identifier: `covenant-fidelity-and-human-plasticity`)*

### 1. Controlling Exegetical Question
If Israel's own "righteousnesses" are like a polluted rag (`Isa 64:6`), if no one rises to call on God's name (`Isa 64:7`), and if the Corinthians are factious and wavering (`1 Cor 1:8–10`), on what foundation can the Church hope to stand blameless on the Day of Christ — human self-improvement, or God's covenant faithfulness acting through the Son of Man without coercing human freedom?

### 2. Consequential Passage-Level Textual Difficulties
1. **Isaiah 63:17 (*indurasti cor nostrum*), 64:3b (*descendisti*), 64:6 (*quasi pannus menstruatae universae justitiae nostrae*), and 64:8 (*nos vero lutum; et fictor noster tu*):**
   - *Textual difficulty:* Does `Isa 64:6` teach that all human virtue is intrinsically worthless, and does `63:17` mean God actively forces hearts into sin?
   - *Principal witness inference:* **St. Thomas Aquinas** (*Expositio super Isaiam ad litteram* ch. 63–64, Parma Vol. 14 pp. 370–371, OCR lines 90930–91220, raw OCR locating witness) gives a sharply distinct account from Jerome: in `63:17`, God "hardens" **permissively by not infusing grace and withholding correction** (*permittendo, non immittens gratiam, et non corrigens*); after citing Israel's terror at Sinai (`Exod. 20`) on the preceding clause `64:3a`, Aquinas reads `64:3b` (*Descendisti, et a facie tua montes defluxerunt*) **literally of God's descent to deliver Israel from Egypt (`Exod. 4`, with `Ps 17:10`) and the melting of the cliffs of Arnon (`Num. 21`)**, treating past Exodus miracles as proof of God's covenant power before noting the Incarnation and Judgment mystically; in `64:6`, he protects authentic graced righteousness by diagnosing **four ways human works of justice become defiled** (mortal sin, negligence, perverse intention, and pride); and in `64:8`, he locates hope strictly in the Creator-Potter's mercy over clay that cannot reshape itself. **St. Augustine** (*De gratia et libero arbitrio* 45 [XXIII], NPNF1-5 lines 46111–46195) corroborates the doctrinal equilibrium: God's just judgment and human free will concur without turning God into the author of sin.
   - *What changes if accepted:* Advent begins in honest self-knowledge rather than Pelagian self-improvement: human effort stripped of grace falls like a withered leaf, yet Israel's hope rests on God's proven covenant fidelity and the Potter's hand.
2. **1 Corinthians 1:4–9 (*in omnibus divites facti estis... qui et confirmabit vos usque in finem sine crimine... Fidelis Deus*):**
   - *Textual difficulty:* How can Paul praise the notoriously quarrelsome, immature Corinthians (`1:10–12`; `3:1–3`) as "enriched in all utterance and all knowledge" and "wanting in no grace," and does `1:8–9` ("God is faithful... who will confirm you blameless") guarantee final perseverance unconditionally?
   - *Principal witness inferences:* **St. John Chrysostom** (*Hom. in 1 Cor.* II.3–5, 7–9, NPNF1-12 lines 654–920; admitted passages `2.1cor-1.4-6`, `2.1cor-1.7`, `2.1cor-1.8`, `2.1cor-1.9`) tackles the seeming contradiction head-on: Paul praises their initial fervor and faithful remnant while using pastoral economy (*oikonomikos*) to anchor every single endowment in **grace** (*in gratia Dei*) and hammer the Name of Christ to dismantle factional pride. Far from flattering them, the verbs *confirm* and *unreprovable* (1:8) quietly diagnose a **wavering** assembly; and on *Fidelis Deus* (1:9), Chrysostom explicitly rejects mechanical certainty — God is faithful to His call *"if we be not quite impatient of His rein,"* naming the promised goods through **Paul's own words in `1 Corinthians 2:9`** (*"which eye hath not seen, nor ear heard"*, lines 847–848 — echoing `Isa 64:4` via Paul's epistle rather than quoting Isaiah directly). **Cornelius a Lapide** (*In 1 Cor.* 1:8–9, Vol. 7 OCR lines 455–510; supporting witness) sharpens the same Catholic balance through the Council of Trent (Sess. VI cc. 11 & 13, citing Augustine): God confirms *quantum in se est* (so far as His part is) and does not forsake unless first forsaken.
   - *What changes if accepted:* Grace and human plasticity are held in taut harmony — God supplies every spiritual gift needed to stand blameless, yet His faithfulness never overrides a stubborn refusal to be molded.
3. **Psalm 80(79):15–19 (*visita vineam istam: et perfice eam... super filium hominis*) & Psalm 85(84):13 (*Dominus dabit benignitatem, et terra nostra dabit fructum suum*):**
   - *Textual difficulty:* How does a ravaged vine become fruitful again, and how does sinful human soil yield righteousness?
   - *Principal witness inferences:* **St. Augustine** (*Enarr.* Ps 79, `psalm-79-coxe-9`, `psalm-79-coxe-11`) and **St. Robert Bellarmine** (*Explanatio* Ps 79:15–19) infer that God does **not** uproot His covenant vine to start over elsewhere, but **perfects it upon the Son of Man** — literally Christ formed by the Holy Spirit (Bellarmine, citing Luke 1:35, noting post-exilic Israel never regained former glory) and established as the sole foundation of the Church (Augustine, 1 Cor 3:11). Thus conversion itself is petitioned from God (*converte nos*), leading to *"we will call upon thy name"* (`Ps 80[79]:19`, resonating verbally with `Isa 64:7`). On `Ps 85(84):13`, **Augustine** (*Enarr.* Ps 84, `psalm-84-coxe-10`, `psalm-84-coxe-11`, `psalm-84-coxe-12`) explains that "our earth" yields fruit only when confession of sin breaks open the soil and God pours down *benignitas* — **sweetness / delight in righteousness** replacing delight in sin — while **Bellarmine** (`psalm-84-verse-13`) anchors that fruitfulness in the Incarnation, where the Spirit overshadows the Virgin Mary so human earth yields Christ Himself.

### 3. Carrying Authors and Supporting Witnesses
- **Eligible carrying authors (`author-standing-v1.toml`):** **St. Thomas Aquinas** (`doctor`), **St. Augustine** (`father`), **St. Robert Bellarmine** (`doctor`), **St. John Chrysostom** (`father`).
- **Supporting witnesses:** **St. Bede** (`father`, on `Mark 13:34` *potestatem cujusque operis* = grace of the Holy Spirit), **St. Jerome** (`father`, on `Isa 64:8` clay/Potter), and **Cornelius a Lapide** (`ecclesiastical-writer`, on `1 Cor 1:8–9`).

### 4. Whole-Formulary Synthesis Across All Twelve Elements
- **Introductory Rites (`entrance-antiphon`, `collect`):** In *Ad te levavi* (`Ps 25[24]:1–3`), Augustine (*Enarr.* Ps 24, `24.1-2`) stresses that trusting in oneself brings shame while lifting the soul to God alone rescues it from carnal entanglement (`textual observation`, `documented reception`). The **Collect** (*Da, quaesumus, omnipotens Deus*; `seasonal or ritual`, illuminated historically by the Gelasian antecedent II.lxxxiv asking Almighty God for *voluntatem*) underscores the priority of grace: believers ask God to grant the very will to meet the coming Christ.
- **Liturgy of the Word (`first-reading`, `responsorial-psalm`, `second-reading`, `gospel-acclamation`, `gospel`):**
  - The **First Reading** (`Isa 63–64`) strips away illusions of self-righteousness (Aquinas's four stains on `64:6`) and leaves the assembly as clay before the Potter (`64:8`), remembering God's historical covenant descent (`64:3b`, deliverance from Egypt [`Exod. 4`] and the cliffs of Arnon [`Num. 21`] literally in Aquinas, after Sinai terror [`Exod. 20`] on `64:3a`).
  - The **Responsorial Psalm** (`Ps 80[79]`) moves from clay to vine (`responsorial`, `textual observation`): God Himself must turn us back (*converte nos*, v. 4) and perfect His vine upon the **Son of Man** (vv. 15–18, Augustine/Bellarmine), restoring the voice that Isaiah lamented had fallen silent (*"There is none that calleth upon thy name"*, `Isa 64:7` → *"and we will call upon thy name"*, `Ps 80[79]:19`).
  - The **Second Reading** (`1 Cor 1:3–9`) shows how God sustains frail, quarrelsome believers in the New Covenant: the faithful God (*Fidelis Deus*) calls them into the **fellowship (`koinonia`) of His Son** and enriches them with every grace, while warning against pride or presumption (Chrysostom, Lapide; `source-grounded synthesis`).
  - The **Gospel Acclamation** (`Ps 85[84]:8`, *Ostende nobis, Domine, misericordiam tuam*) grounds all salvation strictly in mercy, not desert (Augustine, `psalm-84-coxe-6`; `acclamatory`).
  - The **Gospel** (`Mark 13:33–37`) completes the circle of grace and cooperation: the Master does not demand work without endowment — He *"gave authority to his servants over every work"* (`13:34`), which **Bede** interprets explicitly as the **grace of the Holy Spirit** empowering every good work (`source-grounded synthesis`). Watching and praying (`13:33`) is therefore staying plastic to the Potter's hand and alert in the Spirit's gift.
- **Liturgy of the Eucharist and Concluding Rites (`prayer-over-the-offerings`, `preface`, `communion-antiphon`, `prayer-after-communion`, `concluding-blessing`):**
  - The **Prayer over the Offerings** (*Suscipe, quaesumus, Domine*, bounded comparison against *Veronense* XXVIIII.372) articulates the theology of grace in one clause: *munera quae de tuis offerimus collata beneficiis* — the Church offers back to God gifts drawn from His own prior generosity, asking Him to turn temporal devotion into the reward of eternal redemption (`seasonal or ritual`, `source-grounded synthesis`).
  - At the **Seasonal Preface** (`seasonal or ritual`: one of the two Prefaces of Advent verified in `ordo-missae-praefationes`, exact subtitle and text uninspected offline), the assembly gives thanks (`GIRM 364`) to the faithful God whose covenant ways Augustine (*Enarr.* Ps 24:10) identifies as *"mercy and truth"* across Christ's two Advents (`documented reception`).
  - The **Communion Antiphon** (`Ps 85[84]:13`, *Dominus dabit benignitatem, et terra nostra dabit fructum suum*) crowns the clay-and-vine arc (`seasonal or ritual`, `source-grounded synthesis`): Christ, the fruit born of Mary (Bellarmine), enters our earth in the Eucharist and pours out divine *sweetness* (Augustine, `psalm-84-coxe-12`) so sinful human soil actually bears fruit. The **Prayer after Communion** (*Prosint nobis*, closing the Communion Rite; protected body uninspected offline) and the **Concluding Blessing** (`GIRM 90`; `seasonal or ritual`) seal the Eucharistic celebration and send the assembly forth in perseverance through grace.

### 5. Four Senses of Scripture (Under Interpretation 2)
- **Literal:** Postexilic Israel confesses covenant failure and appeals to God's Exodus deliverance (`Exod. 4`, `Num. 21` in Aquinas) and Creator-Fatherhood (`Isa 63–64`); Israel begs the Shepherd of Joseph to visit and restore His ruined vine (`Ps 80[79]`); Paul grounds the gifted yet divided Corinthian church in God's grace and faithfulness (`1 Cor 1:3–9`); Jesus entrusts His household and its tasks to His servants (`Mark 13:33–37`).
- **Allegorical:** The vine perfected upon the *Son of Man* (`Ps 80[79]:16–18`, Augustine/Bellarmine) and the earth yielding her fruit through divine *benignitas* (`Ps 85[84]:13`, Bellarmine) signify the Church founded upon the Incarnate Christ born of the Virgin Mary, enriched with the Spirit's gifts (`1 Cor 1:5–7`; `Mark 13:34`, Bede).
- **Moral:** Because human works easily become stained by mortal sin, negligence, perverse intention, or pride (`Isa 64:6`, Aquinas) and *Fidelis Deus* never coerces unwilling hearts (`1 Cor 1:9`, Chrysostom/Lapide), the Christian lives in humble confession, yielding like clay to the Potter and cooperating vigilantly with the grace that makes righteousness sweet (Augustine on `Ps 84:13`).
- **Anagogical:** The faithful God who began the work in the fellowship of His Son (`1 Cor 1:9`) will confirm His persevering Church *sine crimine* ("without crime", `1:8`) on the Day of Christ's coming, transforming temporal devotion into eternal redemption (*Veronense* antecedent).

### 6. Limits and Trade-offs of Interpretation 2
- **Strength:** Directly engages the hardest theological cruxes in Isaiah 63–64 and 1 Corinthians 1 (hardening, defiled righteousness, grace vs. free will, perseverance), creating a concrete bridge across clay (`Isa 64:8`), vine (`Ps 80[79]:15–16`), *tuis beneficiis* (Offerings), and fruitful earth (`Ps 85[84]:13`).
- **Limit:** Because Mark 13:33–37 says little directly about sin or the Potter/clay metaphor, the Gospel plays a secondary confirmatory role (via Bede's *authority = grace* and prayerful vigilance) unless care is taken not to let the Pauline/Isaian grace theology overshadow Mark's urgent apocalyptic watchfulness.

## Interpretation 3: Vigilant Stewardship of the Household Between the Two Advents
*(Identifier: `vigilant-stewardship-between-two-advents`)*

### 1. Controlling Exegetical Question
Whom does Mark 13:33–37 command to "watch" — pastors alone, the final generation alive at the Parousia, or every believer across every age of life — and what concrete pattern of ecclesial vocation and moral action constitutes watching during the interval between Christ's First Advent in mercy and His Second Advent in judgment?

### 2. Consequential Passage-Level Textual Difficulties
1. **Mark 13:34–37 (*dedit servis suis potestatem cujusque operis, et janitori praecepit ut vigilet... sero, an media nocte, an galli cantu, an mane... Quod autem vobis dico, omnibus dico: Vigilate*):**
   - *Textual difficulties:* (a) Why does Christ single out a *janitor* ("porter") yet finish by saying *"I say to all: Watch"*? (b) Why does He name four specific watches of the Roman night? (c) How can *"I say to all"* apply genuinely to Christians of every century if only one generation will be physically alive at the Parousia?
   - *Principal witness inferences:*
     - **St. Bede** (*In Marcum* IV.13, lines 402–405) & **St. Gregory the Great** (*Hom. in Evang.* 1, 9, 13, lines 14–19, 90–97, 139–145; admitted `1.1-5-luke-21`, `13.1-6-luke-12`) distinguish the ordered **ecclesial stewardship** of the household: the *janitor* designates pastors and spiritual rulers charged with guarding the Church's gates, while v. 37 extends doorkeeping to **every believer** guarding the door of the heart against sin and torpor. Crucially, Bede (quoting Gregory Hom. 13) defines vigilance not as passive anxiety or date-speculation, but as **three concrete actions**: *"Vigilat qui ad aspectum veri luminis mentis oculos apertos tenet; vigilat qui servat operando quod credit; vigilat qui a se torporis et negligentiae tenebras repellit"* (keeping the mind's eyes open to the true light, keeping in deeds what one believes, and driving off the darkness of sloth and negligence).
     - **St. Gregory the Great** (*Hom. in Evang.* 13, on parallel Luke 12:35–40) maps the night watches onto the **ages of human life** (boyhood, youth, old age) — paralleled by **Theophylact of Ohrid** (mediated via Aquinas's *Catena Aurea in Marcum* 13:32–37: evening = old age, midnight = mid-youth, cockcrow = maturity of reason, morning = childhood) — proving that God's *patiens redditor* leaves no season of life either hopeless or exempt.
     - **St. Augustine** (*Epistula 199* §3, mediated via **St. Thomas Aquinas**, *Catena Aurea in Marcum* 13:32–37) solves the generational puzzle decisively: *"that day comes to each man when his day comes for departing from this life such as he is to be, when judged in that day."*
   - *What changes if accepted:* Mark 13:33–37 ceases to be a speculative end-times warning aimed only at a distant future generation; it becomes an immediate, lifelong charter of differentiated stewardship in Christ's household, culminating for every Christian at the hour of personal death and at the universal Parousia.
2. **Christ's Two Advents (`Ps 25[24]:10`, Cyril *Cat.* 15, Leo *Sermo 19*) and Active Righteousness (`Isa 64:5`, *Occurristi laetanti, et facienti justitiam*):**
   - *Textual difficulty:* How does the Church's interval between the First and Second Advents hold together mercy and judgment without sliding into either dread or laxity?
   - *Principal witness inferences:* **St. Cyril of Jerusalem** (*Catechesis 15* §§1, 2, 4, 26) sets forth the classic theology of the **two Advents** — the first in patience, swaddling clothes in the manger, and the Cross (§1); the second in light, a host of angels, glory, and the crown of a divine kingdom (§1), expounded from Malachi 3:1–2 (§2) — forbidding curiosity about dates (Acts 1:7, §4) while insisting that true watching means keeping lamps burning through works of mercy (Matt 25:35–41, §26). **St. Augustine** (*Enarr.* Ps 24:10, `24.10`, directly relevant to Advent Common Psalm `Ps 25[24]`) explicitly identifies *"all the ways of the Lord are mercy and truth"* with the **two Advents of the Son of God — one in mercy, the other in judgment**. **St. Leo the Great** (*Sermon 19* §§1–3) translates Advent watchfulness into concrete Roman parish discipline: guarding against surfeiting and worldly cares (Luke 21:34, §1) by joining bodily temperance with fasting of the mind from vice (§2) and generous almsgiving to the poor (§3).

### 3. Carrying Authors and Supporting Witnesses
- **Eligible carrying authors (`author-standing-v1.toml`):** **St. Bede** (`father`), **St. Gregory the Great** (`father`), **St. Augustine** (`father`), **St. Cyril of Jerusalem** (`father`), **St. Leo the Great** (`father`).
- **Supporting witnesses:** **St. Thomas Aquinas** (`doctor`, *Catena Aurea in Marcum* 13:32–37 mediating Augustine *Ep. 199*, Pseudo-Jerome, and Theophylact), **Theophylact of Ohrid** (`ecclesiastical-writer`), and **St. Robert Bellarmine** (`doctor`, on Ps 24:1–3 steadfast waiting and Ps 79:15 visitation).

### 4. Whole-Formulary Synthesis Across All Twelve Elements
- **Introductory Rites (`entrance-antiphon`, `collect`):** *Ad te levavi* (`Ps 25[24]:1–3`) tears the soul away from worldly entanglements (Bellarmine's briers and thorns; Augustine's carnal weight) at the start of the new liturgical year, fixing its gaze on the two ways of the Lord — mercy and judgment (Augustine, `24.10`; `seasonal or ritual`, `documented reception`). The **Collect** (*Da, quaesumus, omnipotens Deus*; `seasonal or ritual`, illuminated historically by the Gelasian antecedent II.lxxxiv: *venienti in operibus iustis aptos occurrere*) frames active righteous deeds as the mode of going out to meet the coming Lord, echoing `Isa 64:5` (*Occurristi laetanti, et facienti justitiam* — `textual observation`, `source-grounded synthesis`).
- **Liturgy of the Word (`first-reading`, `responsorial-psalm`, `second-reading`, `gospel-acclamation`, `gospel`):**
  - The **First Reading** (`Isa 63–64`) diagnoses the exact spiritual sleep the Gospel warns against: a people drifting from God's ways (`63:17`), swept away like fallen leaves (`64:6`), with *"none that riseth up, and taketh hold of thee"* (`64:7`), yet remembering that God *"hast met him that rejoiceth, and doth justice"* (`64:5`; `source-grounded synthesis`).
  - The **Responsorial Psalm** (`Ps 80[79]`) calls the Shepherd of Israel to stir up His might (`v. 3b`) and visit His vineyard (`v. 15`) — evoking Luke 19:44's warning not to miss the hour of visitation (Bellarmine on `Ps 79:15`, `documented reception`; `responsorial`) — so that the restored community vows steadfast fidelity (*"and we depart not from thee"*, `v. 19`).
  - The **Second Reading** (`1 Cor 1:3–9`) reveals that the household is fully equipped for its watch: the Corinthians lack no spiritual gift (`1:7`) while waiting for the Day of the Lord, yet must remain confirmed *"unto the end without crime"* (`1:8`) rather than fracturing the Master's house by party-spirit (Chrysostom on `1 Cor 1:4–10`; `source-grounded synthesis`).
  - The **Gospel Acclamation** (`Ps 85[84]:8`, *Ostende nobis, Domine, misericordiam tuam*) voices the watchful church praying between mercy and judgment (`acclamatory`).
  - The **Gospel** (`Mark 13:33–37`) anchors this reading: every pastor (*janitor*) and every parishioner (*omnibus dico*) has an assigned work (*cujusque operis*) across every watch of human life (Bede, Gregory, Theophylact), because each person's own hour of departure brings the Lord's Day to the threshold (Augustine *Ep. 199* via *Catena Aurea*), warning against chasing the phantoms of worldly attachment as in sleep (Pseudo-Jerome via *Catena Aurea*; Gregory Hom. 1 on a world grown old).
- **Liturgy of the Eucharist and Concluding Rites (`prayer-over-the-offerings`, `preface`, `communion-antiphon`, `prayer-after-communion`, `concluding-blessing`):**
  - The **Prayer over the Offerings** (*Suscipe, quaesumus, Domine*, bounded comparison against *Veronense* XXVIIII.372) consecrates temporal service (*devotioni... temporali*) toward eternal redemption — uniting present liturgical stewardship with the final reward (`seasonal or ritual`, `source-grounded synthesis`).
  - At the **Seasonal Preface** (`seasonal or ritual`: one of the two Prefaces of Advent verified in `ordo-missae-praefationes`, exact subtitle and text uninspected offline), the Church lifts up its thanksgiving (`GIRM 364`) within the two-Advent moral horizon expounded by Cyril of Jerusalem (*Cat.* 15.1–2, 4, 26: from the manger and Cross to the crown of a divine kingdom, prepared by watchful lamps and works of mercy) and Augustine (*Enarr.* Ps 24:10; `documented reception`).
  - The **Communion Antiphon** (`Ps 85[84]:13`, *Dominus dabit benignitatem, et terra nostra dabit fructum suum*) strengthens the watchmen with Eucharistic sustenance so their lives bear concrete fruit of justice and charity (Leo *Sermo 19*; Augustine, `psalm-84-coxe-12`; `seasonal or ritual`, `source-grounded synthesis`). The **Prayer after Communion** (*Prosint nobis*, closing the Communion Rite; protected modern body uninspected offline) and the **Concluding Blessing** (ordinary blessing or optional solemn blessing / prayer over the people, `GIRM 90`; `seasonal or ritual`) commission the assembly for daily faithful works upon dismissal (*GIRM 90*: *"so that each may go back to doing good works, praising and blessing God"*).

### 5. Four Senses of Scripture (Under Interpretation 3)
- **Literal:** Isaiah laments a complacent, drifting Israel and remembers God meeting those who do justice (`Isa 63–64`); the Psalmist pleads for God to visit His vineyard and bind His people not to turn away (`Ps 80[79]`); Paul charges a gifted urban assembly to persevere blameless until the Day of Christ (`1 Cor 1:3–9`); Jesus on Mount Olivet delivers His parting charge of watchfulness to the disciples and to all (`Mark 13:33–37`).
- **Allegorical:** The household entrusted to servants and a doorkeeper (`Mark 13:34`, Bede) and the vineyard visited by God (`Ps 80[79]:15`, Bellarmine/Augustine) signify the pilgrim Church structured with pastors and faithful operating between Christ's First Advent in humility and His Second Advent in glory (Cyril of Jerusalem *Cat.* 15.1–2; Augustine *Enarr.* Ps 24:10).
- **Moral:** Watching means concrete active fidelity — keeping the mind's eyes open to truth, putting faith into deeds, driving off torpor (Bede/Gregory), practicing sobriety, fasting from vice, and feeding the poor (Leo *Sermo 19*) across every watch of life from childhood to old age (Gregory *Hom.* 13; Theophylact).
- **Anagogical:** Each believer's hour of death (`Ep. 199`, Augustine via *Catena Aurea*) and the universal Parousia (`1 Cor 1:8`; Cyril *Cat.* 15.1, 26) converge as the Master's sudden return, when servants found awake in works of justice are gathered to Christ's right hand (*Gelasianum Vetus* II.lxxxiv historical antecedent) to inherit the heavenly kingdom.

### 6. Limits and Trade-offs of Interpretation 3
- **Strength:** Puts the Gospel pericope (`Mark 13:33–37`) and patristic two-Advent theology (Cyril *Cat.* 15; Augustine *Enarr.* Ps 24:10) directly at the center, provides immediate concrete pastoral guidance across every life stage of an adult parish assembly, and integrates the Gelasian Collect antecedent (*in operibus iustis aptos occurrere*) and Roman Advent discipline (Leo *Sermo 19*) seamlessly.
- **Limit:** Can feel overly moralistic or activist if detached from Interpretation 2's radical confession of human frailty (`Isa 64:6–8`) and priority of grace (`1 Cor 1:4–9`) or Interpretation 1's consolation in the face of God's apparent silence (`Isa 63:17`).
