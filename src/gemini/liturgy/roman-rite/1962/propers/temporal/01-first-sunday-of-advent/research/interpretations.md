# Whole-Formulary Interpretations: First Sunday of Advent (1962 *Missale Romanum*)

This record develops **three genuinely distinct, coherent whole-formulary interpretations** of *Dominica I Adventus* in the 1962 *Missale Romanum*. Each reading asks a different governing exegetical question, turns on specific textual difficulties in the appointed Latin and Greek Scripture, rests on at least two distinct compatible carrying authors registered in `src/sources/inventories/author-standing-v1.toml` (with at least one Church Father in every reading), keeps verified author exegesis strictly separate from editorial whole-formulary synthesis, accounts substantively for all ten proper elements plus the governed Ordinary variables, and yields its own distinct Literal, Allegorical, Moral, and Anagogical four-sense profile.

---

## Interpretation 1: Eschatological Detachment and Vigilant Judgment — *The World Grown Old and the Coming of the Strict Judge*

### 1.1 Controlling Exegetical Question and Unifying Thesis
- **Governing exegetical question:** How can cosmic collapse and the terrifying signs of universal judgment (Luke 21:25–27) become a direct command to *rejoice and lift up your heads* (Luke 21:28) — and what "fruit" does the fig tree put forth when the world is visibly falling apart (Luke 21:29–30)?
- **Unifying thesis:** The Roman Church opens the liturgical year by breaking the spell of an aging, passing world. Because human sin already surrounds the faithful with pressing danger (*imminentibus peccatorum nostrorum periculis*), Advent begins not with sentimental anticipation but with an urgent midnight summons: detach your heart from a decaying age, throw off nocturnal vice, put on the glittering armor of light, and await with unashamed perseverance the strict Judge whose coming brings terror to lovers of the world and final redemption — including bodily resurrection and cosmic renewal — to those who love His appearing.

### 1.2 Passage-Level Difficulty Tests
1. **Luke 21:28–30 (*levate capita vestra*; *Videte ficulneam... cum producunt jam ex se fructum*) and v. 33 (*verba autem mea non transibunt*):**
   - *The difficulty:* Why does Christ command joy ("lift up your heads") at the sight of men withering for terror? Why compare cosmic dissolution to trees putting forth fruit? And why contrast heaven and earth with spoken words?
   - *Verified patristic reasoning:*
     - **St. Gregory the Great** (*Homiliae in Evangelia*, Hom. 1 §§1–6) resolves all three knots sharply. First, *virtutes caelorum* are the angelic choirs who will appear visibly as ministers of the strict Judge, shaking even the upright with awe (§2). Second, *levate capita* means *exhilarate corda* ("gladden your hearts", §3): "head" signifies the mind, and whoever loves the world proves himself God's enemy (Jas 4:4); only one who treats mortal life as a road rather than a home rejoices when the world's end signals the approach of his Friend. Third, in the same section (§3), Gregory gives the fig tree a piercing ironic turn: *"fructus mundi ruina est"* — **"the fruit of the world is its ruin"**! Just as ripening fruit announces summer, the world's decay announces the eternal summer of God's kingdom clearing away the clouds of earthly sorrow. Fourth, v. 33 sets up a deliberate paradox (§4): among material things nothing seems more durable than sky and earth, and among human things nothing passes more swiftly than a spoken utterance; yet God's spoken word abides eternally while the physical frame passes. Fifth, Gregory applies the reading to Rome's aging world and the sudden whirlwind two days prior (*nudius tertius*, *subito turbine*, §5), concluding that present terrors are merely the herald (*sequentis irae praecones*) running before the Judge (§6).
     - **St. Cyril of Alexandria** (*Commentary on Luke*, Sermon CXXXIX) marks v. 25 as the pivot from Jerusalem's historical siege to the universal consummation and anchors v. 28's *"your redemption is near"* concretely in **bodily resurrection and cosmic renewal**: the earthly body puts off corruption to put on incorruption and be conformed to Christ's glorious body (joining 2 Pet 3:10–11 and Luke 21:36).
     - **St. Bede** (*In Lucae Evangelium Expositio* VI, on vv. 28–29, 32–33) preserves Gregory's reading of vv. 28–29, explains *generatio haec* (v. 32) as either the whole human race or specifically the Jewish people, and directly resolves the apparent contradiction between Luke 21:33 and Eccl 1:4 (*terra in aeternum stat*): heaven and earth pass away *per imaginem* (in their outward figure, 1 Cor 7:31), not *per essentiam*, cleansed of their present appearance by fire (*ab ea quam nunc habet specie per ignem tergetur*) while preserved in their nature (*in sua semper natura servabitur*), with only the lower atmospheric heaven (*aereum*, not *aethereum sive sidereum*) perishing as in the Flood (2 Pet 3:5–7), foreshadowed by the alternation of night/day and winter/spring.
     - **St. Augustine** (*De civitate Dei* XX.14, 16, 24, NPNF I.2, trans. Marcus Dods) supplies the distinct patristic foundations that Bede synthesizes onto Luke 21:33 (without Augustine himself expounding Luke 21:33 directly): in **XX.14** (expounding Apoc 20:9–13 [esp. v. 11], anticipating Apoc 21:1, and quoting 1 Cor 7:31–32), Augustine teaches that *"this world shall pass away by transmutation, not by absolute destruction"* and *"The figure, therefore, passes away, not the nature"*; in **XX.16** (expounding Apoc 21:1 alongside Apoc 20:11 and 1 Cor 7:31), he teaches that *"then shall the figure of this world pass away in a conflagration of universal fire... so that, as the world itself is renewed to some better thing, it is fitly accommodated to men, themselves renewed in their flesh to some better thing"*; and in **XX.24** (arguing against Porphyry from Ps 101:26–28 [Heb. 102:25–27], citing 1 Cor 7:31, 1 John 2:17, and Matt 24:35 — not Luke 21:33 — in passing as milder expressions, and correlating 2 Pet 3:6–11 with the Flood, before expounding Ps 49:3–5), he argues that only the lower atmospheric heavens perish while the starry heavens remain intact.
2. **Romans 13:11–14a (*Nunc enim propior est nostra salus*; *opera tenebrarum* vs. *arma lucis*; *induimini Dominum Jesum Christum*):**
   - *The difficulty:* Why does Paul say salvation is "nearer than when we believed," and why contrast *works* of darkness with *arms* (not merely "works") of light?
   - *Verified patristic reasoning:* **St. John Chrysostom** (*Homilies on Romans*, Hom. 24) explains that v. 11 points to the approach of resurrection and judgment — as runners intensify their stride when the crown comes into view, Christians must awaken as dawn breaks (like neighbors rousing one another at the swallow's morning twitter). Paul switches from *works* of darkness to *arms* of light because virtue is a vigilant warfare whose bright armor makes the believer "at once soldier and bridegroom," turning combat into solemn festivity; v. 13 boldly shifts from "near" to "already day" so believers shed gluttony and drunkenness (the twin roots of lust and wrath) and ascend in v. 14a from *works* to *arms* to **putting on the King Himself**, who dwells within and clothes without.
   - **St. Leo the Great** (*Sermons* 19.1, 12.4) directly expounds the verse immediately following our Gospel (Luke 21:34) for Advent vigilance: fasting, prayer, and almsgiving keep the heart from being weighed down by surfeiting, drunkenness, and worldly cares before that sudden Day.
- *What changes if this reading is adopted:* Advent I stands squarely as an **eschatological wake-up call** bridging the end of the old liturgical year (Last Sunday after Pentecost, Matt 24) and the beginning of the new; comfort comes *only through detachment from a passing world*, and Christmas preparation is framed under the horizon of the Final Judgment.

### 1.3 Carrying Authors vs. Editorial Whole-Formulary Synthesis
- **Compatible carrying authors (all registered in `author-standing-v1.toml`):** **St. Gregory the Great** (`father` — Luke 21:25–33), **St. John Chrysostom** (`father` — Rom 13:11–14a), **St. Cyril of Alexandria** (`father` — Luke 21:25–28), **St. Bede** (`father` — Luke 21:28–33, synthesizing onto Luke 21:33 the eschatological teaching of **St. Augustine** `father`, *De civ. Dei* XX.14, 16, 24 on transmutation/renewal and the lower atmospheric heavens), **St. Leo the Great** (`father` — Luke 21:34 Advent vigilance), with **St. Robert Bellarmine** (`doctor` — Ps 24:1–5 tearing the soul from earthly briers toward certain hope).
- **Supporting witnesses:** Cornelius a Lapide (Antwerp 1614, pp. 195–198: *arma lucis* as offensive and defensive virtues against world, flesh, and devil on p. 196; *Non in comessationibus* / *komos* and *Induimini* on pp. 197–198), William Durandus (*Rationale* VI.2–3: *Gloria* silenced while *Alleluia* sounds because Advent is *partim exultationis, et partim moeroris*; cantor's rising melody on *Ad te levavi* rousing sleepers).
- **Explicit boundary (Verified Author Exegesis vs. Editorial Synthesis):** Gregory expounds Luke 21:25–33 (originally for *Dominica secunda Adventus*), Chrysostom expounds Rom 13:11–14, Cyril expounds Luke 21:25–28, Bede expounds Luke 21:28–33 (synthesizing Augustinian arguments directly onto v. 33), Augustine (*De civ. Dei* XX.14, 16, 24) expounds Apoc 20:9–13, Apoc 21:1, 1 Cor 7:31–32, Ps 101:26–28, 2 Pet 3:6–11, and Ps 49:3–5 (citing Matt 24:35, not Luke 21:33, in passing in XX.24), Leo expounds Luke 21:34, and Bellarmine expounds Ps 24; none of them wrote a commentary on the full ten-element 1962 formulary as a single unit. Linking their exegetical threads across all ten elements and the governed Ordinary below is **explicitly editorial whole-formulary synthesis**.

### 1.4 Whole-Formulary Contribution (All 10 Elements + Governed Ordinary)
- **Introit (Ps 24:1–3, 4b [Heb. Ps 25:1–3a, 4]):** Lifting the soul off earthly ground (*Ad te levavi*), tearing free from the briers of creatures (Bellarmine), trusting not to be put to shame before mocking enemies because *universi qui te exspectant* — all who endure in watchful expectation — will never be confounded.
- **Collect (*Excita, quaesumus...*):** Names the emergency immediately: *imminentibus peccatorum nostrorum periculis* — dangers brought on by our own sins pressing in right now, from which only Christ's roused power can snatch (*eripi*) and save (*salvari*) us.
- **Epistle (Rom 13:11–14a):** Midnight trumpet rousing the church from spiritual sleep: the night of this passing age is far spent, the Day of Judgment is at hand; cast off nocturnal revelry, strife, and impurity, put on the glittering armor of light and Christ the King Himself (Chrysostom).
- **Gradual (Ps 24:3b, 4b [Heb. Ps 25:3a, 4]):** Repeats the promise of unashamed waiting (*qui te exspectant*) and begs for practical instruction along God's ways (*notas fac mihi*) so the vigilant soul does not lose the road during the night watch.
- **Alleluia (Ps 84:8):** Even amid violet sobriety and silent *Gloria*, the Church keeps *Alleluia* on Sunday (*partim exultationis, et partim moeroris*, Durandus) to cry for the Judge who is also *salutare tuum* — our Savior.
- **Gospel (Luke 21:25–33):** The centerpiece of eschatological detachment: cosmic signs, men withering in terror, the Son of Man coming on the cloud; yet disciples who do not love the decaying world lift up their heads (*exhilarate corda*, Gregory) because its ruin is the summer sign of final redemption and bodily resurrection (Cyril), anchored in Christ's unfailing Word (Gregory, Bede).
- **Offertory (Ps 24:1–3 [Heb. Ps 25:1–3a]):** Returns to *Ad te levavi* at the altar: offering bread and wine becomes an act of transferring one's allegiance out of a passing world into God's keeping.
- **Secret (*Haec sacra nos...*):** Prays that the offered Mysteries cleanse us by their mighty power (*potenti virtute mundatos*) so we may stand purer before our Source (*principium*) — the very Judge whose coming the Gospel announced.
- **Communion (Ps 84:13):** God's *benignitas* sustains faithful endurance during the watch so that our mortal ground yields fruits worthy of the coming Kingdom rather than the world's barren ruin.
- **Postcommunion (*Suscipiamus, Domine...*):** Receives God's mercy within His temple now so we may go before the coming feast of our reparation with fitting reverence (*congruis honoribus*) — watching in sobriety for both Bethlehem and the Parousia.
- **Governed Ordinary:** Violet vestments and omitted *Gloria in excelsis* mark penitential watchfulness; *Credo* confesses the coming Judge ("who shall come again with glory to judge the living and the dead"); the **Trinity Preface** lifts the assembly into the eternal praise of the unshaken angelic choirs (contrasting with the shaken *virtutes caelorum* of Luke 21:26!).

### 1.5 Four Senses (Interpretation 1)
- **Literal:** David's prayer of unashamed trust in God's ways (Ps 24); post-exilic Israel's plea for restored mercy and fruitfulness (Ps 84); Paul's A.D. 57–58 exhortation to Roman Christians to abandon nocturnal vice because the Day approaches (Rom 13:11–14a); Jesus' Passion-Week Temple discourse foretelling cosmic convulsions and the Son of Man's return (Luke 21:25–33).
- **Allegorical:** The *virtutes caelorum* signify angelic ministers of strict judgment (Gregory); the *arma lucis* signify Christian virtues worn as Christ Himself (Chrysostom); the fig tree's "fruit" signifies the world's own ruin signaling the approach of Christ's Kingdom (Gregory).
- **Moral:** Detachment from a decaying age, sobriety and purity against gluttony, lust, and faction (Rom 13:13; Leo Serm. 19.1), and patient perseverance (*exspectatio*) in God's narrow paths without shame before enemies.
- **Anagogical:** The Parousia of the Son of Man in majesty, the renewal of heaven and earth *per imaginem* rather than destruction *per essentiam* as creation is cleansed by fire (St. Bede on Luke 21:33, *per ignem tergetur*; cf. St. Augustine, *De civ. Dei* XX.14, 16 on transmutation and renewal through universal fire), the bodily resurrection of the saints into incorruption (Cyril), and eternal summer in the Kingdom of God (Gregory).

### 1.6 Limits and Intra-Reading Nuances
- Gregory's stark world-renunciation (*fructus mundi ruina est*) is tempered within the reading itself by Cyril's emphasis on positive bodily/cosmic renewal and the clarification of St. Bede (Luke 21:33: *per imaginem*, not *per essentiam*; *per ignem tergetur*) and St. Augustine (*De civ. Dei* XX.14, 16: transmutation and fiery renewal of the world's figure, not destruction of its nature). On its own, Interpretation 1 gives less weight to the Basilica of St. Mary Major station and to the explicitly Incarnational/Marian exegesis of Ps 84:13 and Luke 21:27.

## Interpretation 2: Incarnational and Marian Fulfillment — *Truth Sprung from Our Earth and the Cloud Overshadowed by the Spirit*

### 2.1 Controlling Exegetical Question and Unifying Thesis
- **Governing exegetical question:** Why does the Roman Missal station the opening Mass of Advent at **St. Mary Major** (*ad praesepe*) and pray directly for *"the coming festival of our reparation"* (Postcommunion) beside *"our earth shall yield her fruit"* (Communion, Ps 84:13) — and what do the **"cloud"** (Luke 21:27) and Luke's unique **"and all the trees"** (Luke 21:29) mean when read through the mystery of the Incarnation?
- **Unifying thesis:** The First Sunday of Advent binds the Church's expectation of the final Parousia inseparably to the historical Incarnation through the Virgin Mary. The same eternal Son whom the Collect invokes directly (*Qui vivis*), whom the Trinity Preface confesses as co-equal with Father and Spirit, and whom the Alleluia pleads for as *salutare tuum*, came first as Fruit of our virgin earth (*terra nostra*, Ps 84:13) and on the "light cloud" of Mary overshadowed by the Holy Spirit (Luke 21:27, Ambrose), so that the Church and all nations — "all the trees" of Luke 21:29 — might blossom with the harvest of redemption before He comes again in majesty.

### 2.2 Passage-Level Difficulty Tests
1. **Luke 21:25–30 (*signa in sole, et luna, et stellis*; *venientem in nube*; Luke's unique *et omnes arbores* against Matt 24:32's sole fig tree):**
   - *The difficulty:* Why does Luke add *et omnes arbores* ("and all the trees") where Matthew mentions only the fig tree? Why does Christ come *in nube* ("in a cloud")? And how can heavenly powers be "moved" if angels are steadfast in bliss?
   - *Verified patristic reasoning:* **St. Ambrose of Milan** (*Expositio Evangelii secundum Lucam* X.37–45) constructs a sustained christological, ecclesial, and Marian exegesis that diverges sharply from Gregory's literal-catastrophic reading:
     - The signs in sun, moon, and stars signify the obscuring of **Christ** (the Sun of justice) by unbelief, the **Church** (the moon) darkened by carnal vice, and the **saints/patriarchs** (the stars) falling from men's sight; *arescentibus* ("withering") signifies the drying up of baptismal dew (*aret perfidia, fides rorat*, X.37–38).
     - The *virtutes caelorum* are "moved" not in mere panic, but by an **increase of grace** as Christ draws closer — an advent that happens both at the consummation of the world and *in each individual soul* (*in singulis*) that receives Him (X.39–40).
     - Rejecting a mere physical rain-cloud, Ambrose identifies the ***nube*** ("cloud") of v. 27 with the **prophets** and explicitly with **the Virgin Mary** — linking Isaiah 19:1 (*"Behold the Lord sits upon a light cloud"*) directly to Luke 1:35 (*"the power of the Most High shall overshadow thee"*, X.42)!
     - Confronting Luke's unique addition ***et omnes arbores*** beside Matthew's solitary fig tree, Ambrose proposes a deliberate *double figure* (X.43–45): positively, **the fig tree = Israel** and **all the trees = all the nations of the Church**, whose burgeoning fruit of faith announces the summer harvest of resurrection (the exact reverse of Gregory's ironic *fructus mundi ruina est*!); negatively, a barren fig tree clothed only in leaves warns against the fraud of sin and Antichrist (Gen 3:7).
2. **Psalm 84:8 (*salutare tuum da nobis*) and Psalm 84:13 (*Dominus dabit benignitatem: et terra nostra dabit fructum suum*):**
   - *The difficulty:* How can sinful, barren human "earth" yield saving fruit — and what is the precise causal link between God's *benignitas* and *terra nostra*?
   - *Verified patristic and Doctoral reasoning:*
     - **St. Robert Bellarmine** (*Explanatio in Psalmos*, on Ps 84:8 and 84:13) reads both chants explicitly of the **Incarnation**: in v. 8, *salutare tuum* is the Incarnate Son Himself (citing Titus 2:11, 3:4); in v. 13, ***benignitas*** is the **Holy Spirit** sent from heaven, and ***terra nostra*** ("our earth") is the **untouched Virgin Mary** — virgin soil neither ploughed nor sown by human labor, which by the Spirit's goodness yields **Christ**, the Flower of the field and true Fruit of our humanity!
     - **St. Augustine** (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* Ps 84 §10 [NPNF] & *Sermo 185.1*) grounds the Incarnational sense directly in the immediately preceding verse, Ps 84:12 (*Veritas de terra orta est*): Truth (Christ, John 14:6) sprang from our earth by being born of Virgin flesh so that the immortal Word could offer Himself in sacrifice — prompting Augustine's Advent-like cry in *Sermo 185.1*: *"Expergiscere, homo... Surge, qui dormis!"*
     - **St. Bernard of Clairvaux** (*In laudibus Virginis Matris / Missus est*, Hom. 1, Browne & Nolan 1921, pp. 56, 58–60, esp. p. 59; *Second Sermon for Advent*, p. 19; *Sermones in Adventu* 4.1, 5.1 — checked tracked texts; *note: `bernard-of-clairvaux` standing-table registration flagged in `scope.md`*) traces the organic ripening of salvation history on pp. 58–60 (especially p. 59): the divine promises to the patriarchs are the seed, the Old Testament figures and wonders are the blossom of Nazareth, and **Christ is the Fruit**, quoting Ps 84:13 (*"The Lord will give goodness, and our earth shall yield her Fruit"*) beside Ps 131:11 (*"Of the Fruit of thy womb I will set upon thy throne"*) as God's promise to David (without explicitly equating "our earth" with Mary's womb on p. 59; in the same volume's *Second Sermon for Advent*, p. 19, Bernard separately compares Mary's inviolate womb to an unploughed, unsown field yielding the divine Flower of Isa 11:1 and Song 2:1). Meanwhile, *In Adventu* 4.1 exhorts meditating on both the first and second advents together — pondering *"how much we have received by the first, how much we are promised at the second"* and comparing these two comings to the Bridegroom's two arms (citing Song 2:6 and Prov 3:16) — while *In Adventu* 5.1 contrasts the visible advents directly: *"in the first He came in the flesh and in weakness ... and in the third He shall come in glory and majesty"* (with the hidden intermediate advent *"in spirit and in virtue"* serving as the way between them).
- *What changes if this reading is adopted:* Advent I ceases to be a purely negative warning against an ending world; instead, the Gospel's "cloud" and "blossoming trees" and the Communion's "earth yielding fruit" illuminate the **Station at St. Mary Major** and show that the Second Coming is the cosmic ripening of the Incarnation already planted in Mary's womb.

### 2.3 Carrying Authors vs. Editorial Whole-Formulary Synthesis
- **Compatible carrying authors (registered in `author-standing-v1.toml`):** **St. Ambrose of Milan** (`father` — Luke 21:25–30 ecclesial/Marian *nube* and blossoming nations), **St. Robert Bellarmine** (`doctor` — Ps 84:8 Christological *salutare*, Ps 84:13 Marian *terra nostra*, Ps 79:3 Collect echo *Excita potentiam tuam*), **St. Augustine of Hippo** (`father` — *Sermo 185.1* & *Enarr. in Ps. 84* §10 on *Veritas de terra orta est* and Christ born of Virgin flesh).
- **Additional checked Doctor (awaiting `author-standing-v1.toml` row):** **St. Bernard of Clairvaux** (*Missus est* Hom. 1, pp. 56, 58–60, esp. p. 59; *In Adventu* 2, p. 19; *In Adventu* 4.1, 5.1).
- **Supporting liturgical witnesses:** Sicard of Cremona (*Mitrale* V.2, cited strictly for his Marian/Incarnational reading of the shared Communion verse Ps 84:13, never for any connection to Luke 21:25–33 and without mentioning his differing Gospel in reader-facing prose per `guidance/liturgy/propers-three-documents.md`), Dom Prosper Guéranger (*Liturgical Year* I, Dublin: Duffy, 1870, pp. 127–138 [Mass pp. 130–138], St. Mary Major station p. 127 and Communion p. 137 calling Mary "made fruitful by the dew of heaven" after Isaiah alongside Eucharistic anticipation, omitting abolished pre-1955 extra orations), William Durandus (*Rationale* VI.3 on Rom 13:14, cited strictly for elements his Mass shares with 1962 Advent I).
- **Explicit boundary (Verified Author Exegesis vs. Editorial Synthesis):** Ambrose's Marian/ecclesial Luke 21 exegesis, Bellarmine's Marian Ps 84:13 exegesis, Bernard's Christological-Fruit exposition of Ps 84:13 (*Missus est* Hom. 1, pp. 56, 58–60, esp. p. 59) alongside his Marian unploughed-field image (*In Adventu* 2, p. 19), and Augustine's Incarnational Ps 84:12 exegesis are verified in their own loci; weaving them together with the Roman Station at St. Mary Major, the Trinity Preface, and all ten elements of the 1962 Mass is **editorial whole-formulary synthesis**.

### 2.4 Whole-Formulary Contribution (All 10 Elements + Governed Ordinary)
- **Station & Opening Context (*Statio ad S. Mariam Maiorem*):** Anchors the entire Mass at Rome's great Marian basilica of the Crib (*ad praesepe*), immediately orienting the assembly toward the Mother who bore the Coming One.
- **Introit (Ps 24:1–3, 4b [Heb. Ps 25:1–3a, 4]):** The voice of waiting Israel and the whole Church (*totus Christus*, Augustine) lifting up her soul across the centuries of expectation for the promised Messiah (*qui te exspectant*).
- **Collect (*Excita, quaesumus...*):** Addressed directly to Christ the eternal Son (*Qui vivis et regnas cum Deo Patre*), echoing Ps 79:3 (*Excita potentiam tuam, et veni*, Bellarmine) — begging the Word to rouse His divine power and come to save humanity from sin's peril.
- **Epistle (Rom 13:11–14a):** The dawn has broken because Christ has entered history (*dies autem appropinquavit*); "put on the Lord Jesus Christ" is only possible because the Son first put on our human flesh (Durandus VI.3), clothing believers in His own light.
- **Gradual (Ps 24:3b, 4b [Heb. Ps 25:3a, 4]):** Long-suffering hope of the patriarchs and prophets (*universi qui te exspectant*) asking the Lord to make known the paths by which His salvation comes.
- **Alleluia (Ps 84:8):** The direct cry for the Incarnation — *Ostende nobis, Domine, misericordiam tuam: et salutare tuum da nobis* ("grant us Thy Savior, Thy Christ", Augustine & Bellarmine).
- **Gospel (Luke 21:25–33):** Read through St. Ambrose (X.37–45): when unbelief obscures the Sun and dries up baptismal grace, the Son of Man comes on the "light cloud" — the prophets and **the Virgin Mary** overshadowed by the Spirit — moving the heavenly powers with an increase of grace, until Israel (the fig tree) and all Gentile nations ("all the trees") put forth the true fruit of faith announcing the summer of resurrection.
- **Offertory (Ps 24:1–3 [Heb. Ps 25:1–3a]):** At the altar of St. Mary Major, the Church offers earthly bread and wine in the posture of unashamed trust, waiting for God to transform the fruit of the earth into the Incarnate Son.
- **Secret (*Haec sacra nos...*):** Prays that these Mysteries make us come purer to their *principium* — the divine Source and Author from whom both the Incarnation and the Eucharist proceed.
- **Communion (Ps 84:13):** The climactic Incarnational and Marian verse of the formulary: *Dominus dabit benignitatem* (in Bellarmine, the goodness of the Holy Spirit) *et terra nostra dabit fructum suum* — our untouched virgin earth, Mary, yields her blessed Fruit, Christ Himself (Bellarmine on Ps 84:13; Sicard; Guéranger p. 137, who calls Mary "made fruitful by the dew of heaven" after Isaiah and notes Christ's Eucharistic anticipation; alongside Bernard's *Missus est* Hom. 1, p. 59 on Christ as the Fruit of Ps 84:13 and Ps 131:11, and *In Adventu* 2, p. 19 on Mary's womb as an unploughed field).
- **Postcommunion (*Suscipiamus, Domine...*):** Explicitly names the liturgical destination of Advent — *reparationis nostrae ventura solemnia* ("the coming festival of our reparation," i.e., Christmas) — uniting Temple mercy (Ps 47:10 [Heb. 48:10; modern English 48:9] echo) with reverent readiness for the Nativity.
- **Governed Ordinary:** The **Trinity Preface** confesses the co-equal divinity of the Father, the only-begotten Son, and the Holy Spirit — the Trinitarian fountain of the Incarnation (Spirit overshadowing Mary to conceive the Son of the Father); silent *Gloria* holds back the angels' Bethlehem hymn (*Gloria in excelsis Deo*) until Christmas night!

### 2.5 Four Senses (Interpretation 2)
- **Literal:** Israel's yearning for God's deliverance and promised Messiah (Pss 24, 84); Paul's call to live in the newly dawned day of Christ (Rom 13:11–14a); Jesus' prophecy of the Son of Man's glorious advent and the sprouting trees (Luke 21:25–33); the Roman Church's prayers preparing for the coming feast of reparation (Collect, Secret, Postcommunion).
- **Allegorical:** The *salutare* (Ps 84:8) is Christ incarnate (Augustine, Bellarmine); the *nube* (Luke 21:27) signifies the prophets and the Virgin Mary overshadowed by the Spirit (Ambrose); *benignitas* is the Holy Spirit and *terra nostra* is Mary bearing Christ the Fruit (Bellarmine; complemented by Bernard's exposition of Christ as the Fruit of Ps 84:13 and Ps 131:11 in *Missus est* Hom. 1, p. 59, and Mary's womb as an unploughed field in *In Adventu* 2, p. 19); the fig tree and *all the trees* signify Israel and all Gentile peoples blossoming with faith (Ambrose).
- **Moral:** Receiving Christ's coming within each soul (*in singulis*, Ambrose X.39–40), preserving the dew of baptismal faith against the drought of unbelief (*fides rorat*, Ambrose X.38), putting on Christ in daily conduct (Rom 13:14a), and preparing with reverent purity for the Christmas mysteries.
- **Anagogical:** The final harvest ("summer") of the resurrection when the seed planted at the Incarnation reaches its full cosmic flowering in the visible Kingdom of God, adored eternally with the angels of the Trinity Preface.

### 2.6 Limits and Intra-Reading Nuances
- Ambrose himself keeps the warning side of Luke 21:29 alive (a barren fig tree bearing only leaves of deceit, Gen 3:7), so Interpretation 2 does not erase judgment. Its limitation is that the Gospel's plain literal surface ("men withering for fear," "roaring of the sea and waves") is more immediately captured by Interpretation 1, while Ambrose's reading is strongly symbolic/allegorical.

## Interpretation 3: Interior Conversion, Prevenient Grace, and the Sacramental Middle Coming — *The Soil of Confession, the Path of Grace, and the Altar Bridge*

### 3.1 Controlling Exegetical Question and Unifying Thesis
- **Governing exegetical question:** Between Bethlehem's historical past and the Last Day's terrifying future, what actually happens *today* to sinners surrounded by pressing perils (*imminentibus peccatorum nostrorum periculis*) — how does human "earth" actually yield fruit (Ps 84:13), how does a sinner walk God's ways without being put to shame (Ps 24:2–4), and how does the altar bridge the First and Second Comings?
- **Unifying thesis:** The First Sunday of Advent is the liturgy of the **Middle Coming** — Christ's hidden, healing advent in grace, interior conversion, and the Eucharistic Mysteries. Because salvation is wholly God's gift, grace must first humble pride, awaken interior delight in righteousness (*suavitas*, Augustine), furrow the hardened heart with confession, teach the soul both the broad highway of commandments and the narrow shortcut of counsels (Aquinas), and purify worshipers through the offered Mysteries (*Haec sacra*, Schuster) so that Christ's present advent in spirit and sacrament makes us fit to meet Him in glory.

### 3.2 Passage-Level Difficulty Tests
1. **Psalm 84:8 & 84:13 (*Ostende nobis... misericordiam*; Old Latin *suavitatem* vs. Gallican *benignitatem*; *terra nostra dabit fructum suum*):**
   - *The difficulty:* Why does the psalmist ask God to *show* mercy (v. 8) as though mercy were hidden, and how can our earth yield *its own* fruit (*fructum suum*, v. 13) if all good comes from God?
   - *Verified patristic reasoning:* **St. Augustine** (*Enarrationes in Psalmos*, Ps 84 §§6, 11–13 [PL §§9, 14–16]) solves both difficulties through his doctrine of **prevenient and healing grace** — directly contrasting with Bellarmine's Marian reading of v. 13 and Bernard's Christological-Fruit reading of v. 13!
     - On v. 8, God "shows" His mercy by proving to proud humanity that *every good we possess is His gift*: once a man knows this, he ceases to boast, stands firm, clings to God, abides, and rejoices (§6 [PL §9]).
     - On v. 12–13, Augustine reads *Veritas de terra orta est* penitentially as well as Incarnationally: truth springs from earth when the sinner **confesses his sins** rather than excusing them! Reading his Old Latin Psalter's ***suavitatem*** ("sweetness") against Gallican *benignitatem*, Augustine explains that God pours interior **delight in righteousness** into the soul — displacing the bitter-sweet lure of sin — like rain watering a field freshly furrowed by the **plough of confession**, so that our own soil (*terra nostra*) yields the fruit of amended lives, opening the very road before Christ's footsteps (v. 14 / Matt 3:3, §§11–13)!
     - **St. Leo the Great** (*Sermon* 17.1) echoes this exact interior-soil theology in Advent preaching: fasting and almsgiving cultivate *"the ground of our heart"* so it brings forth *"the germ of righteousness and the fruit of love."*
2. **Psalm 24:1–4 (*Ad te levavi*; *non erubescam / non confundentur*; *vias tuas* vs. *semitas tuas*):**
   - *The difficulty:* Why does the Introit pair lifting up the soul with the fear of *shame* and *mocking enemies*, and why does the verse ask separately for God's *ways* (*vias*) and His *paths* (*semitas*)?
   - *Verified patristic and Doctoral reasoning:*
     - **St. Augustine** (*Enarr. in Ps. 24* §§1–4) hears the **whole Christ** (*totus Christus* — Christ speaking in the person of His Church) lifting the heart out of earthly desires; pride brought Adam to shame before even the smallest creature, and invisible spiritual enemies whisper subtle flattery ("Well done, well done!") to derail the soul off the narrow *semitae* of Matt 7:14.
     - **St. Thomas Aquinas** (*Postilla super Psalmos* Ps 24, Parma Vol. 14, pp. 229–230) gives a rigorous scholastic anatomy of the same verses on his Gallican Psalter text: lifting the soul is St. John Damascene's *ascensus intellectus in Deum* through contemplation and affection; *sustinent* demands patient longanimity (Hab 2:3); *confusio* is twofold (salutary shame of penance vs. ruinous shame of damnation); and ***viae*** vs. ***semitae*** marks a real ascent within the active life — ***viae*** are the common highway of the **commandments** (*praecepta*, centered in charity), while ***semitae*** are the narrow, direct shortcut of the **evangelical counsels** (*consilia*, citing Jer 6 and Isa 40:3). Commenting on the Gallican lemma *Vias tuas, Domine, demonstra mihi* (never the Gradual's *notas fac*), Aquinas explains under *Vias tuas* that because man does not know these ways (*Has vias ego non cognosco*), the psalmist asks God to show them (*ostende eas mihi*) both to the intellect (*quantum ad intellectum*) and to the affections (*quantum ad affectum*), while *Et semitas* returns to the outer division of the active life to expound the narrow footpath of the evangelical counsels, before treating *et doce me* under v. 5 (*Dirige me in veritate tua, et doce me*) for the contemplative life.
3. **Romans 13:13–14 (*induimini Dominum Jesum Christum*) and the Sacramental Middle Coming:**
   - *Verified patristic, Doctoral, and Blessed witnesses:*
     - **St. Augustine** (*Confessions* VIII.12.29) provides the Western Church's classic witness to Rom 13:13–14 as an instrument of **instantaneous interior conversion** (*tolle, lege*): hearing the Apostle's words dissipates all shadows of hesitation and clothes the divided will in Christ — notably turning on v. 14b (*and make not provision for the flesh in its concupiscences*), whose omission from the 1962 Epistle underscores that the liturgy ends squarely on the positive gift (*sed induimini Dominum Jesum Christum*).
     - **St. Bernard of Clairvaux** (*Sermones in Adventu* 3.4, 5.1–3 — checked tracked texts; *standing-table registration flagged in `scope.md`*) names this interior reality explicitly as the **Middle Coming** (*adventus medius*): hidden in spirit and virtue, it is our **repose and consolation** and the sole **road leading from the First Coming (Redemption) to the Third (Glory)**; keeping the Word means eating and assimilating it into our inner life like bread so no thief can steal it.
     - **Bl. Ildefonso Schuster** (*The Sacramentary* I, pp. 321–323, following the general Advent introduction on pp. 319–321 top, esp. p. 320 — an eligible carrying `blessed` covering the exact 1962 formulary) expounds the Mass's sacrificial prayers and Communion chant in explicitly **Eucharistic** terms: the Secret (*Haec sacra nos...*, p. 322) expresses the ancient idea of the Roman epiklesis, beseeching the Savior that the virtue of the Eucharistic sacrifice purify our minds so we may worthily receive its Author; the Communion (Ps 84:13, pp. 322–323, exposition p. 323) celebrates the Eucharist as a pledge of the Lord's infinite goodness refreshing hearts rendered arid and sterile by sin with the dews of grace so they bring forth fruit; and the Postcommunion (*Suscipiamus...*, p. 323) is the true *Eucharistia* inspired by Ps 47, praying that the divine virtue of the heavenly bread prepare us suitably for the approaching feast of our common ransom. Schuster himself does **not** invoke St. Bernard's Middle Coming (*adventus medius*) schema (on p. 322 he refers to the First and Second Comings); connecting Schuster's Eucharistic exposition of Secret, Communion, and Postcommunion to Bernard's Middle Coming is **explicitly editorial synthesis within Interpretation 3**.
- *What changes if this reading is adopted:* The center of gravity shifts from remote cosmic horizon (Interpretation 1) and historical-Marian origin (Interpretation 2) to **present interior and sacramental transformation**: Advent I becomes a concrete pedagogy of grace, confession, moral discipline, and Eucharistic purification happening *now* inside the assembly.

### 3.3 Carrying Authors vs. Editorial Whole-Formulary Synthesis
- **Compatible carrying authors (all registered in `author-standing-v1.toml`):** **St. Augustine of Hippo** (`father` — Ps 24 *totus Christus*, Ps 84:8 & 13 prevenient grace / *suavitas* / plough of confession, *Confessions* VIII.12.29 on Rom 13:13–14), **St. Thomas Aquinas** (`doctor` — Ps 24 contemplative ascent, twofold *confusio*, *viae* commandments vs. *semitae* counsels), **Bl. Ildefonso Schuster** (`blessed` — exact whole-1962-formulary Eucharistic reading of Secret, Communion, Postcommunion), **St. Leo the Great** (`father` — *Serm.* 12.4, 17.1, 19.1 interior cultivation of the heart's ground through prayer, fasting, and alms), with **St. Ambrose** (`father` — *Exp. in Luc.* X.39–40 on Christ's advent *in singulis* moving the soul's powers by an increase of grace).
- **Additional checked Doctor (awaiting `author-standing-v1.toml` row):** **St. Bernard of Clairvaux** (*In Adventu* 3.4, 5.1–3, Middle Coming in spirit and virtue).
- **Supporting witness:** William Durandus (*Rationale* VI.3: *terra nostra* = our flesh, *benignitas* = good interior affection, *fructus* = good works).
- **Explicit boundary (Verified Author Exegesis vs. Editorial Synthesis):** Bl. Ildefonso Schuster directly expounds the complete 1962 Advent I formulary in historical and Eucharistic terms (referring on p. 322 to the First and Second Comings without invoking Bernard's Middle Coming schema); Augustine's Ps 24/84/*Confessions* exegesis, Aquinas's Ps 24 *Postilla*, Leo's Advent sermons, Ambrose's *in singulis* note, and Bernard's Middle Coming sermons are verified in their own loci. Linking Schuster's Eucharistic exposition to Bernard's Middle Coming and synthesizing all these witnesses into one unified sacramental-conversion reading across all ten elements is **explicitly editorial synthesis**.

### 3.4 Whole-Formulary Contribution (All 10 Elements + Governed Ordinary)
- **Introit (Ps 24:1–3, 4b [Heb. Ps 25:1–3a, 4]):** The first movement of grace is prayer as ascent (*ascensus intellectus in Deum*, Aquinas; *totus Christus*, Augustine), lifting the mind from sin's shame toward God's ways and narrow paths.
- **Collect (*Excita, quaesumus...*):** Confesses radical human incapacity before *imminentibus peccatorum nostrorum periculis* — we cannot save ourselves, so we beg Christ's prevenient power to rescue (*te protegente*) and deliver (*te liberante*).
- **Epistle (Rom 13:11–14a):** The decisive crisis of personal conversion (Augustine, *Confessions* VIII.12.29): leaving behind the specific habits of darkness (gluttony, impurity, strife) and actively putting on Christ in the daylight of grace.
- **Gradual (Ps 24:3b, 4b [Heb. Ps 25:3a, 4]):** Patient, humble waiting (*longanimitas*, Aquinas on v. 3) paired with the Old Latin chant plea *notas fac mihi* — illuminating, alongside Aquinas's Gallican exposition of *Vias tuas ... demonstra mihi* (praying that God show these ways to both intellect and affections) and *Et semitas*, the prayer to be guided along precepts (*viae*) and evangelical counsels (*semitae*).
- **Alleluia (Ps 84:8):** Anti-Pelagian prayer of humility (Augustine): *"Show us Thy mercy"* — teach us that every good is Thy gift so pride dies and we receive Thy Savior.
- **Gospel (Luke 21:25–33):** Read through the interior horizon (Ambrose X.39–40 *in singulis*; Leo Serm. 19.1): Christ's advent shakes the powers of the soul with an increase of grace, exposing the instability of every false earthly refuge so the believing mind lifts up its head in hope, anchored in Christ's abiding Word.
- **Offertory (Ps 24:1–3 [Heb. Ps 25:1–3a]):** Offering our own lives with the gifts on the altar in humble confession and confidence — refusing the mocking whisper of the enemy (Augustine).
- **Secret (*Haec sacra nos...*):** The sacramental hinge of the Mass (Schuster): the Mysteries themselves purify us by mighty power (*potenti virtute mundatos*) so we draw near *puriores* to our divine Source.
- **Communion (Ps 84:13):** God pours His *benignitas* / *suavitas* (interior delight in goodness, Augustine; Eucharistic refreshment of arid hearts, Schuster) into the soil of the confessed heart so our own lives yield the fruit of charity (Leo Serm. 17.1; Durandus).
- **Postcommunion (*Suscipiamus, Domine...*):** Having received mercy in the midst of God's temple, the assembly is strengthened by the divine virtue of the heavenly bread to prepare suitably for the approaching feast of our reparation (Schuster, p. 323).
- **Governed Ordinary:** Violet and omitted *Gloria* cultivate penitential interiority; *Ite, missa est* (`RGMR 507`) sends the converted, nourished assembly back into the world; the **Trinity Preface** crowns interior healing with the worship of the indwelling Triune God.

### 3.5 Four Senses (Interpretation 3)
- **Literal:** Personal plea for deliverance from shame and guidance in God's law (Ps 24); community plea for mercy and renewed fruitfulness in the land (Ps 84); Paul's ethical exhortation to Roman believers (Rom 13:11–14a); Jesus' call to fearless constancy before the coming Son of Man (Luke 21:25–33); the Roman Church's prayers for cleansing and worthy celebration (Collect, Secret, Postcommunion).
- **Allegorical:** The *totus Christus* praying in His Body the Church (Augustine, Ps 24); Christ's hidden Middle Coming in spirit and virtue (Bernard *In Adventu* 5) and *in singulis* (Ambrose X.39–40), joined editorially with the Eucharistic Mysteries as active instrument of cleansing and pledge of divine goodness (Schuster, Secret p. 322 & Ps 84:13 p. 323).
- **Moral:** Prevenient grace curing pride (Augustine, Ps 84:8); confession ploughing the heart so interior sweetness (*suavitas*) replaces delight in sin (Augustine, Ps 84:13); instant abandonment of carnal vice to put on Christ (Augustine *Confessions* VIII.12.29); ascending from commandments (*viae*) to evangelical counsels (*semitae*, Aquinas); Advent fasting and almsgiving cultivating the heart's ground (Leo Serm. 17.1).
- **Anagogical:** The Middle Coming in grace and Eucharist as the living bridge that prepares purified souls to stand unashamed (*non confundentur*) before their divine *principium* at the Third Coming in glory.

### 3.6 Limits and Intra-Reading Nuances
- Augustine's penetrating *suavitatem* exegesis of Ps 84:13 rests on the Old Latin Psalter rather than the Roman Missal's Gallican *benignitatem* — a textual difference that must always be disclosed transparently rather than blurred. Similarly, his conversion narrative (*Confessions* VIII.12.29) leans heavily on Rom 13:14b, which the 1962 Epistle omits. On its own, Interpretation 3 could risk under-playing the dramatic cosmic scale of Luke 21:25–27 unless kept in dialogue with Interpretation 1.
