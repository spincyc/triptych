# Pope Saint Gregory the Great — tradition audit

Provider: Google Gemini (`gemini-4-neon`, effort `xhigh`). Inspection date:
2026-11-27. Each received tradition is audited for earliest located witness,
contemporary or countervailing evidence, historical status, and ecclesial
meaning.

## 1. *Non Angli sed Angeli* (the Roman slave-market wordplay)

- **Tradition:** Before becoming pope, Gregory saw fair-haired boys from
  Deira (Northumbria) for sale in the Roman forum and exclaimed that they were
  *Non Angli sed Angeli* ("not Angles, but angels"), punning further on *Deira*
  (*de ira*, plucked from wrath) and King *Aella* (*Alleluia*), which moved
  him to attempt a personal mission to Britain before the Roman people forced
  his recall.
- **Earliest located witness:** Anonymous monk of Whitby, *Vita Gregorii*
  (early 8th century, c. 704–714), and Bede, *Historia Ecclesiastica* II.1
  (731), where Bede explicitly introduces the tale as *opinio quae de beato
  Gregorio traditione maiorum ad nos usque perlata est* ("an account which has
  come down to us by the tradition of our elders") — both reported via Barmby's
  Prolegomena in NPNF2-12.
- **Contemporary evidence and tension:** Completely absent from Gregory's own
  *Registrum*, including his July 596 missionary dossier (*Ep.* VI.51–59) and
  his July 598 triumphal report to Eulogius (*Ep.* VIII.30). However, *Ep.*
  VI.7 (September 595, inspected verbatim in NPNF2-12) proves that Gregory
  instructed his Gallic steward Candidus to use patrimonial revenues to buy
  English youths aged seventeen or eighteen so they might be educated in
  monasteries for God's service.
- **Historical status:** **Early received English tradition (E)** — neither
  provable as verbatim anecdote nor dismissible as pure fabrication. It
  plausibly preserves a real memory of Gregory's compassion for English
  captives, crystallized into classic Latin puns among grateful eighth-century
  English Christians.
- **Publication treatment:** Narrated respectfully in the mission chapter and
  audited in the Tradition Audit section, paired directly with *Ep.* VI.7.

## 2. Archangel Michael sheathing his sword atop Hadrian's Mausoleum (Castel Sant'Angelo)

- **Tradition:** During Gregory's 590 penitential plague procession (*litania
  septiformis*), he saw the Archangel Michael hovering over the Mausoleum of
  Hadrian, wiping and sheathing a bloody sword to signal the end of the plague,
  giving the fortress its name Castel Sant'Angelo.
- **Earliest located witness:** Medieval Roman legend, popularized widely in
  the thirteenth century (*Legenda Aurea*, reported; not tracked verbatim
  locally).
- **Countervailing contemporary evidence:** Gregory of Tours, *Historia
  Francorum* X.1 (inspected verbatim in both Dalton 1927 and Arndt 1884),
  relying on an eyewitness deacon of Tours who marched in Rome in 590, records
  the flood, the plague, Gregory's sermon, the sevenfold litany converging on
  St. Mary Major, and eighty deaths occurring within a single hour during the
  march — with **zero** mention of any angelic apparition, sword, or Hadrian's
  Mausoleum. Nor does Bede's 731 biographical notice report the vision
  (reported via Barmby).
- **Historical status:** **Later devotional legend (L)**, unattested in 590 or
  the early eighth century. Probably grew around a later chapel of St. Michael
  built atop the Mausoleum and fused with the genuine historical memory of
  Gregory's 590 litany.
- **Publication treatment:** Distinguished plainly in Section 3 (plague and
  election) and audited in Section 10; the genuine penitential liturgy of 590
  stands on eyewitness bedrock without needing the medieval apparition.

## 3. The whispering white dove of the Holy Spirit

- **Tradition:** While Gregory dictated his biblical commentaries (especially
  on Ezekiel) behind a curtain, his scribe (often identified as his deacon
  Peter) peeped through a hole during a long silence and saw a pure white dove
  perched near Gregory's ear, speaking into it whenever the pope resumed
  dictation.
- **Earliest located witness:** Paul the Deacon's late-eighth-century *Vita*
  and John the Deacon's late-ninth-century *Vita* (reported via Barmby),
  becoming Gregory's signature medieval iconographic emblem.
- **Countervailing Class A evidence:** Gregory's own prefaces (*Moralia*, Ep.
  Ded. 1–5; *Hom. in Evang.* Preface to Secundinus; *Hom. in Hiez.* II.10.24,
  all inspected directly) describe ordinary, painstaking human authorship:
  oral conferences taken down by shorthand notaries, years of painful revision
  amid stomach spasms and fevers, a weak voice forcing a notary to read
  homilies aloud, and sudden interruption by Agilulf's siege.
- **Historical status:** **Later hagiographic and iconographic emblem (L)**.
- **Publication treatment:** Treated as a symbolic visual tribute to the
  Church's conviction that Gregory expounded Scripture in the Spirit — never
  as a historical dictation transcript.

## 4. The "Gregorian Mass" (thirty consecutive daily Masses / trental)

- **Tradition:** Offering thirty consecutive daily Masses for a single departed
  soul secures deliverance from purgatorial suffering.
- **Earliest located witness:** Gregory's own *Dialogi* IV.55 (numbered IV.57
  in some editions, written 593–594; reported via Barmby and Migne PL 76
  cross-references), narrating how Gregory ordered thirty consecutive daily
  Masses at St. Andrew's monastery for a deceased monk named Justus who had
  violated monastic poverty by hiding three gold pieces, after which Justus
  appeared to his brother declaring himself freed. Corroborated in genre and
  theology by Gregory's directly inspected *Hom. in Evang.* 37.8 (Latin
  `gregory-whole.txt`), where a captive's weekly chains fall off whenever his
  wife has Mass offered for him.
- **Countervailing limit:** Gregory himself never issued a universal liturgical
  decree or canonical guarantee promising automatic deliverance after thirty
  Masses; the single monastic episode in *Dialogi* IV was formalized into the
  "Gregorian trental" custom only across later medieval centuries.
- **Historical status:** **Authentic Gregorian narrative origin (A, reported)**
  combined with **medieval liturgical formalization (L)**.
- **Publication treatment:** Separates what Gregory actually wrote (an
  extraordinary pastoral narrative rooted in Eucharistic intercession for the
  dead) from later mechanical or automatic readings.

## 5. Gregory's intercession delivering the pagan Emperor Trajan from hell

- **Tradition:** Walking across Trajan's Forum and recalling the pagan emperor's
  justice toward a poor widow, Gregory wept and prayed in St. Peter's until
  God delivered Trajan's soul from hell (with a warning never to pray thus for
  another pagan).
- **Earliest located witness:** Anonymous Whitby *Vita Gregorii* ch. 29 (early
  8th century), transmitted by Paul the Deacon and John the Deacon (reported
  via Barmby) and famously discussed in medieval scholasticism (Aquinas) and
  Dante's *Paradiso*.
- **Countervailing Class A evidence:** Entirely absent from Gregory's genuine
  writings, and in direct theological tension with Gregory's own directly
  inspected preaching in *Hom. in Evang.* 40.7 (Latin `gregory-whole.txt`, on
  the Rich Man and Lazarus), where Gregory explicitly teaches the fixed,
  irreversible chasm separating the damned from mercy after death.
- **Historical status:** **Early medieval legend (E/L)**; historically
  unfounded as an episode in Gregory's life, though immensely revealing of
  medieval admiration for Roman justice and Gregory's reputation for fearless
  compassion.
- **Publication treatment:** Audited squarely against *Hom. in Evang.* 40.7,
  explaining why scholastics had to invent posthumous resuscitation theories to
  reconcile the tale with orthodox eschatology.

## 6. Personal authorship of the complete Gregorian Sacramentary and Gregorian chant corpus

- **Tradition:** Gregory personally compiled the entire Roman Sacramentary
  (*Liber Sacramentorum*), composed or fixed the whole liturgical chant
  repertory (*Antiphonarius*), founded the *schola cantorum*, and taught choir
  boys from his sickbed with a whip (*flagellum*).
- **Earliest located witnesses:** Carolingian rubrics attributing the papal
  Mass book sent by Pope Hadrian I to Charlemagne (784–791) to St. Gregory
  (Wilson 1915, Migne PL 78 cols. 23–26); John the Deacon, *Vita S. Gregorii*
  II.6–9 (c. 873–875, inspected verbatim in Migne PL 78 cols. 650–651), the
  earliest witness claiming Gregory compiled the *Antiphonarium centonem* and
  ruled the *schola cantorum* with a preserved couch and whip.
- **Countervailing primary and critical evidence:**
  - *Genuine reforms attested (A/N/K):* *Ep.* IX.12 (quoted in Fortescue 1922
    pp. 234–235) confirms Gregory's distinctive Roman daily *Kyrie* and
    *Christe eleison* usage; Fortescue 1922 (p. 172) summarizes his addition to
    *Hanc igitur* and placement of the *Pater noster* before Communion; checked
    *Hadrianum* Canon confirms the clause *diesque nostros in tua pace
    disponas...* in *Hanc igitur*; *Hom. in Evang.* confirms stational Masses
    (including three Christmas Masses on one day, Hom. 8.1); *HF* X.1 confirms
    the sevenfold litany.
  - *Disproof of whole-book authorship:* Wilson 1915 *Hadrianum* directly
    contains the Mass *Natale Sancti Gregorii Papae* on p. 25 (lines 3393–3404)
    alongside seventh-century Roman feasts (e.g., St. Leo, St. Sabina changes,
    Thursday Lenten stations), and the Carolingian *Hucusque* author himself
    (pp. 145–146, lines 9245–9316) explicitly marks Gregory's own feast with
    *virgulae* as added by later popes!
  - *Chant anachronism:* Fortescue 1922 (pp. 234–235) rejects John the Deacon's
    claim that Gregory introduced the *Kyrie* (citing the Council of Vaison,
    529); modern musicology (K) shows that "Gregorian chant" as a codified
    musical repertory crystallized in eighth- and ninth-century Frankish–Roman
    synthesis under Pepin and Charlemagne, two centuries after Gregory.
- **Historical status:** **Later Carolingian and ninth-century attribution (L)**
  resting on a real core of **specific Gregorian liturgical reforms (A/N)**.
- **Publication treatment:** Dedicated Section 8 + Tradition Audit row, citing
  Wilson 1915, Migne PL 78, and Fortescue 1922 directly.

## 7. Secondary audited details (lineage, relics, epitaph, doctorate)

- ***Gens Anicia* descent:** Widely repeated in secondary biographies and late
  tradition, but **never stated** in Gregory's own *Hom. in Evang.* 38.15 (which
  only establishes Pope Felix III as *atavus* and names his aunts) or in *HF*
  X.1. Recorded transparently as an unverified traditional inference rather than
  established fact.
- **Epitaph (*Consul Dei*) and Old St. Peter's burial:** Reported via Barmby
  and Bede *HE* II.1; cited at reported ceiling without archaeological claims.
- **Title *Magnus* and 1298 Doctorate:** Attested in *Martyrologium Romanum*
  1902 (p. 36, M) and Boniface VIII's 1298 decretal (M, reported); framed
  strictly as ecclesial reception.
