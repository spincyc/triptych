# Seventh Sunday after Pentecost — Research Scope

**Last updated:** 2026-07-10

This record preserves research scope and evidence roles that are intentionally compressed in the reader-facing guide. Textual retrieval, checksums, facsimile locations, and the complete verified propers remain in `../propers/verified.md`.

## Scriptural and Liturgical Scope

- Appointed and immediate contexts studied: Psalms 30, 33, and 46; Daniel 3:24–45; Matthew 7:13–27; and Romans 6.
- Closely related historical and doctrinal contexts actually used: Numbers 26:9–11; 1 Samuel 16:1–13 and 21:11–22:2; 1 Chronicles 6:16–23; 2 Chronicles 20:18–19; Genesis 4:1–10; Matthew 3:13–5:2; Luke 23:46; Acts 7:59; Romans 12:1–2, 15:22–29, and 16:21–23; and Hebrews 9:23–10:18.
- The Collect, Secret, and Postcommunion were analyzed in their complete verified Latin forms; the guide publishes only the clauses needed for its argument because it is designed for use beside a hand missal.

## Historical Coordinates of the Direct Scriptural Propers

The one-page historical orientation inventories six distinct directly appointed passages in Catholic canonical order: Psalm 30:3 (Communion), Psalm 33:6 and 12 (Gradual), Psalm 46:2–3 (Introit, with verse 2 repeated at the Alleluia), Daniel 3:40 (Offertory), Matthew 7:15–21 (Gospel), and Romans 6:19–23 (Epistle). Psalm numbering here follows the Vulgate and missal; the modern Hebrew-based equivalents are Psalms 31, 34, and 47. Composed orations and their biblical echoes were not mislabeled as direct quotations. Each proper row deliberately reports a traditional composition location and approximate date under the neutral headings `Location` and `Date`; the spanning prose then distinguishes that inherited placement from modern historical judgment. The Gospel alone adds a citation row for the narrated event, separating Jesus' Galilean setting from Matthew's composition setting.

- **Psalms:** the traditional metadata follows the superscriptions and the inherited Davidic/Korahite setting reflected in the *Catholic Encyclopedia* articles “Psalms” and “King David.” The broad dates `c. 1010 BC` for the fugitive David of Psalm 33 and `c. 1000 BC` for the Davidic or Davidically ordered Psalms 30 and 46 are orientation dates within the usual traditional chronology, not claims of recoverable autograph dates. The *New American Bible, Revised Edition* Psalms introduction and notes to modern Psalms 31, 34, and 47 govern the distinction between superscription, poetic speaker, performance setting, and demonstrable composition. There is no secure way to date or localize these individual psalms. Psalm 46's Korahite sanctuary association makes Jerusalem a traditional sanctuary setting, not a proven composition place. Psalm 33's superscription places David's deliverance at Gath before his kingship, with Adullam next in the narrative, but neither place can be demonstrated as the site of writing. Psalm 30 supplies no Davidic episode at all, so its traditional place remains only “Israel; exact place unrecorded.”
- **Daniel:** the first row follows the traditional attribution summarized by the *Catholic Encyclopedia*: Daniel writing in Babylon during the exile, here represented broadly as `c. 540 BC`. Daniel 3 nevertheless distinguishes Azariah, the literary speaker in the Babylonian furnace scene, from the work's penman. The *NABRE* introduction and 3:24–90 note support the Greek textual preservation, a probable lost Hebrew or Aramaic antecedent, and the wider Maccabean horizon. Thomas Hieke, “Atonement in the Prayer of Azariah (Dan 3:40),” pp. 44–48, specifically argues for origin during Antiochus IV's persecution and Temple profanation in 167–164 BC. Hieke also records Ernst Haag's competing proposal that the prayer arose after the Temple's rededication as a reflection on that persecution. The guide follows Hieke's reading of the lament as a present crisis, while preserving the alternative here; the writer's physical location and the prayer's exact provenance remain disputed.
- **Matthew:** the composition row follows ancient ecclesiastical tradition as summarized by the *Catholic Encyclopedia*: St. Matthew composed for Jewish believers in Judea or Palestine, with one received early range around AD 40–45. Ancient witnesses offer other pre-70 dates, so the row is deliberately approximate. The added narrated-event row cites Matthew 4:12–5:2 and 7:28–29 for Jesus' early Galilean ministry, the unnamed mountain, his disciples and listening crowds, and the close of the same discourse. Its `c. AD 27` follows the traditional harmonized chronology in the *Catholic Encyclopedia*, “Chronology of the Life of Jesus Christ,” which places the sermon late in a second journey ending about Pentecost A.U.C. 780; Matthew itself supplies no civil year. The encyclopedia's “Mount of Beatitudes” confirms that no site more exact than a mountain in Galilee can be established and that the named-site tradition is late. The *NABRE* introduction records the majority judgment that the final Greek author is unknown, a post-70 date, Antioch as a plausible rather than certain composition place, and a church moving from strongly Jewish-Christian roots toward Gentile predominance.
- **Romans:** the *NABRE* introduction to Romans and Romans 15–16 support Pauline authorship, likely composition at Corinth in AD 56–58 (represented in the table as `c. AD 57`), the mixed Jewish and Gentile Roman house churches, Paul's completed eastern work, the Jerusalem collection, and the planned Rome–Spain mission. This is the most historically determinate dossier in the section; Tertius is identified as the letter's scribe, not substituted for Paul as author.

Geographic equivalents are deliberately broad where ancient identification or modern borders make greater precision misleading: Corinth is in present-day Greece; Rome in Italy; Antioch is modern Antakya in Türkiye; Galilee is in northern present-day Israel; Philistine Gath belongs to the southern Levant; and the Babylonian narrative is set in present-day Iraq. These equivalents locate the texts for the reader without claiming that a narrated place is necessarily the writer's place.

## Direct Reception Retained

- St. Augustine, *Expositions on the Psalms* 46, 33, and 30.
- St. John Chrysostom, *Homilies 23–24 on Matthew* and *Homily 12 on Romans*.
- St. Augustine, *Sermon on the Mount* II.24–25.
- St. Jerome, *Commentary on Matthew* I at Matthew 7:15–22.

These sources directly expound appointed biblical passages, not the later assembled Roman formulary.

## Related Illumination Retained

- *Didache* 11 and St. Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* III.3, on testing teaching and apostolic transmission.
- St. Thomas Aquinas, *Summa theologiae* I, q. 22, a. 4, on providence and free causes.
- St. John Chrysostom, *Homily 20 on Romans*, on embodied sacrifice and the furnace prayer.
- St. Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* IV.18, and St. Augustine, *City of God* X, on Abel, sacrifice, and ecclesial offering.
- St. Augustine, *Grace and Free Will* 18–21, on grace-enabled works and reward.
- St. Ignatius, *Ephesians* 20; St. Thomas Aquinas, *Summa theologiae* III, q. 79, a. 6; and the Ambrosian *De sacramentis* IV.6.27–28, on Eucharistic medicine, Abel, and repeated need for remission.

These witnesses supply doctrine or close liturgical analogy; they are not presented as direct commentary on the Collect, Offertory, Secret, or Postcommunion.

## Survey and Material Limits

- Surveyed accessible ANF/NPNF and CCEL collections, New Advent texts, Jerome and the Ambrosian *De sacramentis* in Latin, the *Catena Aurea* as a lead index, and targeted Latin and English web searches.
- No secure direct patristic commentary was located for the Collect, the exact Offertory verse Daniel 3:40, the Secret, or the Postcommunion.
- The survey does not exhaust manuscripts, untranslated works, subscription databases, or modern critical scholarship. Older public-domain translations are working texts.
- Sources were retained because they establish or materially qualify claims made in the guide; no numerical or source-category quota was applied.

## Cultural Reception

Exact and variant wording from Matthew 7, Romans 6, and the appointed psalms was tested across digitized literature, film, recorded music, and web indexes. Four examples survived verification: *The Night of the Hunter*, William James's *The Varieties of Religious Experience*, Terry Pratchett's *Witches Abroad*, and the Beatles' “Revolution 9.” Coincidental phrases and unsupported quotation-aggregate leads were discarded.
