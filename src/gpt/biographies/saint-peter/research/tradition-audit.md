# Saint Peter — tradition audit

This record asks four separate questions for every vivid or consequential
tradition: what is the earliest located witness, what does that witness
actually say, what later development adds, and what status the publication may
claim. “Devotional value” never upgrades chronology, and “late” never means
“worthless.” All links were accessed 15 July 2026.

| Tradition | Earliest located witness / date / genre | Later development | Publication assessment | Theological or cultural value / caution |

| Jesus gave Simon the name Cephas / Peter | Distinct canonical traditions: [John 1:42](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/john/1), [Mark 3:16](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/mark/3), [Matt 16:17–19](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/matthew/16). First century canonical narrative. | The name becomes a title of office and a dominant iconographic/theological identity. | **N**, strong core that the name and vocation were associated with Jesus; exact narrated moment differs. | Rock imagery supports vocation and ecclesial service; it should not erase Simon's humanity or be reduced to a modern job title. |
| Peter was the first apostle called | Mark/Matthew call Simon and Andrew together; John has Andrew encounter Jesus first and bring Simon; Luke has a distinct lakeside scene. | “Prince of the Apostles” and “first-called” language sometimes turns literary precedence into a minute-by-minute contest. | Peter belongs to the earliest circle and is first in apostolic lists; exact first-contact sequence is source-dependent. | Primacy need not be proved by erasing Andrew or other first witnesses. |
| Peter's wife traveled and was martyred | Marriage and travel right: [Mark 1:29–31](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/mark/1), [1 Cor 9:5](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1corinthians/9). Martyrdom: Clement of Alexandria, preserved in [Eusebius, *HE* 3.30.2](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm), late second / early fourth-century transmission. | Later retellings assign names and detailed dialogues. | Marriage **N**, secure. Accompaniment probable from Paul's wording. Wife's martyrdom **E**, possible but late; name unknown. | Recovers the real cost borne by an apostolic household. Do not invent a domestic biography. |
| Saint Petronilla was Peter's biological daughter | Ancient Roman cult of Petronilla; biological claim appears in later apocryphal and hagiographic transmission, not in a first-century source. | Medieval art and patronage make the genealogy familiar. | **L**, unestablished as biological kinship. | Petronilla's sanctity and cult do not require a provable genealogy. |
| Mark wrote Peter's preaching | Papias, early second century, preserved at [Eusebius, *HE* 3.39.15](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm). | Irenaeus and later prologues strengthen the Peter–Mark link; popular accounts call Mark “Peter's memoir.” | **E**, an important early received association. It does not make every Markan sentence a dictated recollection. | Explains mediated apostolic memory while preserving Mark's authorship and literary agency. |
| Peter was bishop of Antioch for seven years | [Gal 2:11–14](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/galatians/2) securely places Cephas at Antioch; exact tenure comes from later episcopal chronologies and feast reception. | Chair-of-Antioch feast and succession lists supply a seven-year period. | Antioch ministry **N**, secure in some form. “Bishop” in later administrative sense and seven years **L**, unproved. | Supports Antioch's apostolic memory without manufacturing an uninterrupted timetable. |
| Peter founded the Roman church | Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 3.3.2, c. 180, [text](https://ccel.org/ccel/irenaeus/against_heresies_iii/anf01.ix.iv.iv.html), speaks of Peter and Paul founding and organizing Rome. | Later lists make Peter first residential bishop and sole institutional founder. | **E/M** for apostolic foundation in reception. Not literal first conversion: Romans addresses an existing church before Paul's arrival. | “Founder” can name apostolic teaching, martyr witness, and succession rather than the first Christian household. |
| Peter governed Rome for twenty-five years, AD 42–67 | Later chronographic and episcopal-list tradition; no first-century source gives arrival year or term. | Widely repeated in devotional lives and institutional chronologies. | **L**, chronologically weak; difficult to reconcile with Peter's mid-century eastern presence and Romans' silence. | May symbolize long Roman stewardship, but must not be used as exact itinerary. |
| Peter and Paul both ordered the Roman church | [Ignatius, *Romans* 4.3](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0107.htm), c. 107–110, says he does not command as they did; Irenaeus later gives succession language. | Harmonized into a detailed co-episcopate or sole-Peter constitutional scheme. | **E**, strong early Roman apostolic association; form and duration unknown. | Paired authority is compatible with distinct missions and does not erase pre-existing Roman Christians. |
| Peter died in Nero's persecution after the fire of 64 | [1 Clement 5](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Ante-Nicene_Fathers/Volume_IX/The_Epistles_of_Clement/The_First_Epistle_of_Clement_to_the_Corinthians/Chapter_5) remembers his completed witness; [Tacitus, *Ann.* 15.44](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Annals_%28Tacitus%29/Book_15) supplies the persecution; later witnesses place Peter at Rome. | Exact years 64 and 67 become competing conventional dates. | **S**, Roman Neronian martyrdom probable; 64–68 is the responsible range. Direct participation in the post-fire round is possible, not proven. | Locates apostolic witness in imperial violence without making Tacitus name Peter. |
| Peter was crucified | John 21:18–19 canonically foreshadows a death with outstretched hands but names no method; Tertullian c. 200 explicitly gives a passion like the Lord's / cross: [*Prescription* 36](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0311.htm), [*Scorpiace* 15](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0318.htm). | Martyrdom narratives dramatize arrest, sentence, speeches, and burial. | **E/S**, early, plausible, and strongly received; no contemporary execution narrative. | Conforms Peter's shepherding to the Lord's self-gift; should not be overstated as eyewitness reportage. |
| Peter was crucified head-down | [*Acts of Peter* 37–38](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/actspeter.html), late second century; Origen, preserved in [Eusebius, *HE* 3.1.2](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250103.htm), early third-century report. | Becomes standard art and hagiography. | **E/L**, ancient and possibly historical, but unattested in first-century sources. | Powerful reversal symbolism; the inverted cross remains a traditional Petrine emblem. |
| Peter chose inversion because he was unworthy to die like Jesus | The surviving *Acts of Peter* gives a complex cosmological/reversal explanation, not merely the familiar humility sentence. A later joint *Acts of Peter and Paul* explicitly gives the unworthiness rationale. | Simplified in preaching, art captions, and popular lives. | **L**, devotional motive not established as Peter's spoken reason. | Humility is consonant with Christian devotion; exact motive must not be quoted as eyewitness speech. |
| “Quo vadis?” and Peter's return to Rome | [*Acts of Peter* 35](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/actspeter.html), late second-century apocryphal narrative. | Localized on the Appian Way; Latin wording, footprints, chapel, novels and film. | **L**, ancient apocryphal reception, not near-contemporary fact. | Expresses that Christ suffers in the martyr and that a shepherd returns to a threatened flock. |
| Peter and Paul were held in the Mamertine Prison | Late antique / medieval Roman localization; no canonical or first-century source identifies the prison. | Guards Processus and Martinian, miraculous spring, chains, and precise cell acquire elaborate cult. | **L**, historically unverified. The building's antiquity does not prove apostolic custody. | Sacred geography can form prayer around captivity; distinguish ancient structure from late identification. |
| Peter made water spring from the prison rock and baptized guards | Later Roman hagiography attached to Mamertine localization. | The spring and named guards become objects of pilgrimage and art. | **L**, no controlled first-century attestation. | Baptism-in-captivity motif expresses evangelization under chains; not usable as biography. |
| Peter and Paul died on the same day, 29 June 67 | Dionysius, preserved at [Eusebius, *HE* 2.25.8](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250102.htm), says at about the same time. Benedict XVI's [29 June 2008 homily](https://www.vatican.va/content/benedict-xvi/en/homilies/2008/documents/hf_ben-xvi_hom_20080629_pallio.html) controls the current solemnity. | Liturgical pairing and later chronologies are read as an execution calendar. | **M** for the current solemnity; **L**, unproved, for same day and AD 67. The ancient calendar origin was not directly collated. | The feast confesses concord and Rome's apostolic witness; liturgical date need not be a death certificate. |
| Peter was buried exactly where the high altar now stands | Gaius c. 200 points to a Vatican `tropaion`, preserved at [Eusebius, *HE* 2.25.5–7](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250102.htm); the [official shrine](https://www.basilicasanpietro.va/en/san-pietro/the-tomb-of-saint-peter) receives the location; a checked Bowersock excerpt summarizes the later architectural axis. | Official shrine and pilgrim language call it Peter's tomb. | Early fixed Petrine memory and architectural continuity are supported; exact first-century deposition geometry and stratigraphy were not independently audited. | The altar-over-memory axis makes martyr witness a material ecclesial center. |
| The Vatican bones are scientifically proven to be Peter's | The [official tomb](https://www.basilicasanpietro.va/en/san-pietro/the-tomb-of-saint-peter) and [necropolis](https://www.basilicasanpietro.va/en/san-pietro/the-necropolis) pages establish current official identification and veneration. | Popular summaries add claims about age, context, custody, and scientific certainty. | **M** for official reception. The primary excavation, custody, and osteological reports were not inspected, so this project makes no scientific finding about particular remains. | The faithful may venerate the received tomb without treating this study as forensic authentication. |
| Peter's relics were temporarily moved to the Appian Way | No calendar edition, epigraphic dossier, or archaeological report was directly collated for this claim. | Later scholarship and devotional histories propose a 258 commemoration and transfer scenarios. | Unverified lead; not promoted into the historical narrative. | A possible joint cult may be studied without inventing a relic chain of custody. |
| Peter's chains in San Pietro in Vincoli are the exact chains from Jerusalem and Rome joined miraculously | Acts 12 narrates Jerusalem chains; later Roman relic tradition identifies and joins physical chains. | Miracle of fusion and annual feast become established devotion. | **L/M** as received relic cult; first-century object identity unverified. | Chains signify deliverance, captivity, and apostolic perseverance; object history remains separate. |
| Peter's shadow healed the sick | [Acts 5:12–16](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/acts/5), canonical narrative. | Relic and image traditions sometimes generalize apostolic power mechanically. | **N**, reported as Acts reports it; no naturalistic mechanism invented and no transferable magical power inferred. | Acts attributes healing to God's apostolic mission, not possession or sale of power. |
| Peter killed Ananias and Sapphira | Acts 5:1–11 narrates their falling dead after Peter exposes deceit; Peter does not pronounce or execute a civil death sentence. | Polemical readings turn him into an executor or model for coercive discipline. | **N** for narrated divine judgment; “Peter killed them” is an inaccurate expansion. | The episode warns about lying to God and protects freedom of gift; it does not authorize violence. |
| Peter is always depicted with keys because he literally carried them | [Matt 16:19](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/matthew/16) supplies symbolic keys; later iconography materializes the emblem. | One, two, or three keys and papal insignia vary by period. | **N** for scriptural image, **L/M** for iconographic forms; no first-century physical keys. | Keys visualize stewardship, teaching, absolution, and ecclesial authority. |
| The inverted cross is inherently anti-Christian | Ancient Petrine martyr iconography uses it positively; modern contexts sometimes assign contrary meanings. | Popular culture treats one meaning as universal. | False as a universal historical claim; context controls meaning. | In Christian art it can signify Peter's martyrdom and humility, while modern use must be read case by case. |

## Traditions intentionally not promoted into the narrative core

- a named mother, detailed childhood, or formal rabbinic education;
- an exact age at call or death;
- a securely named wife or children;
- an uninterrupted day-by-day itinerary from Antioch to Rome;
- continuous residence in Rome from AD 42;
- the exact Neronian trial official, execution site, final words, or burial team;
- the three-denial rooster as a preserved animal or object relic;
- every footprint, spring, chain, prison, chair, garment, or bone labeled
  Petrine by later local tradition.

## Audit conclusion

The Roman martyr memory does not depend on the picturesque legends: First
Clement, Ignatius, Gaius, Irenaeus, Tertullian, the Neronian context, and the
Vatican memorial form a cumulative early case. The legends remain in the
publication because they reveal ancient and later Christian interpretation of
pastoral fidelity, reversal, humility, captivity, and apostolic unity. Their
date and genre are part of their meaning, not an embarrassment to suppress.
