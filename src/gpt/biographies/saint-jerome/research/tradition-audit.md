# Saint Jerome — tradition audit

This record separates earliest located witness, later reception, historical
status, and responsible significance. “Not established” does not mean
spiritually meaningless; “early” does not mean neutral or independently
authenticated. Absence means absence from the source set actually checked for
this edition, not a universal proof that no witness exists.

| Jerome was born in 331. | The older scheme traditionally appeals to Prosper, whose exact entry was not collated here; Jerome supplies no birth year in *De viris illustribus* 135. | Standard older biographies and timelines | Possible E/K chronology, not established by an inspected exact locus | Preserves one serious chronology without giving an unseen chronicle false precision. |
| Jerome was born about 347. | Accessible Canellis 2016 chronology gives the later scheme; Jerome's relative life sequence supplies constraints but no year. | Increasing modern critical use | Qualified K, not unanimous | Better coordinates some schooling and living-memory evidence, but must remain an alternative rather than a correction by fiat. |
| Stridon proves Jerome was specifically Croatian, Slovenian, Bosnian, Italian, or another modern nationality. | *De viris illustribus* 135 says the town lay at the Dalmatian-Pannonian border and was destroyed by Goths; exact site unresolved. | Competing regional claims and national commemorations | Unsupported as exclusive modern nationality | Regional devotion may be honored without converting late Roman geography into a modern passport. |
| Jerome's settled full legal name was “Eusebius Sophronius Hieronymus.” | *De viris illustribus* 135 identifies Jerome as son of Eusebius; 134 names Sophronius as a translator of Jerome. The expansion's earliest exact witness was not located. | Encyclopedias, art labels, devotional naming | Unresolved L/E naming history; not used as secure civil name | “Jerome”/“Hieronymus” protects what is known and prevents a familiar expansion from masquerading as autobiography. |
| Jerome's classical learning disappeared when he became ascetic. | Letter 22.30 narrates the Ciceronian dream; Rufinus, *Apology against Jerome* 2.6, uses that account to charge inconsistency with Jerome's later reading and teaching | Conversion stories sometimes make a clean before/after break | False simplification of A evidence; Rufinus is an exact partisan counter-witness, not a neutral verdict | The dream marks real ascetic conscience; the continuing classical practice and contemporary objection prevent a fictive instantaneous erasure. |
| The Ciceronian dream is an objective transcript of a heavenly trial at Chalcis. | Jerome, Letter 22.30, written in 384 to Eustochium after the reported fever. | Art, sermons, ascetic exempla | A retrospective subject report in a rigorous literary treatise; no external control | May express judgment, fear, and reordered study when the genre and time gap travel with it. |
| Jerome lived alone in an empty desert cave. | Letters 14–17 show books, messengers, factions, teachers, and appeals in the Chalcis region. | Later cave imagery was not audited. | Ascetic withdrawal A; total isolation contradicted by A correspondence | The letters establish a networked withdrawal; no claim about a visual cave motif is carried. |
| A presently identified cave or cell is archaeologically proved to be Jerome's exact workspace. | No item-level archaeological, custodial, or current shrine source was inspected. | Uncollated lead only | No identification, date, location, or current-status claim published | Direct competent evidence is required before any place tradition is narrated. |
| Jerome held the modern office of papal secretary. | The Gospel preface and Letters 19–21, 35–36 establish close scholarly service to Damasus. | Papal catecheses and biographies use “secretary,” “counsellor,” or similar shorthand. | Relationship A/M; defined modern portfolio not established | The shorthand may convey proximity if its institutional limits are explicit. |
| Jerome was a cardinal and wore red cardinal dress. | No fourth-century source in the checked set; Francis 2020 explicitly calls the dress anachronistic. | Item-level visual history was not collated. | He did not hold that later office; no visual date or meaning is published | The inspected official source controls the anachronism only. |
| Jerome was the agreed next pope or lost an election after Damasus. | Letter 45.3 says people had judged him worthy of episcopacy and Damasus spoke through him; no conclave, vote, or agreed succession appears. | Frustrated-papacy narratives | Unsupported expansion of A self-defense | The claim may illuminate later admiration and Jerome's Roman prominence, but not fourth-century electoral fact. |
| Jerome was formally expelled from Rome after conviction. | Letter 45 and *Against Rufinus* 3.22 defend an August 385 departure; no impartial minutes or verdict were located. | Some modern reconstructions | Event A; legal characterization unresolved | “Contentious departure” preserves what converges without converting self-defense or publisher summaries into a verdict. |
| Jerome was promoted and serenely sent East by the pope. | Damasus had died before the departure; Letter 45 is defensive and controversy is explicit. | Harmonizing devotional timelines | Unsupported opposite simplification | The journey can be providentially significant without suppressing Roman conflict. |
| Paula was merely Jerome's purse. | Letter 108 records pilgrimage, governance, Scripture, buildings, hospitality, and charity; Francis 2020 emphasizes learned collaboration. | Male-centered shorthand, “Jerome's monastery” | Contradicted by A/M evidence, though Jerome controls much of the archive | Patronage is real but includes material founding, learned readership, ascetic government, and institutional agency. |
| Jerome's ascetic demands medically caused Blaesilla's death. | Letters 38–39 and 45 preserve grief, defense, and conflict but not a controlled medical account. | Accusatory and apologetic retellings | Cause unresolved; sole responsibility not established | Ethical scrutiny of authority and bodily discipline remains necessary without inventing a diagnosis. |
| “Jerome's monastery” was one male-owned institution. | Letter 108 distinguishes Paula's material founding and the women's and men's communities; Eustochium later led the women. | Convenient singular place-name | Misleading compression unless roles are stated | The phrase can orient geography only if it does not erase ownership, labor, governance, or multiple houses. |
| Jerome translated the entire Bible alone in a cave. | Gospel and Old Testament prefaces show multiple projects; Houghton controls the Gospel-only New Testament revision; letters and external witnesses show a collaborative institution. | Later visual and popular formulations were not audited. | Historically false as a whole-Bible, solitary, one-stage claim | Jerome remains the decisive individual contributor; no uncollated image carries the argument. |
| Damasus commissioned Jerome to translate the complete New Testament or whole Bible. | The Gospel preface specifies comparison and revision of the four Gospels. | Simplified commissioning narratives | Four-Gospel commission A; larger claim unsupported | A bounded commission reveals the real risk of revising familiar liturgical wording. |
| Jerome translated Acts, the Pauline and Catholic Epistles, and Revelation in their later Vulgate forms. | Houghton, pp. 31–35, uses manuscript and Jerome-citation evidence against the older attribution. | Older whole-New-Testament attribution | Rejected K at the publication's claim level | Composite authorship protects the many historical layers of the Latin Bible. |
| “The Vulgate” was one authorized codex that left Jerome's desk complete. | Old Latin forms predate Jerome; later Vulgate manuscripts combine his work with other Latin texts and centuries of mixture. | Textbook shorthand | False one-stage model | The eventual ecclesial monument was made through authorial labor, copying, correction, reception, and use. |
| Every Latin Psalter called Jerome's is one translation by the same method. | Jerome is associated with Roman, Hexaplar/“Gallican,” and Hebrew-related Psalter work; manuscript descent remains complex. | Liturgical and manuscript labels | Layered attribution; item-level review required | Specific titles and witnesses should replace a generic possessive. |
| *Hebraica veritas* meant that Jerome rejected Greek Scripture entirely. | Old Testament prefaces invoke Hebrew while Jerome also uses the Septuagint, other Greek versions, Origen's Hexapla, and Greek learning. | Polemical Hebrew-versus-Greek narratives | False binary | His source turn can be honored as philological judgment within a multilingual workshop. |
| Jerome mastered Hebrew wholly alone and without Jewish teachers. | Letter 125.12 and prefaces acknowledge instruction and consultation. | Lone-genius hagiography | Contradicted by A evidence | Naming dependence restores teachers to the history even where the archive withholds their names. |
| “Sense for sense, not word for word” was Jerome's universal translation algorithm. | Letter 57.5 states the preference in a defense of translating Epiphanius and immediately qualifies Scripture because even order may matter. | Translation-theory aphorism | Genuine A wording commonly decontextualized | Useful as contextual reflection on judgment, not a mechanical description of every biblical project. |
| Jerome and Augustine were personal companions who met face to face. | Their dual-numbered letters disclose delays, messengers, accidental circulation, dispute, and cooperation; no meeting is recorded. | Later visual reception was not audited. | Epistolary relationship A/N; no meeting | Friendship can be real through correspondence while retaining distance and disagreement. |
| Augustine simply opposed Hebrew study in the Jonah dispute. | Letters 71/75 and 82/116 show concern for congregational reception and Septuagintal authority alongside learned argument. | Philologist-versus-traditionalist caricature | False simplification | The conflict stages a real pastoral question about correction and communal memory. |
| Doctrinal seriousness makes Jerome's ridicule of Rufinus, Vigilantius, and others exemplary zeal. | Jerome's polemics preserve both substantive issues and personal abuse. | Partisan hagiography may repeat the insults as holy courage. | The doctrinal stakes are A; moral sanctification of invective is S/L overreach | Zeal can explain urgency without making humiliation a virtue. |
| *Against Helvidius* proves every biographical detail Jerome gives about his opponent. | Helvidius is known almost entirely through Jerome's attack in the checked corpus. | Catholic reception of Mary's perpetual virginity | Doctrinal argument A; opponent portrait unilaterally controlled | Catholic reception of a conclusion does not turn every insult or reconstruction into neutral biography. |
| The 416 attackers, casualties, and commander are identified beyond dispute. | Augustine, *Proceedings of Pelagius* 66; Innocent Letters 136–137; Jerome Letters 138–139 | Later confessional controversy narratives | Serious violence and destruction converge; Innocent's lack of named charges and rumor/case distinction prevent a settled casualty, perpetrator, command, or verdict claim | The event may be retained without manufacturing a casualty register or legal judgment. |
| Claims drawn from the lives of Paul, Hilarion, or Malchus are controlled here. | Jerome's three texts and NPNF translation inspected at their prologues | Later monastic origin narratives | A for Jerome's literary projects and stated frames; individual episodes do not become external history without corroboration | The lives show Jerome making ascetic memory, comparing models, and mediating testimony. |
| Jerome historically healed a lion. | Gerasimus receives the lion cycle in John Moschus, *Spiritual Meadow* 107; ninth-century *Plerosque nimirum* attaches it to Jerome; the *Golden Legend* later expands and transmits it | Major medieval and modern visual attribute | L reception; the *Golden Legend* did not invent the Jerome form, but no lion belongs to his recoverable life | The story can signify hospitality, healing, disciplined strength, and restored trust when its migration remains visible. |
| A particular visual object, portrait, tableau, or cave can be assigned to Jerome. | Letter 22.7 controls only Jerome's remembered penitential practice; no item-level visual or place witness was inspected. | Uncollated leads only | No object, image, date, symbolism, or location claim published | Subject-authored practice does not authenticate later visual material. |
| Jerome gave Christ his sins in a literal ancient dialogue. | Francis 2020 explicitly calls the dialogue apocryphal and cites André Louf (1990); no ancient witness was located here. | Modern spiritual retelling | Apocryphal L/M classification; Louf not independently inspected | The story may teach grace and repentance only when not quoted as Jerome or dated to his life. |
| Jerome died with a fully reported final speech or serene tableau. | No Possidius-like continuous near-contemporary death account was located. | Conventional saintly-death narrative expectations | Unsupported | Restraint permits an honest image of late labor, bereavement, illness, and insecurity without invented last words. |
| Jerome certainly died on 30 September 420. | Francis 2020 receives 420 and the anniversary; Benedict XVI 2007 prints 419–420; Canellis preserves the alternative. | Roman memorial on 30 September and common timelines | Death/place received M; year disputed; day received rather than independently proved here | The feast can anchor prayer while the chronology remains open. |
| A tomb was shown as Jerome's at Bethlehem. | Adomnán, *De locis sanctis* 2.5, records Arculf's late-seventh-century first-person report | Later Bethlehem memory and claimed Roman translation | E for a localized pilgrim tomb; not eyewitness burial, continuous custody, or proof of the Roman transfer | An early place-memory can be narrated without authenticating present remains. |
| A visual witness supplies Jerome's historical face or a controlled later image history. | No securely contemporary portrait or item-level later visual witness was inspected. | Uncollated leads only | No physiognomic, visual-development, date, or symbolic claim published | Visual reception awaits its own source audit. |
| A patronage list establishes a current invocation or historical office. | No item-level competent patronage source was checked. | Uncollated leads only | No current patronage, date, list, or life claim published | Each proposed patronage requires a dated competent source. |
| “Ignorance of Scripture is ignorance of Christ” is merely a later slogan. | Jerome, prologue to the Isaiah commentary, exact CCL locus supplied by Francis 2020. | Biblical apostolate, catechesis, quotation culture | Genuine A locus, though Latin not independently collated in this edition | Orders study toward encounter with Christ; does not make information a substitute for charity. |
| Any attractive saying labeled “St Jerome” is authentic. | The checked corpus supplies exact loci for only selected sayings; unattributed quotation lists do not. | Memes, devotional collections, study aids | Unsupported until work, section, edition, and translation are identified | Attribution discipline honors the author and protects readers from pious fabrication. |

## Items requiring further tradition-history work

- exact Prosper text and transmission behind the competing birth dates;
- earliest witness and route for “Sophronius” in Jerome's expanded name;
- external controls for individual episodes in the now-inspected lives of
  Paul, Hilarion, and Malchus;
- item-level witnesses for papal-secretary, frustrated-papacy, cardinal-dress,
  four-Doctor, cave, object, portrait, and other visual claims; further work on
  the precise Gerasimus-to-Jerome lion-transfer mechanism;
- primary burial testimony, each claimed translation stage, current competent
  shrine descriptions, custody evidence, and any published examination of
  remains;
- earliest text of the “give me your sins” dialogue before Louf's modern use;
- item-level competent sources for each patronage and current liturgical or
  institutional claim;
- edition-level authentication and translation history for detached sayings
  before they enter the publication.
