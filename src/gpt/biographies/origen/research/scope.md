# Origen — research scope

## Document identity

- **Publication ID:** `biographies/origen`
- **Title:** *Origen: Scripture, School, and the Long Trial of a Teacher*
- **Genre:** source-first historical biography of an early Christian author and
  teacher under `guidance/biographies.md`; not a critical edition, saint's Life,
  canonization dossier, or general doctrinal treatise
- **Subject:** Origen of Alexandria, also called Adamantius, Christian exegete,
  teacher, presbyter, textual scholar, preacher, ecclesial consultant, and
  confessor
- **Life range:** conventionally c. 184/185–c. 253/254 CE. The birth is inferred
  from Eusebius's statement that Origen was not quite seventeen at Leonides's
  death (*Ecclesiastical History* 6.2.12); the death range depends on Eusebius's
  age of sixty-nine at 7.1. Neither endpoint is a civil record.
- **Principal places:** Alexandria; Rome; Caesarea Maritima and Jerusalem;
  Antioch; Arabia; the route toward Achaia; and Tyre only in Jerome's later
  death-and-burial report
- **Languages and ecclesial frame:** principally Greek-speaking Christianity in
  the Roman eastern Mediterranean, with Hebrew consultation and several Greek
  biblical versions where text-critical work requires them; later Greek, Latin,
  Syriac, and other transmission is treated only where it changes what can be
  attributed to Origen
- **Mutable-claim cutoff:** online institutional, manuscript, papal, and
  release-relevant records were checked through 2026-07-19. No present shrine,
  relic, cult, patronage, or liturgical claim is inferred from an older source.

## Reader and governing question

The intended reader is a serious general reader, cleric, student, or researcher
who needs one sustained life without a false choice between admiration and
condemnation. The governing question is how a martyr's son became one of the
ancient Church's most consequential teachers of Scripture, and how his life, writings,
speculations, ecclesial rupture, confession under torture, and posthumous trial
can be reconstructed when his biography is late and apologetic and much of his
corpus survives through translators, excerpts, and opponents.

## Governing thesis and boundary

Origen joined philology, philosophical inquiry, ecclesial teaching, prayer,
ascetic practice, patronage, collaborative book production, and embodied
confession at an exceptional scale. His work supplied durable exegetical and
theological resources while also opening speculative constructions about
rational creatures, embodiment, resurrection, and the end that the Church did
not receive without qualification. Later Origenists, translators, defenders,
and accusers transformed that inheritance; they are historically related to
Origen without becoming transparent witnesses to his exact words.

This is a historical thesis, not an ecclesiastical verdict. The publication
does not canonize Origen, reverse Constantinople II, decide sanctity, equate
later Origenism with every authentic work, or replace Catholic doctrine with a
reconstruction of pre-Nicene inquiry.

## Included

- Alexandrian household, education, Leonides's martyrdom, poverty, teaching,
  asceticism, and the disputed bodily report;
- the changing and incompletely documented Alexandrian teaching institution,
  philosophical formation, pupils, patrons, women, scribes, and copyists;
- travel, Palestinian lay preaching, ordination, rupture with Demetrius, and
  divided ecclesial reception;
- Caesarean school, workshop, preaching, textual comparison, consultations,
  friendships, opponents, and the material conditions of authorship;
- principal genres and works—scholia, homilies, commentaries, letters,
  apologetic, systematic investigation, dialogue, and Hexaplar materials—with
  their surviving language and transmission layer identified;
- representative exact-locus treatment of Scripture, rule of faith, Trinity,
  Christ, freedom, pre-existence, resurrection, restoration, prayer, and the
  ethical limits of anti-Jewish argument;
- Decian imprisonment and torture, survival, death chronology, and the absence
  of a controlled deathbed or burial chain;
- Pamphilus and Eusebius, Methodius, fourth-century appropriation, the first and
  second Origenist controversies, Justinian's intervention, Constantinople II,
  Latin transmission, modern critical recovery, and Benedict XVI's 2007
  catecheses; and
- memorable biographical, doctrinal, cultic, quotation, tomb, and relic claims
  reviewed as traditions rather than silently admitted to the life.

## Excluded or deliberately bounded

- no complete catalogue of works, manuscripts, catena fragments, Hexaplar
  readings, translations, scholia, homilies, or commentary books;
- no full critical collation of *On First Principles*, the Greek fragments,
  Rufinus, Jerome, hostile dossiers, or every later proposition attributed to
  Origen;
- no complete reconstruction of Celsus, Heracleon, Demetrius, the Alexandrian
  proceedings, or the sixth-century Palestinian parties from one-sided
  evidence;
- no whole-corpus lexical or ethical audit of Origen's language about Jews;
  publication-level conclusions are restricted to identified passages;
- no medical finding about self-castration, torture, disability, or cause of
  death;
- no archaeological authentication of a grave, remains, school, library, or
  workshop, and no custody history for relics;
- no item-level iconographic, patronage, local-cult, feast-calendar, or shrine
  census; and
- no independent theological judgment, canonization judgment, imprimatur,
  ecclesiastical approval, or pastoral recommendation of Origen's ascetic
  practices.

## Evidence classes

- **A — subject-authored or Origenian text:** controlled by work, locus, genre,
  date, audience, language, and transmission. An Origenian work surviving in
  Rufinus is not treated as an unmediated Greek autograph.
- **N — contemporary or near-contemporary external witness:** especially the
  farewell address traditionally attributed to Gregory Thaumaturgus; panegyric
  can establish remembered relationship and curriculum without becoming a
  neutral timetable.
- **E — early received report:** principally Eusebius, Jerome, and embedded
  lost archival notices. Eusebius wrote about a century later as an admirer and
  defender of the Caesarean inheritance; his quotations or references to lost
  letters may preserve A material, but his narration is not eyewitness.
- **L — later tradition:** later biographical, polemical, local, devotional,
  iconographic, relic, cultic, or quotation transmission, evidence first for
  the period that received it.
- **M — official ecclesial reception:** limited to what the particular act did,
  such as Constantinople II's received anathema 11 or a papal general audience.
- **K — named modern critical reconstruction:** used only to the inspected
  claim, premises, and alternatives; a publisher record or abstract does not
  make the book's uninspected argument available.
- **S — source-grounded project synthesis:** the publication's inference from
  identified evidence, never placed in an ancient witness's voice.

## Principal corpus and transmission controls

The ancient biographical spine is Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 6 and
7.1, read as a later sympathetic defense built partly from letters, people, and
records no longer extant. Gregory's *Address of Thanks* is near-contemporary
panegyric for the Caesarean school. Porphyry's hostile notice survives only
through Eusebius. Jerome's *On Illustrious Men* 54 and 56 adds later Latin
reception and the Tyre report. Photius, *Bibliotheca* codex 118, is a ninth-
century summary of a lost apology, not minutes of Alexandrian synods.

Origenian controls used at exact loci include *On First Principles*, *Against
Celsus*, *On Prayer*, *Exhortation to Martyrdom*, *Letter to Gregory*, the
Africanus correspondence, surviving commentary and homily material, and the
*Dialogue with Heraclides*. *On First Principles* is tagged at every use: the
complete continuous witness is chiefly Rufinus's Latin, which he says he
corrected or smoothed; substantial Greek survives only in selected passages,
and hostile fragments are not automatically verbatim. *Against Celsus* survives
in Greek, although the lost Celsus is available only through Origen's response.
The Tura find supplies Greek papyrus witnesses including *On Pascha* and the
*Dialogue*. Bayerische Staatsbibliothek Cod. graec. 314, copied in the first
half of the twelfth century, supplies twenty-nine Greek Psalm homilies
identified in 2012—twenty-five then unknown as complete homilies and four with
Rufinus parallels—not an autograph.

Official and institutional controls are the received conciliar decrees in
Norman Tanner's edition, the Holy See's two 2007 general audiences, the
Bayerische Staatsbibliothek's manuscript record and catalogue notice, and the
modern critical-source records described in the source audit.

## Material uncertainties

- birth year, exact birthplace, ancestry, legal status, and the original status
  or force of “Adamantius”;
- Clement's degree of personal instruction and the institutional continuity,
  curriculum, and episcopal form of the Alexandrian “school”;
- whether Porphyry's Ammonius was the teacher later associated with Plotinus;
- truth, motive, timing, and later use of the self-castration report;
- dates and purposes of the first Arabian journey, Julia Mamaea's audience,
  the Caracallan flight, the stages and locations of Hexaplar work, and
  Juliana's manuscript transfer;
- the original charges, acts, votes, legal theory, and effect of Demetrius's
  proceedings; a juridical “exile” is not established;
- Ambrose's prior Valentinian or Marcionite affiliation;
- fine chronology, authorship process, revision, and transmission of many
  works, homilies, and letters;
- modal force and scope of Origen's restoration language, especially the devil,
  and its relation to freedom, judgment, later Origenism, and Catholic teaching;
- place, duration, bodily consequences, and causal relation of the Decian ordeal
  to Origen's later death; and
- Tyre as death and burial place, any tomb or relic identity, and any stable
  ancient cult.

## Located leads not used beyond their inspected scope

- Arthur P. Urbano's “Difficulties in Writing the Life of Origen” supplied the
  methodological warning that Origen's life is a history of interested
  transmission; the accessible Oxford abstract and handbook framing, not every
  page of the chapter, were controlled.
- Ronald E. Heine, *Origen: Scholarship in the Service of the Church*; Henri
  Crouzel, *Origen*; Peter W. Martens, *Origen and Scripture*; Elizabeth A.
  Clark, *The Origenist Controversy*; and the reception studies by Andrew Louth,
  Richard A. Layton, and Robert J. Daly orient major questions. Only arguments
  or summaries actually inspected and identified in the source audit may carry
  a claim.
- John Behr's Oxford Early Christian Texts edition of *On First Principles*
  controls the work's transmission architecture at the publication-record and
  inspected-edition level; protected critical text and translation are not
  reproduced.
- The uncollated Alexandrian dossier, complete reciprocal first-controversy
  corpus, Justinian dossier, all fifteen anti-Origenist anathemas, local
  calendars, tomb traditions, relic inventories, and modern archaeological or
  scientific claims remain research leads rather than hidden support.

## Rights boundary

Project-created prose and organization are intended for CC BY 4.0. Ancient
Greek and Latin wording is public domain, but modern critical establishment,
apparatus, translations, scholarship, databases, and manuscript photography
retain their own rights. Nineteenth-century NPNF/ANF English was used only as a
public-domain research aid and is not treated as a modern critical translation,
especially for *On First Principles*. Vatican English is copyrighted and is
paraphrased except for no more than brief necessary wording with attribution.
The BSB manuscript images and catalogue metadata retain the institution's
terms. No third-party image, scan, manuscript transcription, modern critical
text, or substantial modern quotation is reproduced.
