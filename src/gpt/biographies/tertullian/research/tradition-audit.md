# Tertullian — tradition audit

Tertullian attracted fewer controlled miracle and relic traditions than many
ancient Christian subjects. His thin biography instead accumulated names,
occupations, motives, institutional hinges, denominational labels, and polished
sayings. This record separates earliest located witness, later reception,
historical status, and responsible significance. “Not established” means not
established within the recorded source search; it is not proof that no local or
lost tradition ever existed.

| His complete legal name was “Quintus Septimius Florens Tertullianus.” | The third-person closing sentence of *On the Veiling of Virgins* 17 names “Septimius Tertullianus” and says the work is his; expanded forms occur in medieval titles and paratext. | Library records, older biographies, encyclopedias | Shorter transmitted internal form secure, but not a modern signature; full form L and variable, not a birth or civil record. | Use “Tertullian” ordinarily. Preserve the expanded name as transmission history, not contemporary identity. |
| He was born at Carthage exactly in 155 or 160. | No ancient birth record. Jerome, DVI 53, gives Carthaginian identity; modern calculations use assumed career length and old age. | Biographical timelines and anniversaries | Carthage is the secure authorial center; exact birthplace and year unproved. K estimates range to c. 170. | Dates must appear as reconstructed ranges, never known anniversaries. |
| Tertullian was straightforwardly Punic, Berber, African nationalist, or anti-Roman resistance writer. | Corpus places him in Roman African institutions and multilingual Carthage; no modern ethnic self-declaration exists. | National, racial, postcolonial, and counter-postcolonial readings | African and Carthaginian placement secure; modern ethnic and nationalist categories disputed K. | Historical Africa matters without turning geography into essence or erasing participation in Roman law and culture. |
| His father was a proconsular centurion. | Jerome, *On Illustrious Men* 53, c. 392/393. | Social-class and military-family biographies | E late report with no earlier located corroboration; office meaning disputed. | It may preserve lost learning, but cannot by itself establish childhood, class, ethnicity, or education. |
| He was a professional advocate in Rome and the jurist Tertullianus cited in the *Digest*. | Legal-rhetorical performance in the corpus; Eusebius, HE 2.2.4, praises Roman-law knowledge; a similarly named jurist survives separately. | “Lawyer theologian” biographies | Legal expertise strong; profession, Roman employment, residence, and prosopographical identity unproved. | Analyze forensic habits without manufacturing a résumé or collapsing names. |
| He converted at Rome after a witnessed conversion scene. | *On Repentance* 1 and *Apologeticum* 18.4 imply a pagan past and conversion but narrate no place, teacher, or scene. | Smooth spiritual biographies | Convert status A; Rome and episode unsupported. | Acknowledge conversion while leaving its human agents, motives, sequence, and baptism unknown. |
| His wife exemplified every detail of the ideal Christian household in *To His Wife* 2.8. | *To His Wife* 1.1 establishes an addressed wife; 2.8 is paraenetic ideal. | Marriage anthologies and Catholic quotation | Marriage A secure; wife's identity, children, household history, and complete conformity unknown. | Receive the ideal without turning exhortation into domestic surveillance or invented biography. |
| Tertullian was certainly a presbyter. | Jerome, DVI 53; compare Tertullian's lay self-placement in *Exhortation to Chastity* 7.3 and *On Monogamy* 12.2. | Clerical biographies and titles | E late and internally tense; no ordination act or contemporary external confirmation. | Leave presbyterate doubtful rather than using office to explain all authority. |
| He edited the *Passion of Perpetua and Felicity*. | Modern literary attribution from affinities in style, martyr theology, and New Prophecy themes; no named ancient editorial notice. | Editions and Carthaginian literary histories | Plausible K hypothesis, not established authorship or editorship. | The *Passion* remains vital context even when its anonymous editorial layer is left anonymous. |
| He became a Montanist in exactly 207. | Modern phase tables coordinate relative work order and increasing New Prophecy language. | Textbooks and Catholic/Montanist two-period narratives | Explicit allegiance A secure; exact hinge and simple institutional binary unproved. | Trace work-by-work trajectories and use “Montanism” as conventional shorthand with explanation. |
| A named pope excommunicated him in 207. | No located act. *Against Praxeas* 1 reports division and revoked Roman peace letters but names no bishop or sentence. | Schism timelines naming Victor or Zephyrinus | Unsupported as a dated juridical event; opponent identification remains contested K. | His isolation is real in self-description, but its mechanism and competent acts are unknown. |
| Pope Callistus issued the edict mocked in *On Modesty*. | *On Modesty* 1 names neither author nor see; chapter 21 develops the authority dispute. | Roman papal biographies and absolution controversies | Callistus is an influential K identification; an African bishop remains possible. No edict text survives independently. | The absolution controversy is secure; name, place, date, and institutional reach remain open. |
| Tertullian personally founded a separate Tertullianist church and owned its basilica. | Augustine, *On Heresies* 86, in the 420s, reports later Carthaginian Tertullianists, their dwindling return, and basilica transfer. | Denominational family trees and local property stories | E strong for a fifth-century group and property memory, late for Tertullian's own act. | Do not backdate an institution or chain of custody by two centuries. |
| Cyprian asked every day, “Give me the master.” | Jerome, DVI 53, through a reported oral chain reaching a person who knew Cyprian's secretary. | Patristic reading anecdotes | Memorable late E anecdote; broad Cyprianic literary dependence independently strong. | Use it as reception memory, not proof of personal contact, a surviving library, or verbatim daily practice. |
| Tertullian invented Christian Latin or was the first Latin Christian author. | The surviving corpus is the first extensive Latin Christian theological corpus; the Scillitan Acts and other Latin Christian evidence precede it. | “Father of Latin Christianity” and “first Latin theologian” formulas | Exceptional scale and creativity secure; solitary invention and chronological firstness overstated. | Credit durable formation of vocabulary and prose without erasing communities and earlier texts. |
| He invented the words and complete doctrine of the Trinity, person, and substance. | *Against Praxeas* 2–3, 8–9, 25–30 contains a powerful pre-Nicene technical cluster. | Doctrinal genealogies and papal catechesis | A formulations secure; absolute lexical invention and identity with later Nicene-Chalcedonian precision unproved. | Treat him as a major architect whose terms retain their historical semantic range. |
| “I believe because it is absurd” is Tertullian's creed. | Loose reduction of *On the Flesh of Christ* 5, which uses a sequence of anti-docetic paradoxes. | Philosophy-of-religion polemic, quotation collections, memes | Not Tertullian's sentence and misleading as a general theory of faith. | Cite the work and chapter; explain incarnation controversy rather than making him an irrationalist mascot. |
| “The blood of the martyrs is the seed of the Church” is an exact quotation. | *Apologeticum* 50.13 has *semen est sanguis Christianorum*, “the blood of Christians is seed.” | Martyr anthologies, sermons, apologetics | Familiar English is a faithful expansion in sense, not the exact Latin sentence. | Attribute exact wording and retain the forensic argument that execution generates adherents. |
| “See how these Christians love one another” is Tertullian's direct neutral observation. | *Apologeticum* 39 reports what pagan outsiders exclaim. | Charity sermons, community slogans, quotations | Tertullianian reported speech in an apologetic, not survey evidence or his own freestanding maxim. | Retain speaker, chapter, genre, and the practices that the literary exclamation interprets. |
| “The soul is naturally Christian” summarizes his complete anthropology. | *Apologeticum* 17.6 within an appeal to spontaneous popular testimony. | Natural-theology slogans | Exact A wording and locus; decontextualized universal doctrinal summary false. | Read beside *On the Soul*, explicit faith, baptism, and the apologetic purpose. |
| “The flesh is the hinge of salvation” proves every later doctrine in finished form. | *On the Resurrection of the Flesh* 8.2, *caro salutis est cardo*. | Catechism, theology, sacramental and embodiment studies | Exact A maxim and significant Catholic reception; not a substitute for the treatise or later definitions. | Its force lies in the bodily sequence it introduces, with historical semantic limits intact. |
| “What has Athens to do with Jerusalem?” means he rejected philosophy and reason altogether. | *Prescription against Heretics* 7 in an argument against curiosity that destabilizes the received rule. | Anti-intellectual and anti-philosophy slogans | Exact rhetorical question; universal anti-reason reading contradicted by his extensive legal and philosophical argument. | Restore opponent, genre, and the distinction between reasoned defense and curiosity without rule. |
| His recommendation to delay baptism proves infant baptism did not exist. | *On Baptism* 18 recommends delay for little children under specified prudential concerns. | Paedobaptist and credobaptist controversy | A evidence for existing practice and debate; neither absence nor universal mandate. | Use as an early witness to disagreement, not as a complete demographic or later sacramental rule. |
| He either universally endorsed or universally forbade Christian military service. | *Apologeticum* 42 mentions Christians in camps; *On the Crown* 11 argues incompatibility in rigorist terms. | Pacifist and military proof-text traditions | Both A; genre and relative development remain in tension. | Preserve tension instead of harmonizing a timeless policy from one passage. |
| He was the founder of modern religious liberty. | *To Scapula* 2 argues that worship should not be compelled; apologetic setting is Severan provincial coercion. | Religious-freedom genealogies | Significant ancient anti-coercion argument; not a modern constitutional system or universal rights charter. | Historical analogy may orient only if legal setting, theological argument, and limits remain stated. |
| “The devil's gateway” is wholly forged—or fully describes Tertullian's account of women. | *On the Apparel of Women* 1.1 contains the harsh address; *On Veiling* 9 and *On Baptism* 17 restrict roles, while *On the Soul* 9 and New Prophecy references acknowledge women visionaries and prophets. | Misogyny debates, apologetic sanitization, feminist recovery | The misogynist rhetoric and restrictions are genuine A; the corpus is internally more complex without becoming egalitarian by default. | Do not suppress harm or use charismatic speech to erase institutional restrictions. |
| Tertullian possessed New Testament autographs in apostolic churches. | *Prescription against Heretics* 36 uses *authenticae litterae* within an appeal to apostolic churches. | Autograph and textual-preservation apologetics | The phrase's referent is debated; surviving physical autographs are not established. | Translate and explain the claim without turning rhetoric into a manuscript provenance certificate. |
| Tertullian's quotations reconstruct one fixed personal Old Latin Bible or Marcion's exact Bible. | Scriptural wording varies across works; *Against Marcion* mediates an adversary through a multi-recension hostile text. | Canon lists, Marcion reconstructions, Vetus Latina databases | Important A witnesses, but source, memory, translation, revision, and transmission vary. | Every citation needs work, locus, critical text, and reciprocal control; no isolated verse carries the whole reconstruction. |
| All of *Against the Jews* is securely one composition by Tertullian. | Early chapters and later material overlap *Against Marcion* 3; work history preserves unity and authorship disputes. | Printed corpora and anti-Jewish reception | Core attribution commonly received; final portion and unity contested K. | Mark textual boundary and ethical harm rather than citing the work as a uniform eyewitness account of Jews. |
| Tertullian wrote an exactly fixed number of authentic works. | Authorial cross-references, Jerome, uneven manuscript collections, lost-work and spuria inventories. | “Thirty-one works,” “forty works,” and other totals | Large corpus secure; totals depend on titles, books, fragments, loss, authenticity, and revision. | Name the counted rule and do not make numerical abundance evidence of status or certainty. |
| The surviving medieval manuscripts preserve Tertullian unchanged. | Earliest located item is late-eighth-century; Agobardinum, Trecense, Cluniacense, Corbeiense, excerpts, and separate *Apologeticum* routes are incomplete and divergent. | Facsimile and edition confidence | Material transmission L/K secure, but no autograph and no uniform route. | Work-specific textual criticism, lost witnesses, early collations, and editorial intervention must remain visible. |
| Augustine's report proves Tertullian returned to the Catholic Church at death. | Augustine, *On Heresies* 86, reports later Tertullianists returning; he supplies no deathbed of Tertullian. | Reconciliation narratives | Unsupported personal inference from a fifth-century group history. | Neither award a final reconciliation nor condemn final obstinacy when the ending is undocumented. |
| Tertullian died a martyr. | No passion or execution record; Jerome says only that he lived to advanced old age. | Confusion with the martyrs he praised, private honor, online biographies | Unsupported. His advocacy of martyrdom is not evidence of his manner of death. | Preserve the unknown ending and distinguish author, confessor ideals, and executed witnesses. |
| Tertullian is a canonized saint or Doctor of the Church with a feast day. | No competent canonization, Doctor declaration, stable received feast, or early cult located; Benedict XVI's 2007 audience is catechesis. | Honorific “Saint,” patristic lists, online feast claims | Not established in Catholic official reception audited here. | Historical greatness and selective Catholic citation do not create juridical or liturgical status. |
| His tomb and relics have a continuous chain of custody. | No controlled death place, burial notice, translation dossier, shrine record, or custody chain located. | Unsourced local and online claims | Unestablished within the bounded search. | No site or object should be authenticated by repetition, signage, or biographical expectation. |
| Miracles or posthumous appearances authenticate Tertullian. | No controlled early miracle, vision, apparition, healing, or posthumous appearance dossier was located. | No stable received cycle identified in the audited sources | Unestablished; not proof that no local story ever circulated. | His reception is chiefly textual and controversial; silence must not be filled with generic hagiography. |
| A familiar bearded portrait is a contemporary likeness with fixed attributes. | No securely contemporary portrait or physically authenticated likeness located. Later book, church, and online images were not exhaustively inventoried. | Generic patristic portraiture and author illustrations | Later iconography may witness its own period; resemblance to the historical person unproved. | Label later art by creator/date/source before using it; do not infer face, clothing, office, or sanctity from convention. |
| Tertullian is an official patron of lawyers, apologists, Carthage, or another cause. | No competent current patronage designation located in the official and historical controls inspected. | Informal association based on legal learning or authorship | Unestablished in this audit. | Thematic affinity is not ecclesial appointment; name the competent act if a future claim is added. |
| Benedict XVI canonized, rehabilitated, or formally declared him Doctor in 2007. | General audience of 30 May 2007 praises his contribution and warns about rigorist isolation. | Headlines and devotional summaries of Catholic recovery | M catechesis only; no canonization, feast, Doctor declaration, or reversal of historical judgments. | Receive the audience within its actual genre: selective teaching can honor achievement without approving the whole corpus. |

## Traditions not advanced because no controlled witness was located

- an exact contemporary birth or baptism record;
- a competent ancient or modern canonization or Doctor-of-the-Church act;
- a stable early cult, received feast, suppressed feast, or universal liturgical
  commemoration;
- execution, martyr act, deathbed speech, exact final confession, or controlled
  cause and place of death;
- burial act, authenticated grave, relic inventory, translation, custody chain,
  shrine, or scientifically identified remains;
- miracle, healing, apparition, posthumous appearance, or answered-prayer
  dossier tied to a dated earliest witness;
- securely contemporary portrait, physical description, or authenticated
  likeness;
- competent patronage designation;
- a legal-employment or Roman-residence record;
- an ordination act, named excommunication, or preserved episcopal edict; or
- an authenticated detached saying lacking work, exact locus, language,
  edition, and translator.

These negative findings derive from the reasonably accessible subject corpus,
ancient reception, official Catholic material, manuscript and institutional
records, and current scholarship actually inspected. They do not claim that
every municipal archive, archaeological report, local calendar, image,
manuscript, or oral tradition has been exhausted.

## Items requiring further tradition-history work

1. earliest manuscript occurrence and semantic history of every expanded name
   form;
2. full reception history of the centurion, Roman lawyer, presbyter, and
   Roman-clergy-envy reports in Jerome 53;
3. earliest precise dates and proponents of the 207 conversion and named-papal-
   excommunication narratives;
4. complete genealogy of Callistus, Victor, and Zephyrinus identifications in
   *On Modesty* and *Against Praxeas*;
5. item-level history of the Perpetua editor attribution;
6. reception and translation history of the “absurd,” “blood,” “love,” “soul,”
   “flesh,” and “Athens” sayings;
7. institutional and property research on Augustine's Tertullianists and their
   basilica without backdating the results;
8. complete local-calendar, cult, tomb, relic, miracle, patronage, and shrine
   search across relevant African, Roman, and other archives;
9. item-level iconographic history before assigning a conventional portrait,
   attribute, office, or halo; and
10. history of modern “rehabilitation” language before assigning legal,
    liturgical, or doctrinal force to selective Catholic praise.
