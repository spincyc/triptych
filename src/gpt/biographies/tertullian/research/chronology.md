# Tertullian — chronology audit

## Method

Dates use proleptic Common Era. No ancient life supplies a birth date, annual
career, conversion date, schism date, or death date. The controlled chronology
therefore begins with two unusually strong internal termini: the allusion to
Clodius Albinus's defeat after 19 February 197 in *To the Nations* 1.17.4 and
the eclipse of 14 August 212 recalled in *To Scapula* 3. Authorial
cross-references and revision notices supply relative order; named modern
reconstructions supply ranges. Thematic change alone is not converted into an
exact calendar.

Codes follow `guidance/biographies.md`: A is subject-authored evidence; N is
contemporary or near-contemporary external evidence; E is early received
memory; L is later tradition or transmission; M is an official ecclesial act;
K is named modern reconstruction; S is project synthesis.

## Competing chronology and dependencies

1. **Birth and death are terminal hypotheses.** A c. 155–170 birth range is
   inferred from competing career reconstructions. Death after 212 is secure
   only as a terminus; c. 220/225 combines later old-age memory and relative
   literary order. Neither endpoint is independently dated.
2. **The 197 anchor is local.** The Albinus allusion fixes *To the Nations*
   after 19 February and supports the usual later-197 placement of the
   *Apologeticum*. It does not date the conversion, baptism, marriage, education,
   or every early treatise.
3. **The 212 anchor is local.** The eclipse fixes *To Scapula* after 14 August.
   It does not prove that all works assigned after it were written in a known
   order or that literary activity ended in 212.
4. **Revision differs from date.** *Against Marcion* 1.1 establishes three
   stages. It does not provide complete contents or civil years for the two
   unavailable forms.
5. **Theological development is not a clock.** Increasing invocation of the
   Paraclete and intensifying positions on marriage, fasting, and absolution
   support relative development. They do not establish a single 207
   “conversion to Montanism,” one formal schism day, or a named papal decree.
6. **Opponent synchronization is disputed.** Identifying the unnamed Roman
   bishop of *Against Praxeas* 1 with Victor or Zephyrinus, and the issuer in
   *On Modesty* 1 with Callistus, changes proposed dates. The publication does
   not use either identification as a fixed point.
7. **Barnes and Secord are reconstructions.** Barnes's foundational revision,
   older c. 150/160 birth tables, Secord's c. 170 and c. 196–212 compression,
   and broader current reference ranges are preserved as alternatives. No one
   table is disguised as an ancient chronology.
8. **Transmission milestones date witnesses, not composition.** A late-eighth-
   century fragment, ninth-century Agobardinus, later collections, and
   sixteenth-century collations establish survival routes. Their dates cannot
   date an ancient work except through textual-critical argument.

## Chronological questions deliberately left open

- birth year and place, childhood, ancestry, teachers, education, and legal
  status;
- conversion, baptism, marriage, any Roman travel or residence, and any legal
  employment;
- absolute dates and fine order of nearly all writings beyond the 197 and 212
  termini;
- exact stages of *Against Marcion* and relationship among anti-heretical
  works;
- beginning, institutional form, and regional relationship of New Prophecy
  allegiance;
- date and identity behind *Against Praxeas* 1 and *On Modesty* 1;
- ordination or ministerial office and any formal ecclesial separation;
- Tertullian's relation, if any, to composition or editing of the Perpetua
  *Passion*;
- final works, final communion, death year, cause and place, and burial; and
- exact formation dates of manuscript families, the composite Pseudo-Gelasian
  dossier, and every later quotation, cult, or institutional claim.
