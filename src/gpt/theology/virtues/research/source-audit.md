# Source Audit — The Virtues

The sources below were checked on 2026-07-16. `Verified—primary` means that the claim was checked at the stated locus in an original-language witness and/or an identified human translation. `Verified—official` means that the paragraph was checked in the Holy See's official English text. `Qualified` means that the source was used to mark a boundary or unstable classification, not to add a coordinate virtue. Online pages are access witnesses; their wrappers, annotations, and typography are not treated as part of the underlying edition.

This ledger records the sources that govern the work. The candidate-by-candidate loci and classification decisions belong in `virtue-inventory.md`, and translation collisions and vice-name choices belong in `terminology-audit.md`. The number fifty-two is a count of treated strict entries and dossiers, not a claim that every principal virtue, species, potential part, and nested special virtue is a coordinate habit.

The appendix distinguishes seven relation statuses so that its physical defect–virtue–excess layout does not imply one theory of opposition: a mean flank; a named contrary; an objective outcome in justice; a counterfeit or corruption; **Unnamed**, where the source recognizes a departure but gives it no proper noun; **Unpaired**, where no dedicated opposite is proper to the row although inherited failures, descriptions, or counterfeits may remain relevant; and **Inapplicable**, where the axis itself does not govern that side. These are editorial audit classifications, not terms attributed to Aristotle, Augustine, or Aquinas.

## Aristotle

### Witnesses and source role

- **Greek:** Aristotle, *Ethica Nicomachea*, ed. J. Bywater (Oxford, 1894), checked by Bekker locus in the [Perseus Greek witness](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0053). The underlying Greek edition is public domain; Perseus's electronic presentation retains its own status.
- **Working English:** W. D. Ross, trans., *The Nicomachean Ethics* (Oxford, 1925), checked in the [Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.html) and the [Wikisource transcription](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nicomachean_Ethics_(Ross)). The 1925 translation is public domain in the United States. The publication uses project-created paraphrase and only short source phrases.
- **Role:** the *Nicomachean Ethics* controls the natural-philosophical account of habituation, choice, the rational mean, the principal acquired moral virtues and their named or unnamed flanks, justice's special structure, the intellectual virtues, prudence, and qualified neighboring states. It does not supply infused or theological virtue.

Both uses are qualified. No item enters the controlling census from a disputed *Magna Moralia* or *De virtutibus et vitiis* attribution.

## Augustine

### Witnesses and source role

The direct Augustinian corpus for this edition is bounded to the nine works tabulated below: *De moribus Ecclesiae catholicae*, *De libero arbitrio*, *Enchiridion de fide, spe et caritate*, *De doctrina christiana*, *De vera religione*, selected loci of *De civitate Dei*, *De patientia*, *De continentia*, and *De sancta virginitate*. No unlisted Augustinian work is silently included; a passage encountered only through Aquinas remains marked mediated unless it was independently checked. The working English witnesses are the public-domain translations in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* series as reproduced by New Advent. The Latin of *De libero arbitrio* and *De vera religione* was checked independently; the former is not included in the same NPNF set, and the latter's Latin controls the focused claim about worship. Augustine supplies Christian ordering by the supreme good, the faith–hope–love architecture, and exact boundaries for patience, continence, and virginity. He does not replace every distinct virtue with an undifferentiated use of the word “love.”

| ST II–II, q. 108, a. 2 ad 3 ([English](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3108.htm)) | Vindication can fail by remissness or exceed by *crudelitas vel saevitia*, represented in the identified English witness as “cruelty or brutality.” | **Remissness** and **brutality** provide the terse table pair. **Cruelty** remains explicitly recorded as the source alternative rather than becoming a second excess or disappearing. |
| ST II–II, q. 120, aa. 1–2 ([English](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3120.htm)) | Equity or *epieikeia* is a virtue and a subjective part of justice taken generally; it corresponds properly to legal justice and governs cases in which following the law's letter would defeat the lawgiver's intention. | Equity follows legal justice in the exposition and is not misplaced among potential parts or represented as mere laxity. |
| ST II–II, q. 30, a. 3 ([English](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3030.htm)); q. 159, a. 1 ([English](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3159.htm)) | Mercy is a virtue when its movement is governed by reason and justice; pity as an unregulated passion can impede counsel and wander from justice. Q. 159 uses *immisericordia* for the broad lack of mercy rather than establishing a dedicated vice article proper to mercy. | **Mercilessness** is a marked concise translation of the broad contrary. The other cell preserves **unregulated pity** as affective corruption and **indulgence** as counterfeit; it does not claim that perfected mercy becomes vice by quantity. |
| ST II–II, qq. 129–133, especially q. 131, a. 2 and q. 132, a. 2 ([ambition](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3131.htm); [vainglory](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3132.htm)) | Magnanimity's rational mean can be exceeded under distinct formal respects: presumption relative to capacity, ambition in desire for honor, and vainglory in desire for glory. | The dossier and table preserve all three source-named excesses rather than letting presumption stand for the whole excess side. |
| *NE* IV.2; ST II–II, qq. 134–135, especially q. 135 ([English](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3135.htm)) | Aristotle gives *mikroprepeia* on the defect side and *banausia* or *apeirokalia* on the excess side of magnificence; Aquinas receives Latin *parvificentia* and *consumptio*. | **Paltriness** is the immediate English defect headword and **waste** the compact rendering of *consumptio*. The source terms and Greek vulgar-or-tasteless-display distinction remain in the relation and terminology audit, where they can preserve precision without crowding the cells. |
| ST II–II, q. 136, a. 5 ([English](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3136.htm)) | Longanimity regards a good distant in time and is more allied by object to magnanimity; it is comprised under patience insofar as delay causes sorrow. | Longanimity is shown as cross-related, not simply as a coordinate child of patience. Impatience at delay is shared language rather than a unique vice species. |
| ST II–II, q. 137, a. 3 ([English](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3137.htm)) | Perseverance resists the difficulty arising from duration; constancy resists external impediments, and “pertains to perseverance” while differing in this respect. | Constancy is a subordinate specification pertaining to perseverance, not a coordinate potential part on the same level. No dedicated defect proper to it is supplied, so that side is **Unpaired**; obstinacy remains a counterfeit excess. |
| ST II–II, q. 143, a. 1; q. 151, a. 4 ([parts](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3143.htm); [chastity and purity](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3151.htm)) | The preliminary parts-list makes abstinence about food and sobriety about drink as coordinate subjective species. The later express resolution controls the preliminary enumeration. |
| ST II–II, q. 157, aa. 1–3; q. 159, aa. 1–2, especially q. 159, a. 2 obj. 3 and ad 3 ([clemency and meekness](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3157.htm); [cruelty](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3159.htm)) | Clemency rationally mitigates punishment; severity is a compatible virtue and part of legal justice. The identified English witness uses “laxity” in the objection and reply for deficient remission, but Aquinas does not establish it as a separately analyzed proper vice species. Cruelty exceeds in the mode of punishment, whereas savagery or brutality seeks pleasure in another's suffering and is directly opposed to heroic or godlike virtue rather than to clemency in the same way. | Severity remains by legal justice; clemency remains a potential part of temperance. **Laxity** is permitted only as a marked, attested compact description, while **cruelty** is the direct contrary. Vindication's separately sourced “brutality” alternative is not collapsed into clemency's relation. |
| ST II–II, q. 166, aa. 1–2 ([English](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3166.htm)) | Studiousness moderates appetite in applying the mind to knowledge and is a potential part of temperance under modesty. Aquinas explicitly identifies neglect of study as default and curiosity as excess. | The table uses the source-grounded pair neglect–studiousness–curiosity rather than a generic diligence scale. |
| ST III, q. 85, aa. 1–6 ([English](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/4085.htm)) | Penance is a special virtue in the will, a species or part of justice because amendment includes compensation, and is comprised under commutative justice; it is distinct from the passion of sorrow and from the sacrament considered as sacrament. | One penance row follows commutative justice. Sacramental acts and contextual specifications do not become additional habits, and the pseudo-Augustinian source cited in an objection does not establish Augustinian provenance. |

Question 80's reductions require particular care. Innocence is integral; external friendship is affability; concord, benevolence, and beneficence are relations or acts of friendship; obedience is included under observance before q. 104 establishes it independently; fidelity is truthfulness; just exchange and legislative justice return to commutative and legal justice; good judgment belongs to gnome as director and justice as executor; *eusebeia* and holiness return to religion; and *eucharistia* to gratitude. Independently established piety, religion, gratitude, vindication, liberality, truthfulness, obedience, and equity are therefore retained once.

ST II–II, q. 103, aa. 3–4 distinguishes strict dulia, one species of observance with no further species, from broad dulia, which embraces piety, observance, and similar forms of reverence. Hyperdulia is called the highest species only in that broad sense; it is not a second species of strict dulia. ST III, q. 90 continues to control the boundary between penance's stable virtue, its contextual exercises, and the sacrament's integral human parts.

## Translation and rights treatment

- All dossier definitions and cross-author conclusions are project-created paraphrases. Quotation marks are reserved for short wording actually checked in the identified translation.
- Greek and Latin terms are retained where an English rendering could create or erase a taxonomic distinction. A source-language term, alternate translation, or duplicate name does not add another virtue.
- Ross's 1925 *Nicomachean Ethics*, Solomon's historical *Eudemian Ethics*, Edghill's historical *Categories*, the NPNF Augustine translations, and the English Dominican Fathers' 1920 Aquinas translation are public domain in the United States by publication date. Their public-domain status does not place the websites' wrappers, annotations, or typography under the project license.
- Ancient and medieval source texts remain attributed primary material. The Holy See's official English *Catechism* remains excluded official text under `THIRD_PARTY.md`.
- No complete external text, scan, or bulk transcription is vendored. URLs are verification routes, not permission claims or substitutes for exact loci.

## Exclusions and consequential negative results

- Modern “virtue charts,” seven-versus-seven devotional lists, dictionaries, search snippets, personality inventories, and character-strength taxonomies were not used to add candidates or supply historical vice names.
- The project does not infer a virtue merely because Aquinas quotes an earlier enumeration. Integral conditions, acts, passions, gifts, fruits, beatitudes, counsels, relations, and states of life remain separately classified.
- Aristotle's *nemesis*, shame, continence, friendship, heroic virtue, and *kalokagathia* remain qualified because the controlling texts do not establish them all as coordinate moral virtues in one stable sense.
- No source supports an intrinsic excess of faith or charity, an agent-vice of “excess justice,” a vice in the person who suffers injustice, or a defect consisting simply in marriage rather than virginity or abstention rather than sobriety. Those symmetries are rejected.
- No unnamed Aristotelian extreme is silently converted into an ancient technical term. Any serviceable English descriptor is marked editorial in `terminology-audit.md`.
- Modern summaries and search results were treated only as leads to the cited primary loci. No secondary work controls the census or supplies an otherwise unattested historical term.

## Mollities reception control

Exact Greek and Ross witnesses for Aristotle, exact Latin and historical
English witnesses for ST II--II, qq.138 and 154, four identified witnesses to
1 Corinthians 6:9--11 and its Thomistic commentary, and two contrasting
penitential passages were checked for the bounded
`mollities-reception-audit.md`. They establish distinct lexical and
classificatory uses, not a single transhistorical category.

Aristotle, *NE* VII.7, 1150a9--1150b28 controls ordinary endurance and its
extraordinary-pressure, nature, and illness limits. VII.9,
1151b17--1152a6 contains no *malakia* occurrence; it instead controls the
right-reason boundary between steadfastness and stubbornness. ST II--II,
q.138, aa.1--2 controls Aquinas's perseverance classification, while q.154,
a.11 uses the same Latin noun as an alternative name within a distinct
sexual-act taxonomy.

The Greek text places *malakoi* beside *arsenokoitai* in 1 Corinthians 6:9;
the Clementine and Douay witnesses place their inherited renderings in verse
10, and all witnesses retain verse 11's restorative turn. Aquinas,
*Super I ad Corinthios* 6, lectio 2 gives a medieval sexual exegesis. These
are identified translation and reception decisions, not exhaustive modern
lexical definitions.

Wasserschleben's 1851 collection supplies one sexual penitential tariff and
one use paired with infirmity in inability to bear fasting. The collection is
heterogeneous and is not represented as one universal code. Bounded literal
searches of the official Latin 1917 CIC, current 1983 CIC, and CCEO digital
corpora found no `moll*` occurrence on 2026-07-27. That is a lexical negative
only; it says nothing by itself about source collections, schemata,
interpretations, commentaries, particular law, or the substance of Catholic
sexual teaching.

The stated loci, source roles, public-domain translations, and official *Catechism* paragraphs were internally checked during generation. This is a source audit, not a critical edition: manuscripts were not collated, the full history of Greek and Latin technical terms was not reconstructed, and no claim is made that the working historical translations settle every lexical question. Web availability may change without changing the cited work and locus.

Independent Aristotelian, patristic, Thomistic, moral-theological, pastoral, or clinical review has not occurred and is not claimed. Before installation, the final prose and appendix must be checked against the completed inventory and terminology audit so that no late dossier, vice label, quotation, or source claim escapes this ledger.
