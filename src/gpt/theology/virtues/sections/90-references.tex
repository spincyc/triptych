\section{References, Editions, and Source Boundaries}
\label{sec:references}

This work is a source-grounded synthesis rather than a translation. Definitions and taxonomic judgments were checked against the loci below; words in quotation marks are kept brief, while dossier definitions are original paraphrases. Bekker numbers control Aristotle citations; book, chapter, and section control Augustine; part, question, and article control Aquinas; paragraph numbers control the \emph{Catechism}. The research records identify where an online transcription served as access witness and where a term remains editorial.

The fifty-two strict entries are treated dossiers and table rows, not fifty-two coordinate habits. The appendix distinguishes mean flanks, named contraries, objective outcomes, counterfeits or corruptions, and three different residuals: a recognized but unnamed departure, an unpaired side without a dedicated opposite, and an inapplicable axis. Those statuses are project audit devices; they are not attributed to the historical authors.

\subsection{Aristotle}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.6em,style=nextline,itemsep=0.45em]
  \item[\emph{Nicomachean Ethics}.]
  Greek loci and the public-domain W.~D. Ross translation (1925), consulted through the Perseus \href{https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0053}{Greek} and \href{https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0054}{English} editions, the \href{https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nicomachean_Ethics_(Ross)}{Wikisource transcription}, and the \href{https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.html}{Internet Classics Archive}. Governing loci: II.1--9 (habit, definition, mean, map, shame and indignation); III.6--12 (courage and temperance); IV.1--9 (liberality, magnificence, magnanimity, ordinary honor, gentleness, affability, truthfulness, eutrapelia, shame); V.1--10 (general, distributive, commutative justice, equity); VI.3--13 (five intellectual virtues, euboulia, synesis, gnome, connection, and natural virtue's incompleteness without prudence at 1144b1--17); VII.1--10 (heroic virtue, continence, incontinence); VIII--IX (friendship).

  \item[\emph{Eudemian Ethics}.]
  III.7, 1234a24--34, for the uncertain status of shame and righteous indignation, and VIII.3, 1248b8--1249a17, for \emph{kalokagathia} as an integrative capstone, using Joseph Solomon's public-domain translation in W.~D. Ross's 1925 edition, consulted through the \href{https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Eudemian_Ethics}{Wikisource transcription}. Both uses are qualified rather than allowed to enlarge the strict virtue count.

  \item[\emph{Categories}.]
  Chapter 8 for durable habit and less stable disposition, using E.~M. Edghill's public-domain translation through the \href{https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/categories.1.1.html}{Internet Classics Archive}.
\end{description}

The Ross and Solomon renderings guide English terminology but do not settle every Greek ambiguity. In \emph{Nicomachean Ethics} IV.4 Aristotle leaves the ordinary-honor mean unnamed and observes that ambition or love of honor and unambitiousness can each attract praise or blame according to how the desire is measured. For the appendix's Aristotelian flank display, \emph{aphilotimia} and \emph{philotimia} mark deficient and excessive desire in that context. Aquinas, by contrast, calls the virtue itself \emph{philotimia} in ST I--II, q.~60, a.~5. The publication states this cross-author difference rather than fabricating one ancient technical name common to both. In VI.4, Aristotle's \emph{atechnia}, rendered by Ross as “lack of art,” supplies art's named contrary; because it is a false productive state rather than a quantitative deficiency, the excess side remains unpaired.

Most are public-domain English translations from the nineteenth-century \emph{Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers} through New Advent's transcriptions; the two entries marked as Latin checks use the identified Augustinus.it witness:

Augustine does not offer a single scholastic census corresponding to Aquinas's question sequence. His contribution is therefore used where direct: theological orientation, ordered love, Christian patience, continence, virginity, humility, and the fourfold interpretation of cardinal virtue. Later classifications are not retroactively placed in Augustine's mouth.

Three general controls prevent the table from being flattened. \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2061.htm}{ST I--II, q.~61, aa.~1--5} explicitly names and numbers the four cardinal virtues, distinguishes their determinate habits from general conditions and overflow, and treats social, perfecting, perfect, and exemplar predicates as modes rather than sixteen new habits. \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3048.htm}{ST II--II, q.~48, a.~1} distinguishes integral conditions, subjective species, and potential parts. \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2064.htm}{ST I--II, q.~64, aa.~1--4} distinguishes the rational mean of moral virtue, justice's objective equality, intellectual conformity to truth, and theological virtue's lack of intrinsic excess relative to God.

The more local opposition controls are equally specific. \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3053.htm}{ST II--II, q.~53} supplies the named contrary \emph{imprudence} and the act-level failures precipitation, thoughtlessness, and inconstancy. \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3080.htm}{ST II--II, q.~80} reduces reported justice lists rather than adding them wholesale: among other cases, innocence is integral, external friendship is affability, fidelity is truthfulness, \emph{eucharistia} is gratitude, and legislative and exchange justice return to legal and commutative justice. \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3104.htm}{ST II--II, q.~104} and \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3105.htm}{q.~105} establish obedience as a special virtue, disobedience as its named contrary, and “indiscreet obedience” as the source phrase for obeying what one ought not. \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3106.htm}{ST II--II, q.~106} and \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3107.htm}{q.~107} similarly establish gratitude and ingratitude; premature material return can itself be ungrateful, while a greater return as possible is not vice. \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3108.htm}{ST II--II, q.~108, a.~2 ad 3} gives cruelty or brutality as the excessive flank of vindication; the appendix selects brutality there to reserve cruelty for its direct opposition to clemency. \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3120.htm}{ST II--II, q.~120} makes equity a subjective part of justice in general corresponding properly to legal justice. \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2064.htm}{ST I--II, q.~64, a.~1 ad 3} identifies circumstantial defect and excess in virginity without treating marriage as defect. \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3131.htm}{ST II--II, q.~131, a.~2} and \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3132.htm}{q.~132, a.~2} distinguish ambition and vainglory from presumption as excesses opposed to magnanimity under different respects. \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3135.htm}{ST II--II, q.~135} supplies \emph{parvificentia} and \emph{consumptio}, rendered tersely in the appendix as paltriness and waste. \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3030.htm}{ST II--II, q.~30, a.~3} distinguishes reason-governed mercy from pity that impedes counsel and departs from justice.

Within fortitude, \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3136.htm}{ST II--II, q.~136, a.~5} relates longanimity chiefly to the distant hoped-for good and to patience insofar as delay causes sorrow; \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3137.htm}{q.~137, a.~3} says constancy pertains to perseverance while differing by its response to external impediments. Within temperance, \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3143.htm}{q.~143, a.~1} makes abstinence and sobriety coordinate subjective species, while \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3151.htm}{q.~151, a.~4} resolves purity as a circumstance of chastity rather than a second virtue; \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3157.htm}{q.~157} and \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3159.htm}{q.~159} distinguish clemency, severity, cruelty, and brutality rather than treating all mitigation or punishment as one quantitative scale. Question 159 also attests laxity as a compact description of undue remission, but does not establish a dedicated vice species by that name. \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3166.htm}{q.~166} names neglect and curiosity as studiousness's default and excess. \href{https://www.newadvent.org/summa/4085.htm}{ST III, q.~85} establishes penance as a special virtue in the will comprised under commutative justice, while q.~90 keeps its sacramental parts and contextual exercises from becoming additional habit rows. Question 103's strict and broad senses of dulia, natural inchoation (I--II, q.~63, a.~1), mercy (II--II, q.~30), continence (q.~155), heroic virtue (q.~159, a.~2, ad 1), and the modesty applications (qq.~160--169) retain the fuller controls recorded in the source audit.

\subsection{Scripture and present Catholic synopsis}

Scriptural references identify the canonical locus without reproducing a proprietary modern translation. Principal texts are Wisdom 8:7; Matthew 5:1--12; Romans 5:1--5; 1 Corinthians 13; Galatians 5:6 and 5:22--23; Hebrews 11:1; and 2 Peter 1:5--7.

The present Catholic synopsis was checked in the Holy See's English text of the \href{https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_one/chapter_one/article_7/the_virtues.html}{\emph{Catechism of the Catholic Church}, 1803--1845}: human virtues (1804), prudence (1806), justice (1807), fortitude (1808), temperance (1809), formation and grace (1810--1811), theological virtues (1812--1829), gifts and fruits (1830--1832), and the summary (1833--1845). Supplemental official checks cover the \href{https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_one/chapter_one/article_2.html}{beatitudes, 1716--1729}, and the \href{https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_two/chapter_two/article_6/ii_the_vocation_to_chastity.index.html}{definition of chastity, 2337}. The \emph{Catechism} confirms the received Catholic frame; it does not supply every historical species or Aristotelian name in the expanded census.

\subsection{Rights and verification limits}

Aristotle and Augustine's original texts are public domain. The Ross, Solomon, Edghill, NPNF, and 1920 Dominican translations used as access witnesses are public-domain editions in the United States; their host sites' editorial wrappers remain independent. The Holy See's official text and all third-party translations retain their own rights and are cited only by focused reference or very short phrase. Project-created definitions, classifications, tables, and editorial labels fall under the repository's stated license only to the extent the project holds the rights.

This work was source-audited against the cited loci but has not received independent philosophical, patristic, or theological review. It does not adjudicate disputed critical-text readings, provide a full history of later virtue terminology, replace a moral theology of individual acts and sins, or constitute magisterial or pastoral judgment.
