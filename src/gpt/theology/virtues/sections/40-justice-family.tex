\section{Justice: Cardinal Virtue and Its Parts}
\label{sec:justice}

Justice perfects the will in relation to another. Its basic act is to render what is due; its measure therefore lies in an objective equality or proportion, not first in a mean of passion. Aristotle develops general and particular justice in \emph{Nicomachean Ethics} V. Aquinas receives that distinction and places under justice the virtues that concern a due relation but cannot establish strict equality with their recipient, together with lesser social dues (ST II--II, qq.~57--122).

\cardinalgroup{The principal virtue and its objective mean}

\virtuedossier
  {Justice}
  {Principal cardinal virtue; Aristotle, \emph{Nicomachean Ethics} V.1--10; Augustine, \emph{On the Morals of the Catholic Church} I.15.25; ST I--II, q.~61; II--II, qq.~57--79; CCC 1807.}
  {The right or thing due to another, whether by equality, proportion, office, promise, law, or the order of the common good.}
  {By a constant and firm will, to render to each person and community what is due.}
  {\textbf{Injustice} is the contrary genus in the responsible will (\oppositioncode{K}). In a particular transaction, an unjust loss or shortfall is an objective outcome (\oppositioncode{O}), not a vice in the person deprived.}
  {An unjust gain or surplus is the opposite objective outcome (\oppositioncode{O}); the wrongdoer's grasping (Greek \emph{pleonexia}) is not “too much justice.”}
  {Justice concerns an objective due and outward action even when passions are calm. Judgment and restitution are acts of justice; law is a rule and measure; mercy and equity perfect rather than cancel what justice seeks.}

In moral virtues measured chiefly by passion, the agent's response is excessive or deficient. In justice, the equality itself stands between more and less. A court may restore a loss without the victim thereby acquiring a “defect vice,” and a generous gift beyond strict debt need not be unjust. The appendix therefore names objective loss and gain where they apply, labels them as outcomes rather than habits, and separately records injustice as the responsible agent's contrary vice.

\begin{studybox}{Integral parts: doing good and declining evil}
Justice has two integral acts: to do the good that is due and to decline the evil that violates another's due (ST II--II, q.~79, a.~1). They are required for complete justice but do not become two additional virtues. “Doing good” is especially tied to justice's affirmative act; “declining evil” sets the minimum boundary without exhausting the positive due.
\end{studybox}

\cardinalgroup{General or legal justice}

\virtuedossier
  {Legal or general justice}
  {General ordering of virtue to the common good and, in Aquinas, a virtue with this directive power; Aristotle, \emph{Nicomachean Ethics} V.1--2; ST II--II, q.~58, aa.~5--6.}
  {The common good under a just ordering of law and civic responsibility.}
  {To direct the acts of every virtue, insofar as they bear upon others, toward the community's just good.}
  {\textbf{Lawlessness} or injustice is the contrary orientation (\oppositioncode{K}), not one side of the loss-and-gain structure proper to particular justice.}
  {\textbf{Tyranny} invokes a purported common good while subordinating it to private advantage; it is counterfeit rule (\oppositioncode{X}), not legal justice in excess.}
  {General justice does not absorb the elicited acts of courage, temperance, or charity. It commands them toward the common good while their proximate objects continue to specify their own virtues.}

The common good is not merely the aggregate of private satisfactions, nor is it an excuse to erase personal dignity and subsidiary responsibility. Legal justice asks how one's acts participate in a shared just order. It presupposes that law itself is measured by reason and the genuine good; compliance with an unjust command does not become virtue because authority uttered it.

\cardinalgroup{Equity and severity in relation to legal justice}

\virtuedossier
  {Equity (\emph{epieikeia})}
  {Subjective part of justice in general, corresponding properly to legal justice; Aristotle, \emph{Nicomachean Ethics} V.10; ST II--II, q.~120, aa.~1--2. Aquinas also calls it a higher rule of human acts when justice is taken more broadly.}
  {The just intention of law when universal wording cannot fittingly govern an exceptional case.}
  {To set aside the letter in the exceptional case so as to fulfill the higher justice the lawmaker intended.}
  {Rigid literalism can destroy a law's reason, but the sources supply no dedicated vice noun for this failure (\oppositioncode{U}).}
  {\textbf{Arbitrariness} counterfeits equity by abandoning both letter and rational intention (\oppositioncode{X}); it is not an excess of equity.}
  {Equity is neither ordinary dispensation, civil disobedience at will, nor a denial that clear law normally binds. It differs from \emph{gnome}: higher judgment perfects practical reason generally; equity performs justice in relation to law.}

\virtuedossier
  {Severity}
  {Virtue and part of legal justice in the application of punishment; ST II--II, q.~157, a.~2, ad 1.}
  {A penalty due under law and right reason for the protection, correction, and order of the common good.}
  {To remain firm in imposing or maintaining the punishment right reason requires when no particular circumstance warrants mitigation.}
  {Culpable remission can fail justice, but the source gives severity no dedicated defect-side vice (\oppositioncode{U}).}
  {\textbf{Cruelty} corrupts rational punishment (\oppositioncode{X}); it is clemency's direct contrary, not severity perfected too far.}
  {Severity is not harshness. It and clemency are both ruled by reason: severity holds to a due penalty; clemency rationally mitigates it where a particular consideration warrants. Vindication concerns the morally ordered pursuit of redress, while equity reaches the law's just intention in an exceptional case.}

\cardinalgroup{Particular justice}

Particular justice renders a determinate due to another rather than commanding the acts of all virtues toward the common good. It is the non-counted parent genus of two subjective species: distributive justice orders a whole's allocation to its members; commutative justice orders dealings of one party with another (ST II--II, q.~58, a.~7; q.~61, a.~1).

\virtuedossier
  {Distributive justice}
  {Subjective species of particular justice; Aristotle, \emph{Nicomachean Ethics} V.3; ST II--II, q.~61, a.~1--2.}
  {Common goods, honors, burdens, risks, and resources assigned to persons according to the proportion relevant to the community and good distributed.}
  {To allot shares by a just proportion rather than private favor or irrelevant status.}
  {A \textbf{disproportionate shortfall} is the deprived party's objective outcome (\oppositioncode{O}), not that party's vice; in the distributor, partiality corrupts the proportion.}
  {A \textbf{disproportionate surplus} is the favored party's objective outcome (\oppositioncode{O}); collusion can make a recipient culpable, but quantity alone does not name a second habit.}
  {Distributive equality is proportional rather than automatically identical. Relevant need, contribution, office, risk, and equal dignity must not be collapsed into one numerical rule.}

\virtuedossier
  {Commutative justice}
  {Subjective species of particular justice; Aristotle, \emph{Nicomachean Ethics} V.4--5; ST II--II, q.~61, aa.~1--4; q.~62.}
  {What one person owes another in exchange, damage, taking, contract, or restitution.}
  {To preserve or restore arithmetic equality between parties by performance, payment, return, or proportionate reparation.}
  {A party's \textbf{loss} is the deficient objective outcome (\oppositioncode{O}), not a vice in the injured party.}
  {The corresponding \textbf{gain} identifies objective inequality (\oppositioncode{O}); fraud, theft, coercion, and usury name different wrongful acts, not an “excess of commutative justice.”}
  {Restitution repairs an objective disorder but does not by itself erase every further duty, penalty, scandal, or need for forgiveness. Commutative justice is not market price treated as an infallible moral oracle.}

\virtuedossier
  {Penance (as a virtue)}
  {Special infused moral virtue directly within justice and, by its voluntary compensation for offense, comprised under commutative justice; ST III, q.~85, aa.~1--6. This is Thomistic provenance; the work cited as Augustine in one objection is not used to establish Augustinian authorship.}
  {One's past sin considered as a remediable offense against God: not the impossible erasure of the historical deed, but the disorder, guilt, and debt toward which grace-enabled amendment and satisfaction are directed.}
  {To detest the past sin by a right act of will, purpose a changed life, and cooperate with God through fitting amendment and voluntary satisfaction. Charity commands the act toward reconciliation with God, while penance elicits its special work.}
  {\textbf{Impenitence}, understood as the purpose not to repent, is a named contrary (\oppositioncode{K}; ST II--II, q.~14, a.~2), though it is not one half of a quantitative mean-pair.}
  {No intrinsic excess-side vice exists (\oppositioncode{NA}). The “excess” named in ST III, q.~85, a.~3, ad 2 means doing all one can toward a superior whom one cannot repay equally; self-harm, despair, and scrupulosity are other disorders.}
  {Penance as virtue is a habit in the will, not sensitive sorrow, tears, shame, self-loathing, a single penitential work, or the sacrament bearing the same name. The sacramental reference treats the sacrament's sign and rite; this dossier controls the virtue that can dispose, animate, or accompany its human acts.}

\begin{studybox}{One virtue, sacramental parts, and context-conditioned species}
ST III, q.~90 requires three distinctions. First, \textbf{contrition, confession, and satisfaction} are integral parts of the sacrament's human matter; they are not three additional virtues (aa.~1--3). Second, q.~90, a.~4 calls penance before baptism, after baptismal mortal sin, and for venial sin ``species'' of the virtue according to the change intended: regeneration, restoration after grave sin, or advance toward more perfect operation. The inventory records that express language, but does not print three additional habit rows: these are context-conditioned specifications of the one virtue's act, not three separately named stable habits. Third, the pre-baptismal act is not the sacrament of Penance, while post-baptismal sacramental acts presuppose the Church's sacramental order. In every case, the virtue remains in the will and is governed by amendment of sin as offense against God.
\end{studybox}

\cardinalgroup{Potential parts: dues without full equality}

Some relations contain a real debt that cannot be repaid by strict equality. Aquinas calls the corresponding virtues potential parts of justice: they share justice's other-directed act but fall short of its equal return. Their order follows the excellence of the person to whom the debt is owed.

\virtuegrouplabel{Unequal debts: religion, piety, and observance}

\virtuedossier
  {Religion (\emph{religio})}
  {Potential part of justice and a distinct moral virtue; ST II--II, qq.~80--100; Augustine, \emph{On True Religion} 55.}
  {The honor and service owed to God as first principle, sovereign good, and governor of all.}
  {To offer worship---interior and exterior---under truth: devotion, prayer, adoration, sacrifice, vows, and reverent use of holy realities.}
  {\textbf{Irreligion} is the source-exact contrary by deficiency in due divine worship; sacrilege, tempting God, perjury, and simony specify failures in that field (\oppositioncode{M}).}
  {\textbf{Superstition} offers worship to a false object or offers the true God worship in a gravely false manner (\oppositioncode{M}).}
  {Religion is not a theological virtue: it has worship of God as its act, whereas faith, hope, and charity have God immediately as supernatural object. Devotional intensity does not authorize magic, false revelation, or injustice.}

\virtuedossier
  {Piety (\emph{pietas})}
  {Potential part of justice; ST II--II, q.~101; the natural debt is received from parents and country.}
  {Parents, kindred, and one's political community insofar as they are principles of life, nurture, and common identity.}
  {To give fitting honor, service, and support while preserving higher duties to God and justice.}
  {Dereliction may describe abandoning a due of care, but the source gives piety no dedicated vice noun on this side (\oppositioncode{U}).}
  {\textbf{Favoritism} that injures a higher or equal duty is a corruption of partiality (\oppositioncode{X}), not piety made too strong.}
  {Piety does not demand compliance with wrongdoing, concealment of abuse, nationalism, or treating inherited custom as divine law. It differs from the gift of piety.}

\virtuedossier
  {Observance (\emph{observantia})}
  {Potential part of justice; ST II--II, q.~102. Dulia and obedience specify acts within its field.}
  {Persons who bear excellence or public dignity and mediate a real common good.}
  {To accord fitting honor and service according to office, excellence, and the order of justice.}
  {Contempt can withhold due honor, but Aquinas supplies no dedicated defect vice for observance (\oppositioncode{U}).}
  {Adulation directed to power counterfeits honor through flattery and falsehood (\oppositioncode{X}); it is not heightened observance.}
  {The due follows real office and good, not celebrity, wealth, intimidation, or a claim to immunity. Honor never cancels accountability.}

\virtuegrouplabel{Dulia and obedience under observance}

\virtuedossier
  {Dulia}
  {Special species within observance; ST II--II, q.~103. The traditional term names due service or honor to a superior creature; \emph{latria} belongs to God alone.}
  {A human superior or, in developed Christian usage, a holy creature honored for participation in God's gifts.}
  {To render subordinate honor without transferring to a creature the worship due to God.}
  {Disdain can refuse a due honor but does not furnish a source-given dedicated flank (\oppositioncode{U}).}
  {Adulation or creature-worship corrupts the due relation (\oppositioncode{X}); it is not excessive dulia.}
  {Dulia is analogical service, not slavery of conscience. It is formally distinct from latria; honor terminates differently according to the excellence and relation recognized.}

\begin{studybox}{Strict dulia, wide dulia, and hyperdulia}
ST II--II, q.~103, a.~4 distinguishes two uses. \textbf{Strict dulia} is the one virtue by which a servant renders a human lord due service; it is a species of observance and is not further divided. \textbf{Dulia in a wide sense} names reverence for human excellence generally and therefore ranges over already distinguished virtues such as piety and observance. \textbf{Hyperdulia} is the highest species of that wide usage, the singular reverence owed to the Blessed Virgin because of her affinity to God. It receives explicit treatment here but no additional census row: Aquinas locates it within the wide analogical grouping, not as a species of the one strict habit. None is latria, the worship owed to God alone.
\end{studybox}

\virtuedossier
  {Obedience}
  {Distinct virtue annexed to observance; ST II--II, q.~104.}
  {A legitimate command insofar as the commander's authority participates in a just order.}
  {To fulfill a lawful precept because it is commanded by one with authority.}
  {\textbf{Disobedience} is the special contrary sin when a precept is treated with contempt (\oppositioncode{K}; ST II--II, qq.~104--105).}
  {A circumstantial excess exists when one obeys a person or matter one ought not, but Aquinas gives it no dedicated vice noun (\oppositioncode{U}); unlawful compliance is not obedience perfected beyond measure.}
  {Obedience is bounded by the superior's competence and by higher law. Counsel, voluntary service, coercion, and mere conformity are not identical with its elicited act.}

\virtuegrouplabel{Necessary moral dues among equals and neighbors}

The remaining potential parts render things owed by moral fittingness rather than an enforceable equality in every instance. Their lesser strictness does not make them optional decorations: shared life disintegrates when benefits, truth, sociability, or proportionate defense are persistently refused.

\virtuedossier
  {Gratitude}
  {Potential part of justice; ST II--II, qq.~106--107.}
  {A benefactor and the gratuitous good received, considered according to the giver's intention, cost, and the recipient's need.}
  {To recognize the benefit, give thanks, and make a fitting return at a fitting time when possible.}
  {\textbf{Ingratitude} is the special contrary vice by deficiency, with failures of recognition, thanks, and fitting return as degrees (\oppositioncode{K}).}
  {An unnamed excess returns thanks for what is not due or sooner than it is due (\oppositioncode{U}; ST II--II, q.~107, a.~2). A larger fitting return can still be excellent.}
  {Gratitude is not a commercial discharge that makes grace cease to be gift. It does not bind the recipient to conceal the benefactor's later injustice.}

\virtuedossier
  {Vindication (proportionate vengeance)}
  {Potential part of justice; ST II--II, q.~108. The traditional \emph{vindicta} concerns morally ordered redress, not private rage.}
  {A culpable wrong and the restoration of justice, protection, correction, and peace through proportionate penalty.}
  {To resist or punish wrongdoing under lawful authority and a just intention directed to the good.}
  {\textbf{Remissness} culpably abandons necessary defense, correction, or public order (\oppositioncode{M} by Aquinas's explicit excess-and-defect account).}
  {\textbf{Cruelty or brutality} exceeds rational proportion in punishment (\oppositioncode{M}); the source's own alternatives are retained even though cruelty also stands in a distinct formal relation to clemency.}
  {Vindication is not hatred, retaliation for emotional satisfaction, or denial of mercy. Forgiveness of personal resentment can coexist with restitution, protection, and public penalty.}

\virtuedossier
  {Truthfulness}
  {Potential part of justice and Aristotelian mean; Aristotle, \emph{Nicomachean Ethics} II.7 and IV.7; ST II--II, qq.~109--113.}
  {The manifestation of oneself in signs, deeds, and speech according to what one is and knows.}
  {To signify truthfully without making oneself greater or lesser than one is.}
  {\textbf{Eironeia} or false self-deprecation falsely diminishes one's qualities or deeds (\oppositioncode{M}); the Greek term prevents modern “irony” from obscuring the technical sense.}
  {\textbf{Boastfulness} falsely magnifies them (\oppositioncode{M}).}
  {Truthfulness does not require revealing every truth to everyone. Discretion, confidentiality, silence, and material concealment differ from assertion of falsehood; legal testimony carries additional duties.}

\virtuegrouplabel{Dues conducive to fuller rectitude}

Affability and liberality concern goods that improve shared life beyond a strictly enforceable return: fitting social pleasantness and fitting readiness with external goods. Their looser juridical status does not make them optional to full moral rectitude.

\virtuedossier
  {Affability (social friendship)}
  {Potential part of justice and Aristotelian mean; Aristotle, \emph{Nicomachean Ethics} II.7 and IV.6; ST II--II, qq.~114--116.}
  {The ordinary pleasure, pain, words, and conduct through which people share social life.}
  {To be agreeable or corrective as truth, relationship, and the common good reasonably require.}
  {\textbf{Quarrelsomeness} needlessly opposes and gives pain in ordinary association (\oppositioncode{M}).}
  {\textbf{Obsequiousness} seeks to please against truth or another's good (\oppositioncode{M}); flattery is its gain-seeking form in Aquinas's analysis.}
  {Affability is not theological charity, intimate friendship, conflict avoidance, or personality-based extroversion. Fraternal correction can be its act when pleasing would harm.}

\virtuedossier
  {Liberality}
  {Potential part of justice and Aristotelian character virtue; Aristotle, \emph{Nicomachean Ethics} II.7 and IV.1; ST II--II, qq.~117--119.}
  {Ordinary external goods considered as instruments to be given, received, kept, and spent under reason.}
  {To release and use possessions readily for fitting persons, ends, amounts, and times.}
  {\textbf{Avarice} clings to wealth or takes it contrary to reason (\oppositioncode{M}).}
  {\textbf{Prodigality} dissipates resources contrary to their fitting purposes and obligations (\oppositioncode{M}).}
  {Liberality differs from justice's strict payment of debt and from magnificence's great expenditure for a great work. Poverty, wealth, or the size of a gift alone does not establish the habit.}

\begin{studybox}{How Aquinas reduces the longer justice lists}
ST II--II, q.~80, a.~1 reports several inherited catalogues and then classifies their names rather than simply adding them. \textbf{Innocence} belongs to justice's integral work of declining evil. External \textbf{friendship} is affability; interior \textbf{concord}, \textbf{benevolence}, and \textbf{beneficence} are relations or acts of friendship and charity. \textbf{Obedience} is first included under observance, then q.~104 establishes its own special virtue; \textbf{fidelity} is contained in truthfulness, while \textbf{discipline} toward inferiors is a duty reducible to the reported humanity or beneficence. \textbf{Holiness} and \textbf{eusebeia} coincide here with religion; \emph{eucharistia} is gratitude. \textbf{Kindliness}, \textbf{humanity}, and \textbf{condescension} reduce to beneficence, liberality, or affability according to their act. \textbf{Just exchange} and \textbf{legislative justice} are commutative and legal justice; \textbf{good judgment} or \emph{eugnomosyne} is referred to gnome as director and to justice or equity as executor; and the reported \textbf{equity} returns to \emph{epieikeia} or affability according to sense. Piety, religion, gratitude, vindication, liberality, truthfulness, and the later independent treatment of obedience remain counted once. These reductions are named so that comprehensiveness does not turn aliases, acts, integrals, and genera into duplicate habits.
\end{studybox}

\cardinalgroup{Justice corrected by neither sentiment nor mechanism}

Justice without charity can become externally exact while indifferent to communion; sentiment without justice can favor the near, visible, or agreeable while neglecting a genuine due. Mercy attends to misery, equity to a universal rule's exceptional failure, and vindication to ordered redress. Each requires prudence. None permits a private actor to assume authority the actor does not possess.
