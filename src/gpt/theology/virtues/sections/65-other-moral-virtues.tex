\section{Other Moral Virtues with Secure Provenance}
\label{sec:other-moral}

The cardinal architecture is comprehensive at the level of principal subject and formal mode, but Aquinas's named parts-lists do not supply a non-editorial family placement for every moral virtue securely attested in the controlling corpus. This section therefore retains two strict moral virtues without disguising either one as theological or assigning it a Thomistic parent that the source does not establish.

\textbf{Mercy} is a distinct moral virtue treated by Aquinas among charity's effects; charity can command its act, but mercy is not a fourth theological virtue (ST II--II, q.~30, a.~3). The \textbf{unnamed mean concerning ordinary honor} is established in Aristotle's moral catalogue and received in Aquinas's elevenfold account, but it is not named as a potential part of fortitude in ST II--II, q.~128 (Aristotle, \emph{Nicomachean Ethics} II.7 and IV.4; ST I--II, q.~60, a.~5).

Their separate placement does not put them outside the cardinal order in every sense: all moral virtues remain reducible to the four principal virtues by subject or formal principle (ST I--II, q.~61, a.~2, ad 3). Because these two entries share no single parts-list or source sequence, they are ordered alphabetically by their preferred English names: mercy, then the unnamed mean concerning ordinary honor.

\virtuedossier
  {Mercy (\emph{misericordia})}
  {Distinct moral virtue treated among charity's effects; ST II--II, q.~30, aa.~1--4; Augustine, \emph{City of God} IX.5. Charity can command mercy's act, and mercy can fulfill justice, but neither relation changes its class.}
  {Another's misery considered as an evil to be removed, under the order of reason and the common good.}
  {To take another's misery as one's own in compassionate concern and to supply an effective remedy when possible.}
  {\textbf{Mercilessness} (Latin \emph{immisericordia}) is a broad contrary description, and cruelty can sometimes be used in that extended sense, but the controlling sources supply no virtue-specific contrary noun or mean flank (\oppositioncode{U}).}
  {\textbf{Unregulated pity} can impede counsel and wander from justice (\oppositioncode{X}); indulgence that confirms harm is one counterfeit of mercy, not mercy perfected beyond measure.}
  {The passion of pity can dispose one to mercy but is not the virtue itself. Clemency moderates punishment by a superior; liberality gives external goods; beneficence is a broader act of charity.}

Mercy is greatest among virtues concerning the neighbor because it supplies another's defect from one's abundance. Charity remains greatest simply because it joins the person to God. Divine mercy does not arise from affective suffering in God; it names God's effective removal of misery. Human mercy ordinarily includes affective resonance, but its moral excellence lies in reason-governed willing and assistance rather than the strength of emotion alone.

\virtuedossier
  {Unnamed mean concerning ordinary honor}
  {Aristotelian character virtue; Aristotle, \emph{Nicomachean Ethics} II.7 and IV.4; received in Aquinas's general catalogue at ST I--II, q.~60, a.~5. Aristotle leaves the mean unnamed; Aquinas uses \emph{philotimia} for the virtue. This residual placement is editorial, not a Thomistic parts-list claim.}
  {Lesser and ordinary honors, responsibilities, and recognition proportioned to one's real capacity and to the good served.}
  {To seek, accept, or decline ordinary honor as reason and service require, without making recognition the final end.}
  {Aristotle's \textbf{aphilotimia} names the deficient disposition toward ordinary honor (\oppositioncode{M}); ``unambitiousness'' is a serviceable English gloss, but the term can receive praise when honor ought to be declined.}
  {Aristotle's \textbf{philotimia} names the excessive disposition in this contrast (\oppositioncode{M}); ``ambition'' or ``love of honor'' is a gloss, though the same word can receive praise when honor is rightly sought.}
  {The virtue is not magnanimity, which concerns great honor and a person capable of great deeds. Because Aristotle's two extreme terms are context-sensitive and his mean has no fixed name, the appendix preserves the source terms rather than inventing globally unique English vice names.}
