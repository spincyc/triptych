# Lourdes Cure Recognition and Currentness Audit

Checked through 2026-07-16. This record controls every cure count and process statement in the monograph.

## Category map

| Stage or act | Actor | Object | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Declared cure | Person reporting cure; Medical Bureau permanent physician receives it | Opens a possible file; gathers diagnosis, treatment, pre/post records, circumstances, and credibility information | That a cure occurred as described, is medically unexplained, or is miraculous |
| Unexpected cure | Medical Bureau after preliminary assessment | Case warrants sustained investigation because the course appears outside ordinary expectations | Divine causation or final medical confirmation |
| Confirmed cure | Bureau/consulted specialists and CMIL process | Pre/post evidence supports a real, durable change and the file is sufficiently discussed | Miracle; a rare or unexplained event is not yet a theological judgment |
| Exceptional/unexplained in current knowledge | CMIL medical vote | Present medicine does not supply an adequate explanation and summarized criteria are met | Metaphysical impossibility of a future explanation; sanctity; Marian causation as a medical finding |
| Diocesan miracle proclamation | Bishop of the healed person's diocese after receiving the file and conducting ecclesial discernment | Ecclesial judgment that the cure is a miracle attributed to God's power through the invoked intercession | A medical treatment protocol, apparition re-approval, or guarantee of cure for another pilgrim |

The exact sequence and terminology have developed historically. “Seventy-two canonically recognized miracles” is a useful current summary, not a claim that all seventy-two passed an identical twenty-first-century workflow.

## Institutional chronology

- **1858–1862:** reported cures form one evidentiary stream around the apparition inquiry. Professor Henri Vergez examines cases. A separate decree of Bishop Laurence declares seven cures miraculous on 18 January 1862; it is distinct in object from the apparition judgment issued the same day.
- **1883:** Dr Georges-Fernand Dunot de Saint-Maclou establishes the permanent Medical Bureau so claimed cures receive contemporaneous, collegial scrutiny.
- **1886:** an official Sanctuary history records Leo XIII's assent to the Bureau's rigorous procedures through a letter relayed by Archbishop Vincenzo Gregorio Berchialla.
- **1905:** the Holy See confirms the Bishop of Tarbes's right, as guardian of the grotto, to use the Bureau process.
- **1947–1954:** a Lourdes Medical Committee is created and then internationalized, becoming the CMIL structure.
- **2009–2026:** Dr Alessandro de Franciscis serves as permanent physician.
- **Mid-April 2026:** Dr Giada Monami succeeds him as the first woman appointed permanent physician of the Bureau. Her appointment, rather than the stale historical page naming the prior incumbent, controls current personnel.

## Medical-epistemic safeguards

- “Unexplained” is indexed to present medical knowledge, the available record, and the question asked. It is not equivalent to logical impossibility of a natural mechanism.
- A long observation period can strengthen durability but does not repair missing pre-cure records automatically. Traynor's case illustrates why archival provenance matters.
- Statistical rarity does not by itself identify divine agency. Conversely, a future natural mechanism would not erase the spiritual meaning a person found in prayer; it would change the scientific claim.
- Review of a file is not a randomized trial of pilgrimage, water, or prayer. The 72 are selected case judgments, not an efficacy denominator.
- Medical treatment should continue unless the treating clinician changes it. A pilgrim must not withhold medication, refuse emergency care, or contaminate water in order to “make room” for faith.
- No cure recognition ranks persons. Those who remain ill are not rejected, and disability is not a defect in human dignity.
