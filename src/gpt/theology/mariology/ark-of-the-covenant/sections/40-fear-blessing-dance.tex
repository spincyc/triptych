\section{Fear, Blessing, and the Dance before Zion}

The Ark waits at Kiriath-jearim while kingdoms rise and fall.  When David has
taken Jerusalem and made it the City of David, he gathers Israel to bring up
the Ark from Baalah---the place Chronicles identifies as Kiriath-jearim.  The
long stillness ends in a blaze of sound.  The Ark is set on a new cart.  Uzzah
and Ahio guide it from Abinadab's house.  David and all Israel celebrate before
the Lord with instruments and song (2 Sam 6:1--5; 1 Chr 13:1--8).

At a threshing floor the oxen lurch.  Uzzah reaches out and takes hold of the
Ark.  God strikes him, and he dies there.  Celebration breaks against judgment.
David is troubled at the breach, names the place Perez-uzzah, and becomes
afraid: how can the Ark of the Lord come to him? (2 Sam 6:6--9;
1 Chr 13:9--12).

The ancient name of the threshing floor survives in several forms---Nacon or
Nachon, Nodan in one modern Catholic text, Chidon in Chronicles---and its
location is unknown.  The narrative supplies Uzzah's act but not a hidden
psychology.  It does not call him malicious, lustful, or a man whose inner
good intentions excuse the deed.  The event must first be read through the
appointed order that Scripture itself gives.

\subsection{The breach in the order}

Numbers had commanded that Aaron and his sons cover the Ark, that the
Kohathites bear the holy things without touching them, and that the sanctuary
burden be carried on shoulders rather than wagons (Num 4:5--20; 7:6--9).
Chronicles looks back at the first procession through precisely this law.
David declares that no one but the Levites may bear the Ark.  He tells the
Levitical chiefs to sanctify themselves, because the earlier breach came when
they did not seek God according to the appointed order.  The Levites then bear
the Ark on their shoulders with poles as Moses commanded
(1 Chr 15:2, 11--15).

The correction is not less joyful than the first attempt.  It is more deeply
ordered.  The failure had not been a shortage of music or sincerity.  The
first Davidic transport used a cart like the Philistine return, while
Chronicles interprets the correction through the form of service given to
Israel: sanctified Levites, shoulders, and poles.  The text does not say that
Israel consciously copied Philistia.  Holy enthusiasm does not make obedience
optional.

\subsection{Three months under a roof}

David will not take the Ark into the City.  He turns it aside to the house of
Obed-edom the Gittite.  There it remains three months, and the Lord blesses
Obed-edom, his household, and all that belongs to him
(2 Sam 6:10--12; 1 Chr 13:13--14).  Scripture gives no recoverable location
for the house.  ``Gittite'' is not permission to send the Ark back to
Philistine Gath on a map.

The three months reverse the emotional course of the chapter.  Fear had
stopped the ascent; reported blessing summons David to begin again.  The same
holiness that judges disordered handling is not sterile danger.  Received in
the household according to God's providence, the Ark becomes the center of
blessing.

When David hears what has happened, he goes to bring the Ark up with joy.
Samuel marks the first six paces of the bearers and the sacrifice that follows;
Chronicles describes sanctified Levites, appointed singers, cymbals, harps,
lyres, trumpets, and the sacrifice offered when God helps the bearers
(2 Sam 6:12--15; 1 Chr 15:11--28).  The accounts are complementary without
being mechanically identical.  Samuel does not say that a sacrifice occurs
after every six steps, and Chronicles' seven bulls and seven rams should not
be folded into such a schedule.

\subsection{The king who leaps}

David dances before the Lord with all his might, clothed in a linen ephod.  The
Ark ascends with shouting and trumpet sound.  As it enters the City of David,
Michal looks down from the window and despises the dancing king
(2 Sam 6:14--16).  Her later accusation says that he has abased or exposed
himself before the servants; it does not erase the narrator's explicit notice
of his linen garment.  David answers that he will humble himself still more
before the Lord who chose him (2 Sam 6:20--23).  Chronicles preserves the
glance and contempt, then returns to the ordered worship of the procession
(1 Chr 15:29).

The Ark is placed inside the tent David has pitched.  Burnt offerings and
peace offerings are made.  David blesses the people in the Lord's name and
gives food to every man and woman before the assembly disperses.  He then
returns to bless his own household (2 Sam 6:17--20; 1 Chr 16:1--3).
Chronicles establishes continuing praise before the Ark while the older
tabernacle and altar remain at Gibeon (1 Chr 16:4--40).  Gibeon is not another
Ark station.

This is the scene Luke will later make ring in the hill country of Judah.
David wonders how the Ark can come to him; Elizabeth wonders how the mother of
her Lord can come to her.  David leaps and dances; the unborn John leaps for
joy.  The Ark remains in a household for three months; Mary remains with
Elizabeth for about three months (2 Sam 6:9, 11, 14--16; Luke 1:41--44, 56).
The standard Greek texts use different verbs for David's dance and John's
leap.  The correspondence is a strong narrative image, not a claim of verbal
identity.

\subsection{What the breach does not mean}

Uzzah is not a biblical figure for marital intercourse, and Joseph is not a
second Uzzah spared because he keeps away from something unclean.  Neither
marriage nor the conjugal act is defiled by this episode.  The literal issue is
the handling of the Ark contrary to the cultic order; Chronicles says so.

The bounded project synthesis offered here is therefore a contrast, not a
scriptural or patristic explanation of either episode.  Uzzah reaches out
during a disordered procession.  Joseph is commanded by God to take Mary as
his true wife, receives her into his home, names the Child, and guards the
mystery in obedient virginal love (Matt 1:18--25).  The juxtaposition may
illuminate unappointed handling versus commanded reception; it neither
explains Joseph's continence nor makes him a new Uzzah.  Joseph's vocation is
personal, marital, Davidic, and positive.

\begin{studybox}{From fear to ordered joy}
The second procession is not merely the first procession tried again with more
caution.  It is a conversion of worship: appointed bearers, sanctification,
shoulders and poles, sacrifice, song, humility, blessing, and bread shared with
the people.  Reverence does not silence joy.  It gives joy its truthful form.
\end{studybox}
