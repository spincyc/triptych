\section{Rest, Return, and Hiddenness}

The Ark has reached Zion, but it has not yet reached the Temple.  David sits in
a cedar house while the Ark remains beneath tent cloth and proposes to build a
house for God.  The Lord reverses the gift.  He recalls his journey with Israel
in tent and tabernacle, refuses David's immediate project, and promises instead
to build David a ``house''---a dynasty.  David's son will build the sanctuary
(2 Sam 7:1--17).  The Ark's rest in the City is therefore held inside a larger
promise: a son of David and a kingdom established by God.

Chronicles keeps two sacred centers distinct during this period.  The Ark and
its ministers are before David's tent in Jerusalem; the older Mosaic
tabernacle and bronze altar remain at Gibeon (1 Chr 16:37--40;
2 Chr 1:3--6).  Solomon sacrifices at Gibeon and then returns to Jerusalem to
stand before the Ark (1 Kgs 3:4--15).  The old tent's location does not turn
Gibeon into an Ark station.

One wartime sentence remains deliberately unresolved.  Uriah says that the
Ark, Israel, and Judah dwell in tents during the campaign against Rabbah
(2 Sam 11:11).  This may remember a field presence of the Ark, but the
narrative gives no departure, arrival, or exact camp.  A complete study records
the notice; an honest map does not draw a journey to Rabbah from it.

\subsection{Send the Ark back}

When Absalom drives David from Jerusalem, Zadok and the Levites carry the Ark
out with the fleeing people and set it down while the procession passes.
David refuses to make it a pledge of his own restoration.  He commands Zadok
to return it to the City: if the Lord favors him, he will see the Ark and its
dwelling again; if not, he submits to God's will.  The priests carry it back to
Jerusalem (2 Sam 15:23--29).

The moment quietly answers the disaster at Ebenezer.  David will not recruit
the Ark to guarantee a political outcome.  He entrusts his kingship to the
Lord and sends the covenant sign home.  The text names the broader passage of
the company across the Kidron, but it does not give a secure new Ark station or
place the Ark on the Mount of Olives.

\subsection{The cloud returns}

Solomon constructs the inner sanctuary to receive the Ark, overlaying the
Holy of Holies with gold (1 Kgs 6:19--22).  At the feast the elders and tribal
leaders assemble.  The Ark is brought up from the City of David, which is
Zion.  Sacrifices are offered in such abundance that the narrative does not
number them.  The bearers place the Ark beneath the wings of the great cherubim
in the inner sanctuary (1 Kgs 8:1--8; 2 Chr 5:2--9).

Kings speaks of the priests taking up the Ark; Chronicles speaks of the
Levites bearing it.  Each witness may keep its own emphasis.  Both say that
only the two tablets placed there at Horeb are now inside
(1 Kgs 8:9; 2 Chr 5:10).  Chronicles surrounds the enthronement with singers,
cymbals, harps, lyres, and trumpets.  When their praise becomes one voice, the
house fills with cloud.  Priests cannot stand to minister because the glory of
the Lord has filled the Temple (2 Chr 5:11--14; cf. 1 Kgs 8:10--11).

The road from Sinai has reached a deliberate echo.  At the completion of the
wilderness dwelling, cloud covered the Tent and glory filled it so that Moses
could not enter.  At the completion of the Temple enthronement, cloud fills the
house and the priests cannot stand (Exod 40:34--35).  Yet Solomon immediately
confesses that heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain God, much less the
house he has built (1 Kgs 8:27).  The Presence is real; the Lord remains free.

\subsection{The last historical notice}

Generations later, during his Passover reform, Josiah tells the Levites to put
the holy Ark in the house Solomon built and no longer carry it on their
shoulders (2 Chr 35:3).  Scripture does not say where it had been, how long it
had been away from its place, or that Josiah rediscovered it.  This command is
the last explicit historical notice before Jerusalem's fall.

The Babylonian destruction accounts enumerate Temple metals and vessels but
do not mention the Ark (2 Kgs 25:8--17; Jer 52:12--23).  The later list of
vessels returned under Cyrus likewise omits it (Ezra 1:7--11).  Silence proves
neither destruction, deportation, nor concealment.  Scripture narrates no route
from Jerusalem to Babylon and no recovered earthly destination.

Jeremiah had already opened a different horizon.  In the promised restoration,
the Ark will no longer be spoken of, remembered, visited, or remade; Jerusalem
itself will be called the throne of the Lord, and the nations will gather there
(Jer 3:14--18).  This is prophecy, not a disappearance report.  It looks beyond
the sign toward a wider indwelling and gathering.

\subsection{The cave that cannot be found}

Second Maccabees transmits an account from writings concerning Jeremiah.  The
prophet, warned by God, takes the tent, the Ark, and the incense altar to the
mountain where Moses had seen the inheritance.  He places them in a cave and
seals the entrance.  When some companions try to mark the way, they cannot
find it.  Jeremiah says that the place will remain unknown until God gathers
his people, shows mercy, and manifests glory and cloud
(2 Macc 2:1--8).

Catholic reading receives this passage as inspired Scripture transmitting the
account; it need not demote it to ``mere legend.''  It must also receive the
limits actually written.  The mountain is naturally connected with Nebo or
Pisgah through Deuteronomy's account of Moses viewing the land
(Deut 32:49; 34:1), but 2 Maccabees does not name Nebo or disclose a mappable
cave.  The concealment ends the cartographic road in hiddenness and hope.

At the close of the canon, God's heavenly Temple opens and the Ark of the
Covenant is seen, accompanied by lightning, voices, thunder, earthquake, and
hail.  Immediately afterward a great sign appears: the Woman clothed with the
sun, giving birth to the messianic ruler and opposed by the dragon
(Rev 11:19--12:6).  This is a heavenly vision, not the last coordinate of an
earthly itinerary.  Nor does the grammar simply rename the Ark as the Woman.
The Woman bears the corporate horizons of Israel and the Church and the
personal Marian horizon as mother of the Messiah.

The sequence nevertheless completes the old journey canonically.  Ark,
Temple, Woman, Messiah, and people of God stand within one heaven-opened vision.
What the historical road can no longer locate, Revelation places within the
consummating purpose of God.  The next chapters will show why the Church hears
in that purpose the mystery of the living Ark.

\begin{studybox}{Where the road ends}
The historical trail ends with a command in Josiah's reign, not with a solved
archaeological mystery.  Jeremiah gives prophecy; 2 Maccabees gives hiddenness
and promised gathering; Revelation gives heavenly vision.  Respecting those
different forms does not weaken Catholic hope.  It lets each inspired witness
give the kind of truth it was written to give.
\end{studybox}
