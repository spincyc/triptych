\section{Where the Glory Dwells}

The Ark's journey ends neither in an archaeological puzzle nor in a technique
for multiplying Marian comparisons.  Its deepest question is the one carried
through the whole economy: how does the holy God make his dwelling among his
people without ceasing to be holy, and how can creatures receive his presence
without trying to possess it?  The Christian answer is Jesus Christ.  The
eternal Son does not merely place another sign of God among us; he takes flesh,
dwells among us, offers that flesh for the life of the world, rises bodily, and
draws his people into communion with the Father in the Spirit (John 1:14;
2:19--22; 6:51; 14:23).

Mary's Ark identity is therefore personal and relational.  She is not a sacred
object beside Christ.  She is the daughter of Israel who receives the Father's
word in faith, becomes Mother of the incarnate Son by the Holy Spirit, and
bears the hidden Lord into the hill country.  Every Marian privilege considered
in this study is first a work of the Trinity and a confession about Christ.
Her greatness does not compete with his; it manifests what his grace can do in
a creature who is wholly receptive and truly free.

\subsection{Presence received, not possessed}

Israel could carry the Ark, but it could not domesticate the Lord.  The defeat
at Ebenezer, the humiliation of Dagon, the breach at Perez-uzzah, and David's
later obedience expose the same error from different sides: holy things cannot
be made instruments of presumption.  Yet holiness is not divine reluctance to
draw near.  The Lord gives the covenant, appoints its service, blesses the
house of Obed-edom, fills the Temple, and restores a disordered procession to
joy.  Reverence is the creature's truthful welcome of a gift that remains
God's.

The New Covenant perfects that welcome rather than abolishing it.  Mary's
\latin{fiat} does not seize a divine power or negotiate the Incarnation.  It is
the grace-enabled consent by which she receives the vocation God gives.  Her
haste to Elizabeth then shows that receptivity is not inertia.  The Lord first
dwells within her; because he dwells within her, she rises and carries his
presence into an act of charity.  Contemplation and mission appear in their
right order: grace received becomes service, while the fruit remains Christ's.

\subsection{Joseph's custody as covenantal love}

Joseph stands within this mystery by a vocation given through the angel: he is
to take Mary as his wife.  He obeys, receives her into a true marriage, names
the child, shelters the household, and exercises a real Davidic fatherhood
without claiming the Child's divine origin as his own (Matt 1:18--25;
2:13--23).  His continence belongs to the positive form of a mutually given
virginal marriage ordered to the persons entrusted to it.

Joseph's divinely appointed reception and service govern the comparison.
St.~Ephrem the Syrian pictures him ministering before the Lord dwelling in
Mary as a priest ministers before the Ark (\emph{Hymns on the Nativity}
16.16--17).  The ancient image is Christological and domestic: divine holiness
calls forth Joseph's chaste, personal service within the household entrusted
to him.  He welcomes Mary, names Jesus, protects mother and Child, works for
them, suffers exile with them, and gives himself as husband and guardian.

Uzzah belongs here only as a bounded contrast.  His narrative concerns
handling the Ark contrary to the appointed order; Matthew presents no verbal
or narrative equation between him and Joseph.  The contrast may distinguish
unauthorized taking-hold from an entrusted vocation, but it does not explain
Joseph's continence and cannot make Mary physically dangerous or marital union
unclean.  Joseph and Mary freely share a true virginal marriage.  Holiness
does not cancel human love; grace gives that love a singular covenantal form.

\subsection{From the overshadowed Virgin to the Spirit-filled Church}

Mary's vocation is unique, but it is not sterile.  Vatican II places her within
the mystery of the Church: the Virgin who receives the Word in faith and bears
him bodily is the eminent type of the Church, which receives the same Word by
faith and brings forth Christ in its members (LG 63--65).  The analogy depends
upon the unrepeatable Incarnation.  The Church does not become another Mother
of God, and no disciple repeats Mary's divine maternity.  Yet the pattern of
receptive faith, Spirit-given holiness, communion, and mission is genuinely
ecclesial.

The New Testament accordingly speaks of the whole Church and of the baptized
as God's temple (1 Cor 3:16--17; 6:19--20; Eph 2:19--22; 1 Pet 2:4--5).  This
indwelling is participation by grace, not a confusion of Creator and creature.
It is nourished by the word, ordered to the sacramental life, and made visible
in holiness and charity.  A reader who admires the Ark's gold but refuses the
conversion of the heart has stopped at the sign.  A reader who praises Mary's
welcome but closes life against Christ's command has not yet received the
pattern she embodies.

\subsection{Contemplation becomes charity}

Three modern Christian writers have helped shape this study's pedagogy without
serving as evidence for its biblical or Marian claims.  C.~S. Lewis's
\emph{Miracles} helps imagination awaken to supernatural reality and then
submit its images to truthful judgment.  Dom Jean-Baptiste Chautard's
\emph{The Soul of the Apostolate} keeps outward fruit dependent upon interior
reception of grace.  Réginald Garrigou-Lagrange's \emph{The Three Ages of the
Interior Life} encourages a return to governing truths at increasing depth,
until doctrine opens into contemplation and fidelity.  These are not three
independent systems laid over Scripture.  The project adapts their pedagogical
insights in original prose to assist attention to the movement Scripture itself
displays: Mary hears, believes, conceives, rises, serves, praises, and returns
home carrying the mystery she has received.

Christian contemplation is therefore neither the collection of striking
parallels nor an escape into private religious feeling.  It attends to the
reality God has revealed, consents to that reality in faith, and allows charity
to take concrete form.  The fitting response to the Jordan crossing may be
trust in divine initiative; to the Philistine circuit, repentance for using
religion as an instrument; to David's recovered procession, obedient worship;
to the Annunciation, availability to vocation; to the Visitation, service of a
neighbor with Christ rather than self-display.  Application does not prove the
exegesis.  It is the fruit asked of an exegesis already governed by the truth.

This movement protects the apostolate from two contrary failures.  Activity
without interior life begins to treat holy work as possession, achievement, or
performance.  Interior devotion without charity refuses the very diffusion of
grace displayed in the Visitation.  The Christian carries no autonomous gift
to the world.  The disciple first receives from Christ and then serves in the
strength Christ supplies (John 15:4--5; 1 Pet 4:10--11).

\subsection{The road toward rest}

The Ark's ascent to Zion and the cloud filling Solomon's house gave Israel a
real covenantal resting place, but not the final one.  The Temple could be
destroyed; the Ark could disappear from narrated history; the people still
waited for the Lord to dwell with them definitively.  Christ's risen body is
the indestructible Temple, and the heavenly city needs no sanctuary other than
God and the Lamb (John 2:19--22; Rev 21:22).  All Christian use of Ark imagery
must arrive there.

Mary's Assumption belongs within that Paschal horizon.  The one who bore the
Son in her body is glorified by him in body and soul; her destiny anticipates,
but does not replace, the resurrection promised to the Church.  The final
lesson of the Ark is therefore not that God has found one beautiful container
and left the rest of creation outside.  In Christ, God prepares a people and a
creation in which righteousness dwells.  Mary is the graced beginning and
personal icon of that fulfilled welcome; the Church journeys toward the day
when the dwelling of God is with humanity and all his servants see his face
(Rev 21:3; 22:4).

\begin{studybox}{From wonder to life}
Receive the revealed truth before searching for an application.  Ask what it
confesses about Christ, how Mary receives his grace, what form of ecclesial
life it illuminates, and which concrete act of faith or charity now answers
the gift.  The goal is not mastery of a symbol but communion with the Lord to
whom the symbol leads.
\end{studybox}
