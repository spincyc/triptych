\section{Scripture Recognizes Scripture}

At first a journey may be only a journey.  Then one bell after another rings.
Luke never places the noun ``Ark'' beside Mary's name, and he does not turn the
Visitation into an exact reenactment of 2 Samuel 6.  Instead he composes a
constellation of correspondences.  Read with Israel's Scriptures still open,
the scene teaches the reader to recognize who is coming: Mary enters the house
as the mother bearing Israel's incarnate Lord.

\subsection{Keep both scrolls open}

David rises and goes; Mary rises and goes.  The Septuagint titles the book
that ordinary English Bibles call 2 Samuel ``2 Reigns''; in the older
Douay--Rheims four-book numbering the same book is ``2 Kings.''  Its Greek at
6:2 and Luke 1:39 share the same two lemmas in the same order.  The finite form for
``went'' is identical; the words rendered ``rose'' are different forms of the
same verb.  That wording is too common to establish an allusion alone.  But
both journeys
move through Judah's uplands, and both bring a holy bearer into a household.
The Ark enters Obed-edom's house and the Lord blesses the house; Mary enters
Zechariah's house bearing the Lord, and Spirit-filled blessing breaks forth
(2 Sam 6:2--12; Luke 1:39--42).

The scene then becomes bodily and vocal.  The Ark's ascent sounds with cry and
voice, and David dances before the Lord; Elizabeth cries out with a great cry,
the voice of Mary's greeting reaches her, and John leaps for joy
(2 Sam 6:14--16; Luke 1:41--44).  David asks how the Ark of the Lord can come
to him; Elizabeth asks how the mother of her Lord should come to her.  The Ark
remains in a household for three months; Mary remains with Elizabeth for about
three months (2 Sam 6:9--11; Luke 1:43, 56).  Luke's order is not mechanically
identical to Samuel's---in particular, Elizabeth's cry and John's leap precede
the sojourn---but the linked motifs remain striking.

No single link has to carry the whole case.  Travel formulas can be common,
upland geography can recur, and three months can be an ordinary span.  The
claim rests upon their convergence: arise and go, Judah's heights, entry into
a house, blessing, cry and voice, embodied joy, the wondering question, and
the three-month stay.  The cumulative pattern is more explanatory than any
one resemblance considered alone.

\subsection{The word Luke uses nowhere else}

At the threshold Luke chooses an unusually revealing verb.  Elizabeth
``cried aloud''---\emph{anephōnēsen}, the aorist active indicative, third person
singular, of \emph{anaphōneō} (Luke 1:42).  It is the verb's only occurrence in
the repository-replayable MorphGNT lemma layer aligned to SBLGNT.  The
identified Greek Chronicles witnesses employ the same lemma at
1 Chronicles 15:28; 16:4, 5, 42; and 2 Chronicles 5:13.  Those passages cluster
in Ark and sanctuary worship, although 1 Chronicles 16:42 concerns the music
at Gibeon and does not itself name the Ark.  The point is the witnessed
liturgical setting, not a claim that a restricted electronic corpus has been
exhaustively or uniquely searched.

The word does not operate as a cipher proving Mary's identity.  It does
something more literary and more beautiful: it gives Elizabeth's Spirit-filled
welcome the sound of Israel's Ark-centered worship.  The rare verb matters
because the journey, house, question, joy, and three months have already made
that worship a fitting world for the scene.

\subsection{David's dance and John's leap}

The parallel between David and John is confident, but textual precision makes
it stronger.  The standard Septuagint text of 2 Samuel 6:16 says that David was
``dancing and playing''; it uses verbs from \emph{orcheomai} and
\emph{anakrouō}.  Luke says that John ``leaped,'' using
\emph{skirtaō} (Luke 1:41, 44).  Thus the standard Septuagint does not use
Luke's verb of David.

A Symmachus-attributed rendering preserved in later Hexaplaric witnesses does
use the \emph{skirtaō} family in 2 Samuel 6:16.  Its form is a present active
participle; Luke's is an aorist active indicative, ``leaped.''  Benedict XVI
carefully referred to
``one of the ancient Greek translations'' when he joined David's dance to
John's leap in his Assumption homily of 15 August 2011.  The same-lemma link
therefore belongs to the Symmachus tradition, not to the standard Septuagint;
the grammatical forms themselves are different.

The typological image does not depend upon the fragment.  Because Symmachus is
later than Luke, it is evidence of an ancient Greek rendering of David's joy,
not evidence that Luke copied Symmachus.

\subsection{What the living Ark bears}

The old Ark bore the covenant's sacred witnesses.  The Torah explicitly puts
the stone tablets within it (Deut 10:1--5).  Exodus locates the manna jar before
the covenant, Numbers locates Aaron's staff there, and Deuteronomy places the
Torah scroll beside the Ark (Exod 16:32--34; Num 17:25; Deut 31:24--26).
At Solomon's dedication only the tablets are said to remain inside
(1 Kgs 8:9).  Hebrews later gathers the familiar threefold inventory ``in''
the Ark: manna, Aaron's sprouted staff, and the covenant tablets (Heb 9:4).
These distinct canonical formulations should not be forced into an invented
history of removal.

Their fulfillment is not an inventory game.  Mary bears the one person in whom
the signs converge: the living Word and New Covenant; the true Bread from
heaven; and the definitive High Priest, whose priesthood Hebrews describes
according to the order of Melchizedek (John 6:32--51; Heb 5:5--10; 7:11--17).
The Church calls Mary the Ark because of Christ.  The treasures do not make
him a bundle of symbols, and the comparison does not make her a container
without intellect, freedom, or love.

\subsection{The Ark and the Woman in heaven}

At the end of the biblical canon the heavenly temple opens and the Ark of the
Covenant is seen within it.  Immediately afterward a great sign appears in
heaven: the Woman clothed with the sun, giving birth to the messianic ruler
(Rev 11:19--12:5).  The two appearances share the heavenly setting and the
Greek verb ``was seen.''  Revelation does not grammatically rename the Ark as
the Woman.  The Woman also has an irreducible corporate horizon: she images
God's people, Israel and the Church, as well as Mary, the personal mother of
the Messiah.

Yet the adjacency is canonically fruitful.  Mary personally embodies faithful
Israel and is the preeminent type of the Church; the Ark--Woman sequence is
therefore a natural home for Catholic contemplation of her heavenly glory.
The Roman Assumption liturgy proclaims Revelation 11:19a and 12:1--6a, 10ab as
one reading, and Benedict XVI receives the same sequence as a scriptural image
of the living Ark's entry into God's rest.  This is strong canonical and
liturgical fittingness, not a stand-alone photographic account of the
Assumption.

\subsection{Recognition within the Church}

Catholic reception does not begin by distrusting the pattern and then granting
it the least meaning possible.  It welcomes the ordered correspondences as a
providential feature of the one divine economy.  The Catechism calls Mary the
Ark of the Covenant, the place where the Lord's glory dwells (CCC 2676).
Benedict XVI calls her the living Ark because she bore the living Word and the
new and eternal Covenant.  The Church's Visitation liturgy names her the Ark
of the New Covenant who brings salvation and joy into Elizabeth's house.
Patristic and liturgical reception, treated in the next chapter, show that this
recognition belongs to the Church's memory rather than to a modern taste for
ingenious parallels.

The necessary boundaries are positive truths.  Mary is not the Lord; she is
his mother.  She does not generate his divinity; he takes a true humanity from
her.  She does not replace Israel or the Church; she is Israel's faithful
daughter and the Church's eminent type.  Typology does not substitute for the
proper biblical and traditional grounds of a dogma.  It gathers the economy
around its center: the Word became flesh, and the woman who bore him is the
living Ark of the New Covenant.

\begin{studybox}{A rule for reading the parallels}
First read each passage in its own narrative.  Then notice repeated words,
actions, places, and theological relations across the canon.  Ask whether the
correspondences converge upon Christ.  Receive the Church's liturgical and
patristic reading as evidence of lived interpretation.  Finally preserve the
difference between type and fulfillment.  This discipline does not cool
wonder; it keeps wonder attached to truth.
\end{studybox}
