\section{A Covenant Household: Mary and Joseph's Virginal Marriage}

The living Ark does not enter the New Covenant as an object deposited in a
vault.  Mary is a free believer, a mother, and a wife.  Jesus is not sacred
cargo but the eternal Son made man.  Joseph is not stationed outside a taboo:
he is addressed as ``son of David,'' commanded to receive Mary as his wife,
and entrusted with a real father's service toward her Son.  Under their roof
reverence becomes covenantal communion---obedience, fidelity, work,
protection, tenderness, and the complete gift of self.

\arkthesis{Mary and Joseph's marriage was wholly true and wholly virginal.
Its continence was not fear of Mary's body, contempt for conjugal love, or a
merely legal arrangement.  It was the Spirit-given form of their mutual
spousal gift and common vocation around the incarnate Son.}

\subsection{``Joseph, son of David, do not fear''}

Matthew first calls Jesus son of David and then, at the decisive moment, lets
the angel address Joseph by the same royal lineage (Matt 1:1, 20).  The command
is positive: do not fear to take Mary your wife, because the child conceived
in her is from the Holy Spirit.  Joseph awakes and does what the angel
commands.  He takes his wife and names her Son Jesus (Matt 1:24--25).  In the
next chapter the pattern becomes a refrain.  God commands Joseph to take the
child and his mother through danger; Joseph rises and takes them to Egypt, and
later home to Israel (Matt 2:13--14, 19--21).

Matthew's repeated verb for taking or receiving, \emph{paralamban\=o}, marks
command answered by obedience.  It is not a technical Ark word: the Greek Ark
narratives use other ordinary verbs for bringing, bearing, holding, and
carrying.  The correspondence is therefore canonical and theological rather
than an alleged secret vocabulary.  Luke discloses Mary as the living Ark who
bears the Lord; Matthew shows the Davidic household into which God commands
that mother and Son be received.

The naming is part of this vocation.  Joseph does not generate Jesus, yet his
obedient act gives the child a true legal and familial place in David's house.
St.~John Paul II accordingly teaches that the marriage is the juridical basis
of Joseph's fatherhood and that this fatherhood is authentic service, not an
appearance or convenient fiction (\emph{Redemptoris custos} 7--8, 12, 21).
Joseph guards the mystery with Mary and in relation to her.  His authority is
fatherly and domestic; its form is obedience and self-giving service.

\subsection{A true marriage, a complete gift}

Catholic tradition does not preserve Mary's virginity by dissolving her
marriage.  St.~Augustine insists that Mary and Joseph possessed the goods of
marriage: offspring, fidelity, and an enduring bond.  Their mutually embraced
continence did not make the union unreal; the concord of their hearts made it
firmer (\emph{On Marriage and Concupiscence} I.11.12--13).  He can therefore
call Joseph father in truth and love though not by bodily generation, and he
connects Joseph's naming of Jesus with real paternal authority
(\emph{Sermon} 51.10.16; 13.21).

St.~Thomas Aquinas develops the same account.  Marriage is constituted in its
form by consent to the nuptial bond, an inseparable union of souls.  Mary and
Joseph's marriage was therefore simply and wholly true.  It remained
unconsummated as to intercourse, while reaching its singular parental fruit in
the spouses' mutual service in rearing Christ (ST III, q.29, a.2).  Thomas's
solution is theological, not an attempt to reconstruct an unrecorded exchange
of formulas; its permanent point is that virginity and marriage are not
rivals.

\emph{Redemptoris custos} makes this positive grammar controlling.  The Holy
Spirit did not abolish Joseph's human love for Mary; he renewed and deepened it
as genuine conjugal love, exclusive self-gift, covenant between persons, and
authentic communion (18--20).  The Roman liturgy presents their bond as a love
that is at once marital and virginal.  Mary gives herself as virgin, mother, and wife;
Joseph gives himself as husband and father.  Their continence is the singular,
freely shared form of that love, not its absence.

This truth protects every Christian vocation.  The Church calls the chaste
acts proper to ordinary marriage noble and worthy, and teaches that consecrated
virginity presupposes and confirms the goodness of marriage rather than
condemning it (\emph{Gaudium et spes} 48--49; \emph{Familiaris consortio} 16).
Mary and Joseph's exceptional calling gives no warrant for treating sexuality,
childbirth, bodily affection, or women as contaminating.  Nor is Joseph's
virginity before this marriage a separately defined dogma.  What belongs to
this study's doctrinal center is Mary's perpetual virginity and the true,
virginal form of their common married life.

\subsection{Joseph before the living Ark}

St.~Ephrem the Syrian gives the strongest early positive Ark image for
Joseph.  In his authentic \emph{Hymns on the Nativity} 16.16--17, Joseph rises
to serve his Lord within Mary; Ephrem compares that service to a priest
ministering before the Ark, then sets Joseph beside Moses bearing the pure
tablet in whom the Creator's Son dwells.  The poem does not ordain
Joseph or make him an Aaronic or Levitical priest.  It transfigures ordinary
household care into reverent ministry because the Lord dwells within his wife.

St.~Bernard gives the guardianship still more domestic concreteness.  Joseph is
entrusted to carry, lead, embrace, kiss, nourish, and guard Jesus
(\emph{In Praise of the Virgin Mother} II.16).  Holiness does not evacuate the
home of human affection.  It orders affection to love.

The sanctuary images say the same in another register.  Ezekiel's shut eastern
gate belongs literally to the prophet's restored sanctuary (Ezek 44:1--3), but
Sts.~Ambrose and Jerome receive it as a figure of Mary's enduring virginity.
The Song's enclosed garden and sealed fountain add bridal fragrance and
fruitfulness (Song 4:12).  These are received typologies, not anatomical
diagrams or evidence that Joseph was barred from personal closeness to his
wife.  The gate is reserved because the Lord has entered; the garden is
enclosed in fidelity to the beloved.  Consecration here is fruitful belonging,
not sexual disgust.

\subsection{What Uzzah can---and cannot---teach}

Uzzah's death must not be hidden, but it must be read by Scripture's own
canonical explanation.  The first Davidic procession placed the Ark on a
cart.  When the oxen stumbled, Uzzah reached out and took hold of it, and he
died (2 Sam 6:3, 6--7; 1 Chr 13:7--10).  Chronicles later names the disorder:
the appointed Levites had not borne the Ark according to the ordinance.  The
corrected procession sanctifies the bearers and carries the Ark upon their
shoulders with its poles as Moses commanded (1 Chr 15:2, 11--15; Num 4:15;
7:9).  The story teaches that holy service receives its order from God's word;
an action that appears helpful does not by itself authorize departure from
that order.

The following Joseph--Uzzah contrast is this study's pastoral and theological
synthesis, not an identification made by Matthew or a Father.  Uzzah belongs
to an episode of handling contrary to the appointed order.  Joseph receives a
positive divine command: ``Do not fear to take Mary your wife.''  He obeys.
The point is not ``touch a woman and die'' versus ``avoid touching her.''  It
is disordered handling of the old sacred sign versus obedient reception of
persons within the new covenantal household.  Uzzah does not explain Joseph's
continence, and Joseph is neither a new Levite nor a second Uzzah.

Mary is not physically dangerous; conjugal union is not unclean; Scripture
does not say Joseph feared that contact with his wife would kill him.  He did
not guard Mary from himself.  He gave himself to her as husband and, with her,
guarded the mystery entrusted to their marriage.  Their virginal love is
therefore best remembered through five active verbs: \emph{receive, name,
protect, nourish, serve}.  Beneath their roof the New Covenant enters a true
human family.
