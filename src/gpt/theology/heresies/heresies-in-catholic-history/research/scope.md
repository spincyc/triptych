# Heresies in Catholic History — Research Scope and Qualification Record

## Governing question and identity

This record supports `src/gpt/theology/heresies/heresies-in-catholic-history/main.tex`.

- **Provider and collection:** GPT; theology; heresies.
- **Genre and profile:** comprehensive historical-theology reference governed by `guidance/editorial.md`, `guidance/repository.md`, and `guidance/theology/heresies.md`. It is not a penal process, a canon-law opinion on a person, an ecumenical dialogue document, or a general discursive article.
- **Question:** Across Catholic history, what propositions and doctrinal systems were identified as contrary to the apostolic faith; by whom, through what instrument, at what authority and scope were they answered; what positive doctrine was clarified; and what happened to the persons, movements, and ideas afterward?
- **Intended reader:** a serious Catholic lay reader, catechist, seminarian, clergy member, historian, or study group needing a chronological documentary reference that is more exact than a name-list but more concise than separate monographs.
- **Thesis:** the history of heresy is best understood as the Church's repeated clarification of one received faith in the Trinity, the Incarnation, creation, grace, sacrament, Church, and last things. Condemned propositions must be stated specifically, but propositions, persons, movements, penalties, and descendants must never be collapsed into one verdict.
- **Historical range:** the apostolic period through 16 July 2026. Apostolic texts supply doctrinal boundaries rather than anachronistic trials under later canon law.
- **Geography and jurisdiction:** the undivided early Church; Greek, Latin, Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, and related Christian settings where the evidence bears on later Catholic reception; medieval Latin Christendom; and the Roman Catholic Church's post-medieval doctrinal acts. The survey is global in ecclesial scope but does not claim equal documentary survival from every region.
- **Currentness:** official current canon law, current DDF documents material to the conclusion, and cited modern ecumenical qualifications were checked through 2026-07-16. The current SSPX endpoint keeps three response acts distinct: the 13 May DDF prospective warning, Leo XIV's 29 June prospective letter, and the 2 July signed DDF decree and explanatory note responding to the completed 1 July act. The decree's five reserved canon 1387 cases and Fellay's separate canon 1364 §1 case are not collapsed, and the note's statement that SSPX ministers are subject to canon 1364 §1 is not rewritten as an individual declaration against every minister. Stable historical acts are identified by their own date rather than assigned artificial current legal force.
- **Publication order:** after title and contents, the apostolic boundary is the first body section and the dossiers proceed chronologically through the historical synthesis. Terminal apparatus then gives the response timeline, census and alias/context/exclusion accounting, `Scope, Terms, and Qualifications`, references, and generation metadata.

## Meaning of comprehensive

There is no identified official universal register of every historical heresy. “Comprehensive” here means **enumeratively complete against the named baseline census**, not omniscient or exhaustive of every local accusation, unpublished trial, private error, or nickname.

The controlling `research/corpus-inventory.md` reconciles:

1. the Christian groups, schools, and aliases in the principal broad ancient heresiologies: Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* I; Hippolytus, *Refutation of All Heresies*; Epiphanius, *Panarion*; Philastrius, *Book of Diverse Heresies*; Augustine, *De haeresibus*; and John Damascene, *On Heresies*;
2. the relevant doctrinal acts and named groups of the twenty-one ecumenical councils received by the Catholic Church, while distinguishing dogmatic decrees from disciplinary canons, disputed sessions, and subsequent reception;
3. consequential papal, Roman, regional-synodal, medieval canonical, and inquisitorial doctrinal acts that became durable reference points;
4. the Reformation-era propositions answered by *Exsurge Domine*, the Council of Trent, and related professions and judgments;
5. the principal post-Tridentine proposition lists and doctrinal controversies concerning grace, rigorism, quietism, ecclesial authority, reason, revelation, and Modernism; and
6. representative twentieth- and twenty-first-century Roman doctrinal notifications needed to explain why a censure of a book, author, or tendency does not automatically create a new named heresy.

Every baseline item is classified as a full dossier, a subgroup or alias assigned to a dossier, a compact source-limited capsule, a contextual controversy, or a reasoned exclusion. Ancient numerical catalogues are not combined by adding their totals: they overlap, copy one another, include pre-Christian philosophies and non-Christian religions, and sometimes create several names from one genealogy.

## Included scope

Included are:

- definitions of heresy, apostasy, schism, dogma, anathema, excommunication, proposition-level censure, and evidence grade;
- apostolic boundaries concerning circumcision as a salvation condition, bodily resurrection, and the real Incarnation;
- chronological dossiers from Jewish-Christian currents, Docetism, Gnostic schools, Marcionism, and Montanism through the classical Trinitarian, Christological, anthropological, sacramental, ecclesiological, and icon controversies;
- eastern and western medieval movements, with source limits and jurisdiction identified;
- Reformation and post-Reformation doctrinal complexes, stated through the propositions and Catholic responses actually documented;
- modern rationalist, fideist, immanentist, relativist, and doctrinal-method controversies where an identifiable Roman or conciliar act exists;
- the positive Catholic doctrine articulated in response, not only the rejected sentence;
- recantation, survival, institutional separation, coercive aftermath, later reception, and modern ecumenical clarification;
- a response-event timeline audited separately from the prose; and
- an alias, context, and exclusion accounting that prevents a short list from masquerading as a universal register.

## Excluded or contextualized scope

The census accounts for but does not flatten into heresy:

- Judaism, Islam, pagan religions, philosophical schools, and other religions as religions;
- ordinary moral sins, clerical or institutional abuses, simony, sacrilege, magic, witchcraft accusations, and disciplinary violations unless joined to an identifiable doctrinal proposition;
- Quartodeciman practice and other calendar or disciplinary differences not formally defined as heresy;
- Meletian, Luciferian, Acacian, Photian, East–West, Western, and comparable schisms where the controlling object is communion or jurisdiction rather than a defined heresy;
- the Three Chapters dispute as though it were one new popular sect; the dossier instead identifies the condemned person and writings and their Christological reception;
- the Knights Templar as a coherent heresy, because charges, coerced testimony, administrative suppression, and individual misconduct do not establish one reliably reconstructed doctrinal system;
- all Beguines, Humiliati, Spiritual Franciscans, Flagellants, Waldensians, or other heterogeneous populations merely because some members or propositions were censured;
- the 1277 Paris syllabus, Constance proposition lists, *Exsurge Domine*, *Unigenitus*, or *Auctorem fidei* as though every listed sentence received the single censure “heretical”;
- disputed Catholic schools left open by the Magisterium, including Thomist–Molinist disputes after the *de auxiliis* congregations;
- Freemasonry, Communism, Nazism, secular ideologies, and political movements as Christian heresies, even where Catholic authorities condemned their principles;
- the SSPX canonical rupture and current status acts, sedevacantist claims, or another present canonical controversy as though any were one adjudicated named heresy absent the proposition-level and authority threshold required by this census;
- every twentieth-century theological investigation or notification as a new named heresy; representative cases explain the relevant distinction; and
- a judgment of internal culpability, present canonical crime, or salvation concerning any named person.

## Authority and object classes

The publication maintains independent classifications for:

- **object:** proposition, person, school, organized movement, communion, schism, practice, or allegation;
- **source of wording:** target text, formal act, judicial extract, opponent's paraphrase, near-contemporary report, or later catalogue;
- **response:** patristic or episcopal refutation, local or regional synod, papal or Roman act, ecumenical council, profession or recantation, inquisitorial judgment, or civil act;
- **censure:** the act's exact or tightly translated grade, not a normalized blanket label; and
- **evidence:** A (act and target survive), B (act survives but target is extractive or indirect), C (near-contemporary report without extant act), or D (late, hostile, legendary, or substantially disputed reconstruction).

Evidence grade describes documentary access, not doctrinal seriousness. Ecumenical councils and papal definitions are interpreted according to their genre, text, reception, and object; private compendia such as Denzinger–Hünermann and scholarly collections such as Tanner are finding and textual aids, not promulgating authorities.

## Principal source corpora and roles

### Apostolic and patristic

- Sacred Scripture: the inspired doctrinal boundary; later labels are not projected backward.
- Ignatius, Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Hippolytus, Origen, Eusebius, Athanasius, Hilary, Basil, the Gregories, Cyril of Alexandria, Augustine, Epiphanius, Theodoret, and John Damascene: direct writings, refutations, or historical witnesses according to the exact work and locus.
- Recovered Gnostic and other non-catholic texts: target-side evidence where securely attributed, not a license to treat every modern anthology as a critical edition.

### Councils, papal acts, and canonical collections

- Critical or identified texts of the ecumenical councils, especially *Acta Conciliorum Oecumenicorum*, Tanner, and the original-language or official reception texts identified in the audit.
- Papal letters, constitutions, decretals, Roman decrees, and professions in their *Acta*, registers, bullaria, or official Holy See editions where available.
- Denzinger–Hünermann numbers as stable cross-references to the underlying acts; the publication does not reproduce that copyrighted compendium or treat its editorial headings as promulgated judgments.
- MGH, CSEL, GCS, *Corpus Christianorum*, *Sources Chrétiennes*, Friedberg's *Corpus Iuris Canonici*, and document-specific critical editions as textual controls or recorded outstanding checks.

### Modern official and ecumenical sources

- 1983 Code of Canon Law, especially canons 9, 17–18, 749–754, and 751; the 1990 Eastern Code where Eastern penal terminology materially matters.
- *Unitatis redintegratio* 3–4 and 11; the 1973 Catholic–Coptic, 1984 Catholic–Syriac, and 1994 Catholic–Assyrian common declarations; and the 1999 Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification and its annex.
- DDF or predecessor documents used for proposition-level modern examples, with the act's actual doctrinal conclusion and procedural genre preserved.

## Material qualifications and negative results

1. Nicaea I and Constantinople I do not leave complete stenographic acts; the creed, canons, synodal letters, fragments, and near-contemporary witnesses must carry only what they establish.
2. The ancient category “Gnosticism” names related but diverse currents, not one centrally organized church.
3. “Ebionites,” “Nazarenes,” and “Elchasaites” are not interchangeable; Jewish-Christian taxonomy depends heavily on external witnesses.
4. Montanism combined disputed prophecy claims, discipline, and later regional doctrinal developments; the original New Prophecy is not reduced to one proposition list.
5. Manichaeism was a rival universal religion historically treated in Christian heresiology, not simply a dissenting baptized school.
6. “Semi-Arian” and “Semi-Pelagian” are later organizing labels. The dossiers identify actual formulas and the propositions answered at Constantinople I and Orange II.
7. Modern Oriental Orthodox miaphysite faith is not equated with Eutyches. Modern common declarations qualify inherited labels.
8. The fifteen anti-Origenist anathemas associated with 553 have a disputed conciliar provenance; they are not silently printed as the formal acts of Constantinople II.
9. Honorius I is reported with the exact Sixth Council sentence and Leo II's confirmation; no slogan substitutes for the act or the distinct later definition of papal infallibility.
10. Paulician, Bogomil, Cathar, “Free Spirit,” and several other movements are reconstructed mostly from opponents or judicial sources. Regional diversity and current historiographical dispute are retained.
11. The 1277 Paris condemnation was local and academic; its 219 propositions are not 219 universal dogmatic definitions.
12. Constance's Wyclif and Hus materials contain attribution disputes and mixed censures. The publication does not call every extracted article a heresy or every conciliar session equal in authority.
13. The first Waldensian conflict involved unauthorized preaching and discipline; later Waldensian doctrine and reconciled branches require separate description.
14. Reformation communions are not treated as monolithic persons, and later adherents are not charged with founders' culpability.
15. The 1999 Joint Declaration says the sixteenth-century Lutheran and Catholic condemnations still matter while not applying to the partner's teaching as presented in the agreed basic truths of justification; neither half may be omitted.
16. Baius, Jansenius, Quesnel, Molinos, Fénelon, Pistoia, and Modernism are treated through exact proposition-level or system-level acts; the censure grades remain distinct.
17. Leo XIII's *Testem benevolentiae* conditionally rejected tendencies attributed to “Americanism” while expressing confidence that the American bishops did not intend them; it is not used as proof of an organized American heresy.
18. *Humani generis* warns against dangerous or erroneous opinions but does not turn every theological development it discusses into formal heresy.
19. A DDF notification may find propositions erroneous, dangerous, ambiguous, or incompatible with Catholic doctrine without judging every work by the author or creating a named heresy.
20. Similarity is not genealogy. The conclusion compares recurrent doctrinal fault lines without inventing secret historical descent.

## Copyright and quotation boundaries

The publication is chiefly project-created paraphrase and synthesis. It uses only short identifying clauses from formal definitions or propositions when the exact wording is necessary. It does not reproduce complete conciliar editions, papal documents, modern translations, Denzinger–Hünermann entries, modern scholarly chapters, trial records, or source-site corpora. Ecclesiastical and official texts retain their own status; public-domain originals and translations remain public domain where that status is established; modern editions and translations remain third-party material. Links identify access points and do not imply ownership or permission to republish an edition.
