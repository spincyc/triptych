## Governing question and thesis

The account asks how a plural Old Latin scriptural world became the family of texts later called the Vulgate; which parts can be assigned to Jerome, anonymous revisers, or inherited Old Latin forms; how manuscript and print cultures changed the text; and why the Clementine, modern critical Vulgates, and *Nova Vulgata* must not be treated as interchangeable editions.

The governing thesis is that “Vulgate” does not name one translation made at one moment by one man. It names a Latin biblical tradition whose principal ingredients include pre-Jerome Latin translations, Jerome's conservative Gospel revision, several Greek- and Hebrew-related Old Testament projects, an anonymous revision of much of the remaining New Testament, and centuries of copying, correction, printing, critical reconstruction, and institutional reception. The history therefore has to identify the book, witness, act, and edition each time the title is used.

## Included coverage

1. The plural Old Latin or *Vetus Latina* evidence and its predominantly Greek source relation.
2. Jerome's Roman Gospel revision in 382–384 and the limits of the Damasian evidence.
3. The negative authorship result for Acts, the Epistles, and Revelation.
4. Jerome's Hexaplaric projects, the Roman/Gallican/Hebrew Psalter distinctions, and his staged Hebrew-based translations about 390–405.
5. *Hebraica veritas* as philological principle and polemical rhetoric, tested against Jerome's mixed scholarly sources.
6. The Augustine–Jerome exchange in its actual order: Augustine Letter 71, Jerome Letter 112 (Augustine Letter 75), and Augustine Letter 82.
7. The composite ancestry of the received Vulgate, including distinct procedures for Tobit and Judith and Old Latin deuterocanonical books.
8. Gradual adoption, Codex Amiatinus, Alcuin's and Theodulf's distinct Carolingian programs, Paris Bibles, and medieval *correctoria*.
9. The Gutenberg Bible, print stabilization, Trent's 1546 decree, and the Sixtine and Clementine editions.
10. The Oxford New Testament, Roman/Benedictine Old Testament, Stuttgart hand edition, and continuing Vetus Latina project.
11. The origin, book-specific method, 1979 promulgation, and 1986 second typical edition of the *Nova Vulgata*.
12. Language, liturgical memory, study infrastructure, vernacular mediation, and cultural reception, with causal claims bounded.
13. A claim audit, evidence-class timeline, source-function bibliography, source audit, and evidence map.

## Terminological controls

- **Old Latin / *Vetus Latina*:** the modern collective designation for Latin biblical forms earlier than, or independent of, the Vulgate revisions; not one ancient uniform edition.
- **Vulgate:** a later proper title for the Latin biblical tradition associated principally but not wholly with Jerome. In Jerome's period *editio vulgata* could instead name a common Septuagintal form.
- **Jerome's Gospel revision:** the four Gospels corrected from existing Latin against Greek in 382–384; not a new-from-scratch whole New Testament.
- ***Hebraica veritas*:** Jerome's appeal to Hebrew as governing for many Old Testament questions; not a claim that he used Hebrew exclusively or possessed a modern critical edition.
- **Gallican Psalter:** the Greek/Hexaplaric-based Psalter associated with Jerome that became the dominant medieval Vulgate Psalter; not his direct Hebrew Psalter.
- ***Psalterium iuxta Hebraeos*:** Jerome's new Hebrew-based Psalter; historically Vulgate-related but not the ordinary medieval liturgical Psalter.
- **Roman Psalter:** a light Old Latin revision traditionally attributed to Jerome; the attribution remains disputed.
- **Authentic:** at Trent, a juridical status for the old Vulgate among Latin editions in specified public uses; not a synonym for text-critically pristine or inspired in every transmitted detail.
- **Critical edition:** an editorial reconstruction from selected witnesses with an apparatus; not a manuscript, average medieval text, or act of promulgation.
- **Typical edition:** the normative edition for a defined ecclesial use; not a description of antiquity or critical method.
- ***Nova Vulgata*:** the modern official Latin revision promulgated in 1979 and reissued as a second typical edition in 1986; not simply the Clementine or Stuttgart text.

## Source boundary and evidence hierarchy

The publication distinguishes four evidence classes:

1. **Contemporary or near-contemporary witnesses:** Jerome's prefaces and correspondence, Augustine's correspondence, and material manuscripts used only at their own dates.
2. **Ancient and medieval reception:** Augustine's treatises, complete pandects, Carolingian revisions, Paris Bible forms, glosses, and correction programs.
3. **Institutional acts and editorial records:** Trent's decrees, the Clementine preface, papal and conciliar acts, official *Nova Vulgata* prefaces and *Praenotanda*, and catalog records.
4. **Modern reconstruction:** critical editions and specialist scholarship used to assess authorship, source relation, chronology, manuscript families, and editorial purpose.

A participant's preface establishes what the participant claimed and the purpose the text presents; it is not treated as a neutral administrative file. A manuscript establishes its own contents and readings; it does not automatically reproduce an author's lost exemplar. An official act establishes the status it expressly confers; it does not by itself reconstruct an earlier text. Publisher abstracts and catalog records support only the facts and summaries actually visible there.

## Material uncertainties preserved

- The surviving Old Latin evidence is incomplete and uneven, and no single first Latin translation can be reconstructed for the whole Bible.
- Jerome's Gospel preface is secure evidence for the four Gospels but not a surviving formal commission instrument for a whole Bible.
- The reviser or revisers of Acts, the Pauline and Catholic Epistles, and Revelation remain unnamed. More specific Roman, Pelagian, or other social attributions are hypotheses.
- The Greek manuscripts available to Jerome in Rome cannot be identified with one extant codex.
- The chronology of Jerome's Greek-based Old Testament revisions and the loss of most revised books prevent a seamless reconstruction of his transition to Hebrew-based translation.
- Jerome's authorship of the Roman Psalter is disputed; the Gallican and Hebrew Psalters answer different source and reception questions.
- The date range about 390–405 does not provide an exact production date for every Hebrew-based book or one completion day for a Vulgate Bible.
- Jerome's Hebrew sources were late-antique and principally proto-Masoretic, but exact exemplars do not survive. His use of Greek versions, Old Latin forms, teachers, and commentary varies by book and passage.
- The Augustine–Jerome letters are rhetorically shaped elite correspondence. The reported Jonah congregation cannot quantify Latin Christian opinion.
- Tobit's preface explicitly reports a bilingual interpreter and dictation; Judith's reports rapid sense-for-sense translation from “Chaldean” but no interpreter. The precise source text and allocation of work remain unrecoverable in both cases.
- Codex Amiatinus is about three centuries later than Jerome and was assembled book by book; it is not an autograph or a complete window onto a fifth-century archetype.
- Alcuin's and Theodulf's programs differed, and neither surviving evidence nor wide diffusion proves one exclusive Carolingian official text.
- “The medieval Vulgate” describes a common scriptural culture but not one invariant manuscript reading.
- The exact mix of typographical, textual, political, and reputational reasons in the replacement of the Sixtine printing remains debated; the publication relies principally on the secure 1590/1592 sequence.
- The Clementine, critical historical editions, and *Nova Vulgata* answer different editorial and institutional questions. Agreement or disagreement among them cannot be ranked without naming the question.
