## Governing question and thesis

The account asks how a Jewish Greek Torah produced in Ptolemaic Egypt became the name-bearing center of a wider, book-by-book Greek scriptural corpus; how translation, Hebrew textual plurality, revision, and scribal transmission account for differences; and how Jewish and Christian communities successively authorized, revised, collected, and edited those texts.

The governing thesis is that “Septuagint” does not name one translation of one bound Bible by one committee. It names, according to context, the Greek Torah, a book-specific Old Greek translation, a wider collection of translations and Greek compositions, a later Christian manuscript corpus, or a modern editorial reconstruction. The history must therefore be told as several linked acts rather than one event.

## Included coverage

1. The political, linguistic, material, and Jewish setting of Ptolemaic Alexandria.
2. The *Letter of Aristeas* as the earliest extant continuous origin narrative, including its exact Torah scope, collaborative procedure, communal acceptance, and historiographical limits.
3. Philo, Josephus, Irenaeus, and Augustine as dated witnesses to changing Jewish and Christian reception of the origin story.
4. Translation technique and the distinction among rendering, source-text difference, literary edition, revision, and later transmission.
5. Expansion beyond Torah, anchored by the Greek prologue to Sirach and including translated books, Greek compositions, additions, and alternative literary forms.
6. Book-level case studies in Samuel, Jeremiah, Job, and Daniel.
7. Continued Jewish Greek use and revision, including 8ḤevXIIgr, kaige, Aquila, Symmachus, the Theodotion problem, and nonuniform rabbinic memory.
8. New Testament and early Christian use, stated without positing one uniform apostolic pandect.
9. Origen's comparative work and the later great codices Vaticanus, Sinaiticus, and Alexandrinus.
10. The distinction among diplomatic editions, semi-critical handbooks, book-specific critical editions, and modern translations.
11. A misconception audit, dated evidence-class timeline, source-function bibliography, source audit, and evidence map.

## Excluded or bounded coverage

- No project translation of biblical, pseudepigraphal, patristic, or rabbinic text is created.
- No verse-by-verse textual commentary or apparatus is attempted.
- No complete history of every Septuagint book, daughter version, lectionary, patristic citation, or modern vernacular translation is attempted.
- No theological judgment is made about inspiration, inerrancy, the authority of a translation, or the canon of any communion.
- No modern Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, or Jewish table of contents is projected backward as an Alexandrian Jewish canon.
- No critical edition is selected as “the original Septuagint”; editorial aims are distinguished.
- No precise percentage is assigned to New Testament quotations that “come from the Septuagint,” because citation, adaptation, memory, and textual form vary by passage.
- No manuscript image, facsimile, map, or reconstruction diagram is incorporated.

## Terminological controls

- **Septuagint / LXX:** the Greek Torah when origins are in view; the wider Greek scriptural corpus only when that broader use is explicit.
- **Old Greek (OG):** the earliest recoverable Greek form of a particular translation unit, not an extant autograph.
- **Vorlage / source text:** the Hebrew or Aramaic text form from which a translator worked; not automatically the medieval Masoretic Text.
- **Masoretic Text (MT):** the Hebrew consonantal tradition with medieval vocalization and notes represented in standard editions; a central witness, not every translator's exemplar.
- **Revision / recension:** demonstrated deliberate alteration of an existing Greek form; a label that must not be inferred from one variant.
- **Great codex:** a fourth- or fifth-century Christian parchment Bible; not an inventory of third-century Alexandrian Judaism.
- **Canon:** used only for a named community, witness, author, or institutional judgment. Material inclusion and ecclesial authority are not assumed to be identical.

## Source boundary and evidence hierarchy

The publication distinguishes four evidence classes:

1. **Artifacts and near-contemporary evidence:** Greek biblical papyri and scrolls, manuscript catalog records, and the Greek prologue to Sirach.
2. **Ancient narrative witnesses:** Aristeas, Philo, Josephus, and patristic accounts, each used at the author's date and for the author's purpose.
3. **Reception and institutional memory:** early Christian defenses, rabbinic traditions, codex collection, and later ecclesial use.
4. **Modern reconstruction:** critical editions and professional scholarship used to identify translation technique, textual layers, manuscript relationships, and live disagreements.

An ancient author's agreement with an earlier narrative is not counted as independent corroboration where literary dependence is clear. An official manuscript or edition project page establishes artifact identity or project status, not the originating translation event. Publisher abstracts were used only for claims they expressly support; restricted full chapters were not represented as fully inspected.

## Material uncertainties preserved

- The exact year, patron, institutional setting, translators, number of hands, and working procedure of the Greek Torah are unrecoverable.
- Aristeas's precise royal commission, Demetrius's role, Jerusalem master scroll, personnel, speeches, seventy-two-day period, and administrative mechanism are not independently verified.
- The balance among liturgical, educational, civic/legal, apologetic, and royal-library motives remains disputed.
- Dates and places for most post-Pentateuchal translations remain broad and book-specific.
- A Greek--Hebrew difference cannot be assigned to a different source text without local evidence.
- The boundaries of kaige revision and its relationship to “Theodotion” remain debated.
- The historical identities and affiliations of Aquila and Symmachus cannot be reconstructed securely from the surviving version fragments alone.
- The precise configuration, extent, purpose, and relation of Origen's Hexapla and Tetrapla remain disputed because the complete works are lost.
- No checked witness establishes a universal Jewish rejection of Greek Scripture at “Jamnia” or a fixed Alexandrian Jewish canon matching a modern printed Septuagint.
- The great codices are later Christian collections and cannot settle the contents of earlier Jewish libraries by themselves.
