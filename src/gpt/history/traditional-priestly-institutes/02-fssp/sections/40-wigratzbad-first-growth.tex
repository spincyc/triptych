\section{Wigratzbad and the First Generation}

Canonical erection would have remained fragile without a formation house. The FSSP's Wigratzbad retrospective says that in August 1988 Bishop Josef Stimpfle of Augsburg indicated that he would receive a canonical establishment at the Bavarian pilgrimage center. An Augsburg diocesan history by former diocesan legal counsel Josef Binder independently confirms episcopal approval and says that seminary operations began in the Pilgerheim St.~Josef and other local buildings. It also records a Holy-See-approved agreement with the local church foundation for shared use of the church and a diocesan company created later in 1988 to provide lodging, meals, classrooms, and common rooms. The FSSP says thirty-one seminarians began studies in November 1988, while a separate anniversary retrospective says the first seminary opened in autumn 1989. The later diocesan account corroborates the borrowed-premises stage but neither it nor an original act or register resolves whether ``opened'' denotes a later formal stage. Josef Bisig served simultaneously as superior general and rector, and the Wigratzbad account says instruction was divided between French- and German-language groups. Binder's account further says that the Fraternity occupied its new seminary in autumn 2000 on land held under a diocesan hereditary building right.

The choice did several kinds of work. Geographically, Wigratzbad connected the German, French, Swiss, and Austrian circles from which the founding cohort emerged. Institutionally, it gave the new society a stable house where common life and curriculum could be distinguished from the SSPX formation the founders had left. Ecclesially, it made the cooperation of a diocesan bishop visible at the beginning rather than treating local authority as an obstacle to be bypassed.

The first years also showed the dependence of the society on bishops outside its membership. The FSSP has no bishops of its own. Ordinations require bishops acting within the Church's ordinary structures. Visits by Cardinals Augustin Mayer, Joseph Ratzinger, Édouard Gagnon, and Alfons Stickler, as reported in the seminary history, gave the young institution public Roman and episcopal connections; those visits should not be inflated into a separate juridical approval each time.

\subsection{From borrowed rooms to a purpose-built seminary}

The seminary initially occupied facilities at the pilgrimage center. Growing enrollment led to a neighboring purpose-built complex, opened for the autumn term in 2000 and blessed by Cardinal Darío Castrillón Hoyos on 2 December. The new building coincided with a serious governance dispute, a reminder that physical expansion and institutional peace are different measures.

Formation transmitted more than rubrics. The constitutions place the Mass at the center of priestly spirituality, name Thomistic philosophical and theological formation, and connect fidelity to the pope with the society's foundation. The seminaries had to teach future priests how older liturgical and disciplinary forms could operate within postconciliar dioceses. That involved ceremonial competence, ordinary clerical law, pastoral practice, language, and a corporate memory of 1988.

\subsection{A European network, not one motherhouse alone}

French and German-speaking apostolates began with the foundation and became durable districts. Local forms differed: chaplaincies and entrusted churches, schools and catechesis, pilgrimages and publications. Their unity came from personnel and formation under the general government, not identical diocesan arrangements.

Early growth also carried a structural risk. A small founding generation could make personal trust do the work later required of constitutions, chapters, directories, and differentiated offices. As numbers and countries multiplied, decisions about rectors, superiors, curriculum, and liturgical discipline could no longer remain informal. The crisis around the turn of the millennium was thus not an interruption of growth from outside; it was partly a test of whether rapid growth had produced mature government.
