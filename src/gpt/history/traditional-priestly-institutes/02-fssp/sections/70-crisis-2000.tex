\section{The Crisis of 1999--2000}

Rapid growth did not settle the relationship between corporate specificity and the common liturgical law. On 3 July 1999 the Congregation for Divine Worship and the Discipline of the Sacraments issued official responses under Prot. n. 1411/99. The text is generic: it speaks of a priest who is a member of an institute enjoying the faculty to use the 1962 Missal. It never names the FSSP.

The first response said such a priest could use the Missal promulgated by Paul VI and, when celebrating for a community using the reformed Roman books, should use those books. The second said superiors of such institutes could not prohibit that use in those circumstances. The third said the priest could concelebrate according to the current order and could not be forbidden to do so by a superior or local ordinary. It commended free concelebration, especially at the Holy Thursday Mass presided over by the diocesan bishop; in a distinct sentence, while acknowledging the individual priest's faculty to celebrate separately under \emph{Sacrosanctum Concilium} 57, it said the communion sign of concelebration should not be omitted at the Chrism Mass without grave reasons.

\subsection{From a general answer to an internal conflict}

FSSP sources reported a special assembly near Rome in February 2000 amid disagreements concentrated especially in the French district. A contemporary statement rejected rumors of suppression or compulsory routine celebration of the reformed Mass and said the ordinary General Chapter would proceed. It acknowledged substantive and personal divisions but presented the meeting as a constructive exchange.

At the General Chapter in July, a letter dated 29 June from Cardinal Darío Castrillón Hoyos, president of the Pontifical Commission \emph{Ecclesia Dei}, intervened directly. A later documentary compilation reproduces a translated text in which the cardinal describes a severe crisis, limits superior-general service to two successive six-year terms, names Arnaud Devillers for six years rather than allowing a contested election, directs the selection of new seminary rectors, affirms that FSSP priests normally celebrate with the older books, says they may choose the reformed books in particular infrequent cases, and says no priest is obliged to exercise that possibility.

The Chapter's reproduced response expressed surprise and concern, announced administrative recourse, accepted Devillers within the imposed framework, elected his council, and asked that the founding specificity be preserved. A contemporary North American FSSP release said Devillers was installed on 6 July and credited his preceding leadership with substantial North American growth.

The source boundary is material. The checked set includes the official 1999 \emph{Responsa}; an FSSP statement from February 2000 preserved on a third-party archive; a later Una Voce compilation reproducing the cardinal's letter, FSSP release, and Chapter response; and Pietras's 2026 open-access canonical study, which cites the correspondence and recourse. It does not include authenticated originals from the PCED file or a public decision on the recourse. The account can establish an intervention, appointment, contested reading, and recourse without pretending to reconstruct the entire file.

\subsection{What the dispute was not}

It was not a decree ordering every FSSP priest to celebrate the reformed Mass on a schedule. Even the translated intervention says the ordinary practice remained the older books and no one was forced to use the other faculty. Nor was it simply proof that the FSSP could prohibit any member from ever using the reformed books: the Roman texts denied that inference.

The deeper issue was corporate coherence. Those favoring exclusivity argued that variation at the heart of common spirituality could fragment the young society. The Roman intervention argued that a granted older-book use did not extinguish the individual priest's relation to common Roman liturgical law and that communion with the diocesan presbyterate had visible expressions. Both positions appealed to ecclesial goods; authority resolved the immediate government question without eliminating the long-term legal tension.
