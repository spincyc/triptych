\section{The Object Is a Canonically Embedded Institution}

\historythesis{The Priestly Fraternity of Saint Peter is best understood neither as a canonically approved continuation of the Society of Saint Pius X nor as a mere provider of an older liturgy. It arose from a precise refusal of the unauthorized episcopal consecrations of 1988 and received a new juridical identity from the Holy See. Its history is the effort to make two commitments durable at once: communion and obedience within the Church's ordinary structures, and a corporate priestly life centered on the Roman liturgical books in force in 1962. Its growth depended on seminaries, common life, general government, and invitations from diocesan bishops. Its recurrent crises arose where universal liturgical discipline, local episcopal authority, Roman supervision, and the Fraternity's approved patrimony met.}

The object is therefore an institution, not a preference. The FSSP trains and incardinates clergy, erects houses with episcopal consent, assigns members, conducts apostolates entrusted by bishops, and governs itself under constitutions approved by the Holy See. Its older liturgical use is at the center of that life, but it is not the whole canonical form. A society of apostolic life is constituted by persons, mission, common life, proper law, and government; a faculty to use particular books does not by itself create any of those things.

The opposite reduction is equally misleading. The older books are not an incidental aesthetic within the FSSP's self-understanding. The 1988 erection decree joined priestly sanctification and pastoral ministry to the liturgical and disciplinary traditions recalled in \emph{Ecclesia Dei adflicta}. The constitutions later named faithful observance of those traditions as the particular means by which the Fraternity pursues its purpose. Subsequent disputes over the reformed Missal and the legislation of 2021 consequently touched institutional identity, even though no checked public act in those episodes suppressed the Fraternity.

\subsection{Question, range, and terminal date}

This account asks how a group leaving the SSPX in July 1988 acquired pontifical-right status within three months; how its two seminaries and diocesan apostolates produced a transnational clerical body; how its proper law developed; why a dispute about liturgical freedom and government became acute in 1999--2000; and how the FSSP navigated the different regimes of \emph{Ecclesia Dei}, \emph{Summorum Pontificum}, and \emph{Traditionis custodes}. It ends with the Fraternity's structure, statistics, visitation, and papal contacts as publicly documented through 16 July 2026.

The detailed prehistory of Archbishop Marcel Lefebvre, the SSPX, the 1975--1976 conflict, the 5 May 1988 protocol, and the 30 June consecrations belongs to TPI-01. This volume restates only the branch point necessary to understand the FSSP, then tests the FSSP's own history against its separate declaration of intention, act of foundation, Roman declaration, liturgical decree, and erection decree.

The reader-facing label ``Traditional Priestly Institutes'' is bibliographic, not canonical. The FSSP is, at the cutoff, a clerical society of apostolic life of pontifical right. The SSPX does not share that status. The Institute of Christ the King Sovereign Priest has its own origins and juridical history, which are not reconstructed here.

Several absences shape the account. The checked set did not include the complete original constitutions, every intervening approval, the official file of the 2000 administrative recourse, a Holy See publication of the 2022 decree independent of the copy released by the FSSP, or a public final report from the apostolic visitation announced in 2024. The publication does not fill those gaps with rumor.

\subsection{A bounded canonical and liturgical module}

Current canonical statements use the Latin Church's 1983 Code and public special acts through the cutoff. Liturgical change is described historically under the law then in force. ``Pontifical right,'' ``proper law,'' ``faculty,'' ``diocesan consent,'' ``incardination,'' and ``personal parish'' retain distinct meanings. Nothing here determines a private dispute, advises a bishop or member, or rules on the legal effect of an unpublished act.

It does not remove episcopal authority over public worship, care of souls, and apostolic works, or eliminate the need for written consent to erect a house.}
