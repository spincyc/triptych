\section{Mouila: Diocesan Erection and Missionary Field}

Institutional accounts date the Institute's canonical erection to 1 September 1990 in the Diocese of Mouila, Gabon. They identify Bishop Cyriaque Siméon Obamba as the receiving bishop, say he invited the young community to missionary work, and report that he appointed Wach vicar general. The 2008 Roman decree looks back to Wach and Mora as founders and assumes an existing Institute, but it does not reproduce the 1990 diocesan instrument.

The absence of that original controls the wording. The date, bishop, diocesan-right status, and appointment are strong institutional memory repeated across the Institute's language sites. They should not be expanded into unseen clauses about incorporation, constitutions, property, incardination, or an automatic later ``transfer'' to Florence. Nor should episcopal reception in Gabon be treated as a convenience while the true foundation supposedly occurred only in Europe. On the Institute's own account, Mouila supplied the first canonical home.

The location shaped more than paperwork. Gabon gave the developing community pastoral and missionary work while candidates and government were taking form. Institute histories report continued missions, churches, schools, catechesis, and educational activity there. Exact building totals and claims about public esteem appear in institutional publicity and are not independently audited in this study. The durable point is that African ministry remained part of the Institute after its central formation work moved to Tuscany.

\subsection{A foundation across jurisdictions}

A new priestly society needs more than candidates. It needs competent ecclesiastical authority, a rule, formation, support, and places of ministry. Mouila and Gricigliano answered different parts of that problem. The former gave an episcopal and missionary setting; the latter became the international seminary and general center. Institutional histories sometimes narrate the move rapidly, but canonically a house in Florence required the Archbishop of Florence's action even if the society already existed in Gabon.

The checked sources say Cardinal Augustin Mayer helped locate Gricigliano and Cardinal Silvano Piovanelli received the motherhouse and seminary in the Archdiocese of Florence. They do not publish the original diocesan decree or establish that Mouila's authority vanished at the moment the Italian house opened. Until pontifical-right erection in 2008, the Institute's proper line of dependency and the legal relation among its houses would have been governed by the diocesan act, constitutions, universal law, and permissions not fully public here.

\subsection{Mission as an institutional corrective}

The African origin complicates a purely European story of cultural preservation. The Institute describes its particular aim as missionary: spreading Christ's reign in all dimensions of life. In Gabon that claim met ordinary pastoral realities of churches, schools, formation, and sacramental care. The later recovery of historic urban churches in Europe and North America was another form of the same outward-facing program, not the whole mission.

Missionary self-description does not make every policy successful or every reported result independently verified. It does show why the Institute's history cannot be reduced to a reaction against liturgical change. The emerging society sought to form priests and conduct apostolate under bishops; its older worship, educational culture, and common life were presented as instruments of that purpose.
