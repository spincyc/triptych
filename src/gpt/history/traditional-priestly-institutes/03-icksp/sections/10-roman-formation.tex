\section{Roman Formation and the Ordination of 1979}

The Institute's public histories begin with two French priests: Gilles Wach and Philippe Mora. They describe formation associated with Cardinal Giuseppe Siri and the seminary of Genoa, followed by study and work in Rome. That memory locates the founders within an episcopal and Roman network rather than within the SSPX seminaries treated in TPI-01. It also helps explain the later language of \emph{Romanità}: fidelity to the Apostolic See, universal ecclesial culture, and liturgical and intellectual formation were presented as one program.

The most securely dated early event is the priestly ordination celebrated by John Paul II in Saint Peter's Basilica on 24 June 1979. The pope's homily addresses the ordinands collectively and emphasizes formation in dioceses, seminaries, and Roman colleges. His Angelus later that day records eighty-eight ordinands from fifteen countries. Neither Vatican text names Wach or Mora. The Institute identifies the two men among that cohort and preserves photographs and retrospective accounts; the Holy See sources independently confirm the ceremony and its scale, not the roster.

This distinction matters because later institutional histories sometimes compress different levels of evidence into one sentence. ``Ordained by John Paul II on 24 June 1979'' is well supported by the Institute's identification paired with the Vatican event record. ``Named by the Vatican as members of the group'' would be false on the checked evidence. Historical confidence can be high without claiming that one source proves every component.

\subsection{From individual priests to a formation project}

Institutional accounts say Wach later worked in Rome with Cardinal Silvio Oddi while Mora served in a Roman context, and that young men approached them seeking traditional priestly formation. They name several cardinals and churchmen as advisers or encouragers. Such lists are evidence of how the Institute remembers a Roman circle; without correspondence or mandates they do not prove that each adviser approved every later constitutional feature.

By the later 1980s, according to the Institute, the idea had moved from informal accompaniment toward a community for priestly formation. The evidence does not identify a single founding day in Rome. It shows a process: individual ministry, requests from candidates, association with an older French priestly network, and search for a bishop willing to receive a new work.

That process differs sharply from both preceding series volumes. The ICKSP did not publicly organize around the 30 June 1988 consecrations, and its founding record does not present recognition as a reconciliation of former SSPX members. The older liturgy, Roman loyalty, formation, and missionary ambition converged independently. Similar later apostolates do not establish a shared institutional genealogy.
