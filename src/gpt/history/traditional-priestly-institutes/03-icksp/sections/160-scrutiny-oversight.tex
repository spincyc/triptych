\section{Public Scrutiny and Unresolved Oversight}

Institutional history includes material public reporting without converting journalism into adjudication. Two distinct bodies of reporting require treatment: a 2023 French series about the male Institute and a 2026 report centered on former candidates of the Sister Adorers. They concern different subjects, sources, and alleged facts. Neither checked record contains a public final Holy See judgment.

\subsection{The 2023 French reporting and Wach's response}

In September 2023 \emph{Le Parisien} published a four-part investigation based on statements from current and former members and other interviewees. It alleged concerns involving centralized governance, expenditure and lifestyle, ecclesial culture, and treatment or formation of priests. The series reported that French ecclesiastical officials had raised concerns and sought Roman scrutiny. These are attributed allegations and reported requests, not findings of a tribunal or visitation.

Wach had responded directly to the journalist's questions in a public letter dated 15 June 2023. He criticized the inquiry as prejudged and insufficiently careful. He pointed to the Institute's Roman and episcopal relationships, defended its seminary formation, denied that departure or dismissal itself proved humiliating treatment, and emphasized missions, schools, charitable works, and continued invitations from bishops. That letter is a right of reply and evidence of the Institute's position; it does not independently disprove each allegation.

No checked public Holy See mandate, report, sanction, or exoneration arising from the 2023 coverage was located through the cutoff. The responsible conclusion is unresolved public scrutiny, not guilt by headline or vindication by institutional growth.

\subsection{The 2026 former-Sister accounts}

In March 2026 \emph{The Pillar} reported accounts from women formerly in formation with the Sister Adorers. They alleged psychologically and spiritually harmful formation, intimidation, isolation, unhealthy obedience practices, inadequate conditions, and governance problems. The reporting also described links with ICKSP priests and government. These claims concern the separate women's group, whose last located controlling act erected it as a public association in 2008, and alleged relationships around it; they must not be silently transferred to every male member.

The Sisters' superior disputed the characterization. She said the former candidates struggled to adapt, described their concerns as exaggerations or misunderstandings, said she reviewed houses, and reported requesting an ICKSP-led visitation whose unpublished results she called positive. The article reported that former candidates said officials of the consecrated-life dicastery told them an independent visitation was planned. It also said it was unclear whether one had begun.

No public Vatican mandate, announcement, start notice, report, or outcome was located. This study therefore does not state that a Vatican investigation was underway as established fact. It records that former candidates reported being told of a plan, that the superior described a separate internal review, and that independent scrutiny remained publicly unresolved.

\subsection{Why the 2014 visitation cannot settle later claims}

The 2014 ordinary visitation concerned the male Institute at an earlier date and under a mandate not public here. It cannot be used as a blanket answer to allegations published nine or twelve years later, especially allegations about a distinct association. The confidential review described by the Sisters' superior is likewise not independently testable from the public record.

The Institute's Salesian ideal calls for truth in charity. Historical method imposes a parallel discipline: precise attribution, right of reply, protection of distinct juridical identities, and refusal to invent an official outcome. Competent authorities, not this publication, determine substantiated facts and remedies.
