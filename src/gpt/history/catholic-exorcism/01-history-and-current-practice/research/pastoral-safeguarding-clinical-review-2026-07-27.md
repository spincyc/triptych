## Findings

The study is unusually strong at preventing foreseeable misuse. It is
non-operational: it withholds ritual prayers, alleged signs, interview
prompts, commands, names, sequences, and reaction tests. It repeatedly directs
immediate danger to local emergency or protective services, preserves
prescribed treatment and competent medical and mental-health care, and directs
Catholic pastoral requests to accountable parish, diocesan, or eparchial
authority rather than online or self-authorized ministry.

The clinical boundary is sound. The WHO classification is used only to
identify differential-assessment domains; it is not treated as proof for or
against a preternatural cause. The study neither asks clinicians to certify a
metaphysical judgment nor allows religious belief to diagnose epilepsy,
psychosis, dissociation, delirium, intoxication, trauma, or another condition.
Culture is treated as relevant to understanding distress without making a
culturally framed experience either pathology or proof of possession.

The safeguarding account preserves the person's dignity and ordinary legal
protections. It expressly rejects restraint, confinement, deprivation,
medication withdrawal, pain, humiliation, prolonged interrogation, secrecy
that defeats reporting duties, monetized fear, publicity, isolation, and the
spiritualization of abuse. It addresses minors, disability, trauma, sexual
orientation, domestic violence, trafficking, self-harm, coercive family
pressure, confidentiality, capacity, and jurisdiction-specific reporting.
Named cases and prevalence claims are excluded rather than sensationalized.

The pastoral conclusion is proportionate and usable without becoming
individual medical or legal advice. It separates attentive listening from
ratifying an interpretation, makes ordinary care continue regardless of the
extraordinary hypothesis, and makes Christological care of the person—not
attention to alleged phenomena—the governing measure.
