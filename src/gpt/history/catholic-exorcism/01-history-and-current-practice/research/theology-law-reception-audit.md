## Latin and Eastern law

- **Latin Church:** 1983 CIC cc. 1, 134 §§1–3, and 1166–1172, especially c. 1172, authentic Latin, governing the Latin Church as of 2026-07-27. Canon 134 §§1–2 includes vicars general and episcopal vicars in the generic term *local ordinary* used by c. 1172; §3 separately governs canons that name the diocesan bishop, excluding those vicars absent a special mandate. This category-level conclusion does not decide territorial or personal competence, the scope, duration, or revocation of a concrete grant, or particular law. The exact official Latin and English c. 134 deliveries were re-fetched byte-identically on 2026-07-27; the published authentic-interpretations compilation has no entry for cc. 134 or 1172, a bounded register result rather than a comprehensive amendment inventory. *De Exorcismis et Supplicationibus Quibusdam*, decree dated 22 November 1998, Latin typical edition issued 1999, and emended reprint 2004. The exact Latin of the 22 October 2021 c. 838 implementing decree, DESQ praenotanda 37(a)–(b), controls conference competence for translations and adaptations; it does not create a new DESQ edition or alter c. 1172. CDF letter to ordinaries on exorcism (29 September 1985), official Latin and *AAS* locus controlling; CDF, *Instruction on Prayers for Healing* (14 September 2000), art. 8.
- **1972 office boundary:** Paul VI's *Ministeria quaedam* (15 August 1972;
  effective 1 January 1973), introduction and I–IV, controls the transition
  from former minor orders to ministries. Lector and acolyte were retained
  throughout the Latin Church; porter, exorcist, and catechist were examples
  of other ministries an episcopal conference could ask the Apostolic See to
  institute. The act neither establishes a universal current exorcist
  ministry nor confers c. 1172 authorization.
- **United States:** USCCB, “Exorcism,” Secretariat of Divine Worship, exact official web state accessed 2026-07-26. It controls only the identified U.S. implementation and public explanation.
- **Eastern Catholic boundary:** CCEO cc. 1–2 control scope and interpretive context. They establish that the Eastern Code governs all and only the Eastern Catholic Churches unless it expressly provides otherwise concerning relations with the Latin Church; they do not transplant CIC c. 1172. No Eastern particular law or liturgical book was surveyed, and no Eastern major-exorcism rule is stated.
- **Orthodox boundary:** no Orthodox law or liturgical book controls a retained publication claim. The study makes no statement about present Orthodox authorization or practice.

No current canon, conference page, or liturgical book is represented as universal beyond its jurisdiction. The sections provide no formulas, signs, sequence, commands, names, or instructions.

## Medical and safeguarding evidence rule

Medical facts appear only where natural/preternatural classification intersects a living person's safety and differential assessment. The sections make no prevalence claim, no diagnostic mapping, and no assertion that clinical exclusion proves a preternatural cause.

The operative ecclesial source is the exact dated USCCB page state: it calls
for thorough medical and psychological or psychiatric evaluation and consent
if possible. It is conference guidance for the United States, not a clinical
standard and not universal legislation. The American Psychiatric Association's
public DSM-5-TR assessment-measures page was inspected for the limited
professional proposition that its Cultural Formulation Interview explores
culture's effect on clinical presentation and care and that assessment
measures do not alone establish diagnosis. The host refused an exact command-
line acquisition during this pass, so that page remains an authenticated web
lead rather than a normalized artifact and supports no quotation.

The United Kingdom Department for Education's 2012 *National action plan to
tackle child abuse linked to faith or belief* was checked through its exact
GOV.UK publication-page state, hashed, and normalized on 2026-07-26. It
establishes an England-specific safeguarding concern involving accusations of
witchcraft or spirit possession and identifies abuse and exploitation as the
problem; it is not evidence of Catholic prevalence or universal law. The abuse
discussion is therefore a cross-setting safeguarding synthesis, not a catalog
of adjudicated Catholic cases. No named victim or sensational case is used.
Its evidentiary ceiling is conduct: violence, restraint, deprivation, sexual
abuse, coercion, financial exploitation, concealment, and treatment delay are
harmful regardless of the perpetrator's causal belief.

| Objection family | Strongest retained claim | Catholic answer / concession |

| Secular-clinical / naturalist | Observable signs are culturally mediated and naturally ambiguous; suggestion, misdiagnosis, and delayed treatment produce harm; no controlled diagnostic assay identifies spirits | Concedes ambiguity and safeguarding burden; clinical method does not decide all metaphysics, but metaphysical possibility never establishes a case. |

| Catholic traditionalist | Revised rite and clinical caution allegedly weaken the 1614 tradition | Historical comparison is legitimate; anecdotal preference is not controlled efficacy evidence, and current authority cannot be replaced by private ritual judgment. |
| Feminist, disability, queer, and trauma-informed | Demon language has policed bodies, displaced perpetrator responsibility, and stigmatized difference | Identity, disability, trauma, or dissent is not evidence; safeguarding, reporting, accommodations, consent, and professional care test Catholic anthropology in practice. |
| Popular realist | Institutional caution is functional unbelief and abandons sufferers | Caution presupposes possibility while protecting persons; ordinary pastoral and clinical care is not disbelief. |

Named traditions are internally diverse. The matrix groups arguments rather than attributing one view to every member.
