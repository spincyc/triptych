\newcommand{\ModulePractice}{%
  \SubmoduleExtension
    {Personal and reflexive pronouns}
    {A reference chain separates the grammatical person of a pronoun from the discourse person to whom it points.}
    {For each personal or reflexive example in the lesson, draw an arrow to its referent and label person, number, case, and direct or indirect reflexive status.}
    {State why third-person reflexive \Latin{sui/sibi/se} does not by itself name gender.}
    {Each arrow must identify the clause's controlling subject or other referent and the pronoun's case function. The third-person reflexive paradigm refers back to the relevant subject without distinct gender forms; context supplies the referent.}
  \SubmoduleExtension
    {\Latin{Is, ea, id} and \Latin{hic, hæc, hoc}}
    {Demonstrative morphology and discourse distance are different questions that must both be answered.}
    {Build parallel nominative and accusative cards for the two lesson paradigms, then annotate one lesson example with morphology, antecedent, and discourse role.}
    {Explain why matching gender and number does not yet prove the antecedent.}
    {The cards must preserve the irregular pronominal genitive and dative where taught and the correct neuters. A possible antecedent must match gender and number, but syntax, discourse, and sense must also establish the actual reference.}
  \SubmoduleExtension
    {The relative pronoun}
    {A two-link antecedent chain prevents antecedent agreement from being confused with internal case.}
    {For every relative example in the lesson, draw one arrow from antecedent to gender/number and a second from the relative clause's verb or governor to case/function.}
    {Recite the two-axis rule and give one reason the antecedent's case need not match the relative's case.}
    {The first axis copies gender and number from the antecedent; the second assigns case from the relative pronoun's own clause. The clauses give the two words independent functions, so their cases may differ.}
  \SubmoduleExtension
    {Core preposition government}
    {A two-case preposition map joins spatial relation to the exact case selected in the lesson.}
    {Place each core preposition into accusative, ablative, or two-case columns. For each two-case item, add one motion-oriented and one location-oriented lesson use without inventing a universal English shortcut.}
    {State the government of \Latin{in} and \Latin{sub} in the two ordinary spatial patterns.}
    {The map must assign each one-case preposition correctly. In ordinary spatial uses, \Latin{in/sub} with accusative expresses motion toward or under, while the ablative expresses location; the actual construction and context remain controlling.}
  \SubmoduleExtension
    {Handwritten study and controlled composition}
    {A cumulative transformation reveals whether reference, agreement, and government remain coherent together.}
    {Perform the lesson's gender-and-number transformations for \Latin{per eum, cum eo, in eo}, then add arrows from every pronoun to its referent and from every preposition to its governed case.}
    {Name one change caused by reference agreement and one caused by preposition government.}
    {All transformed pronouns must match their intended referent in gender and number while taking the case required by their preposition. The explanation must keep those two sources of form distinct.}
