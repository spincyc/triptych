\chapter{Start Here}

This curriculum is organized as a course guide, a complete reference grammar,
46 independently printable learning modules, and four stage-assessment
sequences. This guide owns the instructions shared by all of them. Read it once
before EL-I.1; later packets assume its method and previously taught terms
instead of repeating them.

Begin with this guide. Keep the Reference Grammar available for wider lookup,
then work through module IDs EL-I.1 through EL-A8 in order. A normal module is
planned as two focused sessions and prints two contrasting worksheet variants.
Correct them in another color and use the two omitted variants only when the
error record identifies a persistent family or a delayed-recall need.

\section{How every module flows}

After a compact cover and contents, every learning packet proceeds in the same
order:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Lesson}: new definitions and the governing idea, followed by
        worked examples and the unit's own applications;
  \item \textbf{Practice}: transfer tasks and retrieval checks keyed to the
        lesson's internal submodules;
  \item \textbf{Worksheets}: two selected, contrasting forms completed before
        any solution is opened;
  \item \textbf{Assessment}: the module's mastery criterion, delayed-work or
        stage-edge instruction where applicable, and the work/error record;
  \item \textbf{Answer Key}: practice models and solutions from the same
        authoritative sources as the learner forms; and
  \item \textbf{Scope and Sources}: only the module's local boundary and the
        course references and generation record.
\end{enumerate}

The cover names the prerequisite, observable outcome, and next dependency. It
is an identity check, not another lesson plan. A new technical term is defined
where it is first taught. Later modules expect the learner to retrieve that
term from prior work or the Reference Grammar unless its meaning is being
materially refined.

\section{Course-wide working protocol}

Before looking anything up, mark the finite verb or verbal center, propose
dictionary forms, and record formally possible readings where an ending is
syncretic. Use the identified 1962 Missal and the course source record for
received wording. A familiar English rendering does not replace a parse.

The two-session plan is a workload envelope, not permission to advance by
elapsed time. Complete the lesson and guided practice before the selected
worksheets. Stop, correct in a different color, and use an omitted variant when
the mastery evidence is absent. The assessment criterion, not page completion
or confidence, decides whether to continue.

\section{How to use answers}

Every module's solutions are in its terminal \emph{Answer Key} part. Do not
consult that part until the worksheets and mastery record are complete. For a
closed item, locate the form, function, government, or clause relation that
decides the answer; a matching English paraphrase alone is not enough. For an
open task, use the model and rubric to test morphology, syntax, idiom, and
source control rather than treating one model sentence as uniquely correct.

Some translations legitimately admit more than one English wording. An
alternative is acceptable when it preserves Latin agency, negation, time,
dependency, and material ambiguity. Record a defensible alternative in the
error ledger rather than changing it merely to imitate the model's style.
Stage assessments remain separate multi-sitting sequences and are not included
in a module's two-session workload promise.

\section{Source classes and packet limits}

The course distinguishes source passages from teaching examples by citation
and inventory: an excerpt named as Missal, Vulgate, scriptural, or historical
wording is received material; worksheet readings carry stable passage IDs that
map to the repository passage inventory; uncited short drills and model
compositions are project-composed unless a local note says otherwise. Each
English translation or gloss is a project translation, deliberately close
enough to expose the grammar where instruction requires it, and is not a
quotation from a published English missal unless expressly identified. Each
packet's terminal Scope and Qualifications appendix states only its local
boundary. This guide and the course-wide research records govern editions,
normalization, rights, review, and the non-authority limits of the curriculum.

The course teaches recognition and grammar, not pronunciation. Quoted Missal
text follows the 1962 typical edition: consonantal \Latin{i}, as in
\Latin{Iesus} and \Latin{iuventutem}; consonantal \Latin{v}, as in
\Latin{vult}; and the printed ligatures \Latin{æ} and \Latin{œ}. Other books
may expand the ligatures to \Latin{ae/oe}, print consonantal \Latin{j}, or use
older \Latin{u/v} conventions. Treat those as orthographic variants rather
than different vocabulary, while copying an assigned source exactly.
Capitalization and punctuation are editorial helps and do not determine case
or clause structure. The Missal normally omits macrons, so the course does not
add quantity marks to quoted forms.

\begin{studybox}{The first decision}
If the entry assumptions below are true, read \emph{The destination} and begin
the six-pass study loop. If formal English grammar or the structure of the 1962
Mass is unfamiliar, acquire that prerequisite first; the course uses both but
does not teach either from the beginning.
\end{studybox}
