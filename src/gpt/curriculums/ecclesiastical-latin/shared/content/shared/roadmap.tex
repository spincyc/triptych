\chapter{The destination}

This curriculum treats Latin as a language to be read and written, not as a
code in which each word is mechanically replaced by an English word. Its
governing practical test is the \Missal. A graduate should be able to open
the book at an unfamiliar Mass, identify the grammatical spine of each text,
follow the argument or prayer directly in Latin, and consult a dictionary only
for genuinely unfamiliar vocabulary or names. A facing translation should no
longer be the medium through which the Latin is understood.

The course assumes complete knowledge of the shape of the Mass. It therefore
does not explain when the Introit occurs, what a Collect does liturgically, or
how a Common is selected. It asks instead why an Introit may omit a verb, why a
Collect so often moves from a relative clause through a petition to a purpose
clause, and how a participle or subjunctive controls the sense.

\section{What ``complete'' means}

The linguistic range includes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item the Ordinary, including the prayers spoken quietly by the celebrant;
  \item the Roman Canon, prefaces, and the recurring ceremonial formulas;
  \item the Proper of Time, Proper of Saints, Commons, votive formularies,
        Requiem texts, and the principal Holy Week texts found in the 1962 book;
  \item scriptural lessons and chant texts, treated as Latin prose or poetry;
  \item rubrical Latin as a specialized reading register; and
  \item selected Vulgate and historical reading that introduces sources,
        echoes, and inherited habits of liturgical Latin.
\end{itemize}

No finite course can pre-teach every rare word in every local feast. Completion
therefore means grammatical and lexical independence with the universal 1962
corpus, plus the ability to approach an unfamiliar local proper in the same way
an advanced reader approaches any new author. It does not mean memorizing an
English rendering of every page.

\section{What is deliberately absent}

Pronunciation, singing, chant notation, ceremonial instruction, and historical
arguments about liturgical reform are outside scope. Accent marks and vowel
quantity appear only where they help distinguish forms or use a dictionary.
The course is descriptive of the Latin encountered in the chosen corpus.

\chapter{How to study without a calendar}

There are no weeks, daily quotas, or completion dates. Learning modules use two
focused sessions as a planning envelope, but a learner may finish a transparent
module quickly and repeat a difficult one over several sittings. Prerequisites,
practice evidence, and gates—not elapsed time—govern advancement.

\section{Definition and retrieval policy}

The learner begins with no Latin grammar, so a genuinely new technical term is
defined where it is first taught. Boxed entries headed \textbf{Definition}
state the course's working meaning and its deciding feature. The Reference
Grammar gathers those decisions in one retrieval home.

Later modules assume terminology established by their prerequisites. They name
and use a prior term without restating its definition; the learner retrieves it
from memory and consults the Reference Grammar only after an unaided attempt. A
later definition appears only when the course materially refines the term or
introduces a genuinely new contrast. It must identify that refinement rather
than silently repeating or contradicting the earlier decision.

\Definition{working definition}{A concise statement of the features needed to
recognize and use a concept at the present stage. Later units may refine it,
but may not silently contradict it.}

\Definition{mastery gate}{An unseen task that tests whether the learner can
retrieve and apply a unit's knowledge without following its worked example.
A gate measures stable control; it is not a quota of pages completed.}

\Definition{worksheet variant}{One of several interchangeable practice sets
for the same target. Variants permit selective repetition; they are not a
sequence that every learner must finish.}

When a new or materially refined definition still feels abstract, use a
four-part notebook entry:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item copy the definition exactly;
  \item underline the feature that distinguishes the term from its nearest
        rival;
  \item supply one Latin example and label the decisive evidence; and
  \item write one non-example or contrast and explain why it does not qualify.
\end{enumerate}

Knowing a label is never enough. To demonstrate knowledge of a term, the
learner must be able to identify it in an unseen sentence, cite the evidence,
and use it accurately in controlled composition when the construction permits.

\section{The six-pass study loop}

\StudyLoop

The loop is intentionally physical. Handwriting slows recognition just enough
to reveal endings, forces a choice rather than a click, and leaves an artifact
that can be compared with later work. Typed work may supplement but should not
replace the first handwritten attempt.

\section{The error ledger}

Keep one bound notebook divided into five recurring families:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item form identification;
  \item agreement and government;
  \item clause boundaries and syntax;
  \item vocabulary or idiom; and
  \item composition and word choice.
\end{enumerate}

For every corrected error, record the original decision, the corrected form or
analysis, the rule that decides between them, and one new example of your own.
``Careless'' is not a rule. ``I read \Latin{gratia} as nominative although
\Latin{cum} governs the ablative'' is useful evidence.

\section{How many worksheets?}

Every unit has four variants because learners need different amounts and kinds
of repetition. Its normal module prints two deliberately contrasting sheets
and identifies them by stable ID. Use the two unprinted variants only for
remediation or delayed retrieval, then attempt the gate.

\begin{xltabular}{\linewidth}{@{}P{1.25in}P{2.25in}Y@{}}
  \toprule
  \textbf{Result} & \textbf{Meaning} & \textbf{Next action} \\
  \midrule
  \endhead
  At least 90\% forms and 85\% reading, with no repeated structural error &
  The unit is available under mild pressure & Advance; revisit by cumulative
  retrieval later. \\
  Correct meaning but unstable parsing & Recognition is outrunning control &
  Choose a parsing or reconstruction variant and write the governing rule. \\
  Correct forms but weak connected reading & Paradigms are isolated facts &
  Choose a passage and clause-mapping variant. \\
  Reading is sound but composition breaks agreement & Comprehension is ahead
  of production & Choose controlled transformation before another free task. \\
  The same error appears three times & The rule has not become operational &
  Return to the named lesson, copy the worked example, and choose two targeted
  variants. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{xltabular}

No student is expected to complete every variant. Doing so before every gate
would turn a diagnostic bank into busywork and delay the mixed, cumulative
reading that produces fluency.

\chapter{The four-volume course}

\section{Volume I: Foundations}

Volume I builds reliable control of ordinary morphology and simple clauses.
It begins with Latin sentence architecture and dictionary forms, then develops
the noun, adjective, pronoun, and verb systems through recurring Missal phrases.
Controlled composition starts immediately: changing number, replacing a noun,
turning active into passive, and producing short governed phrases.

\begin{xltabular}{\linewidth}{@{}P{0.7in}P{2.15in}Y@{}}
  \toprule
  \textbf{Unit} & \textbf{Center} & \textbf{Observable result} \\
  \midrule\endhead
  I.1 & English grammar to Latin forms & Mark subjects, predicates, objects,
       modifiers, and clauses; read dictionary entries and course orthography. \\
  I.2 & Case, number, gender, agreement & Explain a nominal form by its
       function, agreement, or governing word. \\
  I.3 & First and second declensions & Produce complete paradigms, including
       neuters and vocatives, and use them in governed phrases. \\
  I.4 & Third declension & Recover stems and distinguish consonant, i-stem, and
       neuter patterns in connected reading. \\
  I.5 & Fourth and fifth declensions & Complete the noun system and discriminate
       ambiguous endings by syntax. \\
  I.6 & Adjectives and agreement & Make modifiers agree without assuming that
       linked endings must look identical. \\
  I.7 & \Latin{sum} and \Latin{possum} & Control all six indicative tenses,
       predicate constructions, ellipsis, and complementary infinitives. \\
  I.8 & Present active system & Parse and produce present, imperfect, and future
       active forms in all conjugations. \\
  I.9 & Perfect active system & Relate perfect, pluperfect, and future perfect
       to principal parts and narrative time. \\
  I.10 & Passive and deponent verbs & Form both passive systems, track agreement
       and agency, and read deponents by form and sense. \\
  I.11 & Pronouns and prepositions & Follow government, agreement, and reference
       through recurring liturgical phrases. \\
  I.12 & Consolidated Missal reading & Apply the seven-pass method to connected
       texts and produce controlled sentences. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{xltabular}

\MasteryGate{on a fresh paragraph containing ordinary indicative verbs and
nouns from all five declensions, identify at least 90\% of forms, justify the
case of each noun, give an accurate direct reading, and compose six controlled
sentences with no repeated agreement error.}

\section{Volume II: Core grammar}

Volume II supplies the clause structures that make mature liturgical prose
compact. It completes pronouns and comparison, then treats infinitives,
participles, the full subjunctive system, subordinate clauses, conditions,
commands, verbal nouns, periphrastics, and marked word order.

\begin{xltabular}{\linewidth}{@{}P{0.7in}P{2.15in}Y@{}}
  \toprule
  \textbf{Unit} & \textbf{Center} & \textbf{Observable result} \\
  \midrule\endhead
  II.1 & Pronoun systems & Track reference through relative and demonstrative
       chains. \\
  II.2 & Comparison, adverbs, numerals & Read degree and measure without forcing
       English syntax onto Latin. \\
  II.3 & Infinitives and indirect statement & Locate the controlling verb,
       accusative subject, and relative time. \\
  II.4 & Participles and ablative absolute & Expand compressed modifiers into
       explicit clauses and recompress them accurately. \\
  II.5 & Subjunctive forms and sequence & Produce every tense and distinguish
       time from aspect and sequence. \\
  II.6 & Purpose, result, indirect command & Classify \Latin{ut/ne} clauses from
       governing context and internal signals. \\
  II.7 & Substantive clauses & Explain how a complete clause fills a nominal
       role required by its governing expression. \\
  II.8 & Temporal, causal, concessive clauses & Distinguish indicative facts
       from circumstantial or speaker-framed relations. \\
  II.9 & Conditions & Read protasis and apodosis as one system and identify the
       speaker's stance. \\
  II.10 & Relative clauses of characteristic & Distinguish an identifying fact
       from a characteristic, result-like, or causal relative use. \\
  II.11 & Commands, wishes, and fearing & Control imperatives, independent

  II.12 & Gerund, gerundive, supine, periphrastics & Recognize obligation,

  II.13 & Case syntax & Control place, time, means, manner, cause, and special

\end{xltabular}

\MasteryGate{on an unseen Collect and a connected Vulgate paragraph, map every
clause, parse every finite and non-finite verb, justify the principal case
uses, and write a literal structural version before a natural English version;
then compose a controlled six-clause prayer from supplied vocabulary.}

\section{Volume III: Independent Missal reading}

Volume III reorganizes knowledge by genre. The question is no longer ``Which
chapter taught this construction?'' but ``What does this kind of text normally
omit, foreground, repeat, or inherit?'' The learner reads complete units and
then whole formularies with English progressively removed.

\begin{xltabular}{\linewidth}{@{}P{0.7in}P{2.15in}Y@{}}
  \toprule
  \textbf{Unit} & \textbf{Genre} & \textbf{Reading problem} \\
  \midrule\endhead
  III.1 & Independent-reader workflow & Map clauses, propose lemmas, control
       lookups, reconstruct order, and record uncertainty. \\
  III.2 & Ordinary & Formula, coordination, embedded confession and petition. \\
  III.3 & Collects & Relative openings, imperative petitions, purpose clauses,
       and mediated conclusions. \\
  III.4 & Secrets and Postcommunions & Dense sacrificial and sacramental lexicon. \\
  III.5 & Lessons and Epistles & Long argument, anaphora, quotation, and Pauline
       periods. \\
  III.6 & Gospels & Narrative tense, direct speech, historical present, and
       Semitic coordination. \\
  III.7 & Chants and sequences & Ellipsis, poetic order, parallelism, and
       scriptural fragments. \\
  III.8 & Prefaces and Canon & Long periodic syntax, relative chains, and solemn
       formula. \\
  III.9 & Temporal cycle & The recurring vocabulary and images of seasons. \\
  III.10 & Sanctoral and Commons & Genre transfer, specialized vocabulary, and
       reusable formulary patterns. \\
  III.11 & Requiem and Holy Week & Poetic, prophetic, and ceremonial compression
       across distinctive complete corpora. \\
  III.12 & Whole-Mass capstones & Sustained direct reading without a facing
       translation. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{xltabular}

\MasteryGate{select an unfamiliar Sunday and an unfamiliar feast. Without a
facing translation, read every Latin text in order, annotate clause structure
and only genuinely unknown words, summarize each text directly from the Latin,
and explain five syntactic decisions chosen at random.}

\section{Volume IV: Advanced reading and composition}

Volume IV asks what an already competent reader asks: why this word rather than
its synonym; which scriptural context is activated; how an inherited phrase is
adapted; what a rhetorical pattern does; and how to write new prose without
merely calquing English.

\begin{xltabular}{\linewidth}{@{}P{0.7in}P{2.15in}Y@{}}
  \toprule
  \textbf{Unit} & \textbf{Center} & \textbf{Production result} \\
  \midrule\endhead
  A1 & Diachronic and ecclesiastical register & Distinguish inherited classical
       syntax, late usage, biblical idiom, and liturgical convention. \\
  A2 & Vulgate texture and Semitisms & Recognize coordination, cognate patterns,
       calques, and scriptural cadence. \\
  A3 & Allusion and typology & Trace a phrase to its broader source and explain
       what the liturgical reuse presupposes. \\
  A4 & Rhetoric and periodic prose & Analyze balance, anaphora, antithesis,
       hyperbaton, clausula, and sound pattern without reducing them to labels. \\
  A5 & Textual comparison and commentary & Collate variants and write a concise
       grammatical and literary note. \\
  A6 & Controlled imitation & Preserve a model's syntax while changing its
       vocabulary and theological proposition. \\
  A7 & Guided composition & Move from supplied frame through partial outline to
       a coherent paragraph or collect. \\
  A8 & Free composition and research & Write, revise, defend, and annotate
       original ecclesiastical prose. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{xltabular}

\MasteryGate{produce an independent commentary on an unseen proper that treats
grammar, vocabulary, source, allusion, and rhetoric; then compose and revise a
short Latin collect and a 150--250 word prose meditation whose syntax and word
choices can be defended from credible models.}

\chapter{From translation to direct reading}

Translation is used early as a diagnostic because it exposes whether relations
have been understood. It is then deliberately withdrawn.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Volumes I--II:} write a structural English version that keeps
        Latin relations visible, followed by natural English.
  \item \textbf{Early Volume III:} annotate in Latin symbols and give an English
        summary, not a line-by-line rendering.
  \item \textbf{Late Volume III:} paraphrase simple clauses in easier Latin and
        answer comprehension questions in Latin.
  \item \textbf{Volume IV:} use English chiefly for literary commentary. The
        text itself should be understood and discussed from its Latin form.
\end{enumerate}

Never hide a grammatical uncertainty beneath elegant English. A clumsy literal
version that reveals the syntax is more useful during training than polished
prose built on a mistaken subject or clause relation.

\chapter{Tools and dictionary discipline}

Look up a word only after recording a morphological hypothesis: lemma, part of
speech, likely form, and likely function. Then choose the dictionary sense that
fits the construction and context. Copying the first gloss is not reading.

For an unseen passage, mark words in three classes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{known directly};
  \item \textbf{inferable} from form, family, or context; and
  \item \textbf{lookup required} because the word or idiom remains genuinely
        unknown.
\end{itemize}

The exit standard permits the third class for rare names and uncommon proper
vocabulary. It does not permit a facing translation or a parser that makes the
grammatical decisions for the learner.

\chapter{Assessment and records}

The assessment volume contains parallel forms at four major gates. Take Form A
closed-book except where the directions explicitly permit a dictionary. Mark
it with the separate key, write an error analysis, and remediate by worksheet
family. Form B is a later confirmation, not an immediate second guess.

Use one page of a notebook for each gate:
