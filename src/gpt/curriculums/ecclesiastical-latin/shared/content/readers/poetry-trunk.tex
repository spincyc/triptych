Veni, pater pauperum, veni, dator munerum, veni, lumen cordium.\\

\section{Read syntax across the line}

A line ending is not automatically a clause ending.  Begin with finite verbs:
\Latin{veni} and \Latin{emitte}.  Then attach vocatives:
\Latin{Sancte Spiritus}, \Latin{pater pauperum}, \Latin{dator munerum}, and
\Latin{lumen cordium}.  The third and fourth lines are verbless predications
or appositions sustained by the address.  Recover them in plain prose before
asking what balance and recurrence add.

\textbf{Gloss.} \Latin{caelitus}, from heaven; \Latin{radius}, ray;
\Latin{pauper}, poor person; \Latin{munus}, gift; \Latin{consolator}, comforter;
\Latin{hospes}, guest; \Latin{refrigerium}, refreshment; \Latin{requies}, rest;
\Latin{aestus}, heat; \Latin{temperies}, moderation or coolness;
\Latin{fletus}, weeping; \Latin{solacium}, solace.

Mark repetition, balanced members, written recurrence visible on the page, and
the paired settings \Latin{in labore/in aestu/in fletu}.  Do not make a claim
about spoken realization: this edition does not teach pronunciation.

\section{The common-trunk poetry toolkit}

For any verse or chant, work in this order:

\begin{enumerate}
\item preserve the printed lines and identify the source boundary;
\item find finite verbs, clause boundaries, and governed words across lines;
\item restore a temporary prose order without calling it the source text;
\item mark ellipsis, parallelism, anaphora, chiasmus, enjambment, and marked
placement only where the page supports them;
\item distinguish syllable division, quantity, quantitative meter, and visible
line pattern as different kinds of evidence, without supplying a stress or
pronunciation system;
\item state what remains uncertain before interpretation or imitation.
\end{enumerate}

Formal quantity, elision, scansion, hexameter, elegiac couplet, lyric meters,
Christian quantitative poetry, rhythmic hymnody, commentary, and verse
composition belong to branch P1--P8.  The trunk prepares for that branch by
making poetic syntax and source discipline ordinary reading habits.

\section{Reconstruction and composition}

Reconstruct the four lines from the cues heaven---poor---gifts---heart---labor
---heat---weeping.  Then write six lines of P prose arranged as balanced
members about an ordinary journey or workday.  The wording need not be metrical.
Label every omitted verb, and revise one line whose balance obscures syntax.

\section{Models and points to check}
\textbf{Project translation.} Come, Holy Spirit, and send from heaven a ray of
your light. Come, father of the poor; come, giver of gifts; come, light of
hearts. Best comforter, sweet guest of the soul, sweet refreshment. Rest in
labor, coolness in heat, solace in weeping.

A prose-order check might expand the ellipsis as \Latin{tu es consolator
optimus, hospes animae dulcis, refrigerium dulce; tu es requies in labore,
temperies in aestu, solacium in fletu}.  The supplied \Latin{tu es} is an
analytic aid, not a variant reading.  A sound account can explain the repeated
imperative and tripled prepositional phrases without scanning the text.
