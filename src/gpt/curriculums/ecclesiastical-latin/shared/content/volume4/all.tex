\begin{courseunit}{A1}
\chapter{A1. Register across time}

Advanced reading must not assume that every Latin text belongs to one uniform
linguistic setting. This course compares forms in the \Missal{} with identified
biblical, liturgical, and rubrical texts. Good analysis first describes what a
form does in its passage. A claim about its history or source belongs only
after a dated witness or a cited parallel supplies evidence for that claim.

A \Term{register} is a variety of language associated with a recurring
purpose, community, or textual setting. Register differs from \Term{period},
which locates language in historical time, and from \Term{genre}, which groups
whole texts by communicative and liturgical job. Thus a fourth-century prayer
and a twentieth-century rubric can differ in period, genre, and register; none
of those labels by itself evaluates the Latin's quality.

In this course, an \Term{orthographic} feature concerns spelling or written
form; a \Term{lexical} feature concerns word choice or contextual sense; a
\Term{syntactic} feature concerns dependencies among words and clauses; and a
\Term{stylistic} feature concerns patterned choices among grammatical
possibilities, such as balance, repetition, or order. One feature may belong to
more than one level, but the commentary must say what precisely is being
observed.

\begin{learningobjectives}
  \item distinguish orthographic, lexical, syntactic, and stylistic differences;
  \item use a classical grammar as a baseline without treating later Latin as
        failed classical prose;
  \item identify Christian specialization of an inherited word; and
  \item write an evidence-based register note rather than a vague label.
\end{learningobjectives}

\section{Four questions for a marked feature}

A \Term{marked feature} is one that stands out against the comparison norm
chosen for the inquiry. It is not automatically rare, late, erroneous, or
important. The \Term{baseline} is that stated comparison norm; a classical
grammar supplies a useful baseline for continuity and difference, not a rule
that every later register was trying and failing to imitate.

When a form looks unusual, ask in order:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Is the difference only typographic? \Latin{æ/ae}, \Latin{j/i}, and
        punctuation may vary without grammatical change.
  \item Is it already accounted for by the stated comparison grammar, such as
        extensive coordination or a postpositive particle?
  \item Does the immediate Christian context require a specialized sense?
        \Latin{gratia}, \Latin{sacramentum}, \Latin{confessio}, and
        \Latin{passio} cannot safely be read from one context-free gloss.
  \item Does a cited biblical source or securely dated parallel exhibit the
        same construction? If so, record the comparison; do not assign a cause
        or period from an unusual-looking form alone.
\end{enumerate}

\Term{Coordination} joins units of equal syntactic rank; \Term{parataxis} is
the placing of clauses or phrases side by side with little or no explicit
subordination. A \Term{postpositive} word cannot normally stand first in its
clause or phrase and instead follows its first word or constituent, even when
it logically connects the whole unit. In this course, \Term{Christian
specialization} names a contextually restricted Christian sense of an existing
lexeme. Reconstructing when or how that sense developed is a separate
historical claim and requires dated lexical evidence.

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Et Verbum caro factum est}}{W.IV.1}
\Latin{Et Verbum caro factum est, et habitavit in nobis} (John 1:14,
Clementine Vulgate) uses familiar inflection and clause coordination.
\Latin{Verbum} is a nominative subject; \Latin{caro} is a predicate nominative;
\Latin{factum est} is third-person singular perfect indicative in the perfect
system of \Latin{fio}, which also supplies the passive of \Latin{facio}; its
neuter participle agrees with \Latin{Verbum} and here may be rendered ``became''
or ``was made.'' \Latin{Habitavit} shares the subject. The Christian contextual
sense of \Latin{Verbum} and the paratactic coordination are observable here;
any wider register or historical claim should rest on comparison with cited
texts. Merely writing ``late Latin'' explains nothing.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Contextual sense without etymological fallacy}

The \Term{etymological fallacy} is the mistake of treating a word's origin or
an earlier sense as its necessary meaning in another passage. This exercise
establishes sense from construction and immediate context; it does not by
itself reconstruct semantic history. A claim about change across periods
requires dated, cited witnesses. Etymology may suggest a question, but it does
not answer that question.

Context and construction determine the local sense. In the sacrificial prose
studied here, \Latin{hostia} denotes a victim or oblation; it is not
\Latin{hostis}, ``enemy.'' In a sanctoral heading, \Latin{confessor} names an
ecclesiastical category rather than anyone who happens to admit something.
These are contextual analyses, not a reconstruction of either word's history.
Record a lexical note as:

\begin{quote}
lemma; construction; sense in this passage; one close parallel; contrast with a
plausible but wrong sense.
\end{quote}

\section{Composition laboratory}

Take a classical-looking sentence, a Vulgate verse, and a collect. Underline
what all three share in ordinary morphology. Box only features that genuinely
distinguish register. Then write a 100-word English note that supports every
historical claim with a form or parallel.

\MasteryGate{given three unattributed passages, identify likely register from
specific lexical and syntactic evidence, normalize orthographic variants, and
write a cautious commentary that distinguishes observation from historical
inference.}

\end{courseunit}

\begin{courseunit}{A2}
\chapter{A2. Vulgate texture and biblical idiom}

Liturgical texts quote, adapt, and echo the Vulgate. Direct familiarity with
its habits prevents the reader from ``correcting'' meaningful scriptural
texture into a more Ciceronian shape.

\Term{Texture} is the cumulative linguistic character produced by recurrent
choices in wording, clause linkage, formula, and rhythm on the page. Biblical
idioms remain describable Latin, not a label for whatever seems unfamiliar.
\Term{Source-language influence} is a historical explanation supported by
comparison with the source, not an inference licensed by unusual Latin alone.

\begin{learningobjectives}
  \item recognize recurrent parataxis, formula, and source-language influence;
  \item distinguish a grammatical construction from its discourse frequency;
  \item compare a Missal adaptation with its biblical source; and
  \item imitate a biblical pattern without turning it into a universal rule.
\end{learningobjectives}

\section{Recurring textures}

The table names patterns rather than exceptions to grammar. \Term{Discourse
frequency} means how often or characteristically a construction occurs in a
kind of discourse; it must be distinguished from whether that construction is
grammatical at all. An \Term{event formula} is a recurrent frame that presents
or advances an event. A \Term{cognate expression} places words from the same
root family in one construction. A \Term{genitive chain} is a sequence of two
or more genitives whose head relations must be established one by one, not
assumed to form a single undifferentiated modifier.

\begin{xltabular}{\linewidth}{@{}P{1.45in}P{2.05in}Y@{}}
  \toprule
  \textbf{Texture} & \textbf{Typical signal} & \textbf{Reading task} \\
  \midrule\endhead
  Parataxis & repeated \Latin{et}, finite clauses in sequence & determine the
       logical relation from context rather than assuming every \Latin{et}
       means exactly the same English connector \\
  Event formula & \Latin{factum est} + clause or temporal phrase & separate the
       narrative frame from the event; note possible biblical source pattern \\
  Presentation & \Latin{ecce} & identify what is brought into discourse and
       whether a finite verb is supplied \\
  Cognate expression & related noun and verb & explain emphasis or inherited
       idiom rather than avoiding repetition in analysis \\
  Genitive chain & several nouns linked by genitive & find each head and avoid
       reversing relations in polished English \\
  Abstract-for-concrete or collective expression & \Latin{caro},
       \Latin{semen}, \Latin{domus} & establish sense from biblical context \\
  Formulaic response & \Latin{amen, alleluia, hosanna} & recognize that not all
       liturgical words are Latin or should be forced into a Latin paradigm \\
  \bottomrule
\end{xltabular}

\begin{workedexample}{a source adapted in the Ordinary}{W.IV.2}
Matthew 8:8 reads: \Latin{Domine, non sum dignus ut intres sub tectum meum: sed
tantum dic verbo, et sanabitur puer meus.} The Communion preparation replaces
\Latin{puer meus} with \Latin{anima mea}. Grammar remains stable: \Latin{dignus}
is completed by an \Latin{ut}-clause with present subjunctive \Latin{intres};
\Latin{dic} is imperative; \Latin{verbo} is ablative of means;
\Latin{sanabitur} is future passive. The adaptation transfers the centurion's
trust to the communicant's soul. The allusion is more than shared vocabulary:
the altered final noun depends on the whole remembered scene.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{from psalm to altar prayer}{W.IV.3}
\Latin{Et introibo ad altare Dei: ad Deum qui lætificat iuventutem meam}
(Psalm 42:4) first expresses motion toward \Latin{altare}; the second
\Latin{ad}-phrase, \Latin{ad Deum}, clarifies that goal epexegetically. The
relative \Latin{qui} is nominative subject of
\Latin{lætificat}; \Latin{iuventutem} is its object; \Latin{meam} agrees with
that object. Direct reading retains the forward movement and relative
identification before seeking elegant English.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Controlled imitation}

\Term{Imitation} is composition that preserves specified, observable features
of a model while changing its content. It is \Term{controlled} when the model,
the features to preserve, and the permissible changes are stated in advance.
It differs from translation, which transfers the sense of a supplied text, and
from patchwriting, which joins borrowed phrases without governing them through
an independently planned structure.

Imitate a pattern, not its accidental vocabulary. For
\Latin{Cor mundum crea in me, Deus, et spiritum rectum innova in visceribus
meis} (Psalm 50:12), preserve:

\begin{itemize}
  \item object + adjective before an imperative;
  \item a prepositional phrase of location;
  \item vocative address; and
  \item a coordinated second petition.
\end{itemize}

Write a new sentence with supplied nouns and verbs, then mark where imitation
ends. Do not present an exercise sentence as a liturgical text.

\MasteryGate{compare three short Missal texts with their Vulgate sources,
identify exact quotation, adaptation, and broader echo, explain the grammar of
both versions, and write one controlled imitation whose source pattern is
explicitly documented.}

\end{courseunit}

\begin{courseunit}{A3}
\chapter{A3. Allusion, context, and typological reading}

An allusion is not established merely because two texts share a common word.
The advanced reader tests verbal density, rarity, syntactic shape, inherited
liturgical use, and the relevance of the wider source context.

An \Term{allusion} is a textually signaled relationship that invites a reader
to activate another identifiable text or textual tradition. \Term{Verbal
density} is the concentration of shared wording within a short span;
\Term{rarity} asks how diagnostic that wording is rather than merely how
unfamiliar it feels; and \Term{syntactic shape} is the arrangement of the
shared words in corresponding grammatical relations. A \Term{context window}
is the surrounding source material examined to test whether the proposed
relationship extends beyond an isolated phrase.

\begin{learningobjectives}
  \item distinguish quotation, close adaptation, conventional formula, and
        possible echo;
  \item inspect a sufficient context window around a proposed source;
  \item explain how liturgical placement activates the source; and
  \item avoid claiming authorial intention beyond the evidence.
\end{learningobjectives}

\section{The allusion dossier}

For each proposed allusion, prepare one handwritten page:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item copy the liturgical phrase and parse it completely;
  \item copy the proposed source with at least the surrounding paragraph or
        psalm strophe;
  \item underline exact verbal overlap and circle morphological adaptation;
  \item list meaningful contextual correspondences and disconfirming evidence;
  \item classify the relationship; and
  \item state what additional sense becomes available if the relationship is
        recognized.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{grammarbox}{A cautious classification scale}
\textbf{Quotation}: sustained exact wording, allowing conventional inflection
or orthographic adjustment, that identifies a secure source.
\textbf{Adaptation}: source-identifying wording recast for a new grammatical or
liturgical setting, with the changes themselves requiring explanation.
\textbf{Formula}: recurrent communal wording whose occurrence does not isolate
one source. \textbf{Echo}: a plausible activation supported by several
converging signals but less securely demonstrable than quotation or adaptation.
\textbf{Parallel only}: a useful comparison for language or thought without a
claim that one passage evokes or depends on the other.
\end{grammarbox}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Sanctus} and Isaiah}{W.IV.4}
Isaiah 6:3 in the Clementine Vulgate includes \Latin{Sanctus, sanctus, sanctus
Dominus Deus exercituum; plena est omnis terra gloria eius}. The Mass has
\Latin{Sanctus, Sanctus, Sanctus Dominus Deus Sabaoth. Pleni sunt cæli et terra
gloria tua}. Exact repetition and structure secure the relationship, while
\Latin{Sabaoth}, the plural predicate \Latin{pleni}, the expanded subject
\Latin{cæli et terra}, and second-person \Latin{tua} require comparison rather
than careless identity. The dossier must account for each change.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Typology as contextual reading}

Typology relates persons, events, and institutions across Scripture and
liturgical reception. Linguistic competence contributes by tracing repeated
lemmas, titles, and grammatical recasting; it does not by itself settle every
theological claim. Separate three layers in commentary:

A \Term{type} is an earlier scriptural person, event, or institution received
as a divinely ordered pattern; its \Term{antitype} is the later reality in
which that pattern is fulfilled. \Term{Typological reading} interprets the
relation between them in Scripture and its reception. It is therefore more
specific than noticing thematic similarity and broader than proving a verbal
quotation. Grammar establishes what each text says; context and tradition bear
the further typological claim.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item what the Latin sentence grammatically asserts;
  \item what the source passage contributes; and
  \item what the liturgical reuse invites the reader to connect.
\end{enumerate}

\MasteryGate{produce two allusion dossiers from unfamiliar propers. At least
one must reject or downgrade an initially attractive parallel after examining
context. Defend the final classification with verbal and contextual evidence.}

\end{courseunit}

\begin{courseunit}{A4}
\chapter{A4. Rhetoric and periodic prose}

Rhetorical analysis explains how arrangement guides attention and memory. It
does not decorate a grammatical paraphrase with Greek labels.

\Term{Rhetoric} here means the purposeful arrangement of language to shape a
hearer's or reader's attention, judgment, and response. A \Term{figure} is a
recognizable pattern of wording or structure; naming one is only the beginning
of analysis. Its \Term{effect} is the contribution that the pattern makes in
this passage, established from syntax and context rather than assigned from a
memorized list.

\begin{learningobjectives}
  \item identify parallelism, anaphora, antithesis, climax, hyperbaton, and
        periodic suspension;
  \item connect a figure to syntax and semantic effect;
  \item diagram a long preface or Canon sentence; and
  \item reproduce selected patterns in controlled prose.
\end{learningobjectives}

\section{A functional inventory}

\begin{xltabular}{\linewidth}{@{}P{1.1in}P{2.25in}Y@{}}
  \toprule
  \textbf{Figure} & \textbf{Definition and test} & \textbf{Possible work} \\
  \midrule\endhead
  Anaphora & repetition of the same word or phrase at the beginning of
       successive corresponding members & binds petitions or assertions into a
       cumulative act \\
  Parallelism & two or more members with corresponding grammatical structure;
       correspondence must be shown, not merely asserted & makes likeness,
       contrast, or progression perceptible \\
  Antithesis & semantic opposition sharpened by parallel or balanced structure;
       opposition without structural correspondence is not yet this figure &
       focuses a theological or moral distinction \\
  Tricolon & a sequence of three corresponding members, whether or not they
       increase & gives a threefold sense of balance, range, or completion \\
  Climax & members arranged in demonstrable increase of force, scope, or
       importance & creates ascent; three members alone prove only a tricolon \\
  Hyperbaton & separation of words that syntax binds closely, such as an
       adjective and noun, beyond their expected adjacency & can frame or
       foreground intervening material; rhetorical purpose still needs argument \\
  Chiasm & two pairs arranged in inverted correspondence, schematically ABBA,
       with credible syntactic or semantic matching & concentrates reciprocal
       relation; four loosely related words do not establish it \\
  Polyptoton & repetition of one lexeme in different inflected forms within a
       short span & varies grammatical relation while preserving visible verbal
       identity \\
  Period & a sentence whose governing construction remains suspended while
       subordinate members accumulate toward completion & orders grounds,
       petition, and end toward the main resolution \\
  \bottomrule
\end{xltabular}

\begin{workedexample}{deictic accumulation in the Canon}{W.IV.5}
\Latin{Hæc dona, hæc munera, hæc sancta sacrificia illibata} repeats
\Latin{hæc} and develops three accusative objects, the last expanded by two
adjectives. The sequence does not present three disconnected dictionary
glosses; its anaphora and growing weight focus the objects of
\Latin{habeas et benedicas}. A useful note connects repetition, morphology, and
the governing verbs.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{the preface frame}{W.IV.6}
\Latin{Vere dignum et iustum est, æquum et salutare} coordinates two pairs of
predicate adjectives around \Latin{est}; the following infinitive or
substantive action states what is fitting. Before translating, bracket the
predicate frame and trace how the longer sentence suspends completion through
relative clauses before the angelic conclusion.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Prose rhythm}

\Term{Cadence} is the patterned movement toward a phrase or sentence ending;
a \Term{clausula} is a recognizable closing cadence, especially one described
by conventional metrical or stress patterns. Because identifying a clausula
requires more than noticing that a sentence ends neatly, claims here must cite
a reference pattern and the relevant textual quantities or stresses.

Advanced commentary may observe cadence and clausula, but this course makes no
pronunciation requirement and therefore treats rhythm through textual pattern,
stress information from references, and comparison—not unsupported claims
based on private recitation. Rhetorical structure remains available on the
page through balance, length, recurrence, and closure.

\MasteryGate{diagram one complete preface and one long Canon period; identify
only defensible figures, and for each write a sentence explaining how grammar
and arrangement affect the movement of thought. Then imitate one structural
pattern with new, controlled content.}

\end{courseunit}

\begin{courseunit}{A5}
\chapter{A5. Textual comparison and advanced commentary}

Printed punctuation, capitalization, ligatures, and even word forms can differ
among transcriptions and editions. An advanced reader records the witness before
building an interpretation on a detail.

A \Term{witness} is one particular document that preserves a text, such as a
manuscript or printed copy. A page image is a surrogate for the document it
reproduces; in these exercises it may stand for that witness when the physical
copy is unavailable. An \Term{edition} is an editor's presented text, usually
based on one or more witnesses; a \Term{transcription} is a representation
that transfers the wording of a witness into another medium. Because a
transcription can normalize or err, it is evidence about its source only to
the degree that it represents that source accurately.

\Term{Textual comparison} identifies and evaluates differences among such
witnesses or representations. A \Term{variant} is a difference in the text as
presented. A \Term{substantive variant} can alter wording, morphology, syntax,
or sense; an \Term{accidental} difference concerns such presentation features
as spelling, capitalization, or punctuation unless that feature carries
meaning in the case at hand.

\begin{learningobjectives}
  \item collate short passages from page image and transcription;
  \item distinguish substantive variants from typography and punctuation;
  \item write a concise grammatical commentary; and
  \item document uncertainty and alternative analyses honestly.
\end{learningobjectives}

\section{A minimal collation}

To \Term{collate} is to compare corresponding passages systematically and
record every relevant agreement and difference. The \Term{base witness} is the
copy against which the others are reported; choosing it organizes the record
but does not prove that every one of its readings is original or correct. The
\Term{base text} is the wording printed as the commentary's main text.

Use the 1962 typical-edition page image as the base witness. On ruled paper,
copy one line per phrase. Under it record each comparison witness. Classify a
difference as:

\begin{itemize}
  \item orthography (\Latin{æ/ae}, \Latin{j/i});
  \item punctuation or capitalization;
  \item inflectional/morphological;
  \item lexical substitution, addition, omission, or transposition; or
  \item uncertain because the image or transcription is unreliable.
\end{itemize}

Do not silently create a hybrid text. For course exercises, a normalized form
may be useful, but it must be labeled.

\Term{Normalization} changes presentation to a declared standard---for
example, consonantal \Latin{j} to \Latin{i}---without intending to change the
underlying word or construction. A \Term{hybrid text} silently combines
readings from different witnesses. An \Term{apparatus} is the compact record
that identifies witnesses and reports their variants against the base; it lets
a reader distinguish documentary evidence from editorial choice.

\section{The six-line commentary}

A \Term{grammatical commentary} is a sequence of focused notes that explains
forms and constructions needed to understand a text. It differs from a
translation, which gives continuous target-language sense, and from literary
or theological commentary, which may build on grammar but answers broader
questions. An \Term{alternative analysis} is genuine only if it accounts for
the attested form and context; a mere second English gloss is not an
alternative parse.

For a difficult phrase, write no more than six lines initially:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item lemma and morphology of the decisive form;
  \item head/governor and local syntax;
  \item clause relation;
  \item contextually precise sense;
  \item close parallel or source; and
  \item a genuine alternative, ambiguity, or reason alternatives fail.
\end{enumerate}

Only after this compact note should a longer literary or theological discussion
be added. The constraint prevents commentary from avoiding the grammatical
decision.

\section{Rubrical register}

A \Term{rubric} is an authoritative direction governing the conduct of the
rite, conventionally distinguished typographically from the words to be said.
\Term{Rubrical register} is the characteristic language of those directions:
its function is instructional, even where its Latin uses an indicative rather
than an imperative.

Rubrics commonly use an understood celebrant as subject, present indicatives as
directions, participles for simultaneous action, and specialized adverbs of
position or manner. Parse them as instructions before comparing them with
prayer prose. A form's function in a rubric may be ordinary grammar in a
specialized discourse setting rather than a unique ``rubrical tense.''

\MasteryGate{collate an unseen eight-to-twelve-line passage against a page
image and two transcriptions, produce a clean base text with apparatus, and
write grammatical notes on five selected forms without conflating spelling,
punctuation, and substantive variation.}

\end{courseunit}

\begin{courseunit}{A6}
\chapter{A6. Controlled imitation}

Composition becomes reliable when constraints are changed one at a time.
Imitation here means preserving an observable grammatical or rhetorical frame,
not pretending to manufacture an approved liturgical text.

A \Term{model} is the attested sentence selected as evidence. A \Term{frame}
is the model's reusable architecture after particular vocabulary has been
abstracted; it includes required constructions and their relations, not just a
sequence of blank spaces. A \Term{constraint} states what a draft must
preserve, change, include, or exclude. A \Term{transformation} is one
deliberate change made under those constraints.

\begin{learningobjectives}
  \item transform a model while preserving its syntax;
  \item select vocabulary from attested constructions;
  \item diagnose English calques; and
  \item revise for agreement, government, sequence, and cohesion.
\end{learningobjectives}

\section{The imitation ladder}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Substitution}: replace one noun family, adjusting every agreeing
        or governing form. Substitution changes a selected constituent while
        retaining its syntactic role.
  \item \textbf{Expansion}: add one participial, relative, or prepositional
        modifier without changing the main architecture. Expansion increases
        structure while leaving the original dependency intact.
  \item \textbf{Compression}: replace a subordinate clause with a participle or
        ablative absolute where idiomatic. Compression expresses an existing
        relation with fewer finite clauses; it must not erase an essential
        logical relation.
  \item \textbf{Polarity/person}: change affirmative to negative, singular to
        plural, or third person to first person while preserving the intended
        proposition and adjusting every dependent form.
  \item \textbf{Semantic transfer}: retain the model's syntax and rhetorical
        sequence but replace its proposition, so that the new content is not a
        paraphrase of the model.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{workedexample}{a collect frame}{W.IV.7}
Model: \Latin{Deus, qui hodierna die corda fidelium Sancti Spiritus
illustratione docuisti: da nobis in eodem Spiritu recta sapere; et de eius
semper consolatione gaudere.}

Frame:
\begin{quote}
\Latin{Deus, qui} + past divine act + \Latin{: da nobis} + two coordinated
present infinitives, each with its complement.
\end{quote}

Before substituting content, parse \Latin{corda}, \Latin{fidelium},
\Latin{illustratione}, \Latin{docuisti}, \Latin{nobis}, \Latin{recta}, and both
infinitives. A successful imitation preserves the frame and attested
constructions; it does not merely translate an English prayer word for word.
\end{workedexample}

\section{The calque audit}

A \Term{calque} is an expression reproduced piece by piece from another
language. A calque may become established usage; the problem in this audit is
an \Term{unidiomatic calque}, whose resulting choices or construction lack
support in the target register. Similarity to English is evidence for checking,
not proof of error. \Term{Cohesion} is the set of explicit links---reference,
connectors, repetition, and ordered relations---that makes successive
sentences function as one discourse.

For every draft, circle English-driven choices and ask:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Does the Latin verb govern this case or construction?
  \item Is the abstract noun needed, or would Latin prefer a verb/adjective?
  \item Has English word order hidden agreement or produced ambiguity?
  \item Is the tense chosen for Latin aspect and sequence, not English form?
  \item Can an attested ecclesiastical parallel defend the phrase?
\end{itemize}

\MasteryGate{complete five transformations of one authentic model with no
agreement or government error, then write a semantic-transfer imitation and
annotate every construction with a model or grammar reference.}

\end{courseunit}

\begin{courseunit}{A7}
\chapter{A7. Guided composition}

Guided composition reduces support in stages: full Latin frame, partial Latin
frame, structural outline, then independent draft. A learner may remain at one
stage for several compositions without imposing a calendar.

\Term{Guided composition} is original composition in which some decisions are
supplied: vocabulary, a Latin frame, required constructions, or an outline.
Guidance narrows the decision space but does not turn the task into translation.
A \Term{partial frame} supplies only some of the architecture; a
\Term{structural outline} names relations to express without supplying their
Latin wording. An \Term{independent draft} chooses its own wording within the
task's remaining constraints, though it may still consult references.

\begin{learningobjectives}
  \item plan content in propositions before selecting Latin structures;
  \item compose a collect-shaped exercise and a prose paragraph;
  \item revise through separate grammatical and stylistic passes; and
  \item use models as evidence without patchwriting them together.
\end{learningobjectives}

\section{Proposition before sentence}

An \Term{architecture} is the planned set of clause types and dependency
relations that expresses the propositions. Planning propositions first does
not mean drafting polished English for later word-for-word conversion.

Write the intended claims in spare logical form, not polished English:

\begin{quote}
address God; recall an act; petition for a disposition; state the desired end.
\end{quote}

Choose a Latin architecture for each relation. Only then select vocabulary.
This prevents elegant English from dictating un-Latin structures.

\section{Four guided forms}

\subsection*{Collect-shaped exercise}

Supply an address, relative ground, main petition, and purpose/result. Limit the
first draft to one finite main petition and no more than two subordinate
clauses. Label the result ``composition exercise,'' never ``Collect for use.''

\subsection*{Narrative paragraph}

Retell a short Gospel scene in 80--120 words. Control tense perspective,
pronoun reference, direct/indirect speech, and coordination. Compare with the
Vulgate only after the first draft, then revise consciously rather than copying.

\subsection*{Expository paragraph}

Define a liturgical or theological term in Latin, distinguish it from a nearby
term, and give one example. Use relative clauses and at least one participial
compression.

\subsection*{Commentary note}

Write a 100-word Latin explanation of one grammatical decision in a proper.
This joins metalanguage, citation, and prose cohesion.

\Term{Metalanguage} is language used to describe language---terms such as
\Latin{verbum finitum}, \Latin{subiectum}, or \Latin{casus dativus}. A
\Term{citation} identifies the wording or authority on which a claim relies;
it is not a substitute for stating the grammatical argument.

\section{Three-pass revision}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Skeleton pass}: finite verbs, subjects, clause relations,
        sequence, and required complements.
  \item \textbf{Concord pass}: every noun/adjective/participle/pronoun relation,
        case government, and reference.
  \item \textbf{Idiom pass}: attested vocabulary, unnecessary pronouns,
        connective logic, repetition, order, and cadence on the page.
\end{enumerate}

Keep all drafts. Copy the final version by hand only after explaining each
substantive revision in the margin.

A \Term{revision} changes a draft after diagnosing how well it realizes the
intended proposition, grammar, and register. It differs from \Term{correction},
which repairs a demonstrated error: a grammatically correct sentence may still
need revision for reference, order, or cohesion. A \Term{substantive revision}
changes meaning, structure, or a defensible lexical choice, rather than merely
spelling or presentation.

\MasteryGate{produce one collect-shaped exercise, one narrative, and one
expository paragraph from partial guidance. After a delayed self-edit, no draft
may contain a repeated morphology or government error, and every disputed idiom
must be defended or revised from an attested model.}

\begin{courseunit}{A8}
\chapter{A8. Free composition and independent research}

Free composition is not support-free composition. Advanced writers still use
grammars, dictionaries, corpora, models, revision, and informed readers. What
becomes free is the choice and organization of content.

\Term{Free composition} is composition in which the writer chooses and
organizes the propositions and principal structures rather than completing a
supplied frame. It remains accountable to evidence and revision. A
\Term{corpus} is a defined body of texts searched or compared as evidence for
usage; it is not automatically representative merely because it is large. An
\Term{attested construction} is one actually documented in a relevant source,
though one occurrence alone may not prove that it suits every context.

\begin{learningobjectives}
  \item define an evaluable original ecclesiastical prose project;
  \item maintain a source and idiom ledger;
  \item produce and revise one supported 150--250-word Latin draft; and
  \item distinguish that module milestone from a mature portfolio or final
        claim of independence.
\end{learningobjectives}

\section{From proposal to supported draft}

A \Term{portfolio} is a curated body of work selected to demonstrate range,
development, and final competence; it includes drafts and rationale, not only
clean products. A \Term{source ledger} records borrowed wording and its exact
location. An \Term{idiom ledger} records a construction, its contextual sense,
and a relevant attestation so that evidence can be reused without severing it
from context.

This module produces one bounded portfolio artifact, not the completed
portfolio. Define its audience, register, propositions, evidence, and success
criterion before drafting 150--250 words. Preserve the proposition plan, first
draft, correction layer, source and idiom ledger, revised draft, rubric, and
unresolved list as one auditable record.

For every borrowed phrase, record the exact source. For every unusual
construction, record a grammar section or credible parallel. This is scholarly
hygiene, not an admission that the writing is not free. Revise in three
deliberate passes: proposition and clause skeleton, concord and government,
then lexicon, cohesion, and register.

\section{Planning a later independent reading dossier}

A \Term{dossier} is an organized packet of evidence and analysis directed to
one inquiry. Unlike a portfolio, which demonstrates a writer's development
across pieces, a reading dossier assembles the textual, grammatical, allusive,
rhetorical, and register evidence needed to explain one formulary.

Do not choose or open the dossier's Mass during this module. Prepare a blank
plan for later use with Worksheet IV.31 or EL-GATE-IV. It should reserve places
to:

\begin{itemize}
  \item record the blind selection method and prior-exposure declaration;
  \item preserve the no-aid first pass and every later lookup;
  \item map the most difficult prayer and connected reading;
  \item test allusion, rhetoric, and register claims from exact Latin evidence;
  \item summarize the formulary without a line-by-line translation; and
  \item list residual uncertainties and proposed ways to resolve them.
\end{itemize}

The empty plan is preparation, not evidence that the later unfamiliar reading
has already been completed.

\section{Local module boundary}

Fluency does not mean instant recall of every rare lemma. It means that forms
and ordinary syntax are transparent enough for attention to rest on argument,
image, source, and style. A dictionary lookup should add a word, not assemble
the sentence on the learner's behalf.

Here \Term{fluency} means reliable, increasingly automatic access to ordinary
forms, constructions, and discourse relations during reading and writing. It
is compatible with deliberate analysis of a difficult period and consultation
for a rare word. \Term{Independence} means that those aids refine or verify a
proposed reading rather than supply the grammatical structure the learner
cannot yet see.

Those are course-level standards, not claims conferred by this packet. The
local milestone is one bounded proposal, its source and idiom ledger, and one
supported first revision. A mature portfolio, an unfamiliar-Mass dossier, a
delayed revision, and a defense remain for EL-GATE-IV.
