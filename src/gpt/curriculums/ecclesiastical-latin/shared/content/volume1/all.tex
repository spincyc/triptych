\section{The English bridge}

A \Term{clause} makes an assertion, asks a question, or expresses a command.
Its \Term{subject} is what the clause is about; its \Term{finite verb} is marked
for person and number and anchors the clause. A \Term{transitive verb} is one
whose construction can take a direct object. A \Term{direct object} is the
noun, pronoun, or noun-equivalent selected by that verb without a preposition;
it may name what is affected, perceived, produced, possessed, or otherwise
directly involved in the event.
A \Term{predicate complement} completes a linking verb by identifying or
describing the subject: a predicate noun identifies it, while a predicate
adjective describes it. In ``the priest reads the prayer,'' \English{the
priest} is the subject, \English{reads} is the finite verb, and \English{the
prayer} is the direct object. In ``the prayer is holy,'' \English{holy} is a
predicate adjective linked to the subject by \English{is}. A \Term{modifier}
limits or describes another word; a \Term{prepositional phrase} begins with a
preposition and includes the preposition's object and its modifiers.

The five labels on a finite verb answer different questions. \Term{Person}
identifies speaker (first), addressee (second), or someone or something else
(third). \Term{Number} distinguishes singular from plural. \Term{Tense}
locates or presents the action in time. \Term{Voice} shows whether the subject
acts or receives the action. \Term{Mood} presents the action as a statement,
command, wish, possibility, or another kind of utterance. These labels describe
the Latin form; an idiomatic English translation may express them differently.

English normally signals these relations by word order and helper words. Latin
can signal them by \Term{inflection}: a controlled change in a word's form,
usually its ending, that marks grammatical information.
Thus \Latin{Deus amat hominem} and \Latin{hominem amat Deus} both mean
\English{God loves the human being}. The endings \Latin{-us} and \Latin{-em},
not first and last position, identify subject and object. Word order still
matters for emphasis and rhythm, but it is not the first parsing tool.

\begin{grammarbox}{A five-question first pass}
For every finite clause ask, in this order: (1) What is the finite verb?
(2) What person, number, tense, voice, and mood does its ending show?
(3) What nominative word agrees with its number and can be its subject?
(4) Which remaining forms are required by the verb or a preposition?
(5) Which words agree with, possess, or modify those forms? Do not translate
until each visible ending has a proposed job.
\end{grammarbox}

\section{Orthography without a pronunciation course}

This volume teaches recognition and grammar, not pronunciation. Its quoted
Missal text follows the 1962 typical edition: consonantal \Latin{i}, as in
\Latin{Iesus} and \Latin{iuventutem}; consonantal \Latin{v}, as in
\Latin{vult}; and the printed ligatures \Latin{æ} and \Latin{œ}. Other books
may expand the ligatures to \Latin{ae/oe}, print consonantal \Latin{j}, or use
older \Latin{u/v} conventions. Treat those as orthographic variants, not as
different vocabulary, while copying the assigned source exactly.

Capitalization and punctuation are editorial helps. They do not determine
case or clause structure. The \Missal{} normally omits macrons; this course
therefore does not add quantity marks to quoted forms.

\section{What a dictionary entry promises}

A dictionary's \Term{citation form} is the conventional form under which a
word is listed. The additional forms printed with it are not optional variants:
they reveal information that the first form alone cannot supply. A \Term{stem}
is the stable base to which endings are added. A verb's \Term{principal parts}
are its listed forms from which the major stems and systems can be recovered.
In the noun entries below, the \Term{nominative} is the headword and usual
subject form, while the \Term{genitive} is the ``of'' form that reveals the
stem and declension. \Term{Gender} is the noun's grammatical class (masculine,
feminine, or neuter), which controls agreement. A \Term{declension} is a noun's
pattern of case endings; a \Term{conjugation} is the corresponding inflectional
pattern for a verb. Units 2--5 and 8 develop these preview definitions fully.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}P{1.22in}P{2.15in}Y@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Kind} & \textbf{Citation form} & \textbf{Information supplied} \\
\midrule
Noun & \Latin{gloria, gloriæ f.} & nominative singular, genitive singular,
gender; the genitive reveals declension and stem \\
Adjective & \Latin{sanctus, sancta, sanctum} & nominative singular forms for
masculine, feminine, and neuter \\
Third-declension adjective & \Latin{omnis, omne} & masculine/feminine and
neuter nominative singular; genitive is \Latin{omnis} \\
Verb & \Latin{laudo, laudare, laudavi, laudatum} & first-person present,
present infinitive, first-person perfect, and supine/perfect-participle base \\
Deponent verb & \Latin{sequor, sequi, secutus sum} & present form, infinitive,
and perfect form; passive morphology but active meaning \\
Pronoun & \Latin{hic, hæc, hoc} & masculine, feminine, and neuter nominative
singular forms \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

Never guess a noun's stem from the nominative alone. Remove \Latin{-is} from
its genitive singular: \Latin{regis} gives \Latin{reg-}; \Latin{corporis}
gives \Latin{corpor-}. For a regular verb, the infinitive identifies its
conjugation. Perfect stems, however, must normally be learned from the third
principal part: \Latin{rego, regere, rexi} has perfect stem \Latin{rex-}, not
*\Latin{regiv-}.

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{In principio erat Verbum} (Jn 1:1)}{W.I.1}
\Parse{\Latin{erat}}{\Latin{sum, esse, fui}}{3rd singular, imperfect active
indicative}{finite verb}{was}{The finite verb comes before its grammatical
subject.}\par\medskip
\Parse{\Latin{Verbum}}{\Latin{verbum, -i n.}}{nominative singular neuter}
{subject}{the Word}{The capital is helpful, but the nominative ending supplies
the syntax.}
\Latin{in} governs the ablative \Latin{principio}, \English{in the beginning}.
Translation: \English{In the beginning was the Word.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Dominus vobiscum} (Mass dialogue)}{W.I.2}
\Latin{Dominus} is nominative singular, \English{the Lord}. \Latin{vobiscum}
is \Latin{cum vobis}: \Latin{cum} is attached after the personal pronoun, and
\Latin{vobis} is ablative plural, \English{with you}. The formula is
conventionally construed as a wish; \Latin{[sit]} is an interpretive expansion,
not a printed or uniquely required form. Translation: \English{The Lord be with you.}
The response \Latin{Et cum spiritu tuo} is likewise an elliptical phrase:
\Latin{spiritu} and \Latin{tuo} are ablative singular.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Deo gratias} (Mass conclusion)}{W.I.3}
\Latin{Deo} is dative singular, the recipient \English{to God}.
\Latin{gratias} is accusative plural, \English{thanks}, the object of an
understood expression such as \Latin{agimus}, \English{we give}. Idiomatic
translation: \English{Thanks be to God.} The two visible nouns are not a
subject--verb sentence; supplied English words must not be mistaken for Latin
forms actually present.
\end{workedexample}

\section{The case map}

\Term{Case} is the grammatical category, usually signaled by inflection, that
helps mark a noun, pronoun, or adjective's relation in its sentence.
\Term{Number} says whether the form is
singular or plural. \Term{Gender} classifies a noun as masculine, feminine, or
neuter and controls the gender of words agreeing with it; grammatical gender
does not necessarily describe biological sex. A word's \Term{syntactic
function} is the job it performs in its phrase or clause, such as subject,
object, possessor, or recipient. Case is the form; function is the contextual
job, so the same case can perform several functions.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}P{0.92in}P{2.1in}Y@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Case} & \textbf{Core work} & \textbf{Diagnostic question} \\
\midrule
Nominative & subject; predicate noun or adjective & What controls the finite
verb? What is predicated or identified? \\
Genitive & possession, source, description, part & Of whom or of what? \\
Dative & recipient, beneficiary, reference & To or for whom? \\
Accusative & direct object; goal after certain prepositions & Whom or what is
affected? Toward/into what? \\
Ablative & separation, source, means, manner, accompaniment, location & By,
with, from, or in what? \\
Vocative & direct address & Whom is the speaker addressing? \\
Locative remnants & place where in a few names and nouns & Where? \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

Case is a form, not a translation. An ablative can be translated
\English{with}, \English{by}, \English{from}, or another phrase according to
context. A dative may need \English{to} in English even when no Latin word for
\English{to} appears. First identify the form; then state its syntactic use;
only then choose English.

\section{Agreement and government}

\Term{Agreement} means that one word matches grammatical features of another.
An adjective agrees with its noun in \Term{case, number, and gender}, but the
noun and adjective need not display the same letters. In \Latin{Spiritui
Sancto}, both words are dative singular masculine even though one ends in
\Latin{-ui} and the other in \Latin{-o}. \Term{Government} means that a word
requires its dependent to take a particular form. Thus a preposition governs a
case: \Latin{cum} takes the ablative; \Latin{ad} takes the accusative. A verb
can also govern a case, as \Latin{miserere} takes a genitive or dative object
in ecclesiastical usage. Agreement matches features; government imposes a
form. Do not confuse the two.

\begin{grammarbox}{A complete nominal parse}
For every noun, adjective, or pronoun write: dictionary form; case; number;
gender; syntactic function; contextual sense. If a form permits more than one
case, list the possibilities and eliminate them by agreement, government, and
clause structure.
\end{grammarbox}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Gloria Patri, et Filio, et Spiritui Sancto}}{W.I.4}
\Latin{Gloria} is nominative singular and names what is ascribed: \English{glory}.
\Latin{Patri}, \Latin{Filio}, and \Latin{Spiritui} are coordinated dative
singular recipients: \English{to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Spirit}.
\Latin{Sancto} is dative singular masculine and agrees with \Latin{Spiritui}.
Translation: \English{Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy
Spirit.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Agnus Dei, qui tollis peccata mundi}}{W.I.5}
\Latin{Agnus} is nominative singular masculine, used as direct address with a
nominative-shaped liturgical title. \Latin{Dei} is genitive singular,
\English{of God}. \Latin{qui} is nominative singular masculine because it
refers to \Latin{Agnus} and is the subject of \Latin{tollis}. \Latin{peccata}
is accusative plural neuter, direct object; \Latin{mundi} is genitive singular.
Translation: \English{Lamb of God, who take away the sins of the world.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Pax hominibus bonæ voluntatis} (Lk 2:14)}{W.I.6}
\Latin{Pax} is nominative singular in a verbless nominal acclamation or wish;
conventional English may supply \English{be}, while analytic Latin may mark
interpretive \Latin{[sit]}. \Latin{hominibus} is dative plural, the recipients of peace.
\Latin{voluntatis} is genitive singular; \Latin{bonæ} agrees with it as
genitive singular feminine. Literal translation: \English{Peace to human
beings of good will.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Domine, non sum dignus}}{W.I.7}
\Latin{Domine} is vocative singular of \Latin{dominus}. \Latin{sum} is first
singular present; its subject \Latin{ego} is contained in the ending.
\Latin{dignus} is nominative singular masculine, a predicate adjective agreeing
with the understood subject. Translation: \English{Lord, I am not worthy.}
\end{workedexample}

\section{Exact paradigms}

The \Term{declension} is the noun's pattern of case endings. First-declension
genitive singular ends in \Latin{-æ}; second-declension genitive singular in
\Latin{-i}. A \Term{paradigm} is an ordered display of every form in such a
pattern; each cell represents a particular case and number. Learn forms
vertically and horizontally.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc|cc|cc@{}}
\toprule
& \multicolumn{2}{c}{\Latin{missa, -æ f.}} &
\multicolumn{2}{c}{\Latin{dominus, -i m.}} &
\multicolumn{2}{c}{\Latin{verbum, -i n.}} \\
& sg. & pl. & sg. & pl. & sg. & pl. \\
\midrule
Nom. & missa & missæ & dominus & domini & verbum & verba \\
Gen. & missæ & missarum & domini & dominorum & verbi & verborum \\
Dat. & missæ & missis & domino & dominis & verbo & verbis \\
Acc. & missam & missas & dominum & dominos & verbum & verba \\
Abl. & missa & missis & domino & dominis & verbo & verbis \\
Voc. & missa & missæ & domine & domini & verbum & verba \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

In every neuter noun, nominative and accusative are identical; their plural
ends in \Latin{-a}. A regular masculine noun in \Latin{-us} has vocative
singular \Latin{-e}: \Latin{dominus} $\rightarrow$ \Latin{Domine}.
Nouns in \Latin{-ius} normally have vocative \Latin{-i}, as \Latin{filius}
$\rightarrow$ \Latin{fili}. \Latin{Deus} has liturgical and classical
irregularities; learn its attested forms in phrases rather than forcing every
slot into the regular \Latin{dominus} pattern.

Biblical and liturgical Latin regularly uses masculine plural
\Latin{cæli, cælorum} alongside neuter singular \Latin{cælum, cæli}, rather
than the regular neuter plural \Latin{cæla}. This explains masculine plural
\Latin{pleni} in \Latin{pleni sunt cæli et terra}; record the singular and
liturgical plural behavior together in the lexicon.

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{In principio creavit Deus cælum et terram} (Gn 1:1)}{W.I.8}
\Latin{in} with ablative \Latin{principio} means \English{in the beginning}.
\Latin{creavit} is the finite verb, \English{created}. \Latin{Deus} is
nominative singular subject. \Latin{cælum} is accusative singular neuter and
\Latin{terram} accusative singular feminine; they are coordinated direct
objects. Translation: \English{In the beginning God created heaven and earth.}
The ending and syntax distinguish accusative \Latin{cælum} from the identical
nominative form.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Per omnia sæcula sæculorum}}{W.I.9}
\Latin{per} governs accusative. \Latin{omnia} is accusative plural neuter and
agrees with \Latin{sæcula}, accusative plural neuter. \Latin{sæculorum} is
genitive plural, \English{of ages}. Literal translation: \English{through all
ages of ages}; idiomatically, \English{for ever and ever}.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Deo gratias}}{W.I.10}
The second-declension ending \Latin{-o} could be dative or ablative singular.
Here the implied act of giving thanks needs a recipient, so \Latin{Deo} is
dative, not ablative. \Latin{gratias} is first-declension accusative plural.
Translation: \English{Thanks be to God.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Peccata mundi}}{W.I.11}
\Latin{peccata} is nominative or accusative plural neuter of \Latin{peccatum};
the clause decides which. \Latin{mundi} is genitive singular of
\Latin{mundus}, \English{of the world}. In \Latin{qui tollis peccata mundi},
the verb \Latin{tollis} makes \Latin{peccata} its accusative object:
\English{the sins of the world}.
\end{workedexample}

\section{Stem first, then endings}

Third-declension nominatives are unpredictable. The genitive singular always
ends in \Latin{-is}; remove that ending to find the working stem. Thus
\Latin{rex, regis} gives \Latin{reg-}; \Latin{nomen, nominis} gives
\Latin{nomin-}; \Latin{altare, altaris} gives \Latin{altar-}.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc|cc|cc@{}}
\toprule
& \multicolumn{2}{c}{\Latin{rex, regis m.}} &
\multicolumn{2}{c}{\Latin{nomen, nominis n.}} &
\multicolumn{2}{c}{\Latin{altare, altaris n.} (i-stem)} \\
& sg. & pl. & sg. & pl. & sg. & pl. \\
\midrule
Nom. & rex & reges & nomen & nomina & altare & altaria \\
Gen. & regis & regum & nominis & nominum & altaris & altarium \\
Dat. & regi & regibus & nomini & nominibus & altari & altaribus \\
Acc. & regem & reges & nomen & nomina & altare & altaria \\
Abl. & rege & regibus & nomine & nominibus & altari & altaribus \\
Voc. & rex & reges & nomen & nomina & altare & altaria \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

An \Term{i-stem} is a third-declension subtype that uses a characteristic
\Latin{i} in certain case endings; a \Term{consonant stem} uses the ordinary
third-declension endings without that characteristic \Latin{i}. Masculine and
feminine i-stems normally have genitive plural \Latin{-ium};
neuter i-stems have ablative singular \Latin{-i}, nominative/accusative plural
\Latin{-ia}, and genitive plural \Latin{-ium}. A useful neuter i-stem test is
a nominative in \Latin{-e}, \Latin{-al}, or \Latin{-ar}. Consonant-stem neuters
retain plural \Latin{-a} and genitive plural \Latin{-um}. Dictionaries should
be consulted where stem classification is uncertain.

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Introibo ad altare Dei} (Ps 42:4)}{W.I.12}
\Latin{introibo} is future, \English{I shall go in}. \Latin{ad} governs the
accusative; neuter \Latin{altare} is therefore accusative singular even though
it looks like the nominative. \Latin{Dei} is genitive singular,
\English{of God}. Translation: \English{I shall go unto the altar of God.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Pax hominibus bonæ voluntatis}}{W.I.13}
\Latin{pax, pacis f.} is nominative singular. \Latin{hominibus}, from
\Latin{homo, hominis m.}, is dative plural. \Latin{voluntatis}, from
\Latin{voluntas, voluntatis f.}, is genitive singular. Translation:
\English{Peace to people of good will.} Three different third-declension stems
are recoverable only from their genitives.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Corpus Domini nostri Iesu Christi custodiat animam meam}}{W.I.14}
\Latin{corpus, corporis n.} is nominative singular and subject of
\Latin{custodiat}. The chain \Latin{Domini nostri Iesu Christi} modifies
\Latin{corpus}; its central nouns are genitive. \Latin{animam meam} is
accusative singular object. Translation: \English{May the Body of our Lord
Jesus Christ preserve my soul.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{In nomine Patris, et Filii, et Spiritus Sancti}}{W.I.15}
\Latin{in} takes ablative \Latin{nomine}, from \Latin{nomen, nominis n.}
The coordinated possessors \Latin{Patris}, \Latin{Filii}, and
\Latin{Spiritus Sancti} are genitive singular. Translation: \English{In the
name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit.}
\end{workedexample}

\section{Fourth-declension forms}

The fourth-declension marker is genitive singular \Latin{-us}. Most nouns in
\Latin{-us} are masculine, but \Latin{manus, manus f.}, \English{hand}, is a
frequent exception. Neuters such as \Latin{genu, genus n.}, \English{knee},
obey the universal neuter rule: nominative and accusative are identical, and
their plural ends in \Latin{-a}.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc|cc@{}}
\toprule
& \multicolumn{2}{c}{\Latin{spiritus, spiritus m.}} &
\multicolumn{2}{c}{\Latin{genu, genus n.}} \\
& sg. & pl. & sg. & pl. \\
\midrule
Nom. & spiritus & spiritus & genu & genua \\
Gen. & spiritus & spirituum & genus & genuum \\
Dat. & spiritui & spiritibus & genu & genibus \\
Acc. & spiritum & spiritus & genu & genua \\
Abl. & spiritu & spiritibus & genu & genibus \\
Voc. & spiritus & spiritus & genu & genua \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Length marks would distinguish several forms in a fully marked dictionary,
but ordinary Missals omit them. Therefore \Latin{spiritus} can be nominative
singular, genitive singular, nominative plural, accusative plural, vocative
singular, or vocative plural. Government, agreement, and clause function must
decide among them.

\section{Fifth-declension forms}

The fifth-declension marker is genitive singular \Latin{-ei}. Most nouns are
feminine. \Latin{dies, diei}, \English{day}, is usually masculine, though it
can be feminine when it denotes an appointed day. The regular model is
\Latin{res, rei f.}, whose broad range includes \English{thing, matter,
affair}, and \English{reality}.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
& \multicolumn{2}{c}{\Latin{res, rei f.}} \\
& sg. & pl. \\
\midrule
Nom. & res & res \\
Gen. & rei & rerum \\
Dat. & rei & rebus \\
Acc. & rem & res \\
Abl. & re & rebus \\
Voc. & res & res \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Et cum spiritu tuo}}{W.I.16}
\Latin{cum} governs the ablative. Thus \Latin{spiritu} is ablative singular,
and possessive adjective \Latin{tuo} agrees with it in masculine singular
ablative. Translation: \English{And with your spirit.}

\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Ut in nomine Iesu omne genu flectatur} (Phil 2:10)}{W.I.19}
\Latin{in} governs ablative \Latin{nomine}; \Latin{Iesu} is genitive singular.
Neuter \Latin{genu} is nominative singular, the subject of passive

\end{workedexample}

\section{A five-declension decision procedure}

For every noun, first write its dictionary genitive and gender. Derive the
stem, then let syntax select case and number before selecting an ending. Do
not assign a case from the ending alone. For example, \Latin{-ibus} narrows a
form to dative or ablative plural, but a governing \Latin{cum} settles the
choice; a recipient may instead require the dative. Treat irregular
\Latin{domus, domus f.} as a dictionary item: its forms mix second- and
fourth-declension patterns.

A one-termination adjective lists nominative and genitive, for example

noun, not to the adjective's declension.

\begin{grammarbox}{Agreement is a matching rule}
An adjective must match its noun in gender, number, and case, but its ending
need not look like the noun's ending. In \Latin{Patris omnipotentis}, both
words are masculine singular genitive; in \Latin{gloria magna}, both are
feminine singular nominative. Determine the noun first, then choose the
adjective coordinates.
\end{grammarbox}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Requiem æternam dona eis, Domine, et lux perpetua luceat eis}}{W.I.22}
\Latin{æternam} is feminine singular accusative with object \Latin{requiem};
\Latin{perpetua} is feminine singular nominative with subject \Latin{lux}.

light shine upon them.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Omnipotens sempiterne Deus}}{W.I.23}
\Latin{Deus} is being addressed. One-termination \Latin{omnipotens} has a

\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Corpus Domini nostri Iesu Christi custodiat animam meam in vitam æternam}}{W.I.24}

\section{The indicative of \Latin{sum}}

The \Term{indicative mood} normally presents an action or state as asserted or
asked about, rather than commanded, wished, or treated as contingent.
\Latin{Sum}, \English{be}, is a \Term{linking verb} or \Term{copula}: it joins
a subject to a predicate complement rather than transferring action to a
direct object.

A verb's \Term{present system} comprises present, imperfect, and future; its
\Term{perfect system} comprises perfect, pluperfect, and future perfect. The
present and perfect systems use different stems in ordinary verbs. The
\Term{subjunctive mood}, previewed here in prayer formulas, can present an
action as wished, commanded, possible, or dependent rather than simply
asserted; Volume II teaches its complete formation and uses.

The present infinitive is \Latin{esse}, the perfect infinitive
\Latin{fuisse}, and the imperatives are \Latin{es} and \Latin{este}.

does not take an accusative object. In prayers, present subjunctives
\Latin{sit}, \English{may it be}, and \Latin{sint}, \English{may they be},
often express a wish. For now, recognize those two forms without attempting a
complete subjunctive system.

\section{Conjugations and present endings}

A \Term{conjugation} is a verb's regular pattern of inflection. A full verb
entry begins with \Term{principal parts}, the dictionary forms from which its
major stems and systems are recovered, for example
\Latin{laudo, laudare, laudavi, laudatum}. The second principal part identifies
the conjugation: \Latin{-are}, first; \Latin{-ere} with long stem vowel in a
marked dictionary, second; short-vowel \Latin{-ere}, third; and \Latin{-ire},
fourth. Third-conjugation \Latin{-io} verbs, such as
\Latin{capio, capere, cepi, captum}, share features of the third and fourth.
The present active personal endings are \Latin{-o/-m, -s, -t, -mus, -tis,
-nt}.

The \Term{present stem} is the base used to build the present, imperfect, and
future systems. An \Term{infinitive} is a non-finite verbal form: it has tense
and voice but no grammatical person, and can complete another verb or perform
a noun-like job. An \Term{imperative} directly gives a command. A \Term{finite}
verb, by contrast, carries person and number and can anchor its own clause.

\section{Imperfect and future}

The imperfect denotes continuing, repeated, or incomplete past action. Its
forms are \Latin{laudabam}, \Latin{monebam}, \Latin{regebam},
\Latin{capiebam}, and \Latin{audiebam}, followed through
\Latin{-bas, -bat, -bamus, -batis, -bant}. First- and second-conjugation
futures use \Latin{-bo, -bis, -bit, -bimus, -bitis, -bunt}:
\Latin{laudabo} and \Latin{monebo}. Third, third-\Latin{io}, and fourth use
\Latin{-am, -es, -et, -emus, -etis, -ent}:
\Latin{regam, reges}; \Latin{capiam, capies};
\Latin{audiam, audies}.

Present active infinitives are \Latin{laudare, monere, regere, capere,
audire}. Singular present imperatives remove \Latin{-re}:
\Latin{lauda, mone, rege, cape, audi}. Plural imperatives use the present stem
plus \Latin{-te}; third and third-\Latin{io} insert \Latin{-i-}:
\Latin{laudate, monete, regite, capite, audite}. Common irregular imperatives
should be learned when encountered.

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Laudamus te. Benedicimus te. Adoramus te. Glorificamus te.}}{W.I.30}
Every verb is first-person plural present active indicative, ending in
\Latin{-mus}; \Latin{te} is the singular object each time. Translation:
\English{We praise you. We bless you. We adore you. We glorify you.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Credo in unum Deum}}{W.I.31}
\Latin{credo} is first-person singular present active indicative of
\Latin{credo, credere}. Here \Latin{in} with the accusative expresses the one
toward whom belief is directed. Translation: \English{I believe in one God.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Agnus Dei, qui tollis peccata mundi, miserere nobis}}{W.I.32}
\Latin{tollis} is second-person singular present active indicative because
relative \Latin{qui} addresses the Lamb; \Latin{peccata} is its neuter plural
object. \Latin{miserere} is a deponent imperative, previewed here rather than
forced into the active pattern. Translation: \English{Lamb of God, who take
away the sins of the world, have mercy on us.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Petite, et accipietis} (Jn 16:24)}{W.I.33}
\Latin{petite} is second-person plural present active imperative.
\Latin{accipietis} is second-person plural future active indicative of the
third-\Latin{io} verb \Latin{accipio}: \English{Ask, and you will receive.}
\end{workedexample}

\section{One stem, three tenses}

The third principal part is the first-person singular perfect active. Remove
final \Latin{-i} to find the perfect stem: \Latin{laudavi} gives
\Latin{laudav-}; \Latin{monui} gives \Latin{monu-}; \Latin{rexi} gives
\Latin{rex-}; \Latin{cepi} gives \Latin{cep-}; \Latin{audivi} gives
\Latin{audiv-}. Perfect stems must be learned from principal parts rather than
guessed from conjugation.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccccc@{}}
\toprule
& 1 sg. & 2 sg. & 3 sg. & 1 pl. & 2 pl. & 3 pl. \\
\midrule
Perfect endings & -i & -isti & -it & -imus & -istis & -erunt \\
\Latin{laudo} & laudavi & laudavisti & laudavit & laudavimus & laudavistis & laudaverunt \\
Pluperfect & laudaveram & laudaveras & laudaverat & laudaveramus & laudaveratis & laudaverant \\
Future perfect & laudavero & laudaveris & laudaverit & laudaverimus & laudaveritis & laudaverint \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The three tenses order completed actions from different reference points. The
\Term{perfect} presents an action as completed relative to the present or as a
completed past event; the \Term{pluperfect} presents it as completed before
another past point; the \Term{future perfect} presents it as completed before
a future point. The alternative third-person plural perfect ending \Latin{-ere} occurs in
Latin generally, though \Latin{-erunt} is the safer production form. The
perfect reports a completed past event, often \English{did} or \English{has
done}; the pluperfect reports an event already completed before another past
point, \English{had done}; the future perfect reports completion before a
future point, \English{will have done}. In contrast, the imperfect normally
presents a past action as continuing, repeated, or incomplete.

carried from the preceding Creed. Translation: \English{And he rose again on
the third day, according to the Scriptures.}
\end{workedexample}

\end{workedexample}

  \item recognize common deponents as passive in form but active in meaning.
\end{learningobjectives}

\section{Present-system passive forms}

In the \Term{active voice}, the grammatical subject is presented as acting or
being in a state rather than receiving an action; in the \Term{passive voice},
the subject receives the action. The
\Term{agent} is the person or being that performs a passive action, whether or
not the sentence expresses that agent. The present passive personal endings
are \Latin{-r, -ris} (or
\Latin{-re}), \Latin{-tur, -mur, -mini, -ntur}. The grammatical subject
receives the action; an expressed agent is not required.

\begin{center}\scriptsize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccc@{}}
\toprule
& 1st \Latin{laudor} & 2nd \Latin{moneor} & 3rd \Latin{regor} &
3rd-\Latin{io} \Latin{capior} & 4th \Latin{audior} \\
\midrule
1 sg. & laudor & moneor & regor & capior & audior \\
2 sg. & laudaris & moneris & regeris & caperis & audiris \\
3 sg. & laudatur & monetur & regitur & capitur & auditur \\
1 pl. & laudamur & monemur & regimur & capimur & audimur \\
2 pl. & laudamini & monemini & regimini & capimini & audimini \\
3 pl. & laudantur & monentur & reguntur & capiuntur & audiuntur \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Imperfect passives replace the active endings with passive endings after the
same tense sign: \Latin{laudabar, monebar, regebar, capiebar, audiebar}.
First- and second-conjugation futures begin
\Latin{laudabor, laudaberis, laudabitur} and
\Latin{monebor, moneberis, monebitur}; third, third-\Latin{io}, and fourth
begin \Latin{regar, regeris, regetur};
\Latin{capiar, capieris, capietur}; and
\Latin{audiar, audieris, audietur}. Present passive infinitives are
\Latin{laudari, moneri, regi, capi, audiri}; note the third-conjugation
\Latin{-i}, not \Latin{-eri}.

\section{Perfect-system passives}

The fourth principal part supplies the \Term{perfect passive participle}, a
verbal adjective expressing action received and prior to the reference point.
Combine it
with a form of \Latin{sum}, making the participle agree with the subject:
\Latin{laudatus sum}, \English{I, masculine, was or have been praised};
\Latin{laudata est}, \English{she was praised};
\Latin{laudati sunt}, \English{they, masculine or mixed, were praised}; and
\Latin{laudata sunt}, \English{neuter things were praised}. Present forms of
\Latin{sum} make the perfect passive, imperfect forms make the pluperfect
passive, and future forms make the future perfect passive:
\Latin{laudatus eram}, \English{I had been praised};
\Latin{laudatus ero}, \English{I shall have been praised}.

A personal agent normally takes \Latin{a/ab} plus ablative:
\Latin{a Domino}, \English{by the Lord}. An impersonal means or instrument
normally uses the ablative alone: \Latin{verbo sanatur}, \English{it is healed
by a word}.

\section{Deponents}

\Term{Deponent verbs} use passive morphology with active meaning. Their forms
look passive, but their syntax and English meanings are active; they can, for
example, govern the same kinds of objects as active verbs. Learn their
principal parts in deponent form: \Latin{confiteor, confiteri, confessus sum},
\English{confess}; \Latin{sequor, sequi, secutus sum}, \English{follow};
\Latin{misereor, misereri, misertus sum}, \English{have mercy}. Thus
\Latin{sequitur} means \English{he or she follows}, not \English{is followed},
and \Latin{confessus est} means \English{he confessed}, not \English{he was
confessed}.

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Ite, missa est}}{W.I.41}
\Latin{ite} is the irregular second-person plural imperative of
\Latin{eo}, \English{go}. In this Late Latin formula, \Latin{missa} is a
feminine singular noun meaning \English{dismissal}, serving as the subject or
nominal assertion with \Latin{est}: \English{Go; it is the dismissal}. It is
historically related to \Latin{mitto}, but a participial ellipsis should not be
presented as the sole contextual parse.
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Per quem omnia facta sunt}}{W.I.42}
\Latin{omnia} is neuter plural nominative. \Latin{facta sunt} is neuter plural
perfect of \Latin{fio, fieri, factus sum}, \English{come to be, be made}, not a
regular passive form of \Latin{facio}. Translation: \English{Through whom all
things were made.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Petite, et dabitur vobis} (Mt 7:7)}{W.I.43}
\Latin{dabitur} is third-person singular future passive indicative of
\Latin{do}; its subject, \English{it}, is understood. \Latin{vobis} is dative
plural recipient. Translation: \English{Ask, and it will be given to you.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Confiteor Deo omnipotenti}}{W.I.44}
\Latin{confiteor} is first-person singular present deponent indicative and has
active meaning. \Latin{Deo omnipotenti} is dative singular. Translation:
\English{I confess to almighty God.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Qui sequitur me, non ambulat in tenebris} (Jn 8:12)}{W.I.45}
\Latin{sequitur} is third-person singular present deponent; \Latin{me} is its
object. \Latin{ambulat} is present active. Translation: \English{He who
follows me does not walk in darkness.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Misereatur vestri omnipotens Deus}}{W.I.46}
\Latin{misereatur} is third-person singular present deponent subjunctive,
expressing a prayer; its meaning is active, \English{may he have mercy}.
\Latin{vestri} is the genitive object found in this formula, and
\Latin{omnipotens Deus} is the nominative subject. Translation:
\English{May almighty God have mercy on you.}
\end{workedexample}

\section{Personal and reflexive pronouns}

A \Term{pronoun} stands for a noun phrase or points to a participant without
repeating its noun. A \Term{personal pronoun} identifies speaker, addressee, or
another participant. A \Term{reflexive pronoun} refers back to the subject of
its own clause; a \Term{possessive adjective} identifies a possessor but agrees
with the thing possessed. These categories describe different relations, even
when English translates them with similar words.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc|cc|c@{}}
\toprule
& \multicolumn{2}{c}{First person} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{Second person} & Reflexive \\
& sg. & pl. & sg. & pl. & sg./pl. \\
\midrule
Nom. & ego & nos & tu & vos & --- \\
Gen. & mei & nostri/nostrum & tui & vestri/vestrum & sui \\
Dat. & mihi & nobis & tibi & vobis & sibi \\
Acc. & me & nos & te & vos & se \\
Abl. & me & nobis & te & vobis & se \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Forms in \Latin{-i} such as \Latin{nostri} commonly express an objective
relation, while \Latin{nostrum} is normally partitive; the same distinction
holds for \Latin{vestri/vestrum}. With personal pronouns, \Latin{cum} follows
its object: \Latin{mecum, tecum, nobiscum, vobiscum, secum}. Possessives
\Latin{meus, tuus, noster, vester}, and \Latin{suus} are adjectives and agree
with the thing possessed. \Latin{Suus} refers back to the clause's subject;
\Latin{eius} or \Latin{eorum/earum} normally means \English{his, her}, or
\English{their} when the possessor is not that subject.

In reported thought, a subordinate clause, or some biblical and later usage,
a reflexive may instead follow the sentence's logical point of view. Label this
an \Term{indirect reflexive} only when the wider syntax and source identify that
center; do not use the exception merely to evade the ordinary subject rule.

\section{\Latin{Is, ea, id} and \Latin{hic, hæc, hoc}}

A \Term{demonstrative} points out or identifies a referent (``this,'' ``that,''
or sometimes ``he/she/it''). An \Term{emphatic pronoun} adds identity or
emphasis (``self,'' ``the very one'') rather than reflexively directing the
action back upon the subject.

\section{The relative pronoun}

A \Term{relative pronoun} introduces a subordinate clause that describes or
identifies an \Term{antecedent}, the noun or noun-equivalent to which the
pronoun refers. The antecedent supplies gender and number; the pronoun's own
job inside its clause supplies case.

\begin{center}\scriptsize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc|ccc@{}}
\toprule
& \multicolumn{3}{c}{\Latin{qui, quæ, quod} singular} &
\multicolumn{3}{c}{plural} \\
& m. & f. & n. & m. & f. & n. \\
\midrule
Nom. & qui & quæ & quod & qui & quæ & quæ \\
Gen. & cuius & cuius & cuius & quorum & quarum & quorum \\
Dat. & cui & cui & cui & quibus & quibus & quibus \\
Acc. & quem & quam & quod & quos & quas & quæ \\
Abl. & quo & qua & quo & quibus & quibus & quibus \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A relative pronoun takes gender and number from its antecedent but case from
its own job inside the relative clause. In \Latin{Agnus qui tollis},
\Latin{qui} is masculine singular because of \Latin{Agnus} and nominative
because it is the subject of \Latin{tollis}. In
\Latin{dies quam fecit Dominus}, \Latin{quam} is singular and feminine in its
context because of \Latin{dies}, but accusative because it is the object of
\Latin{fecit}.

\section{Core preposition government}

Learn prepositions together with their case. Core accusative governors are
\Latin{ad} (to), \Latin{ante} (before), \Latin{apud} (with, in the presence
of), \Latin{contra} (against), \Latin{inter} (among), \Latin{ob} (on account
of), \Latin{per} (through), \Latin{post} (after), \Latin{propter} (because
of), \Latin{secundum} (according to), and \Latin{trans} (across). Core
ablative governors are \Latin{a/ab} (from, by), \Latin{cum}
(with), \Latin{de} (down from, concerning), \Latin{e/ex} (out of),
\Latin{pro} (for, on behalf of), and \Latin{sine} (without).
\Latin{In} and \Latin{sub} normally take the accusative for motion toward and
the ablative for location: \Latin{in ecclesiam}, \English{into the church};
\Latin{in ecclesia}, \English{in the church}.

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Dominus vobiscum}}{W.I.47}
\Latin{vobiscum} is \Latin{cum} plus ablative plural \Latin{vobis} in its
required postpositive order. The conventional optative construal may be shown
with interpretive \Latin{[sit]}: \English{The Lord be with you.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Propter nos homines et propter nostram salutem descendit de cælis}}{W.I.48}
Both \Latin{nos} and \Latin{nostram salutem} are accusative after
\Latin{propter}; \Latin{nostram} agrees with \Latin{salutem}, not with
\Latin{nos}. \Latin{de} governs ablative plural \Latin{cælis}. Translation:
\English{For us men and for our salvation he came down from heaven.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Per ipsum, et cum ipso, et in ipso}}{W.I.49}
The same masculine singular pronoun changes case: \Latin{ipsum} is accusative
after \Latin{per}; both instances of \Latin{ipso} are ablative after
\Latin{cum} and after \Latin{in} denoting location or state. Translation: \English{Through him, and
with him, and in him.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Hoc est enim Corpus meum}}{W.I.50}
Neuter singular \Latin{hoc} is nominative subject; \Latin{Corpus} is the
nominative predicate noun, and possessive \Latin{meum} agrees with it.
Translation: \English{For this is my Body.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{\Latin{Pater noster, qui es in cælis, dimitte nobis debita nostra}}{W.I.51}
\Latin{noster} is vocative masculine singular with \Latin{Pater}; relative
\Latin{qui} is nominative subject of \Latin{es}. \Latin{in} governs ablative
plural \Latin{cælis}; \Latin{nobis} is dative recipient; \Latin{debita
nostra} is neuter plural accusative object. Translation: \English{Our Father,
who are in heaven, forgive us our debts.}
\end{workedexample}

\section{The seven-pass reading method}

\Term{Morphology} is the analysis of word forms and the grammatical information
their parts express. \Term{Syntax} is the way those forms combine into phrases
and clauses. A reliable reading needs both: morphology proposes what a form can
be, while syntax selects its contextual function.

Read a Missal sentence in seven deliberate passes.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Mark punctuation and conjunctions; bracket each likely clause.
  \item Underline every finite verb and parse person, number, tense, voice,
        and mood. Supply an omitted form only in square brackets.
  \item Find each verb's explicit or understood nominative subject.
  \item For every noun, write dictionary form, gender, case, number, and
        clause function. List ambiguity before resolving it.
  \item Draw agreement arrows from adjectives, participles, and pronouns.
  \item Box every preposition with its governed phrase and identify every
        genitive or dative relation.
  \item Write a close translation that preserves the grammar, then a natural
        translation that preserves the sense. Record any departure.
\end{enumerate}

Latin word order signals emphasis more often than grammatical function.
Endings, government, and agreement carry the structure. A prayer may omit a
copula, repeat no noun after a clear antecedent, or retain an indeclinable
Hebrew acclamation. Such recoverable omission is \Term{ellipsis}; supply the
smallest missing element in brackets. An \Term{indeclinable} word does not
change form to mark case, number, or gender. Do not invent morphology for \Latin{Amen, Alleluia,
Hosanna}, or \Latin{Sabaoth}; identify each as indeclinable and continue.

\section{Guided Missal readings}

\begin{workedexample}{The opening of the Gloria}{W.I.52}
\Latin{Gloria in excelsis Deo, et in terra pax hominibus bonæ voluntatis.
Laudamus te, benedicimus te, adoramus te, glorificamus te.}

The first sentence is a coordinated verbless acclamation conventionally read
as a wish; an analytic paraphrase may supply interpretive \Latin{[sit]} twice,
but no finite verb is printed. Nominatives \Latin{gloria} and \Latin{pax} are
the wished-for terms. \Latin{Deo} and
\Latin{hominibus} are dative recipients; \Latin{in excelsis} uses
substantival neuter plural ablative, \English{in the highest places};
\Latin{in terra} is \Latin{in} + ablative of place where;
\Latin{bonæ voluntatis} is genitive.
The four following verbs are first-person plural present active and each
takes accusative \Latin{te}. Close translation: \English{Glory [be] to God in
the highest places, and on earth peace [be] to people of good will. We praise
you, bless you, adore you, glorify you.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{The Sanctus and Benedictus}{W.I.53}
\Latin{Sanctus, Sanctus, Sanctus Dominus Deus Sabaoth. Pleni sunt cæli et
terra gloria tua. Hosanna in excelsis. Benedictus qui venit in nomine Domini.
Hosanna in excelsis.}

Three predicate or acclamatory \Latin{Sanctus} forms agree with nominative
\Latin{Dominus}; \Latin{Deus} is appositional and \Latin{Sabaoth} is
indeclinable. Compound subject \Latin{cæli et terra} governs plural
\Latin{sunt}; predicate \Latin{pleni} is plural, while \Latin{gloria tua} is
ablative after \Latin{pleni}. \Latin{Benedictus} is a substantival masculine
singular nominative in a verbless acclamation; conventional English supplies a
copula, but none is printed. Relative \Latin{qui} identifies the blessed one
and is the subject of present \Latin{venit}; \Latin{in nomine Domini} contains an ablative
prepositional phrase plus a genitive. Translation: \English{Holy,
Holy, Holy Lord God of hosts. Heaven and earth are full of your glory. Hosanna
in the highest. Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord. Hosanna in
the highest.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{The Agnus Dei}{W.I.54}
\Latin{Agnus Dei, qui tollis peccata mundi, miserere nobis. Agnus Dei, qui
tollis peccata mundi, dona nobis pacem.}

Nominative \Latin{Agnus} is used as a direct address in this formula and is
modified by genitive \Latin{Dei}; the regular standalone vocative would be
\Latin{Agne}. Relative
\Latin{qui} is nominative subject of second-person singular \Latin{tollis}
because the Lamb is addressed directly. Neuter plural \Latin{peccata} is the
object, and \Latin{mundi} is genitive. Deponent imperative \Latin{miserere}
has active sense and here takes \Latin{nobis}; active imperative \Latin{dona}
takes dative recipient \Latin{nobis} and accusative object \Latin{pacem}.
Translation: \English{Lamb of God, who take away the sins of the world, have
mercy on us. Lamb of God, who take away the sins of the world, grant us
peace.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{The prayer before Communion}{W.I.55}
\Latin{Domine, non sum dignus ut intres sub tectum meum: sed tantum dic verbo,
et sanabitur anima mea.}

\Latin{Domine} is vocative. \Latin{sum} links its implicit first-person
subject to nominative predicate \Latin{dignus}. \Latin{intres} is
second-person singular present active subjunctive in an \Latin{ut}-clause
completing \Latin{dignus};
\Latin{sub} takes accusative \Latin{tectum} because entry is envisioned, and
\Latin{meum} agrees. With irregular singular imperative \Latin{dic},
\Latin{verbo} is ablative of means; \Latin{sanabitur} is third-person singular future
passive with nominative subject \Latin{anima mea}. Translation:
\English{Lord, I am not worthy that you should enter under my roof; but only
say the word, and my soul shall be healed.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{The Johannine Last Gospel}{W.I.56}
\Latin{In principio erat Verbum, et Verbum erat apud Deum, et Deus erat
Verbum. Hoc erat in principio apud Deum. Omnia per ipsum facta sunt.}

Each \Latin{erat} is third-person singular imperfect of \Latin{sum}.
\Latin{Verbum} is subject in the first two clauses. In
\Latin{Deus erat Verbum}, both nouns are nominative; word order and context,
not an accusative ending, distinguish subject \Latin{Verbum} from predicate
\Latin{Deus}. \Latin{apud} governs accusative \Latin{Deum}. Demonstrative
\Latin{Hoc} is neuter singular nominative. In the final sentence,
\Latin{omnia} is neuter plural subject, \Latin{per ipsum} is accusative after
\Latin{per}, and \Latin{facta sunt} agrees in neuter plural. Translation:
\English{In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the
Word was God. He was in the beginning with God. All things were made through
him.}
\end{workedexample}

\begin{workedexample}{A Psalm sentence used at the altar}{W.I.57}
\Latin{Emitte lucem tuam et veritatem tuam: ipsa me deduxerunt et adduxerunt
in montem sanctum tuum, et in tabernacula tua} (Ps 42:3).

\Latin{emitte} is second-person singular present active imperative.
\Latin{lucem tuam et veritatem tuam} are coordinated objects. Neuter plural
\Latin{ipsa} refers to the pair collectively and is the subject of perfect
\Latin{deduxerunt et adduxerunt}; \Latin{me} is their object. Both final
instances of \Latin{in} govern accusative because motion toward is expressed.
\Latin{sanctum tuum} agrees with \Latin{montem}; neuter plural \Latin{tua}
agrees with \Latin{tabernacula}. Translation: \English{Send forth your light
and your truth; they have led me and brought me unto your holy mountain and
into your tabernacles.}
\end{workedexample}
