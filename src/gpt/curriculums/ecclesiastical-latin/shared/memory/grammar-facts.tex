\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G001}{Lemma and printed form}{dictionary literacy}{State the difference between a lemma and a printed word form; give one example.}{A lemma is the dictionary headword for a lexeme; a printed form is an inflected occurrence carrying grammatical features.  In \Latin{vias}, the lemma is \Latin{via}; the printed form is accusative plural.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G002}{Phrase and clause}{sentence structure}{Give the deciding difference between a phrase and a clause.}{A phrase is organized around a head without its own predication.  A clause contains a predicate with an expressed or understood subject; the predicate is normally finite but may be non-finite in a dependent construction.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G003}{Subject, predicate, and object}{sentence structure}{Define subject, predicate, and direct object without equating the subject with the actor.}{The subject is what the clause predicates about and ordinarily controls finite-verb agreement.  The predicate is the assertion centered on the verb.  A direct object is an accusative complement selected by a transitive verb.  A passive subject is not the actor.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G004}{A noun's dictionary form}{dictionary literacy}{Write the three pieces normally learned with a noun and say what each supplies.}{Learn nominative singular, genitive singular, and gender.  The nominative identifies the lemma, the genitive reveals the stem and declension, and gender controls agreement.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G005}{Case, number, and gender}{nominal morphology}{Explain why these three coordinates must be kept distinct.}{Case marks a form's possible syntactic relations, number distinguishes singular and plural, and lexical gender controls agreement.  None can be inferred safely from an English gloss alone.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G006}{Agreement and government}{nominal syntax}{State the difference and give one example of each.}{Agreement copies relevant features: \Latin{via longa}.  Government selects a complement form: \Latin{cum via} has ablative because \Latin{cum} governs it.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G007}{First-declension endings}{noun paradigms}{Write the first-declension singular and plural endings in nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, ablative, vocative order.}{Singular: \Latin{-a, -æ, -æ, -am, -a, -a}.  Plural: \Latin{-æ, -arum, -is, -as, -is, -æ}.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G008}{Second-declension masculine endings}{noun paradigms}{Write the regular \Latin{-us} masculine singular and plural endings in case order, including the vocative.}{Singular: \Latin{-us, -i, -o, -um, -o, -e}.  Plural: \Latin{-i, -orum, -is, -os, -is, -i}.  Nouns in \Latin{-ius} and \Latin{Deus} require their learned vocative patterns.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G009}{The neuter rule}{noun paradigms}{State the neuter rule in full.}{In every declension, neuter nominative, accusative, and vocative are alike; in the plural those three cases end in \Latin{-a}, or the appropriate neuter plural equivalent in the paradigm.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G010}{Recovering a third-declension stem}{noun paradigms}{Give the stem procedure and apply it to \Latin{corpus, corporis}.}{Remove the genitive singular ending \Latin{-is}: \Latin{corporis} gives stem \Latin{corpor-}.  The nominative alone often conceals the stem.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G011}{Third-declension neuters and i-stems}{noun paradigms}{Name the safest evidence for recognizing these patterns.}{Use the complete dictionary entry, genitive stem, gender, and actual paradigm.  Neuter rules are secure; i-stem details depend on the word's attested pattern and cannot be guessed from one surface ending.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G012}{Syncretic forms}{morphological reasoning}{What should be done when one ending represents several cases or numbers?}{List the formally possible analyses, then use agreement, government, clause function, and sense to eliminate impossibilities.  Do not assign the first familiar label.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G013}{First/second-declension adjective agreement}{adjectives}{What coordinates does an adjective copy, and what does it not have to copy?}{It agrees with its head in gender, number, and case.  It need not share the noun's declension or an identical-looking ending.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G014}{Third-declension adjective entries}{adjectives}{Explain the information in \Latin{fidelis, fidele} and \Latin{misericors, misericordis}.}{\Latin{Fidelis, fidele} gives masculine/feminine and neuter nominatives of a two-termination adjective.  \Latin{Misericors, misericordis} gives nominative and genitive of a one-termination adjective, with stem \Latin{misericord-}.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G015}{Attributive and predicate adjectives}{adjectives}{Distinguish the two uses with a short pair.}{An attributive adjective modifies within a noun phrase: \Latin{via longa}.  A predicate adjective is asserted of the subject: \Latin{via longa est}.  Both agree with the noun.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G016}{Four principal parts}{verb morphology}{State what each ordinary active principal part contributes.}{The first-person present identifies the lemma and present shape; the present infinitive identifies conjugation and present stem; the first-person perfect supplies the perfect stem; the supine or perfect-passive form supplies the participial stem.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G017}{Conjugation from the infinitive}{verb morphology}{Give the ordinary infinitive markers for first, second, third, third-io, and fourth conjugations.}{First \Latin{-are}; second long \Latin{-ere}; third short \Latin{-ere}; third-io also short \Latin{-ere} but with a first principal part in \Latin{-io}; fourth \Latin{-ire}.  A dictionary's quantity or full entry distinguishes second from third.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G018}{Active personal endings}{verb morphology}{Write the ordinary active personal endings.}{\Latin{-o/-m, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt}.  Stem and tense markers combine with them, and irregular verbs must be learned separately.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G019}{The active present system}{verb morphology}{Name the three tenses built on the present stem and the coordinates required in a full parse.}{Present, imperfect, and future.  A full finite parse gives lemma, person, number, tense, voice, mood, subject, and clause function.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G020}{Imperfect and future signals}{verb morphology}{State the principal formation contrast between first/second and third/fourth conjugation futures.}{First and second commonly use future \Latin{-bo, -bis, -bit}; third and fourth use forms beginning \Latin{-am, -es, -et}.  The imperfect uses \Latin{-ba-} with the appropriate stem and linking vowel.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G021}{\Latin{sum} and \Latin{possum}}{irregular verbs}{State the relation between these verbs and one construction each commonly serves.}{\Latin{Sum} is an irregular copula or verb of existence; \Latin{possum} is its compound with forms adjusted before initial \Latin{s}.  \Latin{Sum} links predicates, while \Latin{possum} commonly governs a complementary infinitive.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G022}{The perfect active stem}{verb morphology}{How is the perfect stem ordinarily recovered, and why must it be memorized?}{Remove final \Latin{-i} from the third principal part.  Sound change and suppletion make many perfect stems unpredictable from the present stem, so the principal part is a lexical fact.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G023}{Perfect-system active tenses}{verb morphology}{Name the three tenses built on the perfect stem and their basic time relations.}{Perfect presents prior or completed action according to context; pluperfect is prior to a past reference point; future perfect is prior to a future reference point.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G024}{Tense and contextual time}{verb interpretation}{Why is a Latin tense name not automatically an English tense?}{Tense is a grammatical form combining temporal and aspectual information.  Context fixes reference time and discourse force, so one Latin perfect may require different natural English renderings.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G025}{Present-system passive endings}{voice}{Write the ordinary passive personal endings.}{\Latin{-r, -ris/-re, -tur, -mur, -mini, -ntur}.  Parse person, number, tense, mood, and voice before translating.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G026}{Perfect-system passive}{voice}{State the two components and their jobs.}{A perfect passive participle agrees with the subject in gender and number; a finite form of \Latin{sum} carries person, number, mood, and the tense contribution.  Both are required.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G027}{Deponents and \Latin{fio}}{voice}{Explain why passive-looking morphology does not settle meaning.}{A deponent lexeme uses passive-system forms with active or middle lexical sense, so dictionary identity controls.  \Latin{Fio} has its own paradigm and commonly supplies the present passive sense of \Latin{facio}; it is not a regular form guessed from \Latin{facio}.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G028}{Personal and reflexive reference}{pronouns}{State the distinction between a third-person reflexive and a non-reflexive pronoun.}{The reflexive refers back to the subject of its governing domain; a non-reflexive third-person form points elsewhere or resumes an antecedent.  Case still follows the pronoun's own function.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G029}{Demonstratives}{pronouns}{What must be recorded when parsing a demonstrative?}{Give lemma, gender, number, case, syntactic function, and contextual referent or discourse force.  Near/far English glosses are only starting points.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G030}{The relative-pronoun two-axis rule}{pronouns}{State how a relative pronoun receives gender, number, and case.}{Gender and number normally come from the antecedent; case comes from the relative pronoun's function inside its own clause.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G031}{Preposition government}{case syntax}{What must be memorized with every preposition?}{Memorize the case or cases governed and the contrast between them.  Then choose the contextual relation; one English preposition does not define every use.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G032}{\Latin{ad} and \Latin{in}}{case syntax}{Contrast their core place constructions.}{\Latin{Ad} governs accusative and commonly marks approach or relation.  \Latin{In} with accusative commonly marks entry or destination; \Latin{in} with ablative commonly marks location.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G033}{Postpositive particles}{discourse syntax}{Define postpositive and name three common examples.}{A postpositive normally stands after the first word or constituent of its clause while organizing the discourse.  Common examples are \Latin{autem}, \Latin{enim}, and often \Latin{igitur}; \Latin{quoque} normally follows its focus.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G034}{Infinitive time and voice}{non-finite verbs}{Name the six ordinary infinitives and how their time is read.}{Present, perfect, and future, each active and passive.  Their time is relative to the governing verb: simultaneous, prior, or subsequent, not automatically present, past, or future from the reader's moment.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G035}{Indirect statement}{non-finite syntax}{Identify its core architecture.}{A verb of saying, thinking, perceiving, or related activity may govern an accusative subject plus infinitive.  The infinitive's tense expresses relative time, and predicate complements agree as their construction requires.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G036}{Complementary infinitive}{non-finite syntax}{Distinguish it from indirect statement.}{A complementary infinitive completes a verb such as \Latin{possum} without normally introducing a separate accusative subject.  Indirect statement makes a reported predication with its own accusative subject.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G037}{The four participles}{non-finite verbs}{Name their ordinary time and voice relations.}{Present active: simultaneous active action.  Perfect passive: prior passive action.  Future active: subsequent or intended active action.  Gerundive: future/passive verbal adjective whose force depends on construction.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G038}{Ablative absolute}{non-finite syntax}{Give its core form and a responsible translation method.}{It normally contains an ablative noun or pronoun plus an agreeing ablative participle, grammatically detached from the main clause.  First state relative time and voice; then choose a contextual relation such as time, cause, concession, or circumstance without pretending the morphology alone names it.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G039}{Four subjunctive tenses}{subjunctive morphology}{Name the tenses and their ordinary formation bases.}{Present uses the present stem with its subjunctive vowel; imperfect uses the present infinitive plus endings; perfect uses the perfect stem plus \Latin{-eri-}; pluperfect uses the perfect infinitive plus endings.  Irregular verbs retain learned forms.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G040}{Sequence of tenses}{subjunctive syntax}{State the primary/historic starting distinction without turning it into a mechanical translation.}{A primary governing time ordinarily pairs with present or perfect subjunctive for simultaneous/subsequent or prior relation; a historic governing time ordinarily pairs with imperfect or pluperfect.  Construction and context still control interpretation.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G041}{Purpose and result}{subordinate clauses}{Give the principal diagnostic contrast.}{Both may use \Latin{ut} plus subjunctive.  Purpose states an intended end and is negated by \Latin{ne}; result states a consequence, is commonly negated by \Latin{ut non}, and often has degree or anticipatory signals.  The governor and discourse decide.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G042}{Indirect command}{subordinate clauses}{Identify its architecture and negation.}{A verb of asking, ordering, urging, or causing governs a content command, commonly \Latin{ut} or \Latin{ne} plus subjunctive.  Classify the governing verb before assigning the clause.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G043}{Substantive clauses}{subordinate clauses}{What makes a clause substantive?}{The whole clause fills a noun-like slot required or licensed by a governing expression: subject, object, complement, or content.  Its internal verb remains fully parsed.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G044}{Temporal, causal, and concessive relations}{subordinate clauses}{Why does a conjunction alone not settle the relation or mood?}{Forms such as \Latin{cum}, \Latin{dum}, \Latin{quia}, and \Latin{quamvis} take different constructions.  Governor, mood, tense, discourse viewpoint, and wider sentence determine whether time, cause, concession, or another relation is present.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G045}{Condition as a two-clause system}{conditions}{Name the two members and the evidence needed for classification.}{The protasis states the condition and the apodosis its consequence.  Classify the pair from both verbs' moods and tenses, time reference, and speaker stance; do not label from \Latin{si} alone.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G046}{Independent subjunctives}{subjunctive syntax}{Name four broad independent uses and the need for context.}{Jussive or hortatory, potential, deliberative, and optative or wish uses are common broad families.  Person, tense, negation, particles, and discourse force distinguish them.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G047}{Gerund and gerundive}{verbal nouns and adjectives}{State the formal difference.}{A gerund is a neuter singular verbal noun used in oblique cases and does not agree with an object.  A gerundive is a passive verbal adjective that agrees with a noun; constructions may prefer the agreeing gerundive where an object would otherwise accompany a gerund.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G048}{Passive periphrastic and supine}{verbal nouns and adjectives}{State one diagnostic for each.}{Gerundive plus \Latin{sum} can express obligation; the grammatical subject is nominative and the agent is often dative.  A supine is a restricted fourth-declension verbal noun, chiefly accusative in \Latin{-um} after motion for purpose or ablative in \Latin{-u} with certain adjectives.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G049}{Place and time}{adverbial cases}{Give a decision order for a place or time phrase.}{Identify morphology, preposition if any, lexical class of the noun, governing verb, and contextual relation.  Then distinguish location, motion toward, motion from, time when, duration, or another use.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G050}{Agent, means, manner, and cause}{adverbial cases}{Give the principal contrast between personal agent and means.}{A personal agent with a passive commonly uses \Latin{a/ab} plus ablative; an instrument or means commonly uses an unprepositioned ablative thing.  Manner and cause require their own lexical and contextual evidence.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G051}{Marked word order and hyperbaton}{word order}{What must be proved before a stylistic effect is proposed?}{First prove syntactic attachment from agreement, government, and clause structure.  Then compare a neutral scaffold with the printed order and explain what separation or placement foregrounds.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G052}{Ellipsis and reconstruction}{word order}{State the boundary on supplying omitted words.}{Supply a word only when grammar or a strong parallel requires or licenses it; place the supply in brackets.  Reconstruction preserves every printed form and is an analytical scaffold, not a rewritten source.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G053}{Collect architecture}{liturgical genres}{Name the common analytical members without forcing every Collect into one template.}{Look for address, relative or participial ground, main petition, requested purpose or effect, and conclusion.  The actual finite verbs and dependencies control; a member may be absent, combined, or genuinely ambiguous.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G054}{Genre and register}{reading method}{Distinguish genre from register.}{Genre is a recurring kind of text defined by task and convention, such as Collect or narrative.  Register is a variety associated with use or setting, such as biblical, juridical, or liturgical Latin.  Neither overrides the printed grammar.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G055}{Proposition-first composition}{composition}{State a reliable order for drafting a paragraph.}{List the propositions and their logical relations; select Latin clause architecture and attested constructions; choose vocabulary; draft the finite-verb skeleton; then complete agreement and idiom.  Do not let an English sentence dictate the Latin structure.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G056}{Evidence classes in source work}{source discipline}{Distinguish received text, adaptation, and project composition.}{Received text reproduces an identified witness; an adaptation visibly changes a received base and records the change; project composition is new instructional Latin.  None may be presented under another class merely because its wording sounds plausible.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G057}{Poetic syntax remains syntax}{poetry foundations}{State the first rule for approaching an unfamiliar line.}{Identify forms, agreement, government, finite and non-finite predicates, and clause boundaries before rearranging.  Meter and emphasis may separate connected words, but they do not cancel morphology.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G058}{Syllable division for scansion}{poetry foundations}{State a cautious working procedure.}{Divide words into syllables by vowels and diphthongs, assigning intervocalic consonants by the rules and examples printed in P1.  Mark uncertainty and test the division against the supplied quantity sheet and verse; this analytical operation is not a pronunciation guide.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G059}{Quantity by nature and position}{metrical quantity}{Distinguish the two.}{A syllable is long by nature when its vowel or diphthong is inherently long in the word or form; it is long by position when its vowel is followed by the consonantal structure that closes or weights the syllable under the meter's rules.  A printed Missal without macrons does not supply all quantities.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G060}{Common quantitative feet}{metrical quantity}{Write the patterns for dactyl, spondee, iamb, and trochee.}{Dactyl: long--short--short.  Spondee: long--long.  Iamb: short--long.  Trochee: long--short.  A metrical scheme does not by itself specify spoken realization.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G061}{Elision}{scansion}{State the cautious working rule.}{At a word boundary, a final vowel or vowel plus \Latin{m} may be elided before a following vowel or \Latin{h} plus vowel under the verse's rules.  Mark the candidate, test the meter, and retain any genuine textual uncertainty.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G062}{Dactylic hexameter}{classical meters}{Describe its six-foot frame.}{The line has six feet.  The first four may normally be dactyls or spondees; the fifth is usually a dactyl; the sixth is a spondee or trochaic close.  Substitution and elision must be tested on the actual line.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G063}{Caesura}{metrical structure}{Define it and distinguish it from punctuation.}{A caesura is a word break within a metrical foot that helps articulate the line.  Punctuation may coincide with it but does not establish it; scan the feet and word boundaries first.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G064}{Enjambment}{poetic syntax}{Define it in syntactic terms.}{Enjambment carries a syntactic unit across a line boundary.  Identify the unfinished dependency and explain how delayed completion changes emphasis or movement; do not treat every run-on line as equally significant.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G065}{The elegiac couplet}{classical meters}{State its high-level structure.}{An elegiac couplet pairs a dactylic hexameter with an elegiac pentameter.  The second line has the strongly divided two-half structure printed in P3; test its division, substitutions, and ending from that complete supplied pattern rather than analogy alone.}
\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G066}{Lyric strophes}{classical meters}{Why must each lyric form be learned as its own pattern?}{Lyric meters combine long and short positions in fixed line and stanza patterns.  The greater Asclepiadean line taught in P4 cannot be inferred safely from line length; use the supplied scheme and test every position in each line.}

\DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G067}{Poetic word order}{poetic syntax}{Give a disciplined reconstruction method.}{Mark agreement pairs and governed complements, bracket clauses, and write a neutral prose scaffold without changing any form. \DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G068}{Poetic ellipsis and compression}{poetic syntax}{What prevents over-supplying a poetic line?}{Require grammatical necessity, a strong parallel, or clear contextual licensing. \DeclareMemoryGrammarFact{G069}{Poetic diction and allusion}{literary reading}{What evidence supports a claim of marked diction or allusion?}{Identify the exact word, form, construction, or verbal overlap; compare an adequate source context; test ordinary alternatives; and scale the claim to the evidence.
