  \SubmoduleExtension
    {Comparison exactly stated}
    {An irregular comparison map prevents unrelated stems from being regularized by analogy.}
    {Make a three-column positive--comparative--superlative map for every irregular family printed in the lesson. Beside each comparative, add one standard with \Latin{quam} and one ablative-standard frame using lesson vocabulary.}
    {State the case rule on each side of the two standard constructions.}
    {The map must preserve every lesson stem. After \Latin{quam}, the compared members take the cases required by their parallel clause roles; without \Latin{quam}, the standard stands in the ablative.}
  \SubmoduleExtension
    {Reading the forms in context}
    {Adjective degree, adverb degree, and degree-of-difference phrases answer different syntactic questions.}
    {For the lesson's contextual examples, label each comparative or superlative as adjective or adverb, identify what it modifies, and mark any standard or ablative of degree.}
    {Explain why neuter comparative \Latin{-ius} can be either adjectival or adverbial only after context is checked.}
    {Each label must name the modified noun or verbal/adjectival center and any governed comparison phrase. The \Latin{-ius} form alone does not decide use; agreement and syntactic attachment do.}
  \SubmoduleExtension
    {Writing laboratory}
    {A numeral card links form to the distinct jobs of counting, ordering, and distributing.}
    {Build a one-to-ten cardinal/ordinal card from the lesson, then add three project-composed noun phrases: a count, an ordered day, and one-apiece distribution. Label the numeral family and required agreement or construction.}
    {Define cardinal, ordinal, and distributive without relying on the example's English word alone.}
    {Cardinals answer how many, ordinals identify position in a sequence, and distributives specify allocation per member or group. The project-composed phrases must use the lesson forms and agree or govern as that construction requires.}
