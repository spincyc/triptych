# Two defensible readings of PC-S55-A

These are editorial readings of the complete appointed formulary, grounded
in the exact author passages documented in [scope](scope.md). They are not
attributed to a Father as whole-Mass interpretations. Each takes all eleven
element keys seriously, including both unselected Communion options. The
same shared prayers serve all Sunday cycles; the independent course of the
apostolic reading prevents a claim that a single theme explains their
historical selection. A and B differ in their central exegetical inference,
not merely in pastoral examples.

## A. Receiving earthly goods without giving them God's place

### Controlling question and argument

What can be owed to an earthly power when God claims all creation? The
apparent collision between the unknowing anointed Cyrus and the dues of
Caesar is resolved by distinguishing an instrument's real function from the
source and final end of its authority. God can use Cyrus for Israel's
liberation without making his religion true. Jesus can require a real civil
return without dividing the world into an autonomous empire and a small
religious remainder. The Psalm invites every people to the Creator's worship;
the appointed community answers divine action through faith, love and hope.
The sacramental prayers finally disclose that what the assembly offers and
receives is itself God's gift. The worshipper does not own a disposable
religious portion of life while the rest belongs absolutely to Caesar.

Jerome supplies the indispensable literal distinction. In *In Isaiam* XII
on 45:1–7, Cyrus is the Persian king, not Jesus under a disguised name.
The royal title and victorious hand identify an office used for a purpose;
the twice-repeated failure to know God disallows a direct predication of
this whole passage of Christ. Yet the king can prefigure liberation by the
true Christ. The inference is that providential usefulness neither proves
holiness nor removes the ruler's accountability. This gives the reading
more precision than “God is in control.”

Chrysostom supplies the decisive Gospel boundary. In *Matthew* LXX §§1–2,
he interprets “render” as repayment of dues and exposes a question designed
to incriminate either answer. His qualification concerning godliness means
that the command is not unlimited obedience. It is compatible with Jerome's
limited instrumentality: real earthly service can coexist with God's unique
claim, while impiety receives no title from the ruler's office. Both authors
make substantive, independent contributions; neither is a decorative second
name. Augustine's *Psalm* XCV §§2–6 adds the universal scope: divine glory
travels beyond one nation or empire, and the preacher must not take it for
himself. Aquinas's explicitly mystical image argument in *Matthew* 22.2
then deepens the gift returned: a person bearing God's image owes himself,
not merely a coin-shaped portion of his life. Bede's direct *Mark* III,
236A–B, supplies a further distinction: the Lord's saving service is for
the world, while his gloss of “for many” identifies willing believers.
Universal divine lordship does not make everyone's believing return an
already accomplished fact. This connection to rendering oneself to God is
the editor's synthesis; Bede comments on Mark, not on this formulary or on
the tribute question.

### A's four senses

**Literal:** the prophecy addresses Cyrus for Israel's sake; the Gospel
answers a hostile tribute question without its proposed either/or; Paul
thanks God for an actual community's effective faith. The Psalms pray for
protection and praise the Creator. Shared prayers ask God for the assembly's
right response and sacramental benefit. This is a synthesis of distinct
literal acts, not a claim that they originally narrated one event.

**Allegorical:** Jerome's Cyrus-type remains subordinate to the true
liberator; Augustine's universal song and Aquinas's divine image interpret
belonging to God through Christ and his body. The Mark option presents the
liberator's actual self-gift. “From the wood” is explicitly a reception
variant used by Augustine and discussed by John Paul II, never inserted
into the appointed Psalm.

**Moral:** render real obligations with truthfulness and justice, refuse
the demand that office make impiety holy, and return the whole person to
God. The liturgical application includes honest public duty, humble prayer
and charitable work; it does not issue a partisan verdict or give a universal
answer to every disputed modern tax.

**Anagogical:** the righteous government sung in the Psalm and the eternal
gifts sought after Communion free the believer from demanding final rescue
from transient power. The hope of beholding God and deliverance from death
comes from the broader Psalm contexts and is identified as such.

### What A clarifies and what it cannot carry alone

A directly addresses power, rightful dues and idolatry. It can correct the
habit of treating a useful ruler as a saviour, or treating worship as release
from all civic responsibility. Its risk is leaving the hearer with a correct
allocation of obligations but little explanation of how a divided will comes
to serve rightly. B addresses that further question. Neither reading permits
the positive actions of Cyrus to certify all his acts, nor reads the Gospel
as a constitutional separation formula with modern institutions already in
view. A homily developed from A should move from the coin's question to the
hearer's entrusted life, without inventing an unsourced Augustine “coin”
quotation.

## B. From gifts received to a will made ready for service

### Controlling question and argument

How can the people act freely and fruitfully if divine help comes first?
The pivotal contrast is not ruler versus worshipper but the difference
between receiving capacity and recognizing its giver. Cyrus receives strength
without knowing God; Thessalonica's received Gospel bears the visible fruits
of faith, love and hope. The Collect asks for a faithful disposition rather
than congratulating the assembly for possessing it. The acclamation's light
is participated light; the Offerings prayer asks that those who serve the
mysteries themselves be purified. Communion is received before the assembly
can claim its own generosity as the explanation of salvation. The final
prayer keeps present progress open toward the eternal end.

Aquinas is substantive here in three independent checked contexts. In
*Isaiah* 45 he distinguishes liberation accomplished through Cyrus from
recognition not simply achieved in Cyrus. In *Philippians* 2.3 he excludes
both self-originating saving action and the denial of free agency: God
works inwardly in willing and doing. This belongs to the acclamation's
chapter context, not to its two appointed fragments. In *Psalm* 32 §16,
fear without hope despairs and hope without fear presumes; nourishment is
bodily, spiritual and sacramental. The sacrament is therefore no medal for
self-sufficiency. These are compatible theological accounts without claiming
they describe the historical origin of the modern prayers.

Chrysostom provides an independent ascetical and ecclesial argument. In
*1 Thessalonians* I, work, labour and endurance are not passive labels:
the Gospel's divine power is visible in a community remaining faithful
under costly conditions. In *Philippians* VIII the rejection of murmuring
concerns the kind of will with which service is done. His *Matthew* LXV §4,
on the parallel to Mark, argues that Christ's willing abasement overturns
the apostles' competitive ambition. A received gift matures in a manner of
life that gives itself. Augustine's *Psalm* XXXII §§18–22 independently
contributes nourishment in truth and patient hope; he does not turn hunger
into automatic material prosperity. Aquinas and Chrysostom thus contribute
different arguments, not merely two attestations of the same maxim.

Bede's *Mark* III, PL 92, 236B, puts the receiving will inside the
appointed ransom clause itself. After explaining that Christ assumed the
servant's form to shed his blood for the world, he glosses “for many” as
those willing to believe. This is more than a call to imitate helpfulness:
Christ gives the saving gift, and the person believes in the giver. It
sharpens B's contrast between Cyrus's unknowing reception of strength and
a people answering God in faith. That cross-passage comparison is editorial;
Bede does not identify Cyrus with unbelieving recipients in this comment.
Nor does *voluerunt* establish a will originating faith independently of
grace. Aquinas's checked account of divine action in willing remains the
distinct source for that question. Bede and Chrysostom contribute compatible
emphases on reception and the defeat of ambition; no doctrinal dispute
between them is established by these loci.

### B's four senses

**Literal:** the prophecy distinguishes God's initiative from Cyrus's
knowledge; Paul thanks God for an active congregation; the short acclamation
draws on an exhortation to persevering obedience. The three orations ask for
disposition, purification and growth. No invented appointment is created
from the wider Pauline chapter.

**Allegorical:** the true Christ gives himself and gathers a living body
whose light comes from him. Augustine's body-of-Christ prayer and Aquinas's
sacramental nourishment support a corporate, Eucharistic reading, rather
than an analogy about an isolated heroic individual. Cyrus remains a limited
type whose lack of knowledge is not ascribed to Christ.

Bede's account locates the believing members' life in the Son's prior
self-gift; the received gift, rather than the members' achievement, grounds
the corporate response.

**Moral:** acknowledge the giver in one's abilities, work willingly,
practice costly charity and let service expose the need for purification.
Hold fear and hope together. The result is neither earning election nor
waiting for grace as an excuse to refuse action.
The willing belief in Bede's *pro multis* gloss gives that response a
specified object, Christ's saving gift, before it becomes an imitation of
his service.

**Anagogical:** hope sustains action toward a good not yet possessed;
the present sacramental life prepares for the eternal gifts sought in the
closing prayer. The wider Pauline and Psalm contexts explain perseverance
and final vision without placing their unappointed verses in the inventory.

### What B clarifies and what it cannot carry alone

B gives the homily a particularly direct adult-parish argument: abilities
and service can coexist with failure to recognize their giver; receiving
Christ changes how one serves. Its risk is spiritualizing the Gospel so
quickly that actual civil obligations and demands become invisible. A's
literal question and Chrysostom's godliness boundary must remain a corrective.
The moral demand is sincere action, not the presumption that every anxious
person lacks faith or that suffering proves deficient reception of grace.

The expansive study should develop these comparisons at the relevant
passages. The concise study interleaves them where its two readings turn,
instead of presenting two unrelated miniature essays. Each still receives
its own four senses. Its four overview rows on physical page 1 summarize
the entire formulary and do not substitute for these distinct distillations.
The homily can adopt one governing argument, with exact sources and delivery
estimate outside the speech. No cultural-gallery or exploratory-proposal
quota is part of this research plan.
