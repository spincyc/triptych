## Passage dossiers and reception sweep

### Entrance: cf. Psalm 16:6,8 (Vulgate; Hebrew 17)

The complete psalm is a prayer for judgment and protection, with a final
hope of seeing God's glory. The antiphon's two selected verses do not appoint
the whole vindication argument or its ending. Read Augustine, *Enarrationes*,
Psalm XVI, §§1–14 (NPNF English heading XVII), especially §§6,8,14.
His Christ-and-body speaker explains confident prayer from prior divine
hearing, interprets the pupil of the eye through Christ's humanity, and
identifies the wings with merciful love. This is verified Christian exegesis,
not the historical identity of the first psalmist's enemies. His polemical
identifications elsewhere in the exposition are not generalized to present
Jews or other peoples.

Later direct reception: Aquinas, *Super Psalmos* 16, §3, paragraph 86901,
and the following division on enemies. He treats the image as careful,
effective protection, and offers several spiritual applications of the wings,
including charity and the cross. These alternatives must not be flattened
into a single literal meaning. The moral consequence is dependent courage,
not a guarantee that faithful people escape bodily suffering. A search of
both the Augustine and Aquinas complete offered Psalm ranges supplied direct
reception; no unsupported claim of absent reception is made.

### Isaiah 45:1,4–6: anointed, yet unknowing

Isaiah 44 contrasts the helpless manufactured idol with the Creator who
names Cyrus and promises Jerusalem's restoration. Chapter 45 develops
divine agency, the refusal of rival gods, and the summons to the ends of the
earth. Verses 2–3 and 7 onward are context only. The selected text retains
both the startling royal designation and the king's failure to know God.

Direct ancient witness: Jerome, *Commentary on Isaiah*, book XII, on
45:1–7, DCO *Pars 3*, physical/printed 126–129, read in full in text
and page images, including the preceding 44:28 explanation and the boundary
before book XIII. He explicitly rejects reading the proper name Cyrus as
“Lord” and treating this entire address as spoken directly to Jesus. Royal
anointing language names Cyrus's office. “You have not known me” cannot be
predicated of the Son in this literal account. Nevertheless Jerome calls Cyrus
a type and image of the true Christ: literal distinction makes the typology
possible rather than abolishing it. Restoration serves Jacob/Israel and
the world's knowledge of God. His final tropological extension warns the
gifted ecclesiastical teacher to credit the giver. Jerome reports Josephus's
story of Cyrus reading Isaiah; this research has not independently established
that event and must present it, if used, as Jerome's reported tradition.

Direct later witness: Aquinas, *Expositio super Isaiam*, chapter 45,
paragraph 85005, whole chapter read. He distinguishes royal dignity from
actual ritual anointing; divine assistance is ordered toward recognition
and liberation. The intended fruit in Cyrus himself is not simply identical
with the benefit received by the people: Aquinas says he remained in idolatry.
This complements, rather than contradicts, Jerome's account of God's action
through a ruler who does not know him. Both distinguish calamity from moral
evil when explaining verse 7; verse 7 is not appointed. Aquinas's later
mystical reading at verse 8 cannot be imported as though the Sunday read it.

The first corpus search returned the wrong DCO volume (*Pars 4*, books
XV–XVIII) and an Aquinas range beginning at chapter 34. The proportionate
second search located *Pars 3* and *cis40.html*. Only the correct chapter
witnesses are used. DCO is modern typesetting with no established historical
exemplar; it is not a verified PL facsimile. Its TLS certificate failed
validation and the subsequent retrieval disabled that check; this transport
limitation is recorded, not hidden. Agreement with Aquinas and the biblical
context corroborates the interpretation, not DCO's typography.

### Responsorial Psalm: Hebrew 96 / Vulgate 95

The full psalm summons all the earth, contrasts the Creator with idols,
calls the peoples to worship, and rejoices in righteous judgment. The selected
strophes reach the proclamation of divine kingship and equitable government,
while omitting 10b and the concluding cosmic celebration in 11–13.
The response, verse 7b, is a separately identified liturgical refrain.

Direct ancient reception: Augustine, *Enarrationes*, Psalm XCV,
§§1–15 (NPNF heading XCVI), whole exposition read. His new song is
the new love of a Church gathered throughout the world; the preacher must
declare God's glory, not his own (§3). The Creator's greatness and Christ's
humiliation belong together (§§4–6). His gift for entry is the humble
heart (§9); his expectation of the coming judge calls for using the world
without serving it (§§14–15). His “from the wood” lemma at §11 is
an ancient textual/reception feature, not wording of the appointed Psalm.
Nor is the world's fixed stability, explained there, in this Sunday's
actual 10a,c strophe. This distinction is consequential for a cross-centred
reading: it is a Christian rereading supported by witnesses, not an alleged
word hidden in the U.S. refrain.

Later saintly reception: John Paul II, general audience 18 September 2002,
§§1–5, complete official English catechesis read. The first scenes move
from sanctuary worship to universal rule; prayer purifies faith from idols.
He explicitly identifies the patristic reading of the cross as a Christian
rereading and concludes by connecting divine lordship to Mark 10:43–45.
This is particularly useful for the Communion alternative, but proves no
compositional intent of the Sunday editors. The initial search of Aquinas's
Psalm commentary could not supply Psalm 95 because his offered exposition
does not extend to it. A second official Vatican search located this exact
audience. Its modern protected wording is paraphrased, not copied.

### 1 Thessalonians 1:1–5b: whose work proves election?

The whole chapter moves from thanksgiving for faith, love and hope to the
community's reception, example, conversion from idols and waiting for the
risen Son. The appointment stops before the closing conduct clause of verse
5; verses 6–10 illuminate context without becoming a second reading.
Chapter 3's Timothy report establishes why thanksgiving need not deny
vulnerability or the continuing need for encouragement.

Direct ancient witness: Chrysostom, *Homilies on 1 Thessalonians* I,
exegesis of 1:1–7 and adjacent moral argument, NPNF I.13. The “in God”
community is distinguished by its relation to God; faith has work, love
endures costly labour, and hope sustains affliction. Election is recognized
through the effective preaching and the people's reception, rather than
deduced from their social power. His references to affliction in verse 6 are
an explanation of the larger chapter, not part of 1:1–5b.

Direct later witness: Aquinas, *Super I Thessalonicenses* 1, lectio 1,
paragraph 87862, whole lecture read. Election is not earned by prior human
merit; its effects are visible in hearers and preachers. He offers two
interpretive distributions of power, Spirit and fullness: signs and spiritual
efficacy, or the preacher's virtuous life, the Spirit and complete instruction.
Chrysostom's emphasis on costly endurance and Aquinas's analysis of evidence
are compatible but not identical. Neither author licenses a claim that
material success proves the election of a political regime. Both direct
commentaries were sought under book and author titles; no catena attribution
is used as a substitute for the checked author text.

### Philippians 2:15d,16a: light and the word of life

The short acclamation stands within a chapter joining Christ's humility,
exaltation, persevering obedience, divine action, blameless conduct and the
examples of Timothy and Epaphroditus. Its luminous image is neither private
self-improvement nor self-generated brilliance. Verses 12–14 and 16b onward
remain explanatory context, not appointed acclamation text.

Chrysostom, *Homilies on Philippians* VIII, the exposition of 2:12–16
and adjoining moral reasoning, NPNF I.13, treats obedience without murmuring
as willing service and the saints as lights amid darkness. His explanation
of “word of life” as having a principle/seed of life differs from the
NPNF editor's preference for the Gospel-word sense. The editor's note is not
Chrysostom speaking. Aquinas, *Super Philippenses* 2, lectiones 3–4,
paragraphs 87838–87839, both read in full, gives a precise complementary
account: God's inward action makes willing and doing possible without
destroying freedom; the saints shine by participation in the light of the
Word, and hold Christ's life-giving word. These differences affect whether
the author accents transformed life or the word received and borne. Do not
collapse them into a fabricated consensus about the Greek verb. The short
U.S. acclamation does not settle every exegetical possibility in the chapter.

### Matthew 22:15–21: render and image

Matthew 21–22 places the trap within Jerusalem's temple controversies.
The questioners are the particular narrated alliance, not all Jewish people.
The flattering description of truthful teaching is weaponized against Jesus;
the coin and inscription expose the constructed dilemma. The answer neither
states that Caesar is divine nor abolishes all civil obligations. The text
does not provide a complete modern constitution or settle every disputed tax.

Direct ancient witness: Chrysostom, *Homilies on Matthew* LXX §§1–2,
NPNF I.10, read through the tribute explanation and following Sadducee
transition. He exposes the alternatives intended by the trap: sedition before
Roman power or discredit before the people. “Render” concerns dues rather
than a gift. His explicit limit is crucial: obedience is compatible with
godliness; an impious demand is not justified simply by invoking Caesar.

Direct later witness: Aquinas, *Super Matthaeum* 22, lectio 2,
paragraph 87389, whole lecture read. He discusses the coin as evidence of
civil usage, then explicitly distinguishes a mystical extension: the soul
bears God's image and is to be rendered to God; shared civic life still
requires seeking peace. This does not turn every thing into the sovereign's
property. His natural gifts/artificial coin distinction is an interpretive
argument, not a historical legal inventory. His reconstruction of Herodian
origins is reception evidence and is not presented as independently settled
political history. The full Matthew context and both direct commentaries were
consulted; a modern web search served as a locator to the latter, not as its
textual authority.

### Communion, Psalm option: cf. Psalm 32:18–19 / Hebrew 33

The whole hymn contrasts the Creator's counsel with the power of rulers,
armies and horses. Hope is directed toward mercy; the supplied food answers
need. Augustine, *Enarrationes* XXXII, §§1–22 (NPNF heading XXXIII),
especially §§16–22, explicitly rejects self-sufficient strength and reads
famine as deprivation of the life-giving Word, culminating in patient hope.
Aquinas, *Super Psalmos* 32 §§15–17, paragraphs 87102–87104, distinguishes
bodily, spiritual and final rescue; he pairs fear with hope to avoid both
despair and presumption. His discussion of nourishment expressly includes
the sacrament. This gives a sourced Christian Eucharistic reading without
claiming that the literal ancient psalm named the Eucharist. The psalm is not
a promise of immunity from starvation or death. Both author's relevant
divisions and their antecedent contrast with human strength were read.

### Communion, Mark option: Mark 10:45 excerpt

The Missal selects the Son of Man's self-gift as ransom; it does not appoint
the entire verse's preceding service clause or the whole dispute over rank.
Mark 10 was read in full: the Passion prediction and James and John's request
lead to the contrast between domination and service. This context prevents
“ransom” from being reduced either to an isolated financial transaction or
to generic helpfulness without the self-giving death.

Direct Mark reception: Bede, *In Marci evangelium expositio* III, on
10:42–45, PL 92 (Paris reissue, 1862), 236A–B, especially 236B.
The complete Latin Wikisource export (revision 263992, identifying Migne and
Corpus Corporum) was acquired, and the exposition of 10:32–46 was read.
The complete registered PL 92 OCR was reacquired and its hash matched;
it located the printed witness but does not control wording. The entire
scan leaf n122, columns 235–236, was visually inspected, and the two
paragraphs on 10:42–45 were collated against the transcription. The exact
printed passage is bound through its constituent segment, and both that
page image and the complete Latin-only transcription are retained for reuse.
The digital page does not identify its Migne printing year; only the checked
passage, not the entire digital expression, has been matched to the 1862
reissue. No modern English Bede translation is used.

Bede makes humility, rather than power, the way to virtue's summit. Christ
then offers his own deed as the example: the Son of Man takes the servant's
form and sheds his blood for the world. In that same paragraph Bede expressly
contrasts *pro omnibus* with *pro multis* and glosses the latter as
*pro eis qui credere voluerunt*, those who were willing to believe. Thus his
direct reception of the selected words concerns the believing recipients,
not only the death's exemplary service or the identity of ransom's payee.
His “for the world” and his willing-belief gloss must be retained together.
The editorial distinction between universal saving self-gift and believing
reception explains this conjunction; it is not a claim that Bede here supplies
a complete later theory of sufficient/effective redemption, predestination,
or the Greek word's lexical range. Faith does not become a price paid before
Christ gives himself. Nor does this brief gloss establish that every person
already receives redemption's fruit. Bede's servant-form argument draws on
the full verse; that service clause remains context outside the appointed
Communion excerpt.

The previously retained Greek patristic contribution is Chrysostom's
direct explanation of
the Synoptic parallel, Matthew 20:28, in *Homilies on Matthew* LXV §4
with its §5 continuation, read in full at that division. It is a parallel
Gospel witness, not a falsely attributed commentary on Mark. He argues from
Christ's descent and death for enemies against the disciples' ambition:
humility, not domination, manifests true greatness. A second ancient search
checked Gregory Nazianzen, *Oration* XLV §§21–24, especially §22,
in NPNF II.7. Gregory asks to whom ransom is paid, refuses the devil's
proprietary claim, and refuses an account of the Father delighted by the
Son's blood; he speaks of the Father's acceptance, sanctification through
the Incarnation and liberation through the Son. This is doctrinal reception
of ransom, not verse-by-verse Mark exegesis. The relevant Chrysostom and
Gregory paragraphs were reread in the retained NPNF texts when Bede was
added. Bede's direct Mark witness supersedes the earlier dossier's negative
result; his early-medieval Latin reception has its own historical position
beside these earlier Greek witnesses.

Later reception: Aquinas, *Summa theologiae* III q.48 a.4, whole article
and the beginning of a.5, in the retained public-domain English Dominican
translation. The Gutenberg heading mistakenly prints “Art. 3” under
“FOURTH ARTICLE”; cite the structural fourth article, not that typo.
Aquinas distinguishes never ceasing to be under God's power from losing
union in charity. He describes Christ's voluntary self-gift as superabundant
satisfaction and redemption, paid to God rather than the devil. The wording
about payment to the Father is materially different from Gregory's refusal
to explain the Father's acceptance as a demanded ransom. Both oppose the
devil's entitlement, but this shared negative does not erase their different
positive accounts. Present the distinction as a bounded comparison, not as
either a fabricated contradiction in doctrine or an interchangeable theory.
John Paul II's checked audience §5 directly cites Mark 10:43–45 as the
shape of the Lord's kingship; it supplies later saintly engagement with Mark
itself. The study may use this with Chrysostom's parallel, plainly labelled.
