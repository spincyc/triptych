\section{Hearing the appointed texts in their setting}
\subsection{The Entrance: help within affliction}
The Entrance begins with the Lord's relation to his people. Distress is neither hidden nor treated as proof that divine care has failed. The promise of saving help places the assembly before a faithful Lord before it has heard the vineyard's disputes about recompense. The movement is from need toward trust, not from achievement toward a claim to superior treatment. The composed antiphon's identified biblical basis is Psalm 37:39--40; the whole antiphon is not a verbatim extract of those verses.

Psalm 37 begins by forbidding agitation over the prosperity of evildoers. Its conclusion therefore answers a problem larger than a passing emergency: how to remain faithful when visible success seems to contradict God's justice. Augustine, explaining the psalm's final verses, joins help in tribulation to patient endurance and the final separation of good and evil. His hearer must correct the eye that misjudges providence rather than judge God crooked because the wicked flourish. Help does not mean that the righteous never undergo affliction. This prepares a searching comparison with the Gospel's evil eye: in both settings another person's apparent advantage can distort our judgment of God.\footnote{Augustine, \textit{Exposition of Psalm 36}, third exposition, §§14--16; English NPNF I.8, Psalm 37, ``On the Third Part of the Psalm.'' Bellarmine, \textit{Commentary on the Psalms}, Psalm 36 on vv. 30--40, treats the final verses briefly as a recapitulation.}

\subsection{The Collect: what the workers are learning to love}
The Collect's ordering principle is the love of God and neighbor. Its petition places obedient living on the way to eternal life. This supplies a measure of fruitful labor that the vineyard's dissatisfied workers have difficulty applying: another person's good cannot be an injury to charity merely because it is not an exclusive good. Keeping the commandments concerns the kind of person one becomes before God and with others, as well as outward compliance.

The prayer also asks for what it commands. That structure matters when the first Communion alternative returns to the desire for directed ways. A command identifies the good; a petition acknowledges dependence upon God in doing it. The assembly does not begin by asserting that it has mastered the law of love. It begins by receiving that law as the form of the life it needs. Paul's willingness to remain for the benefit of others will give this twofold love a concrete human shape. Aquinas explains that willingness through charity's love of both God and neighbor, rather than through a rivalry between spiritual desire and practical duty.\footnote{Aquinas, \textit{Super Epistolam ad Philippenses} 1, lecture 3, in \textit{In omnes D. Pauli Apostoli epistolas commentaria}, II (Liège: Dessain, 1857), pp. 371--372. This illuminates the Collect; it is not an exposition of the prayer's composition.}

\subsection{Isaiah: God's distance is the height of mercy}
Isaiah 55 opens with an invitation to receive water, wine, and nourishment without the purchase that ordinarily secures them. Listening, covenant, and the gathering of nations precede the verses heard today. The command to seek the Lord thus belongs to a generous invitation. It is not an instruction to locate an unwilling deity. Yet seeking includes an actual turning: the wicked person must relinquish both a way of life and the thoughts that sustain it. Abundant pardon is promised in the same movement as repentance.

The contrast between God's thoughts and human thoughts immediately follows that promise. Aquinas makes the connection precise. At 55:8 he contrasts the human intention to avenge an injury with God's will to show mercy. Divine height is therefore more than a warning that human beings cannot understand everything. It rebukes the retaliatory imagination that measures forgiveness by its own reluctance to forgive. His commentary places the chapter within return from Babylon and also develops its reception as a gift of divine wisdom. The Sunday verses themselves keep the practical summons close: seek, abandon, return.\footnote{Aquinas, \textit{Expositio super Isaiam ad litteram}, chapter 55, \textit{Opera omnia}, XIV (Parma: Fiaccadori, 1863), pp. 556--557.}

Jerome similarly refuses to make seeking a geographical journey. In his exposition of 55:6--7, the opportunity belongs to the present embodied life and requires departure from former conduct and thoughts. At 55:8--9 he insists that salvation promised to Jewish or Gentile sinners need not seem incredible: God's counsel has a stability human plans lack. This brings another shade of meaning to divine transcendence. God is not simply a larger version of our unstable willingness to pardon. The promise can be trusted because his fidelity is his own.\footnote{Jerome, \textit{Commentary on Isaiah}, XV, on Isaiah 55:6--9; Latin DCO \textit{Pars 4}, pp. 27--30. The modern digital witness has no established historical printing; the argument is paraphrased.}

Irenaeus uses 55:8 in a different dispute. He opposes the projection of creaturely passions and divided mental processes into God. His doctrinal use protects the distinction between Creator and creature; Aquinas's continuous exposition emphasizes the mercy named in this passage. These are complementary questions, not two interchangeable explanations. Divine transcendence includes more than forgiveness, while the particular prophetic context prevents transcendence from becoming an excuse to ignore the invitation to return.\footnote{Irenaeus, \textit{Against Heresies} II.13.3--4, Roberts--Rambaut translation in ANF 1 (Buffalo, 1887), pp. 373--374; paraphrase supported by the retained OCR witness, not an image-collated quotation.}

\subsection{The Psalm: great, good, and near}
The three selected strophes of Psalm 145 move from daily and enduring praise, through mercy toward all God's works, to the Lord's righteousness and nearness to truthful prayer. Greatness and nearness are not competing attributes. The God whose greatness cannot be exhausted is also the God who hears. The response from verse 18a gives the assembly a brief answer to Isaiah's summons: calling upon the Lord is possible because he is near.

Augustine reads mercy expansively. Forgiveness, the Spirit who justifies, charity enabling good works, and eternal life belong to God's merciful giving. Mercy is not merely the cancellation of a past fault, leaving the future to unaided effort. His exposition of truthful invocation adds a searching test: does the petitioner desire God himself, or only some benefit God can supply? A person may loudly invoke God's name while wanting something else more than God. Truthful prayer seeks the giver.\footnote{Augustine, \textit{Exposition of Psalm 144}, §§7, 15--18; NPNF I.8, Psalm 145.}

Bellarmine's later exposition develops truthful invocation through faith, hope, charity, desire, and attention. He also reads patience as an invitation to repentance, not approval of continuing sin. The two witnesses therefore make the psalm's reassurance exacting without making it less reassuring. The Lord who is near is not manipulated by a formula; the caller is drawn into a truthful relation with him. The ending of the full psalm also preserves judgment alongside mercy. The selected strophes do not turn divine goodness into indifference to the life of those who praise him.\footnote{Bellarmine, \textit{Commentary on the Psalms}, trans. John O'Sullivan (1866), Psalm 144 on vv. 8--9, 17--21; the English witness is an abridged translation.}

\subsection{Paul: the good of being with Christ and the good of serving}
Philippians 1 places Paul's desire within imprisonment, disputed motives among preachers, and confidence that Christ will be magnified. The appointed selection begins late in verse 20, with Christ magnified in Paul's body, whether through life or death. His alternatives are consequently governed by Christ. Life means fruitful work; departure means being with Christ. Neither is reduced to mere survival or extinction.

Chrysostom is astonished that Paul can desire the greater rest and still willingly remain in hardship. He develops the cost through images of a laden trader delaying safe harbor, an athlete continuing the contest, and a soldier remaining in battle. Paul has not discovered that being with Christ is less desirable. He recognizes a good for his neighbors that makes remaining an act of love for Christ. The apostle's wish for others' progress is the reason for the delay, not evidence that his desire for Christ was insincere.\footnote{Chrysostom, \textit{Homilies on Philippians} 3, exposition of 1:20--21, and 4, opening exposition of 1:22--26; NPNF I.13. Verses 25--26 are context beyond the appointed cut.}

Aquinas distinguishes the attraction toward God from charity's concern for the neighbor, then explains how friendship can prefer God's honor even to one's own enjoyment. This is a precise answer to the apparent opposition. Paul's love of God includes love of those whom his work helps. Verse 27a then brings the hearers into the same field: their conduct must correspond to the Gospel. The later clauses about common steadfastness, read in context by both commentators, explain the communal character of that conduct, although they are not part of today's selection. Paul's desire is neither contempt for embodied life nor a warrant to hasten death; the passage explicitly identifies continued embodied service as fruitful.\footnote{Aquinas, \textit{Super Philippenses} 1, lectures 3--4, Dessain II, pp. 371--373.}

\subsection{The acclamation: an opened heart attends}
The acclamation adapts Acts 16:14b. Acts narrates the Lord opening Lydia's heart to attend to Paul's message; the liturgical text turns that event into the assembly's request for receptive hearing. The narrative's subject and the acclamation's direct address are therefore distinct. The verses printed for study give the narrative source, not a replacement acclamation.

Chrysostom holds together what Acts holds together: the Lord's action and Lydia's attention. Her response becomes concrete in baptism and hospitality. An opened heart is not an inert heart. Augustine, citing the same verse in \textit{On the Predestination of the Saints}, argues that the very beginning of faith is a divine gift, as prayer for opened hearts acknowledges. His concern is the origin of faith; Chrysostom emphasizes the hearer's willing response. In both accounts, hearing needs grace and bears fruit in action.\footnote{Chrysostom, \textit{Homilies on Acts} 35, opening and recapitulation on 16:13--15, NPNF I.11; Augustine, \textit{On the Predestination of the Saints} I.41, NPNF I.5.}

\subsection{The Gospel: a kept promise and an unwelcome equality}
Matthew places the vineyard after the rich man's departure and Peter's question about what those who have left everything will receive. Jesus promises reward and announces the first--last reversal before the story begins. The landowner then repeatedly goes out, extending an invitation to work. The early workers have an explicit agreement; later groups are summoned in trust. Evening reveals a generosity none of the first workers expected.

Their complaint does not allege that they received less than the agreement. It alleges that the latecomers have been made equal to them. The landowner answers on both grounds: he has kept his promise, and his freedom to give does not become injustice because another envies the gift. Augustine presses this distinction in Sermon 87: failing to pay what is owed would be fraud; paying the agreement and giving generously to another is not. The early workers' greater expectation arose when they saw what someone else received.\footnote{Augustine, Sermon 87, §4; NPNF I.6, New Testament Sermon 37.}

The difficulty deepens when the story is read as the kingdom. Does it predict envy among saints? Chrysostom explicitly denies this. Saints who give themselves for sinners now will rejoice in their salvation then. The narrative complaint magnifies the unexpected greatness of the gift; every feature is not a separate prediction. Gregory also denies heavenly murmuring, but assigns the complaint a different meaning: the ancient fathers' long waiting before Christ opened paradise. Augustine answers the payment-order question through common resurrection and unequal waiting times.

Today's Gospel ends at Matthew 20:16a. The traditional Douay--Rheims text and these patristic lemmata continue with the many-called, few-chosen clause. That clause belongs to their textual horizon, but it is not an additional sentence appointed today. Matthew's following narrative includes the Passion prediction and another dispute over precedence, answered through service. That context confirms the gravity of the question of rank without changing the appointed boundary.

\subsection{Offerings, Communion, and the life that follows}
The Prayer over the Offerings joins the people's gifts and confessed faith to sacramental participation. The preceding Word has addressed listeners; the Eucharistic movement now concerns receiving what faith confesses. The relation to the vineyard is theological: God's gift does not terminate in a satisfying explanation of generosity. It gathers recipients into the mystery of Christ's self-giving. No checked Father comments on this particular prayer in the sources used here; Augustine's Eucharistic teaching illuminates its place in the whole Mass.

The first Communion option, Psalm 119:4--5, joins the command to observe God's precepts with the desire for ways made firm in that observance. Augustine hears dependence in the grammatical movement from command to wish: God must be asked for the ability to do what he commands. In the fuller Latin exposition, the speaker is a member of Christ or the whole body. Hearing is to become doing. Bellarmine likewise rejects confidence in unaided strength at verses 5--7. This is obedience prayed for by those receiving God's gift, not a claim to have earned the gift by flawless obedience.\footnote{Augustine, \textit{Exposition of Psalm 118}, sermon 4, §§1--3; NPNF I.8 gives an abridged parallel under Psalm 119, Aleph, §§4--6. Bellarmine, Psalm 118 on vv. 5--7.}

If John 10:14 is chosen instead, Christ's knowledge of his sheep and their knowledge of him stand within the discourse of the Shepherd who gives his life. Augustine explains how Christ enters hearts through the preaching of Christ. The preacher is not the purchaser of the flock: Christ bought it with his Blood. At the Eucharistic table, receiving his Body and Blood summons members to give themselves for their brethren. The sacramental connection is explicit in Augustine's argument; it is not drawn merely from the presence of shepherd imagery.\footnote{Augustine, \textit{Tractates on John} 47.1--3, especially 47.2; NPNF I.7.}

The Prayer after Communion asks for continuing help so that redemption bears fruit in sacramental life and conduct. It gives the whole movement an unfinished moral horizon: reception has occurred, but the life corresponding to it still lies before the assembly. The opening confidence in divine help returns as dependence after the gift. The final petition does not send the workers away to establish their independence; it sends recipients toward the charity that the Collect named at the beginning.
