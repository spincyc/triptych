## Bible contexts actually read

The retained Challoner Douay–Rheims per-book TSV artifacts were opened and
read for the complete chapters Esther 4,13,14; Isaiah 5; Psalm 79; Philippians
4; John 15; Matthew 21; Lamentations 3; and 1 Corinthians 10. All are pinned
in `source-bindings.toml`. They supply lawful historical study Scripture,
not the approved English proclaimed in the U.S. Lectionary and not reconstructed
Missal adaptations. References, speakers and limits matter:

- Esther's entrance basis is Mordecai's prayer in a threatened community,
  within the Susan court narrative. DR 4:17 is a different sentence. The
  creation language is dependence in danger, not a promise of effortless
  worldly success.
- Isaiah's vineyard is explicitly Israel and Judah (5:7). Its fruit is justice;
  the remainder of chapter 5 specifies land accumulation, corruption and
  distorted judgments. This cannot become generic advice about efficiency.
- Psalm 79 remembers the vine's Egyptian origin, expansion and exposed hedge,
  then petitions for visitation, life and God's face. It is a communal lament;
  its question need not be treated as an admission that every sufferer deserves
  the same punishment. Hebrew/DR Psalm numbering is made explicit.
- Philippians 4 places thankful petition and worthy thoughts between an appeal
  for agreement and gratitude for practical assistance. Practice in verse 9
  prevents a purely inward or therapeutic reduction.
- John 15 embeds choice and lasting fruit in abiding in Christ, his love and
  the command to love one another, with persecution still possible.
- Matthew 21 moves through the Jerusalem entry, temple controversy and questions
  of authority into the parable. The chief priests and Pharisees recognize
  its address in verse 45. That context is not a license to extend the appointed
  Gospel beyond verse 43 or assign collective ethnic guilt.
- Lamentations 3 includes despair, recalled mercy, patient hope, the rejection
  of injustice, repentance and renewed lament. Verse 25 does not cancel the
  chapter's grief. Hope and complaint both survive.
- First Corinthians 10 joins Eucharistic participation to the warning against
  idolatry and an obligation to seek others' good. The one-body claim has an
  ethical consequence; ritual participation is not proof of already perfected
  conduct.

## Material differences and contrary evidence

Chrysostom himself distinguishes the culpable vineyard of Isaiah from the
culpable rulers of Matthew. This distinction is mandatory when the images are
compared. His homily's historical anti-Jewish generalizations must not be
silently adopted as the study's account of a whole people; Matthew's immediate
addressees, the Church's own accountability, and Bellarmine's Jewish apostles
and Gentile grafting remain visible. The interpretation is Christian reception,
not proof that Israel's literal history or continuing identity disappears.

Augustine's Psalm exposition identifies the river with Jordan and the ravager
with Roman power; Bellarmine uses Euphrates and Babylonian/Assyrian possibilities.
The images' geographical and historical identifications genuinely differ.
Their agreement about restoration in Christ does not resolve that disagreement
or date the poem. Augustine's transmitted exposition is abridged. Bellarmine's
English is an abridged historical translation. Neither warrants claims about
unseen omitted Latin paragraphs.

Augustine's choice-before-merit reasoning and Chrysostom's exhortation to begin
acting must retain their respective questions and contexts. The proposed first
reading asserts a compatible limited argument about divine gift and active
response, not a harmonized systematic doctrine on every question of grace.
The works' distinct emphases are more useful than a manufactured disagreement.

Lamentations does not promise that hope eliminates distress. Philippians does
not deny grief; Chrysostom explicitly allows repentance and sorrow alongside
joy. One-body Eucharistic teaching itself exposes deficient charity; it does
not certify that communicants already behave as one. The prayer's transformation
and the psalm's future hope cannot be converted into a guarantee of institutional
prosperity or freedom from judgment.
