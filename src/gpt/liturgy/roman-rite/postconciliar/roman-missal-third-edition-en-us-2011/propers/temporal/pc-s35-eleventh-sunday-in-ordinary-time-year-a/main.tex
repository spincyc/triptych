\section*{Study Guide: Scripture and Liturgical Context}

\noindent\emph{Canonical order governs. Composition and narrated events are distinguished; inherited attributions and modern historical judgments are reported without pretending that uncertainty has disappeared.}

\begin{scripturestable}
\scripturerow{First Reading}{Exod.~19:2--6a}{Israel; exact place of final Pentateuchal formation unresolved}{First-millennium BC final form; precise date unresolved}
\scripturerow{Narrated event}{Exod.~19:1--6a}{Sinai wilderness}{After deliverance from Egypt; exact historical date unresolved}
\scripturenote{Jewish and Christian tradition associates the Pentateuch with Moses. Modern Catholic study recognizes extended transmission and literary formation; the precise date and geographical identification of Sinai remain disputed. The scene stands after deliverance and before covenant ratification, addressing Israel as the people first borne by God and then called to covenant obedience.}
\scripturerow{Entrance / Comm.~A}{Ps.~27:4, 7, 9}{Israel; exact composition place unknown}{Traditional Davidic attribution; historical date uncertain}
\scripturenote{The inherited heading attributes the psalm to David. Its movement from danger and petition to desire for God's house makes one psalm serve both the Entrance's plea and Communion's contemplative branch; this shared source is textual fact, not proof of one modern formulary design.}
\scripturerow{Responsorial Psalm}{Ps.~100:1--2, 3, 5}{Israel's worship; precise place unknown}{Date and human author unknown}
\scripturenote{A thanksgiving psalm summons all lands to serve the Lord and confesses creaturely and covenant belonging. Its appointment after Exodus 19 makes “people” and “flock” the assembly's liturgical answer to election and vocation.}
\scripturerow{Gospel}{Matt.~9:36--10:8}{Canonical Gospel: traditional Palestine; modern Antioch proposal uncertain}{Traditionally early apostolic period; modern majority c.~AD 80--90}
\scripturerow{Narrated event}{Matt.~4:23; 9:35--10:8}{Galilee; exact town unrecorded}{Public ministry, c.~AD 28--29}
\scripturenote{Ancient tradition identifies the Apostle Matthew writing for Jewish believers. Modern study usually dates the canonical Greek Gospel after AD 70. The narrated movement passes from Jesus' teaching and healing circuit to compassion, prayer for laborers, and the first commissioning of the named Twelve.}
\scripturerow{Acclamation}{Mark 1:15}{Canonical Gospel: traditional Rome; modern place debated}{Traditional AD 60s; modern proposals c.~AD 65--70}
\scripturerow{Narrated event}{Mark 1:14--15}{Galilee}{Opening ministry, c.~AD 28}
\scripturenote{Tradition associates Mark with Peter's Roman preaching. The appointed verse condenses Jesus' opening proclamation of fulfilled time, near Kingdom, conversion, and faith; liturgically it welcomes the Gospel rather than becoming another reading.}
\scripturerow{Comm.~B}{John 17:11}{Canonical Gospel: traditional Ephesus; modern compositional history debated}{Late first century in tradition and many modern judgments}
\scripturerow{Narrated event}{John 13:1; 17:1--26}{Jerusalem, final supper}{c.~AD 29--30}
\scripturenote{Tradition identifies the Apostle John and an Ephesian setting. The Communion branch takes a clause from Christ's prayer for his disciples' unity; it is an appointed alternative, not evidence that every celebration used this text.}
\scripturerow{Second Reading}{Rom.~5:6--11}{Corinth, Achaia (present-day Greece), to churches at Rome}{c.~AD 56--58}
\scripturenote{Paul writes to mixed Jewish and Gentile house churches he has not yet visited. The passage moves from helplessness and enmity through Christ's death and reconciliation to confidence in salvation through his life. Its semi-continuous place in Romans remains primary.}
\end{scripturestable}

\section*{Study Guide: Liturgical Movement and Textual Relationships}

\begin{massbox}{Governing account: a people received, reconciled, and sent}
The Lectionary joins Israel's covenant vocation to Christ's compassionate commissioning of the Twelve, while Romans supplies an independent apostolic proclamation of reconciliation. The Missal frames this hearing with grace for action, bodily and spiritual renewal, and sacramental unity. The resulting architecture is source-grounded synthesis, not a claim that every text was composed for Year A.
\end{massbox}

\begin{center}
\textbf{Called by grace $\longrightarrow$ gathered as God's people $\longrightarrow$ sent from Christ's compassion $\longrightarrow$ renewed in Communion}
\end{center}

\subsection*{1. Need becomes petition\enspace\elementref{Entrance, Collect}}

The Entrance does not begin with institutional confidence but with a plea to be heard and helped. The Collect likewise acknowledges mortal frailty and asks grace for both willing and doing. Mission therefore begins as dependence: the Church cannot make herself the Lord's harvest, manufacture vocation, or turn resolve into holy action apart from God.

\subsection*{2. Election becomes vocation\enspace\elementref{First Reading, Psalm, Gospel}}

Exodus first announces what God has done in bearing Israel from bondage; only then does it name covenant obedience and priestly vocation. Psalm 100 answers with belonging and grateful service. Matthew's official correlation does not erase Israel: Jesus sends the Twelve first to Israel, and the mission remains governed by the Messiah of Israel and Lord of the harvest.

\subsection*{3. Compassion becomes gratuitous mission\enspace\elementref{Acclamation, Gospel, Second Reading}}

Jesus' compassion sees crowds as harassed and abandoned, then commands prayer and gives authority. Chrysostom stresses that the harvest was already prepared and remains Christ's. “Without cost” disciplines pride and covetousness. Romans independently supplies the deepest prior gift: Christ acts for the weak, sinful, and hostile, so Christian service gives from reconciliation already received.

\subsection*{4. Offering becomes renewal and unity\enspace\elementref{Prayer over Offerings, Communion, Prayer after Communion}}

The Prayer over the Offerings holds body and mind together: ordinary food sustains creaturely life and Sacrament renews it. Communion may voice desire for God's house or Christ's prayer for unity. The concluding prayer names unity as both sign and effect. These shared Week XI texts can frame Year A mission, but they were not proved historically designed for its readings.

\begin{studybox}{Guardrails}
\textbf{Israel:} covenant language may not support contempt or simple replacement. \textbf{Ministry:} gratuitous grace does not deny lawful material support, but it forbids treating divine gifts as personal property. \textbf{Relationship:} Romans remains an independent semi-continuous strand rather than evidence of a single compositional plan.
\end{studybox}

\section*{Study Guide: Theological and Spiritual Synthesis}

The variable texts move from divine initiative to a received vocation. God bears Israel and calls a priestly people; Christ sees the crowds, names the Twelve, and sends them from his own compassion; Paul proclaims reconciliation given while humanity was weak and hostile. The Church's mission is therefore participation in grace already received, never possession of a gift she originated.

That gift calls for prayer, obedient labor, gratuitous service, and action sustained by grace. The offering and post-Communion prayers join bodily and spiritual renewal to ecclesial unity, while the two Communion antiphons place that unity within desire for God's dwelling or Christ's own prayer. The horizon is salvation through the risen life of Christ and perfected communion in God.

\section*{Study Guide: Commentary on the Texts}

\subsection*{The petitioning assembly\enspace\elementref{Entrance, Collect}}

Psalm 27 lets need and desire occupy the same prayer: the threatened speaker asks for God's hearing and help while seeking one thing, life in God's presence. At the Entrance only the plea is appointed; the house-of-God verse belongs to Communion option A. Their common psalm creates a genuine textual arc without proving that both branches were enacted.

The Collect's Latin identity places God himself as the strength of those who hope in him. Its anthropology is neither pessimistic nor voluntarist. Human willing and acting are real, but frail; grace enables the creature to intend and perform what pleases God.

\subsection*{A treasured people under the Lord of the harvest\enspace\elementref{Exodus, Psalm, Matthew}}

Exodus 19 follows liberation and precedes ratification. Israel is not selected because it achieved holiness; God first carried and gathered the people. “Possession,” “kingdom of priests,” and “holy nation” therefore name gift and task together. Catholic reception applies this vocabulary to the Church in Christ while preserving Israel's election and refusing a crude identity swap.

Psalm 100 turns the covenant declaration into congregational response. The sheep do not make their shepherd, and the people do not create their own belonging. Yet service is active and joyful. The psalm holds together creaturehood, covenant identity, worship, and enduring divine fidelity.

Matthew's Jesus first sees. Compassion is not a decorative feeling but the source of prayer, naming, authority, and sending. The mixed images of shepherdless flock and abundant harvest both keep agency with Christ. Chrysostom notes that prophetic labor has preceded the apostles; they reap what grace has prepared, while Christ is expressly the harvest's Lord. Mission therefore resists both despair, as though the field were empty, and triumphalism, as though the workers owned it.

The Twelve are individually named before they are sent. Their mission is corporate and personal, authorized yet bounded. The initial restriction to Israel belongs to this stage of Matthew's narrative and makes Christian anti-Judaism especially incoherent. The final command to give without cost recalls that authority, healing, and proclamation are received gifts. It rules out ownership and exploitation, not the wider New Testament's lawful support of ministers.

\subsection*{The independent apostolic foundation\enspace\elementref{Romans}}

Romans 5 does not function as a hidden allegory of Exodus or the Twelve. Paul intensifies humanity's condition: weak, ungodly, sinful, and hostile. Christ's death does not answer human worthiness but creates reconciliation. Chrysostom's direct exposition emphasizes the sequence from enemies reconciled through death to a stronger hope of salvation through Christ's living power.

This strand changes the spiritual posture of mission without pretending to explain why the Lectionary paired every text. Workers are first beneficiaries. “Without cost” can therefore be heard under Romans' priority of gift as a source-grounded synthesis: the Church gives what she did not originate, to persons she may not classify as deserving or undeserving.

\subsection*{Kingdom acclamation\enspace\elementref{Acclamation}}

Mark's verse is acclamatory: it announces fulfilled time, nearness of the Kingdom, conversion, and faith in order to welcome Christ about to speak. It should not be expanded into another homily before the Gospel. Its Kingdom language nonetheless clarifies the mission's object: the Twelve announce divine reign, not themselves.

\subsection*{Gift and sacramental unity\enspace\elementref{Prayer over Offerings, Communion, Prayer after Communion}}

The Prayer over the Offerings compares the created nourishment of bodily nature with sacramental renewal, then asks aid for both body and mind. This refuses a spirituality that treats embodiment as disposable. It also prevents an automatic promise of present bodily cure: the prayer concerns God's sustaining and sanctifying gift within the Church's sacramental horizon.

Communion option A continues Psalm 27's movement from plea to dwelling. Option B takes Christ's prayer that his disciples be one. The Prayer after Communion makes unity more than sentiment: reception signifies it and petitions its effect. Division cannot be healed by suppressing truth or justice, and sacramental reception is not magic; nevertheless, unity is God's gift before it becomes the Church's ethical work.

\section*{Study Guide: Further Interpretive Possibilities}

\begin{studybox}{Editorial status}
The following are original editorial or AI-generated possibilities. They are attributed to none of the cited authorities and claim no historical compositional intent. Each begins from appointed anchors and remains subordinate to the books' actual relationships.
\end{studybox}

\textbf{From counted names to unpossessed gifts.}\enspace\elementref{Gospel} The Gospel names each apostle yet ends by denying ownership of grace. This can support formation that joins personal responsibility to institutional humility. The limit is important: a named office is real authority, not merely a metaphor, and “free” ministry does not erase just material obligations.

\textbf{Unity as missionary truthfulness.}\enspace\elementref{Communion B, Prayer after Communion} Communion option B and the concluding prayer may illuminate the credibility of a Church sent by one Lord. Unity makes mission intelligible when it is communion in truth and charity. This possibility belongs specifically to Communion alternative B and must not imply that conflict should be concealed or victims silenced.

\textbf{One care for body and soul.}\enspace\elementref{Prayer over Offerings, Gospel} The offering prayer's account of bodily nourishment and sacramental renewal may guide a mission that joins proclamation, healing care, and respect for material need. It does not identify medicine with sacrament, promise miraculous cure, or reduce salvation to social provision.
