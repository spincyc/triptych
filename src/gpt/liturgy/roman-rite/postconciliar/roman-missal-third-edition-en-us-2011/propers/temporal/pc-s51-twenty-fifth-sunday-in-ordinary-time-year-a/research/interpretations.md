## Three coherent interpretive lanes across the whole formulary

These are modern cross-proper syntheses anchored in the named Fathers, **not claims that those Fathers commented on the 2011 formulary**. Each must remain coherent across four senses, with all units integrated and both Communion endings kept conditional.

### 1. Gift and cultivation: God gives himself, and makes a people capable of receiving him

**Stable key:** `gift-cultivation`. **Substantive authors:** Augustine (controlling Gospel, both Psalms, Acts, John); Aquinas (Isa55 cap55, mercy beyond vengeance); Chrysostom (Phil4, Christ-shaped life spent for others). Shared controlling claim: divine goodness is fruitful gift, received in conversion and enacted in charity. Aquinas establishes the specific Isaiah connection; Chrysostom supplies the Epistle's real charitable logic. Their supporting arguments do not assert Augustine's exact hour allocation or resolve later grace controversies.

**Checked reasoning and precise supports:** Augustine87.1–2 makes God's cultivation beneficial to the workers,87.6 identifies the denarius with endless life while preserving different glory, and Ps144.7,16 locates mercy in forgiveness/charity/final life and truthful prayer in desire for God himself. Predestination I.41 and Ps118 sermo4.2 (NPNF119 ALEPH5) supply the initiative/commanded-obedience relation; John47.2 explicitly binds reception of Christ's Blood to self-giving. Aquinas Isaiah55, Parma1863 pp556–557, makes mercy rather than retaliation the particular contrast in Isaiah. His Philippians1 lect3, Dessain1857 pp371–372, explains why love of God can prefer staying to serve the neighbor: friendship prizes God's honor above one's private enjoyment. Chrysostom Phil4 opening independently supplies that charitable choice. Bellarmine Ps118:5–7 corroborates enabled obedience; Jerome In Isaiam XV on55:8–9 corroborates the stable promise of forgiveness. These contributions establish the claim without pretending that any commentator constructed this entire Mass reading.

**Strongest difficulty and boundary:** Equal endless life does not cancel different glory or erase concrete labor. The complaint is not evidence of envious saints; use Chrysostom64.3 as a distinct explanation. The sacramental bridge rests on Augustine's explicit47.2, while linking it to this Sunday's offerings/after-Communion prayers is the editor's inference.

**Literal:** Begin with God's promise to hear the afflicted in the Entrance; the Collect defines commanded life as love. Isaiah summons a real return of life and thought, Psalm145 praises the giver's goodness, and Paul chooses fruitful work for others while desiring Christ. The acclamation requests the opening that hearing needs. In the vineyard the promised wage is paid in full and generosity to the late worker costs the early worker nothing. Offerings pass confessed faith into sacramental participation. Communion Psalm119 asks for enabled obedience; alternatively John10 names the Shepherd who knows his people. The final prayer asks the redemption received sacramentally to inhabit conduct.

**Allegorical:** The one Christ is both the manifest caller in Sermon 87 and the Shepherd entering hearts in John47. His word cultivates hearts (Gospel/acclamation); his law takes the form of charity (Collect/Ps119); his Blood buys the flock and nourishes self-giving members (offerings/John10/final prayer). Isaiah's abundant pardon and Psalm145's mercy disclose the God who gives charity itself. Paul shows a member living this Christ-shaped charity. The Church spans the successive calls of biblical history, all gathered by one Lord.

**Moral:** Do not bargain for a larger God or resent a fellow recipient. Seek God himself, not merely his gifts (Ps144.16); come when called, without despair or postponement (Sermon 87.8–10). Prayer for opened hearts and straight paths rejects self-sufficiency. Paul's willingness to remain makes neighbor-love costly and concrete; offerings and Communion commit the recipient to that same self-gift. Continuing help in the last prayer means conversion continues after reception.

**Anagogical:** The denarius signifies one endless life, not a ration proportionate to years served (Sermon 87.6). Collect's eternal life, psalm's perpetual praise, Paul's being with Christ, Entrance's enduring lordship, and Shepherd's pasture converge on possession of God. Different glory is not denied. Eucharistic participation anticipates that life and trains people to rejoice together in it. One cannot desire that communion while insisting that another must receive less.

**Interleave:** After the wage explanation insert Chrysostom's caution that the complaint is not literal envy in heaven. After the opened heart insert his cooperative emphasis alongside Augustine's insistence that faith's beginning is gift.

### 2. Conversion without rivalry: it is still time to enter, and the work is mercy

**Stable key:** `conversion-without-rivalry`. **Substantive authors:** Chrysostom (controlling Matt64, Phil3–4, Acts 35); Augustine (Sermon 87.7–10 and Ps144.7,18); Aquinas (Isa55). Shared controlling claim: late conversion is neither hopeless nor postponable, and real response becomes mercy toward others. Augustine provides the double safeguard against despair/presumption and the psalm's insistence on present conversion. Distinguish the authors' grace emphases locally; do not convert Chrysostom's readiness language into an Augustinian formula or vice versa.

**Checked reasoning and precise supports:** Chrysostom64.3 identifies commandments/present life/personal ages and gives late converts courage;64.4 distinguishes equal reward within the parable from the maxim's actual reversal;64.5 demands practice and almsgiving. His Acts35 opening/recap relates divine opening to attentive response and hospitality, and Phil4 opening directs personal desire toward another's good. Augustine87.7–10 and Ps144.7,18 prevent both postponement and despair. Jerome In Isaiam XV on55:6–7 grounds urgency in the present embodied opportunity and requires abandoning old conduct/thoughts; Aquinas Isaiah55 pp556–557 reinforces seeking before adversity/death. The controlling agreement is active conversion through mercy; Chrysostom's readiness emphasis is retained beside Augustine's gift emphasis.

**Strongest difficulty and boundary:** The last hour is an invitation to the latecomer, not a plan for deliberate delay. The wage parable does not promise another hour, excuse passivity, or prescribe an economic wage policy. Gregory's historical ages answer a different question and should be compared at the hours without changing this reading's personal-conversion axis.

**Literal:** The summons is repeated through the day, and the eleventh-hour workers actually respond. Isaiah's while/near summons and the psalm's patient mercy give the call urgency without despair. The Entrance promises a hearer in distress, not an exemption from labor. The Collect's twofold love states the work; Paul's further life means benefit to others. Lydia's listening becomes hospitality. The offering prayer and both Communion choices lead toward the final prayer's redeemed behavior: believed, received, lived.

**Allegorical:** Christ's commandments are the vineyard, and present life is its working day (Matt64). The caller and good Shepherd know the persons they summon. The acclamation is a new moment of that call, with the Church listening through the Spirit's help. Sacramental gifts embody the mercy from which the call comes and enable the charity commanded. Paul supplies a living apostolic image of the worker who labors from love rather than comparative entitlement.

**Moral:** Late conversion should be earnest, never hopeless; early conversion must remain fruitful, never entitled. Stop measuring a neighbor's portion. Replace envy with almsgiving and mercy, and readiness to obey with actual attendance to the Word. Paul's desire to be with Christ does not cancel usefulness to others. At Communion Ps119 makes obedience a prayer, or John10 makes it response to a known Shepherd; final prayer asks this response to survive the church door.

**Anagogical:** The late convert's honor is full, while the kingdom itself is free of envy. The offered future releases both despair and rivalry. Collect and Paul orient labor to eternal communion; psalm and Entrance anchor trust in the goodness of the one who gives it. The sacraments feed this hope during labor; they are not a reason to abandon the actual work of mercy.

**Interleave:** At the hours note Gregory's Church-historical alternative; at heavenly equality note Augustine's endless-duration account. Keep Chrysostom's own distinction between equal reward and the maxim's first/last reversal.

### 3. One people through the ages: summoned into a vineyard older and larger than ourselves

**Stable key:** `one-people-through-ages`. **Substantive authors:** Gregory (controlling Hom19.1–4); Augustine (Sermon 87.5,9,14–15; Ps118sermo4.1–3; John47.2). Shared controlling claim: one God cultivates and gathers one people across history, and members must labor for one another. Augustine's Pentecost, ecclesial psalm voice, and Eucharistic charity supply substantive support. His exact hour allocation and common-resurrection payment explanation remain parallel alternatives to Gregory's distinct account; they are not merged. Chrysostom supplies a different primary identification (commandments and ages of persons), not an explicit polemic against the ethnic reading. His closing-maxim discussion permits a hint concerning Jews; he is not enlisted as a witness to Gregory's exact historical timetable.

**Checked reasoning and precise supports:** Gregory19.1 locates the one vineyard from Abel to the last elect;19.2 applies it also to personal ages and makes charity/bringing others toward life the work;19.3–4 explains late entrance and ancient waiting. Augustine87.9,14–15 supplies preaching/Pentecost, sacramental reception by former persecutors, and active recruitment of friends; John47.2 supplies members' self-gift, and the independently reread Latin Ps118 sermo4.1 identifies the psalm voice as member or whole body of Christ. Aquinas Philippians1 lect4, Dessain1857 p373, offers later reception of communal love/concord/cooperation, though the fuller27b is context. These are substantive accounts of corporate gathering and labor, not merely two authors who mention a vineyard.

**Strongest difficulty and boundary:** Gregory's historical timetable and Augustine87.5 are different witnesses, not pieces to merge. Gregory's delay-until-Christ and Augustine's common-resurrection payment-order accounts must be compared explicitly; this reading claims one Lord and end across history, not one agreed mechanism for every narrative detail. Historical Jewish/Gentile imagery is not a warrant for present contempt or a claim that every Jew was faithless: Gregory himself places Israel's holy fathers within the one vineyard.

**Literal:** A common landowner gathers workers at many times; the Sunday sequence includes Israel's prophet and psalmist, Paul's apostolic service, Lydia's reception, and Christ's parable and Shepherd saying. The universal Entrance and charity Collect prevent the hearer from making the work a private possession. Offerings, sacramental participation, and final conduct remain the worship of God's people, with Psalm119 or John10 as alternate Communion accents.

**Allegorical:** Gregory's vineyard is the universal Church extending from Abel to the last elect. Successive calls manifest one economy of salvation; Isaiah/Psalm witness earlier cultivation, Paul/Acts later apostolic gathering. Christ opens the kingdom and calls people into one flock. The common faith confessed in offerings is sacramentally received; the final prayer sends the cultivated people into a common life of charity. This is Gregory's historical lens extended across modern propers by synthesis.

**Moral:** Long membership gives responsibility to cultivate others, not ownership of their admission. Gregory19.2 identifies labor with charity and bringing others toward life. Isaiah asks abandonment of one's old way; Paul stays for another's good; Lydia receives the messengers. The Collect binds duty toward God to neighbor, and final prayer binds worship to conduct. Both Communion options sustain this: grace to keep commands, or recognition of the Shepherd whose flock includes more than oneself.

**Anagogical:** All ages are directed to the same kingdom opened by Christ. Gregory's thief-before-Peter and early-fathers-awaiting-Christ accounts make late arrival compatible with swift entrance. Collect's perpetual life, Paul's departure toward Christ, psalm's praise, and Entrance's lasting lordship point toward that common end. The sacramental banquet anticipates the gathered flock. Earlier service remains honored without monopolizing mercy.

**Interleave:** Chrysostom's principal identification differs from Gregory's corporate-historical account without explicitly rejecting it; Augustine's hour allocation and explanation of payment sequence also differ. Do not say all Fathers teach one precise timetable.

The concise study should interleave three questions: **What is the vineyard?** (commandments/present life versus Church through history); **What makes the wage just?** (full promised gift, one eternal life, no loss to another); **What follows from receiving it?** (opened heart, urgent conversion, neighbor-serving labor, sacramental life becoming conduct). Within those questions retain literal/spiritual distinctions and explicit alternative answers instead of successive lane summaries. The strongest difficulties are the apparent envy of saved workers, different historical hour assignments, and the modern Gospel's shorter endpoint. None may disappear in compression.


## Concrete comparison and editorial construction

Gift-cultivation asks what the giver makes possible: a heart able to hear, love and receive one endless life. Conversion-without-rivalry asks what the called person must do now: enter, abandon envy, and serve. One-people-through-ages asks whose vineyard this is: God's people exceed one generation and must cultivate one another. The first two complement each other while differing in controlling emphasis; the third introduces a materially different first identification of the vineyard and hours. No shared school or unanimous detailed timetable is proposed.

Reading each unit separately would miss the movement from hearing to requested obedience, from Paul's useful labor to sacramental self-giving, and from received redemption to conduct. These are explicit editorial connections among actual appointed units. The semi-continuous Epistle and cycle-shared prayers have not been proven historically designed around the vineyard. Both Communion alternatives close that movement differently: requested obedience in Psalm119, or personal/corporate belonging to the Shepherd in John10. The homily may choose one branch and one argument; it must not report both alternatives as performed.
