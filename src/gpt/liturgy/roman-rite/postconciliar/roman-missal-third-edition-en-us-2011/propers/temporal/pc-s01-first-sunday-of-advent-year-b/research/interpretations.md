# Two readings of the First Sunday of Advent, Year B

Each proposed interpretation is the study editor's reading across the U.S. Missal and Lectionary. Both readings follow the ordinary Sunday route with Psalm 80 and assume no wreath rite. The seasonal sung alternatives and the order for the wreath belong to the branch audit. The Fathers and Doctors are named where their own words warrant the inference. The three orations are illuminated by the USCCB commentary, and the Preface supplies the liturgy's own account of the two comings. Neither is evidence that an ancient exegete interpreted this entire set. The [scope](scope.md) identifies original loci, translations, rights and the literary contexts from which the excerpts come.

## 1. The people who ask God to turn them

**Controlling question.** How can a people who confess that no one calls on God ask to be found doing right? That question uses the U.S. wish at 64:4. Challoner 64:5 instead looks back to a just person whom God has met and adds an assurance of salvation after the admission of sin. Jerome, *In Isaiam* XVII, p. 155, explains that assurance as dependence on mercy alone. The proclamation moves straight into the people's uncleanness. The complaint in Isaiah 63:17 sounds as if the fault were God's. By the time the reading ends, the worshippers own their sin and still call the one whom they addressed their Father. Their ability to ask is already part of the answer they need.

Jerome (*In Isaiam* XVII, pp. 149–151, 158–159) does not tidy the difficulty away: a prayer can contain both confession and a sinner's attempt to shift blame. He brings in Paul's ability to rebuke some Corinthians while praising others in a letter addressed to one church. God may wait to correct, as a physician conceals a frightening instrument from the patient; the patient mistakes mercy for indifference. The final appeal is possible precisely because God's clay is also his children. Its speaker has no independent merits to place before the potter. Augustine (*Enarrationes* 79.3, 9, 11; 84.6, 11–12) hears the repeated plea for God to convert the people. The imperative addresses the one who can do what they cannot do unaided. The planted vine needs its own planter to perfect it. Once made alive the people can call on him. Augustine makes that change observable in Ps 84.12: someone who used to take pleasure in stealing now enjoys giving to someone in need. On the preceding verse he has already identified truthful confession as one way for truth to rise from the earth. The God who changes the delight of the will is neither the author of theft nor merely the auditor of restitution. These two authors jointly answer the problem of how a guilty people comes to pray.

**Consequential comparison.** The Collect names righteous deeds and Isaiah calls the people's justices polluted. Thomas's *Super Isaiam* 64 (§85024) pinpoints bad intention, negligence and pride among the ways an apparently good act can be soiled. The Collect asks God for the willing response itself. The contrast is between what the sick offer as their own righteousness and the real deeds of persons whom God is healing. At Communion the petition preceding the Gospel has become the assurance of the earth's fruit. Augustine's moral referent for the fruit is the ability to love doing right; in the other reading Bellarmine will identify that same fruit with Christ's birth. There is no need to turn one interpretation into the other's wording.

**Literal.** Israel prays from the experience of ruin and guilt. Its ancestral memories leave it dependent on the God who first made it a people. Paul thanks God for a similarly undeserved gift in a community that still needs correction. **Allegorical.** Augustine hears Christ speaking in his Church in Ps 24 and sees in the hidden face the humility of Christ crucified. The rite links his received gift with the people who ask him to appear. **Moral.** The truth of conversion shows up in what a person wants to do with what lies to hand. A hand formerly reaching for another's property can open to give. **Anagogical.** The congregation seeks the giver's face and hopes to stand at Christ's right. The contrast between today's approach and the open appearance to come makes the hope possible while preventing a claim of entitlement.

## 2. The work the master leaves in the house

**Controlling question.** Why does a householder who expects his servants to keep watch entrust them with *work*, and why should a church that lacks no gift still need confirmation? The Gospel does not ask them to estimate how long it takes the master to travel. In his absence he has assigned them real responsibilities. They can be judged on what they do with what was first entrusted to them.

Bede (*In Marci evangelium*, on Mk 13:33–37, lines 403–405) treats the householder's departure through the Ascension. Christ is bodily with the Father and still gives the Spirit to the household. The servants therefore have the means to carry out their assignments. Bede initially understands the porter as a pastor responsible for the Church. When Jesus says the word “all,” Bede sees each person entrusted with a door too: everyone must guard the entry to his own heart. Keeping what one believes by doing it is a form of wakefulness. Chrysostom (*Homily* 2 on 1 Cor, §§4–8) asks why Paul praises people who act like children. One possibility is that the initial enthusiasm of the gifted has waned; another is that the community includes servants who still minister faithfully. Paul goes on to call the Lord's coming a *revelation*: the one they do not see is already present. When Paul tells them he will keep them firm, his future tense also exposes how unsteady they are. They were called into fellowship with a Son and now attach themselves to competing human teachers. Bede explains what holding office and work means during an absence; Chrysostom names the temptation of a gifted household to mistake its endowments for proof that it has carried out its charge.

**Consequential comparison.** Thomas's alternative on 1 Cor 1:7 (*Super I ad Corinthios* 1, lect. 1, §86234) leaves the present plenitude intact without predicating it of each individual. Gifts can be distributed among several people, and charity gives each person a share in another's good. Compare Chrysostom's possibility of decline with Thomas's reading of the plural recipients. One asks whether Corinth has fallen below its beginning, the other whose gifts Paul can praise in the present. Each directs attention beyond an inventory of individual talents. Augustine (*Ep.* 199, I.2–3) adds a separate answer to the Gospel's “all”: Christians who will die before the last day still have to meet it as the people their lives made them. Bede asks about every person's charge and Augustine about every generation's share in the judgment. Do not turn the hour of each death into a public second coming.

| Alleluia | The assembly asks for the salvation which a servant cannot award himself. On Ps 84:8 Bellarmine identifies the Savior as the great mercy Israel seeks. |
| Gospel | The servant with a particular assignment and the porter with his particular charge answer to one master. Each hears the final imperative. The four watches fill out the possible night, with no timetable for the return. |
| Offerings | The gifts carried forward show what people can do with what did not originate with them. For the USCCB commentator, cultivation of grain and grapes and provision for people in need both involve human labor. |
| Preface I | The same Christ comes in humility and in majesty. Cyril (XV.1–3) compares the one who stood under judgment with the one who comes as judge. That identity lets the church speak of fidelity to a living master it cannot now see. |
| Communion | Bellarmine's flower, growing unsown under sun and rain, figures Christ born of the Virgin through the Spirit. Thus the fruit is first the Lord given to the household; what the servants can do proceeds from him. This is Bellarmine on the psalm and the editor's sacramental application. |
| After Communion | Bread and wine, the products of labor, served the encounter at the altar. Those who return to work can learn to use passing things in the light of a good that endures. |
| Solemn blessing if chosen | Its first and last invocations enclose the present race. The virtues named in between describe the kind of service possible before the master's appearance. |

**Literal.** The absent master gives several servants work, commands the porter and finally addresses all. Paul likewise thanks God for the gifts of a church that still has work to do. **Allegorical.** Bede identifies the bodily absent yet divinely present master with the ascended Christ, who has entrusted the Church and supplied his Spirit. Cyril's two comings explain why the judge is the same humbled person the Church already knows. **Moral.** One keeps watch by doing the task entrusted and by making another person's gift available to the whole community. Dividing the household by favorite teacher would betray that charge even in a gifted church. **Anagogical.** When Christ appears the household awaits someone already known through his gifts. Every generation must prepare to render account; the indeterminacy of the time makes faithful service possible at any hour.

The difference persists if the headings disappear. At the fruit of the earth, the first reading asks what changes in the recipient's will, and the second asks who has been given before any human work can be accepted. At the household door, the first asks how a sleepy and guilty people can respond at all, and the second what each servant must now do. A sermon can choose either difficulty and carry its answer through the offering or Communion without pretending the Fathers preached on the U.S. Mass.
