\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}
\subsection{Whose vineyard, and whose work?}
Matthew places the story after Peter's question about the reward for leaving everything to follow Jesus. Literally, the landowner keeps his agreement and repeatedly invites others into real labor. The early workers' complaint concerns equality with the latecomers, not an unpaid debt. The spiritual readings begin from that kept promise and contested generosity.

In Sermon 87.1--2, Augustine pictures God cultivating us: the word ploughs hearts, removes evil seed, and plants commandments. Our worship does not enrich God; his cultivation makes us fruitful. Chrysostom, in Matthew 64.3, identifies the vineyard with the commandments, the day with present life, and the hours with the ages at which people turn to God. Augustine's emphasis falls on the giver who changes the worker; Chrysostom's on the worker who must enter. Gift makes obedience fruitful, while a late invitation still asks for an actual answer.

Gregory's first identification is corporate: the vineyard is the universal Church, from Abel to the last elect. In Gospels I.19.1 he assigns the hours to Adam--Noah, Noah--Abraham, Abraham--Moses, Moses--Christ, and Christ--the end. Augustine's historical sequence differs: Abel and Noah, patriarchs, Moses and Aaron, prophets, Christians (Sermon 87.5). Both place the assembly within a people no generation owns. Gregory also admits personal life stages. Chrysostom's primary personal reading is no explicit rejection of every Jewish--Gentile application; his closing-maxim discussion permits a hint concerning Jews.

This allegorical difference changes the question the hearer faces. The vineyard of commandments asks whether I have begun to live rightly; the Church through history asks whether I am helping others toward life within a people larger than myself. Gregory's vineyard already includes Israel's holy fathers. Their labor is honored, and their presence excludes treating the earlier people as uniformly faithless.

The difference between the personal and corporate vineyards reaches the whole formulary. In Chrysostom's reading, the invitation asks a person to begin living the commandments during the time still available. In Gregory's, that person also enters a history of divine cultivation already under way. Isaiah and the psalmist do not become discarded preliminaries when Paul and Lydia appear. They are earlier voices within the people whose Lord still calls. Augustine's cultivated heart makes the two scales intelligible together: a people is gathered through persons changed by the word, and those persons bear fruit through responsibility for one another.

Augustine's and Gregory's historical hours differ. Gregory makes the ninth hour the age from Moses to Christ, while Augustine assigns the ninth hour to the prophets and places Moses and Aaron at the sixth. Their shared conviction is a Lord gathering his people across history, but their schedules give different accounts of that history. Chrysostom's personal ages answer another question again: when a person begins the work, not simply where a generation stands in a chronology of salvation.

The Entrance, \textit{Salus populi ego sum}, gives the assembly confidence in divine help amid distress. Its identified scriptural basis, Psalm 37:39--40, supports patient fidelity rather than escape from every trial; the composed antiphon is not simply those verses. The Collect, \textit{Deus, qui sacrae legis}, names love of God and neighbor as the form of obedience. Thus the work is neither adding to God's wealth nor securing possession of his people. It is becoming fruitful in a charity received from him.

\subsection{Why does generosity seem unfair?}
Augustine distinguishes an honored agreement from a demand created by comparison (Sermon 87.4--6). The early workers lose nothing when another receives the same. He identifies the denarius with eternal life: one person's endless life cannot last longer than another's. Different glory and attainment remain possible. The equal payment concerns the gift's endless duration, not the erasure of every difference among the saints.

Isaiah gives this wounded sense of justice a searching counterpart. The call to seek and return follows an invitation to nourishment without purchase. Abundant pardon accompanies the demand to abandon wicked ways and thoughts. Aquinas, commenting on Isaiah 55:8, contrasts the human impulse to avenge injury with God's will to show mercy. God's higher thoughts are more than an unexplained remoteness. They challenge the retaliatory measure by which a sinner imagines forgiveness.

Jerome emphasizes the stability of that promise: God's counsel does not fluctuate like human plans. Irenaeus uses the same verse for a different question, resisting the projection of creaturely passions and divided mental processes into God. Mercy's height and the Creator's transcendence are complementary emphases, not interchangeable explanations. The promised pardon can be trusted without reducing God to a more accommodating version of ourselves.

The responsorial Psalm joins greatness, mercy toward all God's works, and nearness to truthful invocation. Augustine understands mercy to include pardon, the justifying Spirit, charity enabling works, and eternal life; truthful prayer desires God himself above his benefits (Psalm 144.7, 16). Bellarmine likewise treats patience as an invitation to repentance. The assembly cannot coherently praise universal goodness while wishing its neighbor excluded from that goodness.

The Entrance's psalmic background makes comparison a problem before the wage is distributed. Psalm 37 warns against measuring justice by the wicked person's apparent success. Augustine asks his hearer to correct the eye that judges providence crooked. Matthew's workers likewise let another's advantage change their judgment of the giver. Yet the objects of their resentment differ: the psalm addresses the prosperity of evildoers, while the vineyard grants a good to late workers whom the householder has called. The resemblance diagnoses a distorted measure; it does not identify the latecomers with the wicked.

The literal account of justice also stands. The early workers are paid what was agreed, and the late workers perform the labor asked of them. Augustine's endless denarius develops the kingdom signified by that event; it does not authorize an employer to disregard an agreement. Chrysostom's spiritual encouragement does not remove the demand to work. Divine generosity transforms the worker's desire without making ordinary honesty dispensable. The Collect's love of God and neighbor belongs here because another's good is precisely what resentful comparison finds difficult to love.

But does the vineyard predict envy in heaven? Chrysostom denies it: saints who give themselves for sinners now will rejoice in their salvation then. The complaint magnifies the latecomer's unexpected honor (Matthew 64.3). Gregory also excludes heavenly murmuring, but reads the complaint as the ancient fathers' long waiting before Christ opened paradise. His thief-before-Peter example shows how a late call can be followed by swift entrance (Gospels I.19.3--4).

Gregory's account gives the earlier workers a particular dignity. Their waiting is not simply punishment for arriving before Christ; his vineyard already contains the holy fathers whose just lives precede the opening of paradise. The story can therefore be read across Israel and the nations without turning the earlier people into a single figure of faithlessness. Augustine's different explanation likewise honors earlier service, even as he denies that it can increase the endless duration of the reward. Chrysostom's primary personal reading then keeps the historical comparison from becoming an excuse to inspect only other groups. The hearer still has to enter and work.

Augustine explains payment order through common resurrection: all receive together, but the first-called have waited longer, while latecomers receive after a shorter interval (Sermon 87.5). These are distinct accounts of the promised end. Chrysostom explains a narrative feature, Gregory Christ's opening of paradise and differing delay, Augustine a common reception after unequal waiting. The anagogical hope they illuminate is shared blessedness, without one unanimous timetable.

Chrysostom further distinguishes equality within the parable from the closing maxim's actual reversal: someone who began well can neglect virtue, while someone formerly sunk in vice advances (Matthew 64.4). His received text includes the many-called, few-chosen clause. Today's Gospel stops at 20:16a, after the first--last saying; that additional clause is not appointed here. The generosity of the story supplies neither permission for negligence nor a rule for modern wage policy.

\subsection{What must happen when the call is heard?}
For Chrysostom, the late worker's full portion gives courage to those converted in extreme old age. For Augustine, the same promise exposes the danger of postponement: a promised reward does not promise another hour of life (Sermon 87.7--10). The two emphases belong together. A late beginning need not destroy hope, and hope cannot justify a refusal to begin. Despair and presumptuous delay both resist the present call.

Isaiah's verbs make the response concrete: seek, abandon, return. Jerome locates the opportunity in embodied life, while Aquinas stresses seeking before adversity and death. The psalm's patience sustains this urgency without turning it into hopelessness. The Lord is near to those who call truthfully; nearness invites conversion rather than declaring the old conduct harmless.

The acclamation adapts Lydia's story in Acts 16:14b into the assembly's request for receptive hearing. Augustine argues that the opening of her heart shows faith's very beginning to be God's gift (Predestination I.41). Chrysostom emphasizes the Lord's action together with Lydia's attention and willing response, which bears fruit in hospitality (Acts 35). Their questions and emphases differ: the origin of faith and its active reception. Neither an inert listener nor a self-sufficient beginner describes the fruit they seek.

Lydia provides a small, concrete scene in which gift and response can be distinguished without being separated. The Lord opens; she attends; hospitality follows. The acclamation brings the request for receptive hearing before the Gospel, where repeated invitations seek an answer. Augustine locates faith's first good movement in God's gift. Chrysostom locates Lydia's attention within a willing reception that bears fruit in hospitality. The two explanations illuminate the same conversion from different questions: divine initiative and active reception remain joined without becoming identical accounts.

Gregory carries that response into the common vineyard. A person occupied only with private advantage has not begun the Lord's work; labor means charity and bringing others toward life (Gospels I.19.2). Augustine develops the gathering through Christ made known in preaching and Pentecost, then urges care for friends with whom one hopes to live eternally (Sermon 87.9, 14--15). Membership becomes responsibility for another's good.

Here the three readings meet morally and still differ. God's cultivation produces gratitude and dependence. The working day calls for conversion without despair or entitlement. The one Church calls for care extending beyond one's own generation and circle. Chrysostom's concluding demand for conduct and almsgiving makes the response tangible: another person's negligence supplies no excuse for our own (Matthew 64.5).

\subsection{How does receiving become self-giving?}
Paul supplies the positive answer to the workers' resentment. In Philippians 1 he desires to be with Christ, yet recognizes that remaining brings fruitful service to others. Chrysostom dwells on the cost: Paul willingly postpones desired rest for their progress. Aquinas explains the choice through friendship, which can prefer God's honor to private enjoyment. Love of neighbor expresses love of God here; it does not displace it. Continued embodied life is fruitful, not something to despise or deliberately shorten.

The cost of Paul's willingness matters. If remaining were simply the comfortable choice, it would scarcely answer the early workers' account of burdens borne. Chrysostom pictures a laden trader delaying harbor, an athlete continuing the contest and a soldier remaining in battle. The service is costly because the desired rest is real. Aquinas then locates the choice within friendship: another's good can become the expression of love for the beloved. Their arguments explain how labor can continue without being sustained by the demand to outrank a neighbor. The apostle's fruitful work supplies something the wage dispute alone does not show: the positive desire that replaces rivalry.

The Collect's twofold love thus becomes visible in a worker who wants others to flourish. Augustine's cultivation acquires a concrete fruit; Chrysostom's converted worker has a task; Gregory's common vineyard has members serving its growth. The early workers ask why another receives so much. Paul remains so that others may receive more fully. His demand for conduct worthy of the Gospel addresses the assembly as well as his own situation. Verses 25--26, which develop his hope for their progress, are context beyond today's selection.

The Prayer over the Offerings, \textit{Munera, quaesumus, Domine}, brings professed faith toward sacramental participation. Augustine gives the receiving-to-giving relation a precise Eucharistic form in John 47.2. Christ purchased the flock with his Blood; its preacher did not. Those receiving Christ's Body and Blood are summoned to give themselves for their brethren. The giver cultivates members who learn his own charity. His gift also makes the corporate vineyard more than a collection of people occupying one place.

The two Communion alternatives close the movement differently. If Psalm 119:4--5 is used, commanded obedience becomes a petition for directed ways. Augustine hears the need to ask God for the ability to do what he commands; the fuller Latin exposition also identifies the speaker as a member or the whole body of Christ. Bellarmine similarly rejects confidence in unaided strength. This ending stresses enabled fidelity, both personal and ecclesial.

If John 10:14 is used instead, the Shepherd's mutual knowledge with his sheep stands within his discourse of self-offering. Augustine explains Christ entering hearts through the preaching of Christ and nourishing members for mutual charity. This ending stresses belonging to the one who gave himself. The Psalm's requested obedience and John's personal relationship are alternatives in the same Communion slot, not consecutive moments in one enacted celebration.

These endings also reveal the difference between a private resolution and a common life. In the Psalm alternative, Augustine's fuller Latin exposition hears a member or the entire body of Christ speaking. The prayer for directed ways can therefore be the body's request for its members to act faithfully. In the John alternative, the personal center is the Shepherd who knows the sheep and has paid the price of their life. Augustine distinguishes that price from the preacher's work: the minister serves a flock he did not purchase. Gregory's vineyard and Augustine's table both exclude possession of the people as a worker's private achievement.

The Prayer after Communion, \textit{Quos tuis, Domine}, asks continuing help for redemption to bear fruit in sacramental life and conduct. Dependence remains after reception, and the work of charity remains before the assembly. Literally, these prayers move from confession through participation toward lived response. Allegorically, Christ nourishes his body; morally, recipients serve its members; anagogically, the gift anticipates shared life with him. Augustine's endless denarius, Chrysostom's full hope for the latecomer, and Gregory's gathered generations give distinct depth to that common end. Another person's arrival can become a joy rather than a subtraction.

\subsection{A shared hope changes the work}
The three readings answer different objections. If equal reward seems to make labor pointless, Augustine shows that the labor itself belongs to God's fruitful cultivation. If a late beginning seems to make hope pointless, Chrysostom directs the latecomer toward an earnest answer, while Augustine denies permission to delay. If long membership seems to make the vineyard a possession, Gregory places it before and beyond one generation, while Augustine's preaching and table gather former opponents into its life. No single identification needs to replace the others for these answers to be compatible.

Their literal ground remains the landowner's kept promise and the actual entry of those called. Their allegorical depth concerns Christ cultivating, commanding and gathering. Their moral fruit is charity toward the person whose reception might once have prompted resentment. Their final hope is communion with him, where another's good is no loss. Isaiah's promised pardon, the Psalm's truthful invocation, Paul's useful remaining, the opened heart and the Eucharistic prayers each give that movement a concrete form. The receiver who asks to hear and obey is nourished to become a giver.
