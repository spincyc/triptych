\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}

\begin{massbox}{Governing account}
The correlated strand joins Solomon's discerning request, the Psalm's valuation of God's instruction, and Jesus' treasure and pearl: wisdom learns what is worth choosing. Romans adds a semi-continuous claim about divine purpose amid suffering. The orations and antiphons place temporal goods, gifts, mercy, memory, and salvation under grace rather than possession.
\end{massbox}

\begin{studybox}{Four-stage movement at a glance}
\textbf{1. Ask for a hearing heart --- Entrance; Collect; First Reading.} God gathers and strengthens a people whose ruler must confess inadequacy and receive discernment.

\textbf{2. Learn what exceeds gold --- Psalm; Gospel.} Instruction, treasure, and pearl form desire toward the Kingdom; joyful surrender is neither purchase of grace nor contempt for creation.

\textbf{3. Receive purpose without fatalism --- Romans; Acclamation.} God's call and conforming purpose meet a suffering people; revelation remains gift to the humble.

\textbf{4. Offer, remember, become merciful --- Offerings; Communion; final prayer.} Goods first received return to God; sacramental memorial bears fruit in mercy, purity, and salvation.
\end{studybox}

\subsection*{1. A hearing heart governs value\enspace\elementref{Entrance; Collect; First Reading}}

Solomon asks not for easy cases but for a heart able to hear and distinguish in service of the people. The Collect broadens that dependence: without God nothing is strong or holy, and temporal goods pass under divine rule without loss of the eternal. Discernment is graced ordering of loves, not cleverness.

\subsection*{2. The Kingdom exceeds what can be priced\enspace\elementref{Responsorial Psalm; Gospel}}

Psalm 119 values divine instruction beyond gold. The treasure finder and merchant sell all with decisiveness and, in the first image, joy. The figures describe the Kingdom's incomparable claim; they do not imply that grace is bought, that material creation is evil, or that reckless financial conduct is holy.

\subsection*{3. Purpose is conformity, not a promise of ease\enspace\elementref{Second Reading; Acclamation}}

Romans 8:28--30 follows groaning and Spirit-aided prayer and leads toward suffering unable to sever believers from Christ's love. ``Good'' is God's conforming purpose, not immediate comfort, wealth, or a claim that every event is good. The acclamation remains a distinct Matthean strand.

\newpage

\subsection*{4. Received goods become offering and mercy\enspace\elementref{Prayer over Offerings; Communion; Prayer after Communion}}

The Prayer over the Offerings says that gifts come from divine bounty and asks that the mysteries sanctify present conduct. Communion A summons grateful memory; Communion B names mercy and purity of heart. The final prayer names the sacrament as the perpetual memorial of the Son's Passion and asks that this gift of ineffable love advance salvation. Neither branch is selected here.

\begin{studybox}{New and old without supersession}
Only the long Gospel names a trained scribe bringing out treasures new and old. Patristic Christians often related these to the two Testaments. The image preserves both as treasure; it cannot justify calling Israel, Jewish Scripture, or Judaism discarded, spiritually barren, or merely obsolete. Christian reading remains indebted to Israel's Scriptures and must reject contempt for the Jewish people.
\end{studybox}

\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

\subsection*{Solomon asks to hear before he judges\enspace\elementref{First Reading; Collect}}

The lectionary preserves Solomon's confession of youth and inexperience, the people entrusted to him, the request for a discerning heart, and God's approval. The omitted material is not silently supplied as proclaimed text. The request is public in consequence even though received in a dream: wisdom is sought for just governance, not private distinction.

Ambrose cites the requested prudent heart in \emph{On the Duties of the Clergy} II.8 as a heart able to hear and judge justly, then develops the later judgment between two mothers. He illuminates the union of prudence and justice; the appointed pericope itself, not Ambrose, supplies the contrast with riches and longevity.

The broader canonical story supplies the indispensable limit. The king who asks well can later accumulate wealth, wives, and divided allegiance. Wisdom received is not fidelity guaranteed. The Collect's repeated dependence---God as protector, ruler, and guide---keeps discernment from becoming self-certifying expertise.

Peter Comestor's medieval \emph{Historia scholastica} retells the dream while preserving the distinction between Solomon's request and God's additional gifts. It witnesses to the episode's long pedagogical life but is not an independent source for the ancient event. Later historical and devotional commentators likewise admire the request while the canonical sequel controls idealization.

\textbf{Confess inadequacy} & Office does not create the wisdom it requires. & Humility does not excuse refusal to learn or decide.\\
\textbf{Ask for a hearing heart} & Discernment serves justice for a concrete people. & It is not intuition exempt from evidence, counsel, or law.\\
\textbf{Receive gift} & God approves a request ordered beyond advantage. & Gift does not make later choices infallible.\\
\end{threestable}

\subsection*{Gathered strength and gifts returned\enspace\elementref{Entrance; Prayer over Offerings}}

The Entrance adapts clauses from Psalm 67 rather than proclaiming a continuous span. The whole processional hymn praises God's coming, defense of the vulnerable, gift of a household, and strength for the people. Augustine treats divine presence, gathering, and strength as gifts rather than human possession (\emph{Exposition on Psalm 68}, 6, 8, 40); Bellarmine later receives the same movement within ecclesial praise. Their Christian applications do not erase Israel's psalm, and anti-Jewish developments elsewhere in the inherited reception are excluded.

The Prayer over the Offerings asks God to receive gifts that first come from divine bounty and to make the mysteries sanctify present conduct. The ritual movement is reception, return, and transformation: the assembly does not purchase grace with its own property. This shared Week XVII prayer may illuminate the Gospel's relinquishment, but it was not proved to have been composed for Year A or Lectionary 109. There is no Missal Offertory antiphon, and the actual music used remains unknown.

\subsection*{Instruction, treasure, and pearl\enspace\elementref{Responsorial Psalm; Gospel branches}}

Psalm 119 repeatedly turns value into prayer: the Lord is the singer's portion; instruction exceeds gold and silver; commands are loved above fine gold; their unfolding gives light. The refrain's love of the law is not abstract admiration but a sustained appetite formed by meditation, mercy, and obedience.

Augustine keeps the appointed loci's portion, love of law, covenant mercy, judgment, wonder, and understanding together; Bellarmine likewise connects valuation and the request for light. Neither composed the later selection, whose separated stanzas answer to verse 97a. Christian reception illuminates only while the Psalm remains Israel's Torah meditation, not obsolete material awaiting Christian ownership.

The two parables intensify valuation. One person encounters treasure and acts in joy; the merchant searches and recognizes one pearl of great price. Chrysostom's \emph{Homily 47 on Matthew} emphasizes the Kingdom's hidden worth and the single-minded response it evokes. The differences remain: discovery and search, field and market, treasure and pearl should not be flattened into one psychology.

Joy does not purchase the Kingdom. In both images the incomparable discovery reorganizes possession; the price imagery cannot make salvation a commodity. Nor can ``selling all'' be abstracted into irresponsible disposal of dependents' goods or contempt for material creation. The Psalm itself values instruction through a material comparison without calling gold evil.

\sectionguard
\subsection*{The long branch: net, judgment, and a scribe with both treasures\enspace\elementref{long Gospel}}

Only the long form proceeds to the net gathering every kind, the shore-side sorting, the angels' final separation, and the householder's new and old treasure. The net gives eschatological seriousness to value: present gathering does not erase final judgment. It is not a mandate for disciples to conduct the angelic sorting now.

Origen's \emph{Commentary on Matthew} X treats the trained scribe and the relation of new and old learning. Augustine's \emph{Sermon 24} likewise insists that the instructed teacher brings both forth rather than discarding one.

Aquinas's own \emph{Super Matthaeum} XIII, lect.~4 arranges the closing parables as dimensions of Gospel teaching: abundance in the treasure, beauty or charity in the pearl, and universal reach in the net, whose shore-side sorting qualifies the present gathering. At verse 52 he turns Jesus' question about understanding toward teaching office: the instructed scribe draws new and old from sacred doctrine's treasury and, as Christ's notary, writes the commands first in his own heart and then in others. Aquinas's \emph{Catena aurea} places Chrysostom's fearful net after treasure and pearl so that hearers do not rely on preaching or bare faith without a corresponding life. These are medieval syntheses, not proof of Matthew's compositional design, and Aquinas is not made the author of the Catena's patristic extracts. Christian Old/New Testament readings require correction whenever they become contempt for Israel; Matthew's own image calls both stores treasure.

The short form ends before all of this. A celebration choosing Matthew 13:44--46 does not proclaim net, angels, furnace, understanding, or the scribe. This guide may document those as an authorized alternative, but preaching claims must follow the branch actually proclaimed.

\subsection*{Called toward the Son amid groaning\enspace\elementref{Second Reading}}

Romans 8:28 is often detached from its argumentative world. Immediately before, creation and believers groan and the Spirit aids weak prayer; immediately after, Paul names affliction, persecution, hunger, peril, and the love from which none can separate the baptized. The ``all things'' claim is therefore consolation within suffering, not denial of it.

Chrysostom's \emph{Homily 15 on Romans} interprets God's action as turning even adversity toward the good of those who love him. He does not call the adversity good in itself. The chain of foreknowledge, predestination, call, justification, and glorification is centered on conformity to the Son, who is firstborn among many brothers and sisters. It supports confidence in divine initiative, not fatalism about individual events or a prosperity guarantee.

Aquinas first distinguishes the good of the whole from the good of the person who suffers, then reads Romans 8:28 as the Spirit's particular ordering for those who love God: affliction can exercise humility, patience, wisdom, and benevolence without becoming good in itself. At verses 29--30 he treats conformity to the Son as the end or effect of predestination, not a foreseen merit that causes it, and unfolds the chain from eternal preparation into calling and justification (\emph{Super Romanos} VIII, lect.~6).

Haydock's later compilation makes complementary moves. At verse 28, ``purpose'' is God's will rather than a human plan. At verse 29, conformity includes suffering with Christ, following his teaching, and imitating his life; the notes join special grace to free cooperation and warn against prying into secret election. At verse 30 they name faith, hope, love, and penance in justification and do not infer final perseverance from every justified state. Divine initiative therefore does not erase agency or authorize assigning a living person a certain final status.

The reading is semi-continuous. Its ``purpose'' should not be casually equated with Solomon's administrative vocation or the merchant's search. Liturgical juxtaposition can ask what sort of good governs discernment; it cannot claim Matthew's treasure decodes Paul's predestination.

\begin{studybox}{A pastoral misuse refused}
The reading does not tell a harmed person that abuse, catastrophe, or injustice was secretly good, nor that protest or protection resists Providence. Christian hope says God can work within what creatures do wickedly and conform sufferers to Christ without blessing evil. Immediate safety, lament, justice, medicine, and communal aid remain compatible with trust.
\end{studybox}

\sectionguard
\subsection*{Revelation received by the humble\enspace\elementref{Acclamation}}

The acclamation adapts rather than reproduces Jesus' thanksgiving in Matthew 11:25. Chrysostom's \emph{Homily 38} says the contrast does not condemn genuine learning; it exposes self-sufficient resistance and praises those who receive revelation without pretension. Aquinas likewise distinguishes the proud posture from teachable littleness. That humility fits Solomon's request but is not identical with royal discernment, and Matthew 11 is not relocated into the Matthew 13 scene.

\subsection*{Two Communion paths and one final petition\enspace\elementref{Communion A; Communion B; Prayer after Communion}}

Communion A takes one verse from Psalm 102 under the Latin numbering: the soul commands itself to bless and not forget divine benefits. Augustine's exposition of Psalm 103 (English page numbering) makes the command self-address and unfolds memory through mercy, healing, rescue, compassion, and creaturely frailty. Bellarmine likewise treats the opening summons as deliberate gratitude. At Communion that canonical memory resists entitlement and amnesia, but neither commentary proves an original Eucharistic referent or selection of this alternative.

Communion B joins two adjacent beatitudes without making them one merit formula. Augustine distinguishes merciful action and its promised mercy from the purified heart and its promised vision; Chrysostom emphasizes generosity and an interior freedom from corrupt desire, while Aquinas later keeps the promised ends distinct. None comments on this later antiphon. At the Eucharist the pair places reception under a demanding horizon: the communicant asks to become merciful and undivided, not merely to possess a sacred object.

The alternatives cannot be blended into a third appointed text. Either can illuminate the final prayer's confession of a perpetual memorial of the Son's Passion and its petition that this gift of ineffable love advance salvation. The requested fruit is divine gift calling for lived response; it is neither mechanical transformation nor achievement by moral effort alone.

\section*{The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities}

\noindent\emph{These exploratory proposals arose in an AI-assisted editorial process. They are attributed to none of the cited authorities and claim no historical compositional intent. Each states its anchors and limits.}

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{A listening treasury.\enspace\elementref{Coll.; First Reading; Responsorial Psalm; long Gospel}}
\emph{Classification: near analogue located.} Solomon's listening heart, the Psalm's word and understanding, and the long-form scribe's new and old treasures can be heard with the Collect's petition for guidance. Their conjunction encourages teachability and responsible handing-on rather than information hoarding. Checked witnesses join parts of this pattern, but none supplies the complete chain; the scribe is long-form only, and no compositional intent is claimed.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Value conversion is not purchase of grace.\enspace\elementref{Coll.; Responsorial Psalm; Gosp.; Prayer over Offerings}}
\emph{Classification: near analogue located.} The Psalm values instruction above gold, the parables picture decisive disposal, the Collect distinguishes passing from lasting goods, and the offering prayer returns gifts first received. Together they invite examination of money, time, attention, and allegiance as gifts ordered toward the Kingdom. Checked sources treat the separate valuations but not the whole chain; grace is not merchandise, possession is not intrinsically evil, and duties to others remain.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{The authorized branches teach different endings.\enspace\elementref{Gospel branches; First Reading}}
\emph{Classification: not located in the checked corpus.} The short form closes with value, joy, and disposal; the long form adds net, judgment, understanding, and the scribe, which can be heard beside Solomon's discernment. Attending to the enacted ending helps preacher and reader respect what was actually proclaimed. The short form is authorized and complete, not deficient; long-only material may never be smuggled into its literal account.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Providence transforms persons without making harm morally good.\enspace\elementref{Second Reading; Prayer after Communion}}
\emph{Classification: near analogue located.} Romans' purpose and conformity to the Son can be heard with the perpetual memorial of the Passion and the prayer that divine love advance salvation. Their conjunction supports resilient hope, truthful lament, and action shaped toward Christ rather than denial of injury. Checked sources treat Providence and sacramental memorial separately; the proposal never sanctifies abuse or displaces safeguarding, treatment, reporting, restitution, or justice.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{House, siblings, and net form an ecclesial test.\enspace\elementref{Entrance; Second Reading; long Gospel; Communion B}}
\emph{Classification: not located in the checked corpus.} A united dwelling, the firstborn among many siblings, a net of every kind, and mercy with purity form a proposed sequence of gathering, formation, and judgment. Its fruit is hospitality joined to integrity and humble accountability. The net and Communion B are unresolved branch-dependent anchors; final separation belongs to God, and no person or social group may be branded disposable.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Memory loosens possession.\enspace\elementref{Communion A; Prayer over Offerings; Gosp.; Prayer after Communion}}
\emph{Classification: not located in the checked corpus.} The summons not to forget benefits, the return of received bounty, the Gospel's selling, and the Passion memorial suggest a movement from gratitude through stewardship toward self-gift. Its fruit is grateful use rather than anxious possession or forgetful consumption. Communion A remains unresolved, and the proposal condemns neither property, contracts, saving, nor provision for dependents.
