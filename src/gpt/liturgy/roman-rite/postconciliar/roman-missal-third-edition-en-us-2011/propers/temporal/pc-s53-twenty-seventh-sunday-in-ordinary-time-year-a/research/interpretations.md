# Proposed readings of PC-S53-A

Research proposal, 2026-09-28. Two readings are supported. Each is the editor's
construction across this postconciliar formulary, not an interpretation of the
whole modern Mass attributed to any ancient author. Precise editions, physical
locators, states and rights are in `source-bindings.toml`; `scope.md` states the
search and attribution limits. Common Missal meanings and witnesses belong to
the canonical Week 27 owner. Both Communion alternatives remain in each reading.
The shared prayers recur across cycles; the apostolic reading is semi-continuous.
The Isaiah/Gospel relation is stronger evidence of the Lectionary's selection
principle than any proposed single theme embracing all the prayers.

## 1. The fruit of a gift

**Controlling claim.** God gives before he asks for fruit. His prior generosity
neither excuses sterility nor makes our obedience a purchase price: it makes
fruit possible and makes refusal serious. The movement is from dependence,
through exposure of failed stewardship, to thanksgiving and a charity that
acts. This reading particularly answers the practical mistake of hearing
“produce fruit” as a command to earn divine election.

**Compatible, substantive author contributions.** Augustine, *Tractates on
John* 86.2–3, reads John 15:16 against the supposition that good works caused
Christ to choose the disciples: he chooses them so that they become good and
bear fruit. Tractate 87.1 identifies that fruit as mutual love; other fruits
flow from charity. His account also limits petition “in my name” by salvation,
so divine generosity is not a guarantee of whatever the petitioner desires.
This is direct commentary on the acclamation's source context, not on the
Missal Collect or a contemporary Sunday.

Chrysostom, *Homilies on Matthew* 68.1–2, supplies the other indispensable
argument. The vineyard's preparation, protection and equipment precede the
tenants' work; repeated messengers and finally the Son disclose the owner's
longsuffering. The expected fruit is obedience shown in works. God's forbearance
makes the tenants' violence less excusable, not unreal. Chrysostom explicitly
distinguishes Isaiah's accusation of the vineyard from Matthew's accusation of
the husbandmen. Thus this reading may connect the two appointed passages without
pretending that the actors or their culpability are identical.

Chrysostom's *Homilies on Philippians* 14 makes the Epistle a substantive part of
the reading: thanksgiving belongs even to petitions in affliction, God's peace
rests on a reconciliation surpassing human calculation, and the list of worthy
thoughts must become practiced deeds. He does not teach immunity from grief.
His later exhortation that we make a beginning is an exhortation to moral action
within the homily's preceding account of divine beneficence; it should not be
turned into Augustine's precise account of election, or used to conceal a
possible difference in their theological emphases. Compatibility here is the
limited claim that gifts call forth active gratitude, not identity of every
claim about grace and human initiative.

Aquinas, *Summa theologiae* III.79.1, adds a medieval theological explanation
where the reading reaches the sacrament: Eucharistic grace comes from Christ
and his Passion, nourishes spiritual life, and arouses charity to act (reply 2).
It is illumination of the orations and Communion, not direct exegesis of their
modern wording. His article makes the connection more than an analogy between
ordinary bread and good behavior.

**Four senses belonging to this reading.**

- **Literal:** The texts retain distinct situations: threatened Israel prays;
  Isaiah indicts injustice; a devastated vineyard asks restoration; Paul urges
  a particular community to thankful prayer and practice; Jesus confronts
  hostile stewards; the common prayers petition at their ritual moments. The
  common sequence is received gift, accountable response and sought renewal.
- **Allegorical:** The Son whom the tenants reject is the cornerstone, and
  fruitful disciples remain in Christ. Augustine's charity and Chrysostom's
  one body disclose a Christological unity of gift and fruit. This Christian
  reading neither abolishes Isaiah's Israel/Judah referent nor attributes
  modern sacramental meanings to the historical intention of every biblical
  speaker.
- **Moral:** Receive gifts without possessiveness; answer them by justice,
  truthful attention, thanksgiving and concrete mutual service. Repentance is
  needed precisely where apparent religious belonging masks withheld fruit.
  Works answer grace; they do not purchase the initial choice.
- **Anagogical:** Lasting fruit and God's own peace point beyond a merely
  successful season. Augustine's Psalm 79 §11 makes God himself the reward;
  Aquinas III.79.1 reply 3 carries present bodily service toward shared future
  incorruption. Judgment remains real, so perseverance cannot be presumed.

**Limits and comparison.** This reading foregrounds the order of gift and
response. It must not make every appointed text a technical treatise on
predestination, reduce the psalm to an illustration of election, or turn the
Lamentations option into a cheerful maxim. Compared with reading 2, its
central question is how fruit becomes possible and why gratitude must become
practice. Its sacramental ending is the enabling source of that practice.
Reading 2 instead asks what becomes of a damaged people and its common life.

## 2. The wounded vineyard made one

**Controlling claim.** Judgment is not the end of the vineyard's story. The
prayer for restoration is answered, in the checked Christian reception, through
Christ who gathers and renews a people. Communion supplies a concrete test of
that restoration: the many are to live as one body. This reading particularly
answers the temptation either to despair over a ruined community or to treat
its restoration as the replacement of one rejected ethnicity by another.

**Compatible, substantive author contributions.** Augustine, *Enarratio on
Psalm* 79 §§6–11 (NPNF heading 80), follows the vine from Egypt, through loss
of protection, into a Christological reading of its renewal. The murdered
heir nevertheless possesses the inheritance (§7). In §9 he refuses a wholly
other vineyard: the vine is perfected, Abraham's seed is understood through
Christ, and Gentile grafting participates in the promise. His reading makes
Christ the center of restoration. Section 10 turns good fear and love against
the evil fears and desires that cause sin; section 11 refuses to make earthly
wealth the reward.

Bellarmine's explanation of Psalm 79, particularly vv. 14–18, independently
contributes the distinction between total destruction and reformation. He names
the Jewish apostles and converts as well as Gentiles grafted into the people,
and explains Christ as the perfection of the vineyard. At the end he separates
grace in this life from glory in the next. This supports continuity, repentance
and eschatological hope, not a promise of institutional security. His O'Sullivan
English witness is an abridged historical translation, and its claims such as
“all agree” remain his assertions, not the editor's consensus statements.

These authors do **not** agree on every historical image. Augustine reads the
river as Jordan and interprets the destroyer with reference to Roman power;
Bellarmine reads Euphrates and offers Babylonian/Assyrian identifications.
The study must display the difference where it discusses geography or the
beast. Neither identification becomes the psalm's settled composition date.
Their compatible contribution is the Christian understanding of restoration,
not an agreed reconstruction of the national disaster.

Chrysostom, *Homilies on First Corinthians* 24.4, prevents the reading from
ending as a decorative metaphor of unity. Many grains form one bread; those
who receive the body of Christ belong to one body, yet the preacher rebukes
actual divisions and lovelessness. Aquinas III.79.1 explains that sacramental
unity is caused by Christ and accompanied by charity, rather than mere
association. These sources deepen the second Communion option without making
it mandatory. Leo 63.7 can illuminate the final prayer by describing receivers
carrying the crucified and risen Christ in body and spirit; his text is
supplemental and is not needed to meet the two-author requirement.

**Four senses belonging to this reading.**

- **Literal:** Israel's carefully planted vineyard is accused and its broken
  condition lamented; Jesus' parable challenges the actual stewards he
  addresses; Paul's communities need peace, sound practice and common
  participation. Distress and culpability have different voices and must not
  be collapsed into one explanation of every sufferer's pain.
- **Allegorical:** Augustine and Bellarmine read the vineyard's perfection in
  Christ; the rejected Son becomes cornerstone, and the one bread gathers a
  body. Continuity through Christ includes Jewish apostles and Gentile
  incorporation. No reading licenses hatred or a doctrine of collective
  ethnic guilt.
- **Moral:** A restored community must repair injustice, resist faction,
  reconcile in truth and serve those with whom it shares the sacrament. It
  may lament honestly while asking for the conversion its own life requires.
  Unity never means concealing wrongdoing or silencing victims.
- **Anagogical:** Visitation and renewal seek a life no longer divided or
  devastated. Bellarmine's distinction between grace and glory, Augustine's
  God-as-reward, and Aquinas's bodily incorruption keep communion open toward
  resurrection and final fellowship. No earthly community is identified
  without remainder with that consummation.

**Limits and comparison.** Restoration is the governing emphasis, but judgment
cannot become a harmless prelude or Israel merely an obsolete symbol. Keep
the differing historical readings visible. Unlike reading 1, which organizes
the evidence around the gift's priority and the demand for fruit, this reading
organizes it around a people's damaged and renewed common life. The same
Epistle accordingly moves from thanksgiving and action in reading 1 to
reconciliation and practiced peace here. The Lamentations Communion sustains
waiting in both, while the Corinthians alternative makes their distinction
concrete: charity as fruit in the first, sacramental incorporation and its
communal obligations in the second. They are complementary proposals, not
fabricated opponents or interchangeable outlines.
