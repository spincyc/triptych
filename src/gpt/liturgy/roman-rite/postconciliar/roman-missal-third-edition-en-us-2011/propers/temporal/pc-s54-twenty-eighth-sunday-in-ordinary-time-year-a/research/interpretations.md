# Two defensible readings of PC-S54-A

These are proposed editorial readings of the whole postconciliar formulary,
not homilies by a Father on the modern arrangement. Exact witness identities,
inspection limits and disagreements are in [scope](scope.md). Two
interpretations suffice because they answer different consequential questions:
**what makes an invited person fit for communion?** and **what can God's
promise of abundance mean while a faithful person still goes hungry?**
The first orders the evidence around grace, charity and judgment within the
Church. The second orders it around present need, reciprocal giving and
future fulfillment. Both are compatible, but neither is a new title for the
other's argument.

All twelve appointed element keys contribute below. Gospel and Communion
pairs are genuine alternatives. The three prayer rows describe the subject
of the liturgical action; they are neither translated prayers nor quotations.
Four-sense language applied to those composed prayers is an editorial
extension by analogy, while scriptural interpretation remains grounded in
literal context. No prayer is assigned an invented biblical origin or date.

## Reading I: The gift of entry must become the life of charity

### Controlling claim and actual inference

The banquet's openness and the garment judgment are intelligible together
if entry is God's gift and charity is the life into which that gift draws
its recipients. Mere presence among the guests cannot be the finished good.
The Church asks for grace before and after action, receives pardon and
nourishment, and is thereby called to love even those it would rather
exclude. This argument does not mean that charity purchases the invitation.
It means that an invitation to communion can be refused from within the
room by refusing communion's life.

Gregory supplies the decisive inference in *Homiliae in Evangelia* 38.1,
7–13. Someone can be expelled from Matthew's feast, so Gregory understands
it as the present Church, where good and bad are gathered, rather than the
completed heavenly assembly. He permits a harmonizing account of Matthew
and Luke in §1; the present-Church argument is his preferred exposition,
not an assertion that another faithful reading is impossible.
In §9 neither baptism nor faith by itself can
be the garment: the guest already has entry, yet lacks it. Gregory identifies
charity as the missing reality. In §§10–11 the garment's twofold weave is
love of God and neighbor, extending beyond friendly reciprocity to enemies.
In §13 the bound hands and feet disclose earlier neglect of sick and needy
people. This is an argument about what the image signifies, not a historical
claim that a king distributed garments at his gate. Section 12 preserves hope
for imperfect charity; §14 denies self-assured presumption about perseverance.

Augustine makes an independent and compatible contribution rather than
merely repeating Gregory's word “charity.” In *Enarrationes* 129.1–8, the
lack of a claim to innocence establishes why entry must be mercy. In
*In epistolam Ioannis* 4.5–7, children of God already possess a real gift but
await its unveiled fullness; desire enlarges capacity, and purification is
something the believer does with God's assistance. Together these arguments
explain how a gracious beginning can entail a real moral task without that
task becoming self-redemption. In *Enarrationes* 33.9 and 13–14, love has two
feet and true riches include virtues and Christ, so charity is communion's
positive substance, not merely the absence of disqualifying behavior.

Chrysostom, Matthew LXIX.1–2, corroborates free calling and the need for
changed life. His interpretation of the destroyed city is historical where
Gregory's handling is spiritual; the study must present that difference.
Thomas's Isaiah 25 exposition independently connects the feast with
Matthew 22 and distinguishes the Church's present banquet from heavenly
completion. Those witnesses enrich the reading; Gregory and Augustine
already provide its two substantive eligible authors.

### This interpretation's own four-sense distillation

**Literal:** God's provision precedes human response. The Gospel distinguishes
refusal outside, mixed gathering and, in the long form, failure within.
The other appointments give the actual language of mercy, guidance, help,
illumination and future likeness. Their local meanings remain distinct;
Paul is thanking donors, not secretly explaining a wedding garment.

**Allegorical:** Gregory's Church is gathered by the King's invitation and
united to the Son; Augustine's Christ shepherds and redeems it. Sacramental
entry is real, but charity is the life proper to that union. Connecting those
expositions to the current prayers is the editor's construction.

**Moral:** Gratitude for unearned mercy becomes concrete love, especially
where the self wants to stop—at an enemy, an inconvenient needy person, or a
fellow guest one considers unworthy. Grace is requested precisely for doing
good, so moral seriousness need not become self-reliance.

**Anagogical:** The present mixed Church is on the way to unveiled communion.
The hope of seeing God entails purification and perseverance; final judgment
and future likeness belong together. Neither fear nor a premature claim of
completion should devour the invitation's generosity.

### Limits and comparison

This is the stronger reading for the **long Gospel**. With the short form,
the argument must move its center from the omitted garment to response and
shared life; it cannot preach the omitted verses as though they had just
been proclaimed. With psalm Communion, charity is the durable good sought;
with Johannine Communion, charity forms a capacity for future sight. These
are alternate emphases, not a claim that both chants are heard.

The interpretation does not explain as fully as Reading II why a faithful
person continues to be hungry or how material help remains necessary despite
contentment. Reading II supplies that inquiry. Conversely, a feast/provision
account by itself may underplay the long Gospel's scrutiny of the guest;
Reading I supplies the sharper explanation of judgment within the gathered
Church. An element-by-element list could identify invitation, mercy and
charity separately; this reading shows their logical relation: a gift that
establishes communion calls for the life of communion.

## Reading II: The promised abundance sustains need and opens into glory

### Controlling claim and actual inference

God's banquet is not disproved by the hunger of a faithful person, because
present provision includes strength to endure, the assistance of others and
a good not exhausted by possessions; the abolition of death belongs to the
promise's completion. Present help remains indispensable. This reading
therefore moves neither toward prosperity teaching nor toward a spiritual
excuse for neglecting bodies. It asks what the banquet gives **now**, what
believers must give **to one another**, and what remains **not yet**.

Chrysostom's Philippians homily XV supplies the decisive logical hinge.
Paul's claim to endurance does not make the Philippians' gift superfluous:
he immediately commends their fellowship. Strength comes from Christ, and
contentment is learned both under want and under abundance, which can also
corrupt. The prayer for their need concerns necessities rather than a wish
that benefactors become rich. The act of giving bears fruit for them without
buying a return. Thus dependence on God and practical dependence on other
people are compatible, not competing explanations.

Augustine raises the counterexample which makes this a distinct argument.
In *Enarrationes* 33.13–14, the wicked rich can remain wealthy until death
while one who worships God remains poor. A flat promise of guaranteed wealth
would make Scripture seem false and tempt the believer to imitation of the
wicked. His answer distinguishes money from true riches—faith, justice,
charity, endurance and Christ the living bread. This is not simply the first
reading's garment thesis: it adjudicates what kind of good the psalm promises
and how to read experience when appearances contradict it.

Thomas strengthens the argument in *Super Psalmo* 33 nn. 10–11: he explicitly
brings Philippians' trained contentment into the psalm, distinguishes spiritual
riches from perishable possessions, and acknowledges that God permits lack.
In *Super Philippenses* 4 lectio 2, he makes the obligation of assistance
plain even when the recipient is content, then places fullness in heavenly
glory. Chrysostom's emphasis on necessities and Thomas's heavenly completion
are compatible but distinct; keep both stages instead of making either
writer say only what the other says. Thomas's Isaiah 25 exposition names
the present Church, the soul and heaven as distinguishable banquets, giving
an explicit received basis for sacramental anticipation without calling it
the end of history. Augustine's 1 John IV.5–7 explains why deferred sight
can enlarge desire instead of proving abandonment.

### This interpretation's own four-sense distillation

**Literal:** The appointed writings do not hide lack. Paul has been hungry;
the psalmist walks in danger; Isaiah's vision promises what present death
and tears contradict; John explicitly distinguishes now from not yet.
The banquet image must be read with these distinctions intact.

**Allegorical:** Christ the shepherd and giver feeds a people through word,
sacrament and mutual fellowship. Thomas's threefold banquet makes the
present Church's feast a real participation with an unfinished horizon.
This is received theological interpretation; the assembly's full sequence
is the editor's synthesis from it.

**Moral:** Learn contentment without abandoning material responsibility.
The poor are not told to become an excuse for the affluent person's
inactivity; donors are not offered a spiritual investment scheme. Discern
what wealth can and cannot give, attend to the invitation, and share actual
necessities while waiting together.

**Anagogical:** The decisive abundance is the defeat of death and the sight
of God. Hope is neither a promise that believers never hunger now nor a
permission to defer love until heaven. The final good makes present gifts
meaningful without making them final.

### Limits and comparison

This interpretation remains substantially intact with **either Gospel form**
and **either Communion option**. The long Gospel strengthens its warning
against entitled consumption; the short retains refusal, judgment and the
unexpected gathering. Psalm Communion tests what good means amid want;
Johannine Communion makes the not-yet horizon explicit. No actual branch
choice is implied by that comparative analysis.

Its danger is to use spiritual riches to soften the scandal of material
need. Chrysostom's commendation of the gift and Thomas's insistence on help
must stand next to Augustine's distinction of goods, not in a distant
footnote. Its other danger is to turn every banquet into the exact same
thing: Thomas himself differentiates Church, soul and heaven; Isaiah's
removal of death cannot simply be declared accomplished in ordinary earthly
comfort. Reading I corrects any neglect of charity and judgment. Reading II
adds what Reading I alone leaves underdeveloped: the difference between a
real present gift and complete fulfillment, and the concrete social form of
providence while people wait.

## Questions the eventual comparison must answer

| Consequential question | Reading I's argument | Reading II's argument | What must not be flattened |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Is the wedding present Church or future heaven? | Gregory's expellable guest requires a present mixed gathering. | Present provision truly anticipates a completion in which death ends. | The present is not the completed kingdom; anticipation is not absence. |
| How can the unworthy be invited and then judged? | Invitation is unearned; charity is its fitting received life. | The offered feast is good, but it can be disregarded or consumed without response. | No entry fee of prior virtue; no exemption from conversion. |
| Why judge someone for clothing? | Gregory explains the garment as charity, Chrysostom as changed life. | It checks a purely consumptive idea of abundance. | No unverified garment-distribution custom or condemnation of poverty. |
| Why does a faithful person still lack necessities? | Shared charity is a response required of the gathered Church. | The core inference distinguishes endurance, others' assistance and final fullness. | Never make spiritual goods excuse withheld material help. |
| Does Paul imply unlimited achievement? | His strength enables the life of an aided member, including receiving. | The grammatical context is endurance under changing conditions. | No success slogan detached from hunger and giving. |
| Are all the texts selected for one theme? | The harmony is a responsible editorial reading of independently appointed units. | Same; Paul's fit is fruitful despite his semicontinuous series. | No undocumented compiler's intention. |
| What does future likeness mean? | Charity and purification ready the adopted person for sight. | Hope's present reality can coexist with incomplete possession. | Neither equality with God nor postponed care of neighbors. |
| What changes with the short Gospel? | Its defining garment argument must be identified as long-form commentary; the proclaimed ending supports gathering and response. | The need/gift/horizon argument survives with less explicit inner-guest judgment. | Both branches retain the burned city; the short form is not judgment-free. |
| Whom does the city's punishment implicate? | Historical and spiritual patristic readings differ, and the Church is also scrutinized. | Refusal of divine generosity is serious, not a license for retaliation. | No inherited ethnic guilt; no transfer of the king's violence into a Christian command. |

The homily should choose one sustained inference rather than recite these
tables. For an adult parish assembly without a declared local branch,
Reading II's argument from Paul's “nevertheless” is especially usable:
Christ's strength never makes the neighbor's help unnecessary. That is a
research-grounded proposal for authoring, not a delivered homily, a local
liturgical selection, or a claim that the optional Communion text was sung.
