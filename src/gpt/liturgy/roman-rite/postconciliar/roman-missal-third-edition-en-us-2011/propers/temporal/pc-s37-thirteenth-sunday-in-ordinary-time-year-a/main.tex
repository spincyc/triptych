\section*{In Illo Tempore\ldots}

\noindent\emph{Canonical order governs. Composition and narrated events are distinguished; inherited attributions and modern historical judgments remain visibly separate.}

\begin{scripturestable}
\scripturerow{First Reading}{2~Kgs.~4:8--11, 14--16a}{Judah or Babylonian exile horizon; exact place of final formation unresolved}{Exilic or early postexilic final form; precise date unresolved}
\scripturerow{Narrated event}{2~Kgs.~4:8--37}{Shunem, northern kingdom of Israel}{Ninth century BC; precise date not recoverable}
\scripturenote{Kings belongs to Israel's received prophetic history. Modern study commonly places its Deuteronomistic literary formation across monarchic and exilic transmission. The narrated Elisha episode occurs at Shunem (modern Sulam area), where a prominent woman provides a permanent upper room; the promise enters a longer story of birth, death, and restoration.}
\scripturerow{Entrance}{Ps.~47:2}{Israel's worship; exact writing place unknown}{Korahite title; historical date uncertain}
\scripturenote{The psalm belongs to the sons-of-Korah collection and acclaims God's universal kingship. Its Entrance use summons all peoples; it does not establish that the psalm was originally written for this Week XIII formulary.}
\scripturerow{Responsorial Psalm}{Ps.~89:2--3, 16--17, 18--19}{Israel / Jerusalem royal and covenant horizon}{Title names Ethan the Ezrahite; date uncertain}
\scripturenote{The psalm praises enduring covenant mercy before lamenting apparent contradiction in the fall of the Davidic king. The appointed verses retain praise, worship, and God-given strength while omitting the later lament; the full canonical tension remains interpretively relevant.}
\scripturerow{Comm.~A}{Ps.~103:1}{Israel; exact place unknown}{Traditional Davidic attribution; historical date uncertain}
\scripturenote{The psalm calls the whole self to bless the Lord and remember divine benefits, then unfolds forgiveness, healing, compassion, and creaturely dependence. Communion option A uses the opening summons and remains an unselected branch.}
\scripturerow{Gospel}{Matt.~10:37--42}{Canonical Gospel: traditional Palestine; modern Antioch proposal uncertain}{Traditionally early apostolic period; modern majority c.~AD 80--90}
\scripturerow{Narrated event}{Matt.~4:23; 10:1--42}{Galilee; exact speaking site unrecorded}{Public ministry, c.~AD 28--29}
\scripturenote{Ancient tradition identifies the Apostle Matthew writing for Jewish believers. The modern majority dates the canonical Greek Gospel after AD 70. The appointed close of the missionary discourse passes from costly allegiance and the cross to receiving apostles, prophets, righteous persons, and little ones.}
\scripturerow{Comm.~B}{John 17:20--21}{Canonical Gospel: traditional Ephesus; modern history debated}{Late first century}
\scripturerow{Narrated event}{John 13:1; 17:1--26}{Jerusalem, final supper}{c.~AD 29--30}
\scripturenote{Tradition associates the Gospel with the Apostle John and Ephesus. Christ's prayer extends beyond the immediate disciples to future believers and orders unity toward the world's belief. The Missal offers this as a Communion alternative, not an enacted fact supplied by the date.}
\scripturerow{Second Reading}{Rom.~6:3--4, 8--11}{Corinth, Achaia (present-day Greece), to churches at Rome}{c.~AD 56--58}
\scripturenote{Paul answers the false inference that abundant grace licenses continued sin. Baptism joins believers to Christ's death and burial and orders them to new life; the appointed reading omits vv.~5--7 and then resumes with dying and living with Christ.}
\scripturerow{Acclamation}{1~Pet.~2:9}{From “Babylon” (received as Rome) to Christians in Asia Minor}{Traditional Petrine setting in AD 60s; authorship and date disputed}
\scripturenote{The letter addresses communities in Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia. Its royal-priestly and holy-nation language receives Exodus within Christological ecclesial identity. The appointed clause emphasizes vocation from darkness to light and the proclamation of God's mighty acts.}
\end{scripturestable}

\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}

\begin{massbox}{Governing account: receive Christ, lose the false life, live from his gift}
The Lectionary officially joins the Shunammite's welcome of Elisha to Jesus' promise that receiving his messenger receives him. Romans contributes an independent baptismal death-and-life strand; 1 Peter acclaims a called people brought into light. The Missal frames these with adoption, truth, divinely effected mysteries, remembered benefits, ecclesial unity, and life in lasting charity.
\end{massbox}

\begin{center}
\textbf{Called into light $\longrightarrow$ Christ preferred $\longrightarrow$ messenger received $\longrightarrow$ life renewed}
\end{center}

\subsection*{1. Gift establishes the household\enspace\elementref{Entrance, Collect, Acclamation}}

The universal acclamation of Psalm 47 opens into the Collect's adoption by grace. The assembly does not generate light but is made children of light and asked to adhere to truth. First Peter gives this identity a vocation: those called from darkness proclaim God's mighty acts. Belonging becomes testimony.

\subsection*{2. Hospitality receives more than it controls\enspace\elementref{First Reading, Psalm, Gospel}}

The Shunammite recognizes Elisha, makes space, and receives a promise she did not manipulate. Matthew explicitly correlates receiving a prophet, righteous person, or little one with receiving Christ and the One who sent him. Psalm 89 answers in covenant mercy and blessed worship. Hospitality is receptive participation, never a transaction for guaranteed reward.

\subsection*{3. The cross orders every love\enspace\elementref{Gospel, Romans}}

Jesus' priority does not abolish family love; it judges and orders every creaturely attachment under love of God. Taking the cross and losing life are costly fidelity, not self-harm. Romans independently names the sacramental foundation of Christian death and life: Baptism joins believers to Christ's once-for-all Pasch and summons continuing death to sin.

\subsection*{4. God effects and Communion gives life\enspace\elementref{Prayer over Offerings, Communion, Prayer after Communion}}

The offering prayer attributes the mysteries' effect to God while asking that servants celebrate worthily. Communion may remember divine benefits or voice Christ's prayer for unity. The final prayer names the offered and received victim as life-giving and orders union toward lasting charity. This sacramental movement can illuminate Romans, but its Week XIII texts are shared across all cycles.

\begin{studybox}{Pastoral control}
Christ's priority never licenses neglect of children, spouse, parents, dependents, safeguarding, or lawful duties. The cross is not avoidable injury sought for itself. Hospitality does not suspend prudence or make access to vulnerable persons a religious entitlement. Baptismal identity grounds conversion; it does not make future sin impossible.
\end{studybox}

\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

\subsection*{Kingship, adoption, light, and truth\enspace\elementref{Entrance, Collect, Acclamation, Sunday structure}}

Psalm 47's worldwide summons gives the Entrance a public horizon: the God acclaimed by all peoples is king over all the earth. The Collect narrows that grandeur to filial gift. Adoption is by grace, so “children of light” names received identity rather than spiritual pedigree. The petition to avoid error and adhere to truth also prevents light language from becoming mere inward feeling.

First Peter's acclamation receives Israel's royal-priestly vocabulary within a Christological address to baptized communities. Its purpose is proclamation of God's mighty acts, not self-congratulation. The ordinary Sunday Gloria and Creed place praise and confession within the assembled Church. A sprinkling rite could replace the Penitential Act; this remains a ritual-substitution branch and not a Week XIII proper.

\subsection*{A room, a promise, and a received messenger\enspace\elementref{First Reading, Psalm, Gospel}}

The Shunammite's hospitality is concrete and sustained: food, recognition, household consultation, and a small furnished room. The appointed omissions move from her welcome to Elisha's question about recompense and the servant's observation that she has no son. The promise is gift, not payment produced by a technique. The larger narrative, in which the son later dies and is restored, intensifies the story's dependence on God beyond the selected verses.

Psalm 89 praises mercy forever, confesses a covenant, blesses those who know the festal cry, and identifies God as strength and shield. Yet the full psalm later laments apparent collapse of the Davidic promise. The response is therefore praise with eschatological tension, not evidence that covenant life is free of contradiction or grief.

Matthew ends the missionary discourse by making reception relationally deep: receiving the apostolic messenger receives Christ, and receiving Christ reaches the Father who sent him. Chrysostom guards the dignity of apparently small persons and acts. Prophet, righteous person, disciple, and giver of water all enter a chain in which Christ takes the treatment of his servants personally.

This does not confer unlimited access or make every claimant a trustworthy messenger. Ecclesial authority, discernment, safeguarding, and prudence remain necessary. Nor is reward commercial payment. The cup matters because it is given in the name of discipleship; grace dignifies the person and act beyond their visible scale.

\subsection*{Christ before family and the cross without self-harm\enspace\elementref{Gospel}}

Jesus' comparative saying demands supreme allegiance. Chrysostom interprets it as preferring the Creator when creaturely attachment conflicts with discipleship, not hating parents as such. Elsewhere Matthew condemns evasion of duties to father and mother. The hard saying therefore orders loves: family is loved truthfully under God, never made an absolute rival and never discarded as though grace opposed nature.

Taking one's cross is readiness for faithful suffering in union with Christ. It does not sacralize abuse, disable self-protection, or turn preventable injury into a spiritual achievement. Losing life for Christ can include martyrial fidelity and daily self-gift, but cannot be invoked to command another person's silence or exposure.

\subsection*{Baptized into death and life\enspace\elementref{Second Reading}}

Romans 6 begins from sacramental fact. Baptism joins believers to Christ's death and burial so that their life may correspond to his resurrection. Paul is not describing a private symbol chosen after conversion; he appeals to a common ecclesial initiation that changes the believer's relation to sin and to Christ.

The appointed boundary requires care. Verses 5--7, with further explanation of union and freedom, are omitted; verses 8--11 resume the movement to living with Christ and reckoning oneself dead to sin. Chrysostom distinguishes Christ's bodily death from the baptized person's death to sin, while joining both through sacramental participation. The once-for-all death of Christ grounds rather than removes ongoing fidelity.

Romans remains semi-continuous. A cross--Baptism connection with Matthew is theologically plausible and canonically grounded, but the Lectionary's Ordinary Time principles do not establish that the second reading was selected to comment on this Gospel. The guide therefore reserves claims of one designed sequence.

\subsection*{Mysteries effected by God\enspace\elementref{Offertory, Prayer over Offerings, Preface, Eucharistic Prayer}}

The Missal supplies no ordinary Offertory antiphon. Any chant at the preparation of gifts is separately selected and remains unknown here. The offering prayer assigns sacramental effect to God and asks that the Church's service accord with the mysteries. Divine efficacy and human disposition are related without competition: God acts first and truly; worshippers are genuinely called to worthy service.

No proper Week XIII Preface or Eucharistic Prayer insert is appointed. The choices are open but not independent. Under \emph{Tempus per annum} rubric 5, a Sunday-in-Ordinary-Time Preface is used unless the selected authorized Eucharistic Prayer carries its own Preface. GIRM 365(b) allows Eucharistic Prayer II with its own or another suitable Preface; 365(d) makes Eucharistic Prayer IV's Preface invariable and inseparable. Because the calendar cannot resolve the exact compatible pairing, this guide keeps it open.

\subsection*{Remembered benefits, unity, life, and charity\enspace\elementref{Communion, Prayer after Communion}}

Communion option A begins Psalm 103's self-address: the whole person blesses God and refuses forgetfulness of his benefits. Option B carries Christ's prayer that later believers be one so the world may believe. Both are appointed alternatives; neither may be described as sung without occurrence evidence.

The Prayer after Communion identifies the Eucharistic gift christologically as the offered and received divine victim and asks that it give life. Its horizon is not isolated vitality but union in lasting charity. If option B is used, ecclesial unity forms an unusually explicit arc. If option A is used, grateful memory comes forward. The concluding prayer remains common to both.

\subsection*{Week XIII and the 29 June replacement\enspace\elementref{Weekday spillover}}

Monday, 29 June 2026, is the Solemnity of Saints Peter and Paul in the General Calendar, so the feria does not occur there. Later eligible ferias retain weekday Cycle II. They do not inherit the Sunday readings, Gloria, Creed, or Sunday Preface. If Week XIII or another Ordinary Time formulary is selected, a weekday manifest must document that actual choice.

\section*{The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities}

\begin{studybox}{Editorial status}
The following are original editorial or AI-generated possibilities. They are attributed to none of the cited authorities and claim no historical compositional intent. Each states its anchors and limits.
\end{studybox}

\textbf{The prepared room as disciplined availability.}\enspace\elementref{2 Kings, Gospel} The Shunammite's modest, durable space and Jesus' cup of water may suggest that hospitality often becomes holy through repeatable structures rather than dramatic sentiment. This is analogy, not a promise that generosity produces miracles, and safe boundaries remain integral to genuine welcome.

\textbf{Baptism as the measure of costly allegiance.}\enspace\elementref{Romans, Gospel} The independent Pauline strand may discipline the Gospel's “losing life”: Christian self-loss is participation in Christ's once-for-all Pasch and death to sin, not annihilation of personality or duties. The proposal does not claim Lectionary selection intent and cannot justify self-harm.

\textbf{Unity as hospitable reception.}\enspace\elementref{Gospel, Communion B} Receiving Christ in his messengers and Christ's prayer that believers be one may illuminate unity as making room for graced difference under one Lord. The branch may be unselected, and unity never requires overlooking falsehood, danger, or injustice.
