# Two whole-formulary readings supported by checked exegesis

These are editorial constructions from verified appointments and scoped
reception, not claims that any Father preached this modern Mass. Each has
at least two distinct eligible Fathers making substantive contributions.
Bonaventure and Aquinas deepen the arguments rather than supply decorative
names. Common prayers are shared across years. The readings remain compatible
in their moral commitments while differing in what explains the virgins'
failure. Precise witnesses and bounds are in `scope.md` and the bindings.

## I. Mercy practiced before the meeting

**Controlling claim.** Preparation to meet the risen Christ takes the form
of mercy practiced while another person can still be helped. Chastity,
religious profession, correct expectation and outward belonging are not
substitutes for that mercy. The Bridegroom is also the Lord encountered in
the neighbor; present hospitality trains recognition, while his resurrection
promises a meeting death cannot prevent.

**Compatible arguments.** Chrysostom's Matthew Homily 78.1–2 reasons from
the choice of virgins: even a demanding virtue does not cover refusal of
neighborly care. He calls the oil humanity and almsgiving and the sellers
the poor. He immediately faces the apparent contradiction that the wise
refuse a request: at the final encounter they cannot transfer a history of
mercy to someone who never practiced it. His exhortation addresses the
present life, when giving is possible; the parable's refusal does not become
a rule of selfishness. His Matthew Homily 77.3 makes stewardship and use of
one's resources for the common good the content of readiness.

Gregory's Gospel Homily 23.1–2 makes a different substantive contribution:
the Emmaus disciples do what the word teaches by receiving the stranger,
and recognition follows at the breaking of bread. Hearing and discussing
are not yet the whole response. Gregory supplies a passage-specific account
of how mercy and recognition belong together; he is not being cited as if
he taught Chrysostom's exact interpretation of the virgins' oil. Together the
arguments permit an editorial movement from readiness for Christ's final
arrival to receiving him in the person now at one's door.

Bonaventure's Wisdom 6:13–17 prevents this account from treating charity as
a stock privately manufactured by human effort: Wisdom's light precedes and
makes the seeker capable of seeking. Aquinas's Thessalonians 4 lectio 2
explains why the meeting is hope for mourners, not merely threat. Christ's
resurrection causes and patterns theirs. The dead are not excluded from
that joy, and grief over separation may be real without being hopeless.

**Four senses within this reading.** Literal: Wisdom encourages receptive
seeking, Paul consoles the bereaved, and Jesus tells of preparation that cannot
be improvised when the wedding party arrives. The Mass asks to be heard,
freed for service and sustained after receiving. Allegorical: Christ is the
Bridegroom, risen Lord and shepherd; Gregory's Emmaus Christ discloses himself
to receiving disciples. Moral: give real help now, welcome the inconvenient
person, console mourners, and refuse the fantasy of repairing a withheld life
at a calculable last moment. Anagogical: the final meeting and lasting life
with the Lord are both gift and judgment; present mercy looks toward a welcome
that death cannot cancel but outward religious status cannot guarantee.

**Hard tests and limits.** In Matthew 25:3 the foolish take no oil with
their lamps, while 25:8 says their lamps are going out. Chrysostom reads an
insufficiency of mercy rather than saying they possessed nothing at all.
Present this as his interpretive inference. At 25:5 everybody sleeps: neither
physical stamina nor uninterrupted wakefulness distinguishes the wise. At
25:9 the refusal tests the reading precisely because oil means generosity;
Chrysostom's answer is impossibility after death, not authorization of present
uncharitableness. The poor are not literal merchants who sell grace. At
25:10–12 the door and unknown petitioners prohibit turning the wedding into
a guarantee of salvation independent of conduct. At 1 Thessalonians 4:13
hope changes mourning; Aquinas explicitly permits sorrow. It is neither
unchristian to cry nor faithful to abandon grief to hopelessness. The
assembly must hear a possible act of mercy, not an accusation against all
bereaved people or all who lacked resources.

## II. A lamp sustained by charity within

**Controlling claim.** The readiness God recognizes is not religious
appearance or applause but inward charity that gives truthful life to outward
works. What is kept in the vessel cannot be borrowed from witnesses at the
last hour. Divine Wisdom first approaches; God's Spirit must preserve the
integrity of the person who receives him.

**Compatible arguments.** Augustine's Sermon 93 develops this reading from
the difficulty that all ten are virgins and all have lamps. The foolish can
restrain their senses and even give alms. Their defect is not that nothing
good can be seen but that the heart's charity is missing, as the sermon argues
through 1 Corinthians 13. Human observers can praise the lamp yet cannot
supply the inward oil. Sellers therefore signify flatterers; the late appeal
to them exposes a life supported by borrowed approval. This is why an author
must not label his oil “outward good works” and then treat his answer as
identical to Chrysostom's.

Gregory's Homily 12.1–6 makes the criterion personal and searching. An act
of generosity itself can seek a human reward. The wise bring the witness of
conscience into the judgment, where companions cannot assure another person's
standing. The closed door separates the present opportunity for conversion
from the final disclosure. Gregory therefore contributes a concrete account
of intentions and testimony, not merely a second endorsement of Augustine.
Neither makes conscience a self-acquitting private tribunal: humility and
God's recognition remain decisive.

Bonaventure's account of Wisdom's light and the opening of intellect and will
supplies the reason this interiority is not self-absorption. Wisdom enables
the movement toward her and seeks the person before the person masters a
technique. Augustine's Psalm 62 commentary joins an inwardly wakeful soul
with a body destined for resurrection; it expressly allows ordinary bodily
sleep. Aquinas's Matthew 25 lecture records the rival symbol readings and
confirms that they answer different exegetical questions. His juxtaposition
of inward defect here and negligence of action in the talents prevents an
interior reading from making outward conduct irrelevant.

| `acclamation` | The unpredictable hour removes the strategy of performing readiness only when watched. Augustine's unexpected midnight interpretation suits constant fidelity; no scheduled nocturnal advent is asserted. |
| `gospel` | Lamps can appear correct while lacking charity. Refusal acknowledges that one cannot supply another's inward testimony; sellers as flatterers disclose the failure of human approval to secure divine recognition. |
| `offerings` | The prayer's relation between enacted mystery and personal response refuses a split between outward ritual and inward allegiance. The Passion is not a religious display; the editor makes the connection without attributing modern prayer exegesis to Gregory. |

**Four senses within this reading.** Literal: identical visible equipment
and shared sleep give way to a distinction disclosed at the arrival; prayers
ask for hearing, freedom and enduring integrity. Wisdom's search and Paul's
consolation keep that warning within God's initiative. Allegorical: the
Bridegroom judges and gathers his Church; Christ's own suffering prayer and
shepherding provide the life it cannot give itself. Moral: examine the purpose
of an outward good act; keep doing real good while refusing to live from
applause, and receive the Spirit's work in mind, body and conscience.
Anagogical: final recognition belongs to God, not an audience. Lasting union
with Christ fulfils the truthful life within; judgment exposes the emptiness
of a borrowed reputation.

**Hard tests and limits.** Matthew 25:1–4 grants both sets apparent virginity
and lamps: the interpretation must explain actual good works without charity,
not erase them. At 25:5 Augustine explicitly rejects cooled charity as the
sleep because the wise also sleep. At 25:9 the fear of insufficient oil is
humility before judgment and the nontransferability of conscience; it is not
salvation by hoarded merit. At 25:10 the closed door prevents turning the whole
scene into another chance to obtain praise. At 25:12 “I know you not” concerns
recognition/approval, not a lapse in divine knowledge. Gregory's enacted
hospitality and Augustine's raised hands of mercy prevent conscience from
being reduced to inward feelings. The Spirit's preservation in the final
prayer forbids a finish that congratulates the prepared for saving themselves.

## Direct comparison for the three eventual documents

| Question forced by the appointment | Mercy reading | Charity-within reading | What changes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What can an apparently virtuous person still lack? | Chrysostom: humanity toward the neighbor despite virginity. | Augustine/Gregory: inward charity even amid observable good deeds. | The first exposes neglected people; the second tests the purpose of the service already visible. |
| Who are the sellers? | The poor, to whom one ought already to have given. | Flatterers, whose praise cannot establish a good conscience. | A real symbolic disagreement; never combine them into one unanimous identification. |
| Why refuse oil if preparation is charity? | Works cannot be transferred after death. | Another's inner testimony cannot be furnished; the wise speak humbly. | Neither permits refusing needed help now. Their explanations locate the personal responsibility differently. |
| Why do the wise sleep? | Delay and mortal death do not annul a life of mercy. | Bodily death is shared; inward charity can endure it. | Complementary agreement, not a disputed point invented to inflate diversity. |
| What does midnight mean? | Chrysostom allows a literal nocturnal inference while stressing suddenness. | Augustine stresses unexpected arrival, not a clock prediction. | Preserve the difference and make no contemporary timetable. |
| How does Communion contribute? | Gregory's welcomed stranger and recognition lead back toward hospitality. | Gift and recognition nourish the inward life that the Spirit must preserve. | Both options retain their own scriptural setting; the Luke option cannot disappear into generic nourishment. |

Both interpretations must remain substantial in the expansive study and
interleaved in the concise study at the actual comparison points. A homily
can develop one argument with its corrective, rather than recite this table.
No choice of sermon argument or actual liturgical option is made by this
research record. No global four-senses table substitutes for the two separate
distillations above.
