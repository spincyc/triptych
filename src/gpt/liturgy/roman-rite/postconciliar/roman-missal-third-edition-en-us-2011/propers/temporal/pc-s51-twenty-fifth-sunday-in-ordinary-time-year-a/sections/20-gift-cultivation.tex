\properlane{gift-cultivation}{God gives himself and cultivates his people}
\subsection{The giver does not become richer}
Augustine begins Sermon 87 with a reversal more fundamental than the reversal of first and last. We cultivate God in worship, he says, but God also cultivates us. These two activities are unequal. Our worship does not improve God or add to his wealth. His cultivation improves us. Augustine pictures the divine word ploughing the heart, uprooting evil seed, planting commandments, and awaiting the fruit of piety. The fruit makes us happier rather than making God richer.\footnote{Augustine, Sermon 87, §§1--2; NPNF New Testament Sermon 37.}

That account changes the worker's relation to labor. If worship were a contribution to God's resources, seniority might look like a bargaining position against a needy employer. But the landowner of this spiritual vineyard supplies the very good for which the work is done. The commandments are not a fee collected from strangers while God remains indifferent to their flourishing. They are planted in hearts so that the persons receiving them may become fruitful. Gratitude is therefore not a pleasant emotion added after obedience. It recognizes the truth about who cultivates whom.

The Entrance's promise of help in affliction establishes this dependence at the beginning. The Collect gives it a shape: the cultivated life loves God and neighbor and moves toward eternal life. These are not separate gifts arranged in a list. Augustine's exposition of Psalm 145 joins pardon, the justifying Spirit, charity that enables good works, and eternal life within the one divine mercy. The psalm's praise is consequently praise for a goodness that reaches into the capacity to act. A congregation asking to love rightly is already asking to be cultivated.

\subsection{Mercy remakes the measure of the reward}
The landowner's reply protects the early workers from an actual wrong: their agreement is honored. Augustine then asks what the denarius signifies. It is eternal life. Its equality lies in its endlessness: one person's eternity cannot last longer than another's. He immediately preserves distinctions of glory and attainment. Equal endless life does not flatten the diverse fruits of faithfulness, chastity, martyrdom, and good works into indistinguishable lives. His claim concerns what the parable's common payment signifies, not the denial of every distinction among the saints.\footnote{Augustine, Sermon 87, §§4--6.}

The answer is exact because the complaint is comparative. If another person receives eternal life, the first worker does not lose some portion of it. The imagined scarcity is false. The common good can be shared without being divided into diminishing shares. What needs healing is the wish that another should receive less so that one's own blessedness can function as a sign of superiority. God's gift is not too small. The recipient's way of measuring it has become too small.

Aquinas's reading of Isaiah gives that moral distortion its prophetic counterpart. Human beings can imagine justice principally through revenge; God summons the sinner into abundant mercy. The height of God's thoughts judges that retaliatory measure. Yet Isaiah's sinner must actually forsake an old way and old thoughts. Mercy does not honor the old life by leaving it unchanged. It makes return possible and gives return a trustworthy destination. Jerome's insistence on the stability of God's promise strengthens the same confidence: the sinner need not calculate whether God's willingness to pardon will prove as erratic as human intentions.\footnote{Aquinas, \textit{Isaiah} 55, Parma XIV, pp. 556--557; Jerome, \textit{Isaiah} XV on 55:6--9.}

Chrysostom's account of the complaint closes the way to a false conclusion about heaven. The saved do not continue envying one another there. The narrative makes the gift's surprising fullness visible; it is not a portrait of perfected charity secretly remaining jealous. Augustine's common denarius and Chrysostom's refusal of heavenly envy agree on the good offered to latecomers, although the latter explains the narrative device differently. The promise is communion in God's goodness, not a permanent system of resentful comparison.\footnote{Chrysostom, \textit{Matthew} 64.3.}

\subsection{The heart receives, and the member gives}
The acclamation's request for an opened heart belongs naturally beside Augustine's plough. Hearing is itself a gift. In his treatment of Lydia, Augustine reasons from the Church's prayer for such an opening to the divine origin of faith's beginning. The hearer cannot regard attention to Christ as a possession acquired entirely before grace. Chrysostom, dwelling on Lydia's attendance and hospitality, emphasizes the active response. These are distinct emphases: Augustine's question is what initiates faith; Chrysostom's is how the hearer receives and acts.

Paul then reveals what a cultivated life looks like from the inside. He desires Christ, yet recognizes the good his continued work can bring others. Chrysostom does more than praise this as admirable behavior. He shows the tension by dwelling on the real cost of delay. Paul remains amid difficult labor because the people he has served can become more fruitful. His choice makes their good part of what he wants for Christ's sake. The early vineyard workers ask why another receives so much; Paul spends himself so that another may receive more fully.\footnote{Chrysostom, \textit{Philippians} 4, opening exposition of 1:22--26.}

Aquinas brings the twofold love into sharp focus. Friendship can prefer the beloved's honor to one's private enjoyment. Paul's willingness to remain is therefore not a compromise in which love of neighbor weakens love of God. It is one way love of God becomes generous. This adds a substantial account of labor to Augustine's account of gift. The common denarius does not make the intervening work irrelevant. It releases work from the calculation of exclusive advantage and makes another's spiritual good a reason to continue.

The Prayer over the Offerings draws confessed faith toward sacramental participation. Augustine's exposition of John supplies the bridge from participation to self-giving: Christ alone has paid the price of the flock, but his members are called to offer their lives for one another because they receive his Body and Blood. The gift establishes a new obligation of charity. It does not leave its recipient merely an observer who admires Christ's generosity. This is why the Prayer after Communion's movement into conduct follows naturally from the mystery received. Continuing divine help sustains the response; gratitude never develops into independence from its giver.\footnote{Augustine, \textit{John} 47.2.}

Each Communion alternative emphasizes a different aspect of that dependence. With Psalm 119, recipients ask for their ways to be directed in obedience. The command becomes a petition for its own fulfillment. With John 10, recipients hear the Shepherd's personal knowledge of his flock within the discourse of his self-offering. The first accent is enabled fidelity; the second is belonging to the one who gave himself. Either alternative leads from dependence on Christ toward sacramental charity.

\subsection{Four senses of the gift}
\begin{fourSenses}
\item[Literal.] Isaiah joins seeking and forsaking to abundant pardon; the psalm joins greatness to truthful invocation; Paul weighs departure against fruitful service; the landowner keeps his promise while giving freely. The Entrance promises help in distress, the Collect names twofold love, and the acclamation seeks receptive hearing. Offerings, either Communion text, and the final prayer carry faith into sacramental participation and conduct.

\item[Allegorical.] Christ is the manifest caller and the Shepherd who enters hearts. His word cultivates the Church, and his Blood purchases and nourishes its members. The law of charity takes flesh in a body whose members receive their life from him. The divine giver is not enriched by them; they are changed by his self-gift.

\item[Moral.] Desire the giver, receive the call, and spend what has been given in another's service. Another's restoration does not subtract from one's own good. Requested obedience and Paul's willing labor replace the effort to turn religion into a claim upon God. Charity becomes fruitful because grace reaches both the beginning and continuation of the response.

\item[Anagogical.] The common denarius is endless life with God, equal in duration without denying different glory. The Collect's eternal end, Paul's desire for Christ, the psalm's enduring praise, and the Shepherd's pasture converge there. The final joy is shared possession of a good that no late arrival can diminish.
\end{fourSenses}
