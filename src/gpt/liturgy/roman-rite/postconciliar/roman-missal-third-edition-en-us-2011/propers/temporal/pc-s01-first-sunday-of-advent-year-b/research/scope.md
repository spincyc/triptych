# First Sunday of Advent, Year B — research scope

Research date 23 November 2026. Appointed-text completeness: `rights-limited`. The [composition audit](../propers/verified.md), [instance](../instance/manifest.md), and [shared Missal audit](../../shared/formularies/pc-s01-first-sunday-of-advent/propers/verified.md) identify the United States celebration on 29 November. Sources were checked for this Advent Sunday in Lectionary cycle B. No local parish selections are known.

## What the liturgical books establish

The image of *Ordo lectionum Missae*, editio typica altera (1981), no. 2, printed p. 5, fixes the first strophe of the response (79:2ac, 3b Vulgate), the refrain at v. 4, and the Isaiah subverses. The current dated U.S. witness gives the proclaimed extent. See the [verse-by-verse comparison](../propers/verified.md). The *Praenotanda* 93, printed p. XL, expressly assigns the first Sunday's Gospel to the Lord's coming at the end of time, describes the prophetic readings of Advent as messianic, and characterizes the apostolic lessons as seasonal exhortations or proclamations. This is evidence for an actual seasonal selection. The psalm responds, and its change from petition for the vine to a promise by the worshippers matters. The acclamation is the people's request before the Gospel, using another psalm. The Missal prayers, Preface and optional blessing recur outside this cycle. The interpretations below argue from their words and places without assigning their authors a plan for Mark or 1 Corinthians.

On the chosen Missal-antiphon and Lectionary route, all eleven appointed units and the twelfth conditional use have been considered. No case is made for particular parish music, the sprinkling rite, a specific Eucharistic Prayer, a proper offertory chant or an actually pronounced solemn blessing. The separate U.S. wreath blessing, when chosen, concludes the general intercessions before the preparation of the gifts. Its alternative prayers and first candle are identified in the [branch record](context.md#branches-and-ordinary-rites). The ordinary route in both interpretations assumes no wreath rite. The introductory rites omit the Gloria; the Creed follows the homily. U.S. GIRM 48, 51, 53–54, 61–63, 68, 74, 87 and 365 were checked in the U.S. Missal. The initial chapter II and VII webpage files were connection checks. A subsequent chapter II delivery supplied the actual text. The U.S. altar-book copy controls the separately checked chapter VII provisions.

## Scripture, context and reception

The printed study witness is the public-domain Challoner Douay–Rheims, Project Gutenberg 1581, in the registered verse artifacts. The U.S. *Lectionary for Mass*, vol. I, no. 2, controls the English proclaimed. Read Isaias 63–64, Psalms 24, 79 and 84 Vulgate in full, 1 Corinthians 1 and Mark 13 in full, with 1 Cor 16:8 for the writing place and Mk 14:29–42 for the garden. The English of an ancient author's exposition can follow a different Psalter, count its superscription differently, or have a textual variant. Identify the verse by its words as well as its number.

| Element and bounded sweep | Native exegesis and consequences |
| --- | --- |
| Entrance, Ps 25 (24):1–3 | In Augustine, *Enarratio* 24.1–3 (NPNF English headed Psalm 25), Christ speaks in the person of the turned Church. She had counted on herself, suffered shame, and now lifts to God the soul her earthly desires had dragged down. Enemies can flatter before they mock. Bellarmine, *Explanatio*, Ps 24, explanations 1–3, connects fear of shame to dependence on God's promise and the experience of those who already waited. The individually voiced prayer ends in trust available to all. Read each against the complete psalm, whose later prayers seek instruction and pardon. Neither explains this Missal adaptation as a unit. |
| Collect | The USCCB commentary on the First Sunday's three prayers identifies grace as the source of the will to go out and of the deeds with which one meets Christ. It applies those deeds to serving people in need. The petition's place at Christ's right evokes final judgment, without changing this Sunday's Gospel to Matthew. No ancient witness found commenting on the actual U.S. text. Cyril of Jerusalem, *Catechesis* XV.1–3, is an analogue concerning the two comings, not its author or a commentator on the Collect. |
| Isaiah 63–64 | 63:7–14 recalls Moses and Israel out of the sea. The petition opens with God's fatherhood after ancestral and temple supports fail (63:15–19). It moves between God's apparent withdrawal and the community's admission that no one rouses himself (64:7 native). The final native verses after the appointment name Zion, the burnt sanctuary and Jerusalem in ruins. Jerome, *In Isaiam* XVII, DCO Latin part IV, printed/PDF pp. 149–151 and 158–159, treats the apparent attribution of sin to God. A mixed assembly can praise and accuse within one speech, as in Paul's mixed Corinth. God's delay in correcting wrongdoers can appear to be its cause. He compares a physician who conceals his knife before treating a frightened patient. The people's plea as children gives them the boldness to pray although their record supplies no grounds for boasting. Aquinas, *Super Isaiam* 63–64, §§85023–24, takes God's allowing error as the absence of restraint and in 64 restores the moral order of the clauses: God's anger follows sin. He identifies the pollution of just works in particular forms, including negligence, bad intention and pride. The clay addresses its maker as Father. At native 64:5, Jerome (p. 155) understands the added assurance of salvation to confess that God's mercy alone saves those long in sin. The question about being found doing right uses the U.S. wish, whereas the native study text describes an encounter already accomplished. The appointed-source audit compares both texts through the concluding clay and potter. Neither author licenses a general assertion that God causes sin or that every obedient deed is corrupt. |
| Responsorial psalm, Ps 80 (79) | The full psalm calls on Israel's shepherd, recalls the transplanting of a vine from Egypt and disputes its broken enclosure. The appointed strophes omit the detailed Exodus and the naming of tribes, but the remembered vine gives referents to the appeal for a visit. Augustine, *Enarratio* 79.1–5, 9–11 (English heading Psalm 80), reads the obscurity of the face in light of Christ's apparent weakness on the cross. At §9 the worshipper asks the planter to complete the very vine he planted. At §11 new life enables the people to invoke God's name. Bellarmine, Ps 79, explanations 1–3, 14–18, treats her renewal and the Son of Man as her chief husbandman. His §15 speaks both of the old vineyard and of a better Church in its place. Preserve that difference where compared; do not assign either writer's Christian interpretation to the original Israelite speakers or identify the destruction with one dated invasion. |
| Epistle, 1 Cor 1:3–9 | Paul thanks God for grace bestowed on a church about to be rebuked for factions (1:10–13). The crucified Christ in 1:18–31 disallows boasting in human teachers. Chrysostom, *Homilies on 1 Corinthians* 1.3–4 and 2.1–9, treats the address and salutation, then the thanksgiving. Homily I §3 construes the relation of the Corinthian church to all who call on Christ in every place (v. 2) in two ways before applying grace and peace (v. 3) to their divisions. Section 4 asks how humility disposes one to receive grace. Verse 2 is preceding context to Sunday's first words at v. 3. In 2.4 he asks how people so gifted can later be called carnal. They may have declined, or Paul may speak to different members; some still use gifts to serve. In 2.5–8 the thanksgiving wins a hearing and gives the credit to God. The future promise of confirmation exposes a present liability to blame, while God's fidelity prevents despair. Aquinas, *Super I ad Corinthios* 1, lect. 1 (§86234), makes a distinct corporate inference: with different gifts distributed to different members, the church can lack none without every member having them all. Charity allows another's gift to benefit one's neighbor. Christ's promise of help follows from the Father's call to fellowship, and being blameless does not mean sinless in every respect. Both witnesses make the address to a divided church substantive. |
| Acclamation and Communion, Ps 85 (84):8 and 13 | First read the full psalm from restored Jacob, through a fresh appeal for mercy, to peace spoken and earth yielding fruit. Augustine, *Enarratio* 84.2–4, 6, 9–13 (English heading Psalm 85), reads the accomplished language as prophetic and “salvation” in §6 as Christ himself, whom the supplicant desires to see. He gives two senses to truth springing from earth at the preceding verse: Christ born of a woman (§10) and honest confession (§11). On the **appointed fruit verse itself**, §12, God makes justice taste sweet to one who had loved injustice; a thief becomes someone who gives. Bellarmine, Ps 84, explanations 7, 10–12, takes the requested salvation as the Savior. In explanation 12, on the very verse used at Communion, the fruit is Christ born of the virgin earth, with the Spirit acting like sun and rain on an unsown flower. Distinguish the two authors precisely at v. 13. Neither claims his exposition concerned Communion on Advent I. |
| Gospel, Mk 13:33–37 | Four named disciples ask privately about the temple in 13:3–4. The full answer distinguishes misdirecting signs, persecution, the gathering of the elect, the fig tree and the unknown day. The travelling master's household remains at work; one servant guards the door, yet Jesus ends with an address to everyone. In 14:32–42 the sleepers are now the disciples beside Jesus in Gethsemane. In *Epistula* 199 to Hesychius, I.2–3, Augustine distinguishes ignorance of the date from culpable neglect: most hearers will die before the last day, but their own last day settles how they will meet that judgment. Bede, *In Marci evangelium*, on 13:33–37 (Latin text lines 403–405), explains an absent master who has not deserted his servants. Christ's flesh is exalted with the Father; his divine presence and the gift of the Spirit make their work possible. The doorkeeper first represents pastoral care. Bede then applies the command to all as custody over the entrance to one's own heart, and explicitly calls acting on one's faith a form of watching. Augustine answers *which generations* can obey; Bede answers *what their work consists in*. Their applications do not disclose the time of the final coming. Bede's biblical text joins prayer to watching at v. 33; the U.S. Gospel does not add that phrase there. |
| Prayer over the Offerings | The prior source of the things returned to God is explicit. The USCCB's First Sunday commentary treats cultivation and offering as real work borne by the divine gift. It also treats the people's self-offering, and distinguishes enacted redemption from a simple after-death reward. No Church Father located commenting on this precise modern form. |
| Preface I of Advent | Its distinct movements concern the Son's lowliness in the flesh and the public manifestation still awaited. In the catechetical analogue Cyril of Jerusalem, *Catechesis* XV.1–3, the one who endured judgment stands as judge; the two comings explain the Church's twofold expectation in the Creed. Cyril addresses catechumens on Daniel 7 and the Creed, centuries before the present preface. No direct literary dependence established. |
| Prayer after Communion | The USCCB explains the education of desire through things the worshippers can receive and use, including bread. Participation calls for reflection and a renewed relation to goods in ordinary life. Augustine's changed delight at Ps 84.12 illuminates this petition across the rite. It is exegesis of the psalm, not of the oration. |
| Conditional solemn blessing | Its middle invocation applies faith, hope and charity to the present race, framed by the first and future Advents. The literal act is a blessing with the people's responses; its use remains open. Cyril XV.1–3 illuminates the temporal contrast; no independent direct exposition of the Advent I.1 blessing was located. |

### When the assembly sings an Advent common psalm

The ordinary response from Psalm 80, the Entrance adaptation from Psalm 25 and the separate acclamation and Communion verses from Psalm 85 have their own directly appointed loci. The additional verses below belong only to the permitted sung branch. Psalm 25 puts the request for instruction on the assembly's lips. Psalm 85 moves from listening for God's answer through the encounter of mercy and truth to the fruit of the land. The two choices replace the Sunday strophes at the same point. No. 173 also supplies a common Advent response without specifying a biblical verse. The [appointed-source audit](../propers/verified.md#sung-psalm-options) records each refrain and all three strophes of each psalm.

| Seasonal option | Passage-level reception |
| --- | --- |
| Ps 25 (24):1, 4–5, 8–10, 14 | In *Enarratio* 24 (§§4–5) Augustine understands the paths as narrow and God's guidance as the condition of the wanderer's return. At §§8–10 pardon puts sinners on the road, the meek consent to be taught, and mercy and truth suggest Christ's two advents. At §14 God himself sustains those who fear him. Bellarmine, Ps 24, explanations 4–5, 8–10 and 14, reads the request as a prayer both to know the commandments and for the help to walk in them. He grounds the penitent’s confidence in God’s willingness to correct and in the covenant revealed to those who fear God. Both expound the biblical psalm. Neither explains the seasonal substitution. |
| Ps 85 (84):8, 9–14 | Augustine, *Enarratio* 84 §§6–13, moves from the petition for salvation to a prophet withdrawing from noise to hear God speak peace (§7). Nearness to those who fear God has a particular history in Israel (§8). At §§9–13 he relates mercy and truth to Gentiles and Jews, the birth of Christ and confession, before explaining the concrete change in a sinner's delight and conduct. Bellarmine, Ps 84, explanations 7–13 (the superscription shifts his verse labels), hears the prophet listen for God’s answer and identifies the near salvation with Christ. He reads the last verse as the light of Christ’s justice guiding human steps. These additional strophes form a continuous response only on this sung route. |

The Entrance and the first seasonal psalm meet at a verse boundary in Augustine. In NPNF I.8, Psalm XXV §3 (lines 6866–6869), the enemies’ mockery and the promise to those who wait both belong to Challoner Psalm 24:3. The Entrance locus ends there. Section 4 (lines 6871–6875) takes up the unjust and then the plea to learn God’s paths, both in Challoner 24:4. The seasonal psalm’s first strophe opens with that plea.

Uncapped Vulgate passage searches for Ps 24:1, 4–5, 8–10 and 14 returned 22 eligible works for each, and Ps 84:8–14 returned 18. The checked Augustine and Bellarmine expositions above supply patristic and later reception across both choices. Uninspected index hits remain leads.

The generic form carries no cycle-specific reading bodies, so this result cannot replace passage discovery. Matched titles and harvest rows supplied leads only.

The search compared direct Latin commentaries and edited historical English translations of Greek and Latin works, including the Psalm expositions, Jerome's and Thomas's Isaiah commentaries, Chrysostom and Thomas on Paul, Bede on Mark and Augustine's explicit quotation of Mark in a letter. Bellarmine supplied later verse-specific reception for all three psalms. John O’Sullivan’s 1866 translation omits some philological discussion and prefaces. His three expositions were checked in this historical English expression, without claiming collation against Bellarmine’s complete Latin. The USCCB provides direct modern liturgical exposition for three orations. Cyril supplies a bounded second search for the Preface and blessing. Index leads to Cyril of Alexandria and Theodoret on Isaiah, Theophylact and Albert on Mark, Cassiodorus and Theodoret on the Psalms, and Nicholas of Lyra and Cornelius a Lapide across several books remain uninspected. No passage-level claim rests on these names. Thomas's Psalter exposition 24 n. 1, found as a registered locator, was not separately acquired in this sweep. No new unqualified historical claim about the institution of the Advent blessing or the composition of the Preface follows from this search.

## Chronology and location

The [typed record](chronology.toml) and its generated table distinguish the traditional 740–701 B.C. ministry of Isaiah in Judah/Jerusalem (Souvay, *Catholic Encyclopedia*, “Isaias”) from the NABRE introduction's postexilic oracles in chapters 56–66. The speaker's ruined Jerusalem in native 64:10–11 is explicit in the larger lament. Neither the section-wide composition period nor the prophet's traditional lifetime dates its recitation.

Challoner Psalm 24 is “for David,” Psalm 79 “for Asaph,” and Psalm 84 “for the sons of Core.” Psalm 24's title assigns no incident. Corbett, “David,” distinguishes the usual chronology of his reign from proposals 30–50 years later. Its Hebron/Jerusalem locations attach to his reign. The Psalter's broad pre-Maccabean limit does not date one psalm. The Asaph superscription gives no rescue date. Its national prayer remembers a vine removed from Egypt in v. 9, outside the appointed strophes, then asks for a fresh visitation. An Exodus date would answer a different question. In Psalm 84, Jacob's reversal at the start can suggest a return from exile; the text names no identifiable return. Drum, “Psalms,” treats the sons of Korah as a family/guild and suggests a range between Isaiah and the return for that collection. This is neither a date of the land's fruit nor an individualized date for the two ritual uses. The graphic separates those ritual citations and does not transfer estimates between the three psalms.

The letter addresses the church at Corinth (1:2), and Paul says he is remaining at Ephesus until Pentecost (16:8). Prat, “St. Paul,” third mission, explicitly places the writing at Ephesus. The generated composition row distinguishes this writing site from the recipients' city. The historical dates in Aherne (“Epistles to the Corinthians”), Prat (“St. Paul”) and the New Testament chronology surveys give alternatives A.D. 56 and 57 for the letter. The revelation Paul anticipates is undated. For Mark, reread the tracked historical encyclopedia “Gospel of Saint Mark,” the AAS 4 (1912) responsa I–IX at 463–465 (VI limits a proposed late date), and the NABRE introduction's historical discussion. Traditional proposals in the generated notes span A.D. 38–70; the modern NABRE proposal of an unknown author, possibly in Syria, perhaps shortly after A.D. 70, has a separate critical row. Rome is a traditional writing-place association. None dates Jesus' speech. Mark explicitly seats him opposite the Temple on Mount Olivet with four disciples (13:3). The speaking of the closing parable belongs to this discourse by narrative continuity. Maas, “Jesus Christ,” Ninth Journey, places the discourse late on Tuesday before the Passion in his harmony and equates A.U.C. 782 with A.D. 29. The event row names the closing charge in Mk 13:33–37 and draws its place from Mark 13:3–4. The generated event date identifies Maas’s derivation; neither the household's fictional return nor Christ's future coming receives a year. No date for the final coming is proposed.
