\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
The first workers receive their promised wage. Their disappointment begins
when they see the last workers receive as much. The vineyard exposes a desire
that can survive faithful service: the desire for another person's good to be
smaller than our own. God's goodness becomes fruitful when those who receive
it learn to desire that other's good instead. Isaiah's summons to return,
Paul's willingness to remain for others, and the prayers for obedient love
draw the hearer from that cramped measure toward a life able to share the
gift. Augustine asks what God's gift makes possible in the receiver;
Chrysostom, how a person called early or late must answer now; Gregory, whose
vineyard the worker has entered.

\begin{center}
\textbf{Help sought $\longrightarrow$ Mercy heard $\longrightarrow$ Labor shared $\longrightarrow$ Gift lived}
\end{center}

\subsection*{Help teaches the heart what to ask}
The Entrance names divine help in distress; it does not make affliction
evidence of abandonment. Its identified psalmic basis answers the temptation
to measure providence by another person's visible advantage. The Collect asks
for love of God and neighbor, placing the work of the coming parable within
charity. Before comparing portions, the assembly asks to become capable of
loving rightly. The giver is sought because his goodness can change the
receiver.

Augustine's picture of God cultivating us makes this dependence fruitful.
Worship does not increase God's wealth; the word ploughs hearts and plants
commandments for our good. Chrysostom's vineyard of commandments directs that
gift toward a real response, while Gregory's vineyard, the Church through the
ages, places each response within a people no generation owns. These emphases
meet in the Collect's twofold love: the person being cultivated must become a
neighbor who cultivates others.

\subsection*{Mercy changes the measure of justice}
Isaiah joins abundant pardon to the command to abandon wicked ways and
thoughts. Aquinas reads God's higher thoughts against the human impulse to
retaliate; Jerome stresses the stability of the divine promise. The
responsorial Psalm answers with mercy toward all God's works and nearness to
truthful prayer. Praise becomes searching when it asks whether the worshipper
desires God himself or only a favorable distribution of benefits. God's
patience invites a changed life rather than an indefinitely postponed return.

The Gospel brings comparison into the open. Augustine distinguishes a kept
agreement from the expectation created by seeing another's reward. The
denarius can signify one endless life without denying different glory.
Chrysostom rejects envy as a description of heaven: saints who labor for
sinners now rejoice in their salvation. Gregory likewise excludes heavenly
murmuring, but interprets the ancient fathers' waiting through Christ's
opening of paradise. The shared hope does not erase these different
explanations of the narrative.

The Isaiah--Gospel pairing and the Psalm's response give this movement a firm
liturgical shape. Paul's apostolic course remains semi-continuous, and the
prayers are shared across cycles. Their charitable resonance adds to the
reading of this Mass without becoming a claim that every text was historically
composed or selected around the vineyard.

\subsection*{An opened heart enters another's service}
The acclamation adapts Lydia's reception of Paul's preaching into a request
for attentive hearing. Augustine asks what begins faith and answers that the
Lord opens the heart. Chrysostom emphasizes the willing attendance that
becomes baptism and hospitality. An opened heart is active without becoming
self-sufficient. Isaiah's seek, abandon and return name a response; the late
workers actually enter the vineyard. Hope for a late beginning therefore
intensifies the summons of the present hour.

Paul gives that response a generous direction. Being with Christ is genuinely
desirable, yet continued life brings fruitful work for others. Chrysostom
dwells on the cost of postponing rest, and Aquinas explains how friendship can
prefer God's honor to private enjoyment. Paul wants others to receive more
fully. The first workers' complaint wants another's portion to be smaller. The
contrast makes love of neighbor concrete while preserving the goodness of
embodied service and the hope of life with Christ.

\subsection*{The received gift becomes a common life}
The Prayer over the Offerings moves professed faith toward sacramental
participation. Augustine gives participation a demanding consequence: Christ
purchased the flock with his Blood, and those receiving his Body and Blood
must give themselves for their brethren. Gregory's labor of bringing others
toward life and Augustine's care for friends become acts of belonging within
that flock. The Church's unity asks more than occupying a place together. It
asks that another member's good become a reason to act.

The two Communion alternatives bring distinct accents. With Psalm 119, the
command becomes a prayer for directed ways; Augustine and Bellarmine make
obedience dependent on divine help. With John 10, the sheep belong to the
Shepherd whose knowledge is joined to self-offering. These are alternative
endings, not consecutive rites. The Prayer after Communion asks that received
redemption shape conduct through continuing help. Sacramental dependence
remains, and its fruit is a life of charity beyond the moment of reception.

The gift may be viewed through Augustine's cultivated receiver, Chrysostom's
converted worker, or Gregory's gathered generations. Each perspective exposes
a different refusal of charity: self-sufficiency, delay or entitlement,
possessiveness about God's people. Together they direct the hearer toward
gratitude that bears fruit. Another person's arrival no longer needs to
threaten the value of one's service. It can become the good for which that
service is offered.
