\section*{In Illo Tempore\ldots}

\noindent\emph{Canonical order governs. Composition and narrated events are distinguished; inherited attributions and modern historical judgments remain visibly separate.}

\begin{scripturestable}
\scripturerow{Entrance}{Ps.~28:8--9}{Israel; exact composition place unknown}{Traditional Davidic attribution; historical date uncertain}
\scripturenote{The inherited title names David. The psalm moves from divine majesty to the Lord as strength of his people and shepherd of his inheritance. The Entrance uses the pastoral conclusion; neither authorship site nor date can be recovered securely.}
\scripturerow{Responsorial Psalm}{Ps.~69:8--10, 14, 17, 33--35}{Israel; Jerusalem imagery, precise writing site unknown}{Traditional Davidic attribution; historical date uncertain}
\scripturenote{The title places the lament in Davidic tradition. Reproach, zeal, petition, and praise of the poor coexist. The appointed verses omit much of the psalm's imprecatory and autobiographical texture; the wider lament remains relevant context but is not silently inserted into the proclamation.}
\scripturerow{Comm.~A}{Ps.~145:15}{Israel's worship; exact place unknown}{Davidic title; historical date uncertain}
\scripturenote{This acrostic praise psalm confesses God's kingship, compassion, justice, and provision for creatures. The Communion branch uses the image of eyes turned to God for food; it remains an unselected option.}

\scripturenote{The book identifies Jeremiah's long ministry across Judah's final decades. Chapter 20 follows Pashhur's beating and confinement of the prophet. The appointed confidence is surrounded by complaint and later curse; preserving that context prevents courage from being reduced to uninterrupted emotional calm.}

\scripturenote{Ancient tradition identifies the Apostle Matthew writing for Jewish believers. The canonical Greek Gospel is commonly dated after AD 70. The narrated sayings stand inside the missionary discourse: persecution is already named before Jesus repeats the command not to fear.}

\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}

\begin{massbox}{Governing account: witness under providence, grace beyond trespass}
Jeremiah and Matthew form the Lectionary's official witness-under-persecution relation; Psalm 69 answers it in lament and praise, and John 15 acclaims the Spirit's testimony. Romans proceeds independently from Adam's trespass to Christ's greater gift. The Missal surrounds both strands with holy fear and love, reconciliation and praise, shepherding nourishment, and promised renewal.
\end{massbox}

\begin{center}
\textbf{Affliction named $\longrightarrow$ fear reordered $\longrightarrow$ witness confessed $\longrightarrow$ life received}
\end{center}

\subsection*{1. Affliction is spoken before courage\enspace\elementref{Jeremiah, Psalm}}

Jeremiah's confidence comes from within hostility, not above it. Psalm 69 likewise preserves reproach, petition, zeal, and praise of the poor. Their liturgical relation refuses two reductions: suffering is neither proof of abandonment nor an emotion to suppress. Trust can ask for deliverance while naming danger truthfully.

\subsection*{2. Fear is reordered by knowledge and love\enspace\elementref{Collect, Gospel}}

The Collect holds fear and love together around God's holy name. Matthew distinguishes fear of persecutors from reverence for the One who judges and preserves the whole person. Chrysostom reads sparrows and numbered hairs as assurance of exhaustive divine knowledge, while denying that every creaturely fall is positively caused by God.

\subsection*{3. Witness belongs to Spirit and disciple\enspace\elementref{Acclamation, Gospel, Romans}}

John's acclamation gives priority to the Spirit's testimony and then names the disciples' witness. Jesus requires public acknowledgment without promising immunity. Romans independently declares a still deeper disproportion: humanity's solidarity in Adam is answered not by an equal counterweight but by Christ's abundant gift. Courage is therefore graced fidelity, not personality or bravado.

\subsection*{4. The Shepherd feeds toward redemption\enspace\elementref{Entrance, Prayer over Offerings, Communion, Prayer after Communion}}

The Entrance and Communion option B supply shepherd language; option A names creaturely dependence for food. The offering prayer joins reconciliation and praise, while the concluding prayer names renewal by Christ's Body and Blood. These shared Week XII texts can frame fearless witness sacramentally, but their use across A/B/C prevents a claim that they were composed for Matthew 10.

\begin{studybox}{Pastoral control}
“Do not be afraid” never commands concealment of abuse, refusal of lawful protection, medical neglect, or reckless self-exposure. Providence includes creaturely causes and prudent action. Christian witness may be costly, but suffering is not sought as an end and perpetrators are not excused as instruments of God.
\end{studybox}

\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

\subsection*{Strength, shepherding, and holy fear\enspace\elementref{Entrance, Collect, Sunday structure}}

The Entrance adapts Psalm 28's closing confession: the majestic Lord is also strength, refuge, savior, and shepherd. The image protects courage from self-reliance. The people facing witness are an inheritance carried by another, not a heroic elite sustained by temperament.

The Collect's paired fear and love rejects both servile terror and sentimental familiarity. Reverence recognizes God's holiness and judgment; love cleaves to his name and trusts his governance. The ordinary Sunday Gloria and Creed locate courage within corporate praise and confession. The sprinkling rite may lawfully replace the Penitential Act, but it is a ritual-substitution branch, not a proper text and not resolved by the date.

\subsection*{Jeremiah's lament and Matthew's disclosure\enspace\elementref{First Reading, Psalm, Gospel}}

Jeremiah hears denunciation from every side, even from those watching for his fall. Yet he submits his cause to the One who tests the just and sees inward truth. The reading ends in praise, but the surrounding chapter proceeds into curse and anguish. Scripture therefore permits the persecuted believer to confess trust without pretending that trauma produces simple emotional serenity.

Psalm 69 answers in the first person. Reproach touches the servant because of zeal for God's house; the petition seeks an answer in divine kindness; the poor are finally summoned to praise. Augustine's ecclesial and Christological reception hears the suffering Christ and his body in the psalm, but it does not erase the lament's Israelite voice or prove the later Lectionary pairing's history.

Matthew's repeated prohibition of fear is reasoned. What is concealed will be disclosed; disciples cannot finally secure truth through silence. Human persecutors possess limited power. God knows creatures down to sparrows and hairs, and Christ will acknowledge those who acknowledge him. These claims establish divine judgment and care, not present immunity.

Chrysostom's distinction is precise: nothing falls outside God's knowledge and providential ordering, yet the text does not require saying that God positively effects every sparrow's fall. Catholic doctrine likewise distinguishes divine first causality, creaturely causes, permission of evil, and God's ability to bring good without making evil good. That distinction is decisive whenever this Gospel is preached to vulnerable persons.

\subsection*{The Spirit's testimony and the disciple's speech\enspace\elementref{Acclamation}}

John 15 places witness amid hatred and expulsion. The Spirit of truth testifies to Christ; disciples also testify because they have been with him. The acclamation therefore protects Matthew 10 from individualism. Public confession is ecclesial and pneumatological before it is a test of private nerve. The verse welcomes Christ's Gospel word; it is not another semi-continuous reading.

\subsection*{The gift is not like the trespass\enspace\elementref{Second Reading}}

Romans 5:12--15 begins a dense comparison whose grammar continues beyond the appointed boundary. Paul traces sin and death through Adam and then interrupts any expectation of equal symmetry: Christ's gracious gift is not like the trespass. Chrysostom emphasizes this excess. One act's devastation becomes the occasion for displaying a gift whose abundance cannot be represented as a mere zero-sum cancellation.

This apostolic strand remains semi-continuous. It should not be forced into an allegory in which Adam equals persecutor or Christ equals Jeremiah. Its liturgical contribution is plural: the same assembly hearing the demand for witness also hears that salvation originates in a gift exceeding inherited ruin.

\subsection*{Reconciliation, praise, and nourishment\enspace\elementref{Offertory, Prayer over Offerings, Preface, Eucharistic Prayer, Communion}}

At the preparation of gifts no ordinary Missal Offertory antiphon is appointed. The actual music remains a local selection. The Prayer over the Offerings identifies the sacrificial movement as reconciliation and praise while asking that those offering be purified. Worship is therefore not a bribe paid by the endangered, nor a reward for courage; it is God's reconciling gift received and returned in praise.

No proper Week XII Preface or Eucharistic Prayer insert determines the thanksgiving. The choices are open but not independent. Under \emph{Tempus per annum} rubric 5, a Sunday-in-Ordinary-Time Preface is used unless the selected authorized Eucharistic Prayer carries its own Preface. GIRM 365(b) allows Eucharistic Prayer II with its own or another suitable Preface; 365(d) makes Eucharistic Prayer IV's Preface invariable and inseparable. An actual instance must resolve the exact compatible pairing; its openness is liturgical fact, not an editorial gap to fill by preference.

Communion option A places every creature before the giver of food. Option B names the Good Shepherd whose self-gift distinguishes him from the hireling who abandons the flock. The Prayer after Communion then asks renewal through the precious Body and Blood toward redemption. If option B is selected, shepherd imagery forms a strong textual arc; if it is not, the guide must not speak as though it was sung.

\subsection*{Week XII is not a seven-day Sunday\enspace\elementref{Weekday spillover}}

The following ferias use weekday Cycle II. An eligible feria may draw on Week XII or another Ordinary Time formulary, but proximity does not appoint one automatically. The weekday retains its own readings, omits Sunday Gloria and Creed, and follows the applicable weekday Preface rule. A separate manifest must record any actual choice.

\section*{The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities}

\begin{studybox}{Editorial status}
The following are original editorial or AI-generated possibilities. They are attributed to none of the cited authorities and claim no historical compositional intent. Each states its anchors and limits.
\end{studybox}

\textbf{Witness as received speech.}\enspace\elementref{Acclamation, Gospel} The Spirit's testimony in the acclamation and Christ's demand for acknowledgment may support formation in which courage begins by receiving truthful speech before speaking it. This does not eliminate preparation, prudence, or the distinct authority of ordained and lay witness.

\textbf{Providence and safeguarding as allies.}\enspace\elementref{Entrance, Gospel} God's knowledge of the sparrow and the Entrance's shepherd image may encourage communities to make vulnerable lives visible through reporting, protection, and material care. The analogy is pastoral, not compositional; it cannot reduce providence to policy or policy to faith.

\textbf{A gift greater than inherited fear.}\enspace\elementref{Romans, Gospel} Romans' unequal Adam--Christ comparison can illuminate how patterns of fear transmitted through families or institutions are real yet not ultimate. The limit is strict: Paul addresses sin and grace, not a modern clinical theory, and spiritual language never substitutes for trauma-informed care.
