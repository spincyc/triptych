## Material disagreements and safeguards

Gregory locates the wedding principally in the present Church and reads
armies/city spiritually; Chrysostom explicitly connects the burning to Roman
destruction. These are different exegetical moves. Do not fuse them into a
single claim that either author made. Gregory's garment is charity;
Chrysostom's is appropriate life and practice. Those are complementary at
this point, not a fabricated dispute. Gregory distinguishes Matthew's feast
from Luke's eternal banquet, while Chrysostom freely uses Luke's details;
the study must maintain Matthew's actual wording and its own long/short cut.
The NPNF Matthew homily’s editorial note 2585 says verses 7–14 in its
opening biblical lemma are supplied from the Oxford/Field tradition rather
than the Migne heading. This is a limitation on that printed lemma, not
evidence that the homily lacks its subsequent garment/judgment exposition.
No Greek manuscript or critical-edition collation was performed here.

No inspected source established the popular claim that the king issued every
guest a garment. Do not resolve the man's silence through that unsourced
custom, or make it about economic inability to buy clothes.

The narrative addresses chief priests and elders in the liturgical incipit;
Matthew 21:45 also identifies chief priests and Pharisees. Ancient authors'
collective anti-Jewish polemic must not become the editor's claim of inherited
ethnic guilt or a divine endorsement of violence. The Church is also judged
in the parable. “Bad and good” describes the scope of gathering, not a
promise that conversion is unnecessary. Judgment cannot be deleted in favor
of hospitality, and welcome cannot be conditioned on already completed virtue.

“No want,” “all things” and “every need” do not erase hunger, danger or the
obligation to give. Augustine raises the counterexample of prosperous wicked
people explicitly. Thomas's medical analogy describes providence, not an
editorial diagnosis that a hungry neighbor deserves hunger. The Pauline
context prevents using contentment to excuse neglect. The anagogical banquet
is more than improved present consumption: death itself ends. Likeness and
participation do not identify creatures with God's essence; Augustine's
“not equality” distinction supplies a checked boundary.
