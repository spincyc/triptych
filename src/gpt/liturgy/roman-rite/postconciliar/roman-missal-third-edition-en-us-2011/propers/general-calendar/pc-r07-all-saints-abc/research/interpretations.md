# All Saints: proposed interpretations

These are two editorial interpretations of the whole postconciliar All Saints
Mass, not interpretations attributed to a Father as though he had expounded
this edition's compilation. The authors' native loci and evidence limits are
in scope.md and the source-registration handoff. The canonical same-edition
Missal owner controls the prayer evidence. Every cross-element argument below
is source-grounded editorial synthesis unless a narrower relation is named.
Both interpretations are proposed for cold research review, not accepted prose.

## 1. Purified desire and the sight of God

Stable key: `purified-desire`. Carrying authors: Augustine and Thomas Aquinas.
Both are eligible under the standing registry. Chrysostom and Bede contribute
compatible subsidiary evidence without being made authors of this construction.

### Question, controlling claim and the inference that distinguishes it

How can the children of God already possess their identity, yet still need
purification before they see him? First John 3:1–3 makes both assertions. The
Psalm asks who can ascend and remain, and the Gospel promises vision to the
clean of heart. This interpretation understands holiness as grace already
received becoming an undivided desire capable of the promised vision. The
future goal changes present desire; it does not make present adoption unreal
or moral effort an independent price paid for salvation.

The decisive inference is from the **object and agency of purification**:
because God himself is promised, rather than merely an external reward, the
heart must be healed for that communion; because adoption and cleansing are
his gifts, this healing takes the form of willing dependence on him. This
differs from interpretation2's question about the identity and conduct of a
people reconciled through suffering. The distinguishing consequence here is
an account of holy desire and growth toward vision, rather than an account
of the Church's public endurance and solidarity.

### The witnesses' reasoning at the consequential words

The difficult words are 1John3:2, “now” and “not yet,” followed by3:3,
“sanctifieth himself” in the DR study witness. Augustine, TractateIV.4–7,
refuses to reduce sonship to its name. It is real in faith, hope and charity.
He distinguishes Christ's humanity, which even the wicked can see at judgment,
from seeing him as the divine Word. The promised likeness therefore concerns
another kind of seeing. Since the object is not yet seen, desire trains the
recipient: his vessel illustration explains capacity, rather than God's
withholding love. InIV.7 he directly confronts the apparent self-purification:
God purifies, but not the unwilling; the will really acts by consenting to
the indwelling helper. IV.9 distinguishes likeness from equality. If his
answer is accepted, the command neither cancels grace nor makes a creature
God's equal.

Aquinas, Super Psalmos23 nn.2–4 (86989–86991), explains the Psalm's double
question: ascent is approach; standing is abiding at the destination. Hands
and heart name exterior work and interior desire. He explicitly cites the
Beatitude's promise of vision. His alternative reading of receiving blessing
and mercy makes them the cause of innocence and access, not simply a later
payment for autonomous innocence. This gives independent reasoning at the
Psalm's own wording, not merely a second author's agreement with Augustine.

In Super Matthaeum5 lectio2,87340, Aquinas asks how Matthew5:8 can promise
the sight of God when Scripture also calls God unseen. He rejects both a
mere created substitute for God's essence and bodily sight of the divine
essence. First John3:2 supports the promised reality; “heart” identifies the
intellectual mode. He distinguishes present beginnings from completed
beatitude, and virtues disposing to beatitude from beatitude itself. His
appeal to both knowledge and love, including1John3:1, keeps contemplation
from becoming impersonal information. This develops the central difficulty
rather than quoting the promise as a slogan.

Augustine, De sermone Domini in monteI.1–4,1–12, supplies the sequence of
humility, meek receptivity, mourning, hunger, mercy, purity and peace. The
eighth Beatitude tests the completed course. His pure heart is single rather
than divided; peace orders the person under God. The interpretation may use
this ordered account, while acknowledging that it is Augustine's reading of
the Gospel, not the established historical design of the Mass.

### Four senses

- **Literal:** Jesus pronounces the Beatitudes to disciples in the presence
  of crowds. The Psalm asks about admission; John distinguishes present
  sonship and future manifestation; the seer depicts those cleansed through
  the Lamb. These are distinct literary acts, not one ancient discourse.
- **Allegorical:** Christ's cleansing and the Spirit's work make the Church
  the community seeking the Father's face. Augustine's Christological Psalm
  and Aquinas's use of1John with Matthew support the connection; the
  whole-Mass formulation is the editor's.
- **Moral:** Dependence on grace takes the form of an active will, innocent
  action, undivided desire and mercy. Purifying hope opposes both passivity
  and self-sufficient merit. A practical application must emerge from one
  of these textual demands, not a generic virtue list.
- **Anagogical:** The promised end is vision and loving communion with God,
  with the gathered saints and the heavenly banquet. Present beginnings
  remain real and incomplete; creaturely likeness is not equality with God.

### Limits and comparison

Do not write an escape from bodily or social life: the Psalm names hands,
the Beatitudes mercy and peace, and the prayers the communion of saints.
Augustine's earth as eternal stable inheritance differs from Chrysostom's
earthly/present account. This interpretation follows Augustine at that point
and can explain Aquinas's future preference. It does not conscript Chrysostom
as a witness for that exclusive identification. Augustine's ordering of
meekness and mourning follows his witness and differs from the U.S.
proclamation's order; state the local textual fact where the sequence matters.

Compared with interpretation2, the same cleansing primarily answers why the
recipient can see God. In interpretation2 it answers how one people can stand
and serve together amid persecution. Both require grace, charity and hope;
they are complementary questions, not mutually exclusive paths to salvation.

## 2. The Lamb forms a people who make peace and endure

Stable key: `reconciled-multitude`. Carrying authors: Bede and John Chrysostom.
Augustine and Aquinas supply compatible clarifications at stated loci. The
registry identifies the carrying authors as eligible; their genuine
contributions, not the registry alone, support the interpretation.

### Question, controlling claim and distinguishing inference

How can the saints be one innumerable people, and why do the promises join
peacemaking to persecution? Revelation names tribes and nations, John's
children are unrecognized by the world, and the Gospel promises the kingdom
to those persecuted for justice. This interpretation reads the Mass as the
celebration of a people made one by Christ and taught to reconcile others
without abandoning justice when reconciliation is resisted.

The decisive inference is from the **kind of unity and victory** the texts
describe: the Lamb gathers and cleanses persons from all peoples; the Son's
work of reconciling the divided explains why peacemakers bear his family
likeness. Endurance is fidelity to that work, not the worship of injury.
Unlike interpretation1, this account asks who the community is and how its
members act toward one another and their opponents. It changes the account
of Christian success: holiness can reconcile and serve while still receiving
opposition, and the saints' glory does not end their solidarity with the living.

### Difficulty followed through the actual witnesses

The first difficult wording is Revelation7:4 and9: a fixed number heard,
then a multitude seen that cannot be numbered. Bede7:4, printed46/PDF58,
reads12×12×1000 as the Church's fullness, including patriarchal descent by
nature or imitation of faith. He reasons from the apostolic twelve and the
Trinity preached throughout the world. The editor may explain the symbolic
argument without turning it into modern demography. At7:9, printed52–53,
Bede reads the resumed glory after recapitulation, yet also permits a sequence
of the first preaching to Israel followed by the Gentiles. Retain that
alternative rather than forcing him to offer only a “same group” solution.

The second difficulty is7:14: white robes in blood and coming through
tribulation. Bede's actual comment says the Lamb's blood cleanses the whole
Church, not martyrs alone, and connects standing together before God with
faithful confession in adversity. Thus the Church's breadth is neither a
denial of the cross nor a restriction of sanctity to those violently killed.
Victorinus7:9 independently explains baptism in the Lamb's blood and
preserving grace. His7:2 angel identification differs from Bede's; that
disagreement is real but peripheral to the compatible baptismal claim.

ChrysostomXV.7 explains Matthew5:9 by the Son's own reconciling of the
divided. He does not stop at avoiding personal quarrels: the peacemaker acts
between other people in conflict. He immediately considers5:10 so that
“peace” does not bless every accommodation. Justice names virtue and help
given to others; the subsequent qualification about false accusations for
Christ prevents suffering from proving its own righteousness. XV.8–9
develops endurance rather than a guarantee of escaping hostile reports.
That argument gives social and moral content to the multitude's unity.

ChrysostomXXXVIII.3 supplies a further step: the Son invites all who are
burdened, and humility makes the yoke restorative. The editor can connect
that universal invitation to the many peoples of Revelation, as synthesis,
without claiming Chrysostom made that cross-book comparison. AugustineIV.4
on1John explains the world's failure to recognize God's children through
Christ's own unrecognized lowliness. This supports endurance while keeping
the identity rooted in adoption rather than popularity.

### Four senses

- **Literal:** The vision depicts a worshipping multitude from the peoples;
  the epistle names God's children in a world that fails to recognize them;
  Jesus blesses peacemaking and suffering for justice. Distinguish the vision's
  imagery from historical geographical events.
- **Allegorical:** Bede's robes and palms signify baptism and Christ's cross;
  Chrysostom's peacemakers imitate the reconciling Son. The editor joins these
  as the identity and conduct of Christ's body.
- **Moral:** Mercy, truthful conduct, reconciling divided neighbors and
  perseverance belong together. A Christian must test the cause of opposition,
  not take opposition as proof of holiness. Peace cannot be bought by
  consenting to injustice, and justice cannot excuse hatred.
- **Anagogical:** The one people praises before God, and the pilgrim assembly
  hopes to share the heavenly banquet. Bede's cleansing beyond martyrs and
  the saints' present intercession express the breadth of that hope without
  guaranteeing the salvation of each person or computing its population.

### Limits and comparison

The Beatitude does not promise effortless public success. Chrysostom's
present earthly inheritance inXV.5 is an argument against the assumption
that violence secures possession; he also retains future gifts. Do not turn
it into a prosperity guarantee. Bede's symbolic exegesis supplies theological
meaning, not an appointment history or a date for the end. The presence of
all nations is not an assertion that differences of faith, repentance or
action are irrelevant: the robes and the Lamb remain essential.

This interpretation accommodates Augustine's inner peace as necessary, but
its developed center is the additional work Chrysostom requires between
neighbors and the faithful people's endurance. Interpretation1 explains
purity in relation to seeing God; this one explains purity alongside public
reconciliation and costly fidelity. The concise study should compare the
actual answers at sonship, purity, peace and persecution, not repeat two
separate summaries of “be holy.”

## Proposed concise and homily use

The concise opening can name the whole inventory, map four overview senses,
then use the generated chronological rows and a two-page argument moving from
the saints' common praise through cleansing/adoption and the Beatitudes to
intercession and banquet. Its later comparison should interleave the two
interpretations at the concrete questions above. Its four overview rows do
not replace each expansive interpretation's own distillation.

An adult-parish homily can develop the difficult link between sonship already
received and the purification still required: hope teaches what to desire,
and mercy and peacemaking show its effect. Alternatively it can develop why
the Lamb's gathered people remain peacemakers when fidelity is opposed. Choose
one intelligible argument; do not deliver this research matrix aloud. All
exact loci and delivery estimates belong in its separate terminal note.
