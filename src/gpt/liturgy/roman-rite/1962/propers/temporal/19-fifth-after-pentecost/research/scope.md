# Fifth Sunday after Pentecost — Research Scope

**Last updated:** 2026-07-18

This record preserves source roles, historical judgments, and claim boundaries compressed in the reader-facing guide. Complete appointed texts, checksums, facsimile locations, and witness differences remain in [the verified propers](../propers/verified.md).

## Scriptural and Liturgical Scope

- Directly appointed biblical wording and immediate contexts studied: Psalms 15, 20, and 26 in full; Matthew 4:12–5:48; and 1 Peter 1–5, with special attention to 3:8–17 and the letter's address and conclusion.
- Materially adapted biblical source studied separately: Psalm 83 in full. The Gradual cites verses 10 then 9, retains their openings, and replaces the source's anointed and Jacob clauses with plural petitions for God's servants. The guide therefore does not count it as a sixth direct excerpt.
- Related texts actually used: 1 Chronicles 6:7–23; Psalm 33:13–17 as quoted in 1 Peter; Acts 2:25–32; and 1 Peter 5:12–13.
- The Collect, Secret, and Postcommunion were analyzed in their complete facsimile-collated Latin forms. The PDF retains only the clauses needed for argument because it accompanies a hand missal.
- Page-one paraphrases were checked against the exact appointed excerpts. In particular, the Gospel now states the remembered grievance without assigning an unprinted injury, the Communion stops at lifelong dwelling rather than importing the verse's unprinted continuation, and the Gradual is labeled as an adaptation.
- The face–heart–peace–offering route and the chants' movement from petition to royal acclamation are editorial syntheses of the appointed formulary, not historical claims about its compilers or any patristic author.

## Historical Coordinates of the Direct Scriptural Propers

The one-page orientation inventories five distinct directly excerpted passages in Catholic canonical order: Psalm 15:7–8 (Offertory), Psalm 20:1 (Alleluia), Psalm 26:1, 4, 7, and 9 (Introit and Communion, including the Introit's appointed psalm verse), Matthew 5:20–24 (Gospel), and 1 Peter 3:8–15 (Epistle). A sixth row situates Psalm 83:9–10 as the Gradual's materially adapted source rather than as verbatim Gradual wording. Psalm numbering follows the Vulgate; the common modern equivalents are Psalms 16, 21, 27, and 84. Composed orations and their loose biblical echoes were excluded. The Gospel alone receives a separate event row so that Jesus' Galilean setting is not confused with the Evangelist's traditional place of composition.

- **Psalms 15, 20, and 26:** the Greek/Latin titles assign all three to David. Psalm 15 and Psalm 20 name no life episode or writing site, so their `c. 1000 BC` entries are broad inherited Davidic orientations rather than autograph dates. Psalm 20/21 is a royal thanksgiving but does not establish whether David composed it for himself, was its royal subject, or inherited a court song. Psalm 26/27 adds “before he was anointed”; because Scripture records anointings in 1 Samuel 16 and 2 Samuel 2 and 5, `c. 1010 BC` and `Judah / Israel` remain deliberately broad. The *NABRE* does not reconstruct demonstrable authorial biographies for these poems.
- **Psalm 83 and the Gradual's adaptation:** the title assigns the song to the sons of Korah. Numbers 26:11 and 1 Chronicles 6 provide the corporate horizon of a preserved Levitical family that became sanctuary singers; no individual writer is named. `Jerusalem sanctuary` and `c. 1000 BC` describe the traditional Davidic cultic association, not a proven autograph place or date. The poem's pilgrimage form may reflect a later Temple horizon. Clause-by-clause comparison on 2026-07-14 established the precise liturgical changes: verse 10's `in faciem christi tui` becomes `super servos tuos`; verse 9's `orationem meam; auribus percipe, Deus Jacob` becomes `preces servorum tuorum`; and the missal orders verse 10 before verse 9. The historical dossier belongs to the Psalm source, not to the adapted wording.
- **Matthew:** the traditional row follows ancient testimony that St. Matthew, apostle and former tax collector, wrote for Jewish believers in Palestine early in the apostolic mission, represented as `c. AD 40–45`. The *NABRE* records the modern majority view of an unknown final Greek author after AD 70, plausibly at Antioch. The narrated-event row cites Matthew 4:12–5:2 and 5:20–24 for an unnamed Galilean mountain in present-day northern Israel during the early public ministry, represented by the harmonized `c. AD 27`; neither the text nor tradition securely identifies the mountain or a civil year.
- **1 Peter:** Christian tradition identifies Peter as author, Rome as “Babylon,” Silvanus as his assistance or bearer, and the five provinces of Asia Minor as the audience. The `c. AD 63–64` entry follows the inherited placement shortly before Peter's Neronian martyrdom. The audience was largely Gentile and socially pressured rather than a single local congregation. The *NABRE* also presents a later Roman Petrine-school judgment, usually AD 70–90. The prose preserves both without collapsing them.

Geographic equivalents are broad: Davidic Israel and Judah belong to the southern Levant; the Korahite row's Jerusalem is a sanctuary association; Palestine and Galilee are distinct composition and event horizons; Rome is in present-day Italy; and the letter's first audience lived in Roman Asia Minor, chiefly present-day Turkey.

## Controlling Distinctions

- Ordered love does not turn the neighbor into a disposable instrument or oppose love of God and neighbor as rival quantities.
- Matthew first directs the offerer toward a grievance another has against the offerer. The guide does not make culpability symmetrical or require unsafe contact.
- Peter's peace includes truth, departure from evil, positive good, and a clean conscience; it is not passive avoidance or moral vagueness.
- The Secret preserves distributive personal agency in *singuli* and asks for common fruit in *cunctis*; the guide does not infer that each gift has identical form or effect.
- The Gradual's `servos tuos` and `preces servorum tuorum` are proper to the missal's adaptation. The source's anointed, Jacob, and singular-prayer clauses may establish Psalm context and reception history but may not be quoted as though sung in this formulary.
- Applying Psalm 26's one house to Eucharistic communion and joining the chants into one face–heart–peace–offering route remain editorial liturgical syntheses.
