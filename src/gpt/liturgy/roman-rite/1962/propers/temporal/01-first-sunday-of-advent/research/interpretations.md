# Two readings of Advent I

## What can Paul mean when he says “nearer”?

Romans 13:11 compares salvation now with salvation at the moment his hearers believed. The Latin lesson begins *Fratres: Scientes, quia hora est*. The Bible’s connective *Et hoc* and its noun *tempus* after *scientes* drop out. Chrysostom’s historical English lemma retains “knowing the time,” and Aquinas explains *scientes hoc tempus*. What they ask about the passing of time also arises from the comparison the Missal retains. Why should the lapse of time make anyone more ready? Chrysostom, Homily 24 on Romans, knows from experience that the passing years can cool the first enthusiasm of a convert. Time makes the finish closer to the runner whether he is training or asleep. That is precisely why Paul wakes him, and promises him a prize. Aquinas, *Super Romanos* 13, lect. 3, distinguishes nearness in time from nearness in disposition. Growing charity prepares someone to receive the end for which faith first set him on the road. Thomas then explicitly asks how the Church can read the same verse in Advent. She can lend her voice to the faithful who lived before the Incarnation and longed for the salvation still to come in the flesh. He distinguishes this liturgical use from his account of the apostle's meaning. That distinction permits two different readings of this Mass without making Christmas the original referent of Paul's sentence.

Both readings must explain two stubborn features of Luke 21. The same signs make people faint in v. 26 and give the disciples cause to raise their heads in v. 28. The fig tree **and all the trees** yield *fructum* in the Missal's v. 30. The kingdom's nearness is compared with summer's nearness, not with a date that a disciple can calculate. Gregory takes summer as the end of the clouds of sorrow; the world's passing tells the faithful they are near the One they have wanted to see (*Homiliae in Evangelia* I.1.3). Ambrose X.44–45 notices that Matthew speaks of the fig tree alone where Luke includes all the trees. Fruit on the trees can mean believing peoples confessing God, with a harvest of resurrection; apparently flourishing leaves can instead depict the swagger of sin. His distinction between fruit and leaves asks what looks like growth but will fail at the judgment. He draws on other biblical uses of the fig, including the unfruitful tree of Luke 13, as well as the Matthean parallel. These are alternative explanations of the sign, not additional botanical details in the Missal.

Bede, *In Lucam*, at 21:32 (PL 92, 590B–C), offers humanity as a whole or specifically the Jewish people as the referent of “this generation.” He explains the solemn Amen as Truth speaking to make sleepy listeners attend. Neither reading dates the end to a single person's lifetime. Gregory I.1.4 quotes the generation saying and dwells on the following verse: a humanly spoken word seems to vanish as soon as one says it; Christ's judgment expressed in speech endures longer than earth or sky. Gregory does not there choose between Bede's two referents. Luke has just distinguished the siege and its consequences in 21:20–24 from the universal prospect in 25–28. His warning against claimants who announce that the time is at hand (v. 8) belongs to the larger discourse. No disputed interpretation of “generation” should be smuggled into the historical date table.

## 1. The Judge whom the faithful long to see

**Carrying witnesses.** Gregory the Great on Luke 21 and John Chrysostom on Romans 13. Augustine's Psalm expositions support the prayer and its moral consequence. Their distinct premises matter. For Chrysostom the nearness of judgment makes delay dangerous even to those who believed long ago. He hears encouragement in Paul's choice of the word *salvation*: the sleepiest Christian is addressed as a competitor who may still win. Gregory asks how the disciples may rejoice when the terrors themselves are real. He takes the head as the mind that directs the members. What the mind loves determines whether losing the present order means losing everything. Those who love the Lord may lift their heads because they are about to meet him. Gregory cites recent devastation among his Roman listeners; no modern disaster earns his inference merely by resembling it.

| Appointment | Contribution to the argument |
| --- | --- |
| Introit | A threatened suppliant lifts his soul. Augustine, on Vulgate Ps 24 §§1–4, hears Christ lending his voice to his people: self-trust brought the speaker to shame, so he can now entrust himself to God. The psalm verse asks to be taught a way he could not find by himself. |
| Collect | The danger is specified as the danger of **our sins**. Before hearing what will happen to the nations, the assembly asks the coming Lord to rescue it from a danger of its own making. Both protection and release are his acts. |
| Epistle | Passing time brings the day nearer to every hearer; it does not guarantee preparedness. Chrysostom's runner quickens his pace near the prize. Paul counts jealousy and quarrelling among the night's works, as well as drunkenness and sexual wrongdoing. His positive command is to put on Christ. |
| Gradual | The first-person appeal now has a warrant concerning *all* who wait. The verse asks to learn the Lord's paths. Waiting can involve obeying the command just heard. |
| Alleluia | The voices about to hear of judgment ask to see mercy and receive salvation. Augustine, on Vulgate Ps 84 §6, understands God's salvation as Christ himself: one who receives him has nothing to boast of. |
| Gospel | Gregory distinguishes a crowd losing what it loves from disciples about to meet the One they love. Summer's approach encourages the latter. Christ's spoken word survives the visible order against which his hearers might test it. |
| Offertory | After the pledge about Christ's words, the identical Latin antiphon asks once more for protection from shame. Bringing gifts does not give the worshipper grounds for boasting that he is secure. |
| Secret | The assembly asks the offered mysteries to purify those who would draw near to their source. Cleansing is received here; Paul's summons to act has no premise that people can purify themselves. |
| Trinity Preface | The recurring act of lifting is now an exchange between celebrant and people. They give thanks to the Father for a majesty shared equally by Son and Spirit. The Son appearing with power in Luke comes as one who shares that majesty. The angelic voices at the close give praise even though Luke's heavenly powers are shaken. The two images need no identification. |
| Communion | Augustine on Ps 84 §12 calls attention to the order in the verse: divine sweetness makes the field of the human heart fruitful. His concrete example is the person who formerly liked taking things and comes to enjoy giving. This supplies an observable meaning for readiness. |
| Postcommunion | The prayer asks for mercy in the temple and fitting observance of the approaching solemnities of restoration. Those who await judgment prepare to keep a feast of mercy; the word *ventura* first concerns those celebrations. |

**Four senses.** Literally, a psalmist in distress waits for help, Paul calls believers to a different manner of life, and Jesus promises redemption amid the signs of his coming. Allegorically, the people whose soul Christ raises learn to address as their Redeemer the Son who comes to judge. Morally, one can relinquish an injury deliberately kept alive through contention or envy. Paul's two public sins belong to the same night as his more notorious pairings. Anagogically, Gregory's summer is the joy of meeting God when the present world's support fails. The signs promise the kingdom; they do not promise every hearer a favorable judgment regardless of his loves.

## 2. The salvation asked for takes flesh and gives himself

**Carrying witnesses.** Thomas Aquinas, *Super Romanos* 13, lect. 3, and Robert Bellarmine, *Commentary on the Book of Psalms*, Ps 84 on the lemma “For the Lord will give goodness” (O'Sullivan's exposition paragraph 12, Douay 84:13). Augustine's identification of salvation with Christ in Ps 84 §6 supplies one link. Guéranger's commentary on this exact pre-1962 Sunday independently brings the Incarnation and reception of Communion together. Thomas makes Paul's Advent hearers contemporaries in prayer of those still awaiting Christ. Bellarmine asks how truth can spring from the earth in the preceding verse: the Holy Spirit overshadows the Virgin and she gives birth to Christ. He takes the ground's fruit as something the earth could not produce by human cultivation. The first claim accounts for the worshippers' time of speaking, the second for what their request can mean. The Mass's complete movement is our inference.

| Appointment | Contribution to the argument |
| --- | --- |
| Introit | Give the psalmist his future tense: the speaker puts his trust in someone whose answer he does not yet see. At the station of St Mary Major the waiting voice is heard in a church dedicated to Mary. The heading establishes a Roman place; it does not identify the original psalmist with Mary. |
| Collect | The petition for the Lord himself to come makes rescue personal. The clause addressed to the Son who lives with the Father identifies the assembly’s addressee in the Mass. |
| Epistle | Thomas explains the Church's Advent use as a prayer from within the long expectation of the Incarnation. The closer salvation is Christ approaching those who first believed he would come. Paul's own admonition still calls actual Christians to conversion and growth in charity. |
| Gradual | “All who wait” permits the congregation to speak with the faithful who awaited the promise; the paths have to be shown to each generation. |
| Alleluia | Augustine on *salutare tuum* (§6) identifies the gift as the Christ whose face one hopes to see. The petitions to see mercy and receive salvation anticipate the two verbs of giving in the Communion. |
| Gospel | The one expected in the flesh will come publicly in majesty. Ambrose X.39–43 takes the clouds as a figure for witnesses through whom Christ speaks and then reads the light cloud as the Virgin overshadowed by the Holy Spirit. His lemma includes plural clouds; the Missal has one cloud. Ambrose's sense supplements the future coming; it cannot replace it. In §§44–45 the fruit on all the trees permits a further hope for the resurrection of peoples who have come to faith. |
| Offertory | The same voice of trust returns over the gifts. For Guéranger the Church keeps her gaze on the One who is to come even as she offers bread and wine (pp. 135–136). |
| Secret | The offered mysteries cleanse those who would approach their *principium*. Lasance takes that source as God, “their author.” Gifts received at the altar can bring their recipients to the Giver; the sacramental gifts are not literally the botanical fruit of Psalm 84. |
| Trinity Preface | The thanksgiving goes to the Father, confesses his only-begotten Son and names the Holy Spirit. Bellarmine's account of the birth by the Spirit thus leads to the confession that becoming human takes nothing from the Son's equality in majesty. |
| Communion | God gives goodness; our earth yields its fruit. The order of the two gifts matters to Bellarmine: the Spirit acts and the Virgin bears the Son. Guéranger, p. 137, explicitly describes the one whom the faithful await at the Nativity as the same one who has just given himself to them. His nineteenth-century formulary has other prayers, which do not enter this Mass. |
| Postcommunion | Having received Communion, the assembly still asks to receive mercy in the temple and prepare for the coming celebration. Receiving Christ today and expecting the feast can coexist because he first came in the flesh and continues to give himself. |

**Four senses.** Literally, Israel asks for God's salvation and expects a good harvest; Luke compares signs of the kingdom to recognizable fruit on trees. Allegorically, Bellarmine reads God's gift and earth's fruit as the Spirit's work and the Son born of Mary. Morally, Thomas distinguishes mere passage of time from nearness born of increasing charity: one who already believes can begin acting on a neighbor's need. Anagogically, the Incarnate Son who can now be received under sacramental signs will be manifest in majesty. The sacramental gift gives hope of personal encounter, while the command to be prepared retains its force.

**Point of comparison.** On the verse before Communion (Ps 84:12), Augustine first takes truth springing from earth as Christ born of Mary. Christ takes from her the mortal flesh in which he can offer himself (§10). Augustine then reads the same words as a sinner telling the truth about himself (§11). When the earth yields her fruit in the Communion verse (v. 13), Augustine points to the changed conduct of someone who confesses and hears God’s word (§12). Bellarmine understands that fruit itself as the Son whom Mary bears through the Holy Spirit’s action. The first reading leads from the end's approach to a change of loves now; the second leads from a promised birth to the identity of the One already received and still expected. At the Gospel the fruit can be the world coming to its appointed end (Gregory) or the fruit of faith ripening for resurrection (one of Ambrose's alternatives). The Fathers do not say the same thing about the image. None purports to explain why a compiler selected every Sunday text.
