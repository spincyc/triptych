# Second Sunday after Pentecost — Research Scope

**Last updated:** 2026-07-18

This record preserves the source roles, historical alternatives, and research limits compressed in the reader-facing guide. Complete appointed texts, checksums, facsimile locations, witness differences, and line-by-line collation results are retained in [the verified propers](../propers/verified.md).

## Scriptural and Liturgical Scope

- Directly appointed passages and their complete contexts studied: Psalms 6, 7, 12, 17, and 119 in Vulgate/Septuagint numbering (modern Psalms 6, 7, 13, 18, and 120); Luke 13–15, especially the complete table scene in 14:1–24; and 1 John 2–3, especially 3:11–24.
- The Introit includes both Psalm 17:19–20 and its appointed psalm verses, 17:2–3. They are consolidated as one historical dossier because they belong to the same directly appointed psalm.
- The Collect, Secret, and Postcommunion were studied in their complete, facsimile-verified Latin forms. They are composed prayers, not direct biblical quotations, and therefore do not receive dossiers in `In Illo Tempore...`.
- The seven historical dossiers are ordered canonically rather than liturgically: Psalm 6:5 (Offertory); Psalm 7:2 (Alleluia); Psalm 12:6 (Communion); Psalm 17:2–3, 19–20 (Introit); Psalm 119:1–2 (Gradual); Luke 14:16–24 (Gospel); and 1 John 3:13–18 (Epistle).
- The guide's central movement—prepared gift, tested response, widened house, and transformed guest—is a textual and source-grounded synthesis of the appointed formulary. It is not presented as the historical compositional intention of any biblical author or as a patristic account of the Roman formulary.

## Historical Coordinates of the Direct Scriptural Propers

### Psalms 6, 7, 12, and 17

- The Greek and Latin superscriptions attribute Psalms 6, 7, 12, and 17 to David. The table therefore uses a broad traditional Davidic horizon around 1000 BC where the title supplies no narrower date. “Israel; exact site unrecorded” is deliberate: none of these titles names a writing place.
- Psalms 6 and 12 name no episode in David's life. Their table descriptions consequently state that the traditional attribution does not locate his exact life stage rather than inventing one. Psalm 6 is a penitential lament appealing to mercy amid bodily and social distress; Psalm 12 moves from apparent abandonment and enemy pressure to trust and thanksgiving.
- Psalm 7's title associates the song with “Cush the Benjaminite.” Cush cannot be securely identified with a person or episode in Samuel, so the table locates David only broadly as a threatened and accused suppliant. A proposed equation with Saul, Shimei, or another Benjaminite was not used.
- Psalm 17 has an unusually specific title and a parallel in 2 Samuel 22: David sings after deliverance from Saul and all his enemies. The table accordingly places him as a mature king looking back over the dangers through which the kingdom was established. The psalm's closing reference to David and his descendants carries the thanksgiving into Davidic-covenant and Christian messianic history.
- Modern historical judgment does not treat a superscription as a recoverable composition certificate. The *NABRE* Psalms introduction says the superscriptions derive from pre-Christian Jewish tradition and that no psalm can be securely dated from them. The descriptions therefore retain Davidic attribution as tradition while marking author, final-form date, and writing site as historically uncertain.

### Psalm 119 (modern 120)

- The traditional title is only *Canticum graduum* (“Song of Ascents”); it names no author, episode, place, or date. The composition row therefore says “Undated in title” rather than assigning the psalm to David or inventing an approximate year.
- Jewish and Christian interpretation associated the fifteen Songs of Ascents with steps at the Temple, ascent from exile, or—more plausibly in the *Catholic Encyclopedia* and *NABRE* notes—with pilgrims going up to Jerusalem. That use does not establish that Psalm 119 was composed in Jerusalem.
- The modern *NABRE* heading, “Prayer of a Returned Exile,” and the psalm's consciousness of dwelling away from God's land make a postexilic communal horizon plausible, but neither an individual author nor a composition site is recoverable. The table distinguishes this judgment from the anonymous traditional title.

### Luke 14:16–24

- Ancient tradition identifies the evangelist as St. Luke, the Antiochene physician and companion of St. Paul. The ancient *Prefatio vel Argumentum Lucae*, reported in the *Catholic Encyclopedia*, places composition in Achaia. The traditional date around AD 60 is a separate early chronological strand based on Luke preceding Acts and Acts ending with Paul's Roman custody; the table does not imply that Achaia and the early date are independently synchronized by a single source.
- Luke explicitly addresses Theophilus and, by extension, other catechized Christians. The Gospel's salvation-historical architecture presents God's promises to Israel as fulfilled in Jesus and extended through the apostolic mission to the Gentiles.
- The modern majority dates Luke around AD 80–90, regards the city of composition as unknown, and infers a largely Gentile, non-Palestinian audience. These judgments are disclosed beside, not substituted for, the requested traditional metadata.
- The Gospel's narrated event is distinct from composition. Luke 9:51–19:27 is the travel narrative toward Jerusalem; 14:1 locates Jesus at a Sabbath meal in a leading Pharisee's house but does not name the town. The parable follows healing and teaching on humility and hospitality and answers a guest's praise of eating bread in God's kingdom. The event row therefore says “journey toward Jerusalem; exact town unrecorded” and uses the traditional late-ministry date c. AD 29 rather than asserting an unsupported Perean or Judean town.
- Traditional chronologies vary over the length and civil dates of Jesus' ministry. The c. AD 29 event date follows a traditional chronology that places the Passion in AD 29 and should be read as approximate, not as a claim of day-level harmonization.

### 1 John 3:13–18

- Early Christian tradition attributes the work to St. John the Apostle. Irenaeus places the letter during John's residence in Asia; the *Catholic Encyclopedia* judges Ephesus and the last years of the first century probable but explicitly says certainty is unavailable. The table therefore locates an elderly apostolic witness in Ephesus around AD 90–100 while retaining that qualification.
- The writing has no named recipients or conventional epistolary greeting. Its first audience is best described as Johannine Christian communities, traditionally associated with Roman Asia, being guarded against teachers who separated from them and denied central claims about Jesus. The address “little children” fits John's traditional late-life pastoral role but is not biographical proof by itself.
- Modern scholarship generally treats the Gospel of John and 1 John as products of the same Johannine school; many date the letter near the end of the first century and after or during debate over the Gospel's interpretation. It does not unanimously identify the apostle as the immediate penman or Ephesus as the certain site.
- In salvation history, the passage joins Israel's story of Cain and Abel to Christ's laying down his life, then makes material mercy toward a needy brother a test of the new life. It belongs to the early Church's work of preserving apostolic confession and embodied charity amid internal division.

## Text Verification and Material Limits

- The Internet Archive OCR was used only to locate and preserve the focused formulary extract. The 1962 typical-edition facsimile on printed pages 377–378 controls every published Latin form. A color image witness on printed pages 371–372 was compared and its material differences are recorded in `propers/verified.md`.
- The baseline and secondary facsimiles differ in the Introit's printed repeat incipit, the Collect conclusion, and Postcommunion punctuation. These differences are documented rather than silently harmonized; the baseline controls the verified transcription.
- The historical table's traditional dates are deliberately approximate. Psalms 6, 7, 12, and 17 use a broad Davidic date because the traditional attribution does not provide modern chronological precision. Psalm 119 receives no fabricated date. Luke's Achaian place and early date come from distinct traditional arguments. Ephesus and c. AD 90–100 for 1 John are traditional probabilities, not certainties.
- No exact identification of Cush the Benjaminite, no town for Luke 14, and no individual author or composition site for Psalm 119 was adopted. These remain unresolved rather than being filled by conjecture.
- The public source survey is sufficient for the historical claims actually made but is not exhaustive of modern monographs, journal literature, manuscript studies, non-English scholarship, or the full patristic corpus.
