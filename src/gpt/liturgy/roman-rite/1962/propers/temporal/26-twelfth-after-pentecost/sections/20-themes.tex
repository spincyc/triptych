\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\label{triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

\begin{studybox}{Governing thesis}
Everything needed for Christian movement comes first as gift: help for the
imperilled, sufficiency for ministers, the law read by Christ, covenant mercy
for sinners, the fruits of creation, and life through the holy mystery. The
gift reaches its liturgical test when the hearer stops defining the neighbour
and becomes one.
\end{studybox}

\subsection*{1. Need confessed; sufficiency received \cue{Int., Coll., Ep.}}

The Introit supplies the grammar of dependence: \latin{inténde} and
\latin{festína} ask help now. The Collect makes a still stronger claim. Even
worthy service proceeds \latin{de cuius múnere}; the petition to run without
stumbling therefore asks grace to carry forward a motion grace has already
begun. Paul gives the doctrinal center: confidence is \emph{through Christ
toward God}, while sufficiency is not \latin{quasi ex nobis} but
\latin{ex Deo}. These elements do not cultivate religious passivity. They
relocate agency so that service can be real without becoming self-authored.

The point is already audible in the Introit's opening petition. Its first
movement is not a report about danger but an address: God is named and help is
asked before the enemies are described. The Collect then turns that emergency
grammar into a rule for the whole Christian course. Grace is not merely a
remedy applied after a failed attempt; it is the gift from which worthy service
comes and the aid by which the runner advances.

\subsection*{2. The written command reaches its living end \cue{Ep., Gosp.}}

Paul's ``letter kills'' is a covenantal contrast, not contempt for Scripture
or law. Jesus proves the point by making the lawyer read: ``What is written?
How readest thou?'' The answer from Deuteronomy and Leviticus is correct, yet
knowledge reaches life only in \emph{doing}. The lawyer asks for a class of
persons; Jesus answers with a sequence of verbs---seeing, approaching,
binding, carrying, paying, promising to return---and reverses the question:
which traveller \emph{became} neighbour? Law is neither abolished nor left on
stone; it is fulfilled as Spirit-enabled mercy.

This also fixes the limits of Paul's antithesis. The ``letter'' is not the
literal sense of Scripture, and the ministry associated with Moses remains
glorious. What kills is command encountered externally by the sinner without
the inward gift that forgives and forms charity. The Gospel accordingly does
not replace Torah with sentiment. It places Deut.~6:5 and Lev.~19:18 on the
lawyer's lips, accepts the answer, and makes its truth visible in conduct.

\subsection*{3. Praise and lament share one faithful address \cue{Grad., All.}}

The Gradual promises blessing ``at all times.'' The Alleluia immediately tests
that universality by taking its verse from Psalm 87, whose prayer descends into
almost unrelieved darkness. The singer nevertheless says ``God of my
salvation'' and prays by day and night. The juxtaposition refuses two easy
reductions: praise is not denial of suffering, and lament is not the loss of
faith. In both, the mouth remains turned toward God; the meek can rejoice not
because pain is imaginary but because dependence has found an addressee.

The received commentaries preserve more than one historical and spiritual
horizon. Theodoret reads Psalm 87 through Israel's calamity and fallen human
suffering, while Augustine reads it through Christ's Passion and through the
Church's prayer in prosperity and adversity. Their disagreement should not be
flattened. Their convergence is narrower and firmer: day and night, the singer
continues to address the God called salvation.

\subsection*{4. Promise is pleaded; pardon honors God's name \cue{Coll., Off., Sec.}}

The Collect names God's promises as the runner's destination. At the
Offertory Moses pleads a prior promise for people who cannot make innocence
their argument: Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob are remembered while judgment
remains real. Theodoret's reading of Exodus keeps both sides together. Mercy
preserves the people and the covenantal future, but it neither calls the sin
good nor removes all discipline and accountability.

The Secret translates that posture into the Church's offering. Its grammar
does not make the gifts a price that purchases forgiveness. The petition asks
God to regard the offerings favorably, so that by obtaining pardon for the
offerers they may give honor to his name. Promise, intercession, and pardon
therefore exclude boasting without making prayer or offering unreal.

The Offertory's received wording sharpens the same restraint. It is not a
continuous excerpt from the Clementine Vulgate: it incorporates matter
associated with verse 12, says Jacob where the Vulgate says Israel, replaces
the stars-and-land promise with the land flowing with milk and honey, and ends
in an Old-Latin construction independently witnessed by Augustine. Those
differences belong to the chant and may not be repaired by silently importing
the Douay or Clementine wording. At the same time, Augustine reads the
threatened ``evil'' as punishment, not moral evil in God, and Aquinas places
intercession within immutable providence rather than against it.

The oration history adds a complementary limit. Shorter ancestors occur
separately in the Veronese collection; the Old Gelasian gathers the three
prayers with variants; the Hadrianum witnesses their later lexical shape
together. This proves a received prayer-family and real development. It proves
neither the name of an author nor that one compiler designed the complete
chant-and-lesson argument reconstructed on these pages.

\subsection*{5. Providence remains visible at sacramental Communion \cue{Comm., Postcomm.}}

Communion sings bread, wine, and oil within Psalm 103's praise of providence:
earth is filled from God's works before sacramental reception is considered.
Theodoret stays at that created horizon; Eusebius develops spiritual readings
without making them plainly Eucharistic; Augustine and Cassiodorus supply an
explicitly sacramental reception. The tradition is richest when these levels
are distinguished rather than collapsed.

The Postcommunion then states what the Church actually asks from the holy
mystery: that participation vivify, and that it grant expiation and
protection. The verbs name requested fruits, not a timetable or an automatic
effect. Their doctrinal horizon includes both the objective gift and the
recipient's disposition. The rite closes with communicants still dependent on
God for the life and defense needed to obey what they have heard.

The Communion antiphon is itself a disciplined act of selection. It begins at
the second half of Psalm 103:13, reorders the clause, inserts
\latin{Dómine}, omits the first half of verse 14, and then sings bread, wine,
and oil from verse 15. The historical English therefore prints the continuous
psalm verses and marks the gap instead of manufacturing a hybrid translation.
The liturgical use intensifies a sacramental hearing, but the underlying
creation hymn continues to confess rain, cultivation, food, gladness, and
human creatureliness.

The Postcommunion also has a history worth preserving. An early Veronese form
has \latin{participatio tui sancta mysterii}; later witnesses transmit the
prayer-family that reaches the 1962 \latin{huius}. Change of wording does not
make the petition unstable. Across the witnesses participation remains a gift
whose fruits are asked from God. Ignatius' ``medicine of immortality'' and
Aquinas's treatment of Eucharistic effects illuminate that petition while
also guarding the need for worthy reception.

\begin{studyframe}
\textbf{Movement in four stages.} Cry and receive; read and enact; praise
through the night; plead, offer, and partake. Every appointed element belongs
to one or more of these stages. The movement is a source-grounded account of
the received juxtaposition, not evidence that one historical compiler designed
all ten texts as a single argument.
\end{studyframe}
