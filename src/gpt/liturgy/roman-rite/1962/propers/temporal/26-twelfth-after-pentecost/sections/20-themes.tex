\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\label{triptych:brief-synthesis:start}
\begingroup\fontsize{9}{9.8}\selectfont

\begin{studybox}{Governing thesis}
The formulary moves from the cry of one who cannot save himself to the life of
one made able to love. God gives the beginning, power, pattern, pardon, and
food of Christian service: the Spirit writes the good command within; Christ
the Samaritan draws near to fallen humanity; his mercy creates a merciful
people; his intercession carries sinners to the altar; and his sacramental
gift vivifies those sent to do likewise. Grace does not leave the human act
behind. It heals, elevates, and completes it.
\end{studybox}

\subsection*{1. The cry for help opens into a grace-given course \cue{Int., Coll., Ep.}}

The Introit begins in the imperative: \latin{inténde}, attend; \latin{festína},
make haste. Before danger is described, the threatened servant turns toward
God. Eusebius hears in this compact cry the voices of David, Christ, and the
Church; Augustine hears the members of Christ confessing a need that remains
throughout the pilgrimage; Cassian gives the verse to the monk in every
condition, and Benedict makes it the threshold of the Hours and the prayer of
humble work. Dependence is not an emergency measure at the edge of Christian
life. It is the creature's truthful posture before the giver of life.

The Collect transforms that cry into ordered movement. Worthy service itself
comes \latin{de cuius múnere}; therefore the Church asks the giver to grant
that his people run without stumbling toward his promises. The prayer holds
together gift, service, motion, and end. Augustine's teaching on grace and free
will supplies its doctrinal cadence: God awakens and strengthens the will so
that the renewed person truly consents and acts. Aquinas, \work{ST} I--II,
q.~109, aa.~2, 4--5, 9--10, says the same with scholastic precision: without
grace fallen man cannot be healed, keep the whole law, merit eternal life, or
persevere; under grace he acts as a living secondary cause. The Benedictine Prologue receives the biblical verb of
running in just this key---the heart expands in the sweetness of love, and the
monk runs toward the heavenly homeland.

Paul gives the Sunday's ministerial form to this anthropology. Confidence is
\latin{per Christum ad Deum}; sufficiency is \latin{ex Deo}; yet those made
sufficient really are ministers. Chrysostom points to the Corinthians
themselves as the Spirit-written commendation of apostolic labor. Theodoret
and Aquinas refuse the rivalry between divine and human agency: God makes the
minister fit, and the minister consequently serves. Thus the Introit does not
end in helplessness, nor the Collect in pious desire, nor the Epistle in a
theory of inward grace. Received help becomes a course, and received
sufficiency becomes ministry.

\subsection*{2. The Spirit brings the written command to its living perfection \cue{Ep., Gosp.}}

Paul's antithesis does not divide the God of Moses from the God of Christ.
The stone-lettered ministry is divine and glorious; its command is holy. What
it cannot do by external inscription alone is forgive the sinner, pour charity
into the heart, and produce the obedience it requires. Chrysostom honors both
glories while proclaiming the greater; Ambrosiaster locates the new ministry's
surpassing splendor in forgiveness and righteousness; Augustine teaches that
even the good precept remains killing letter when the Spirit does not inwardly
give love; Aquinas therefore calls the grace of the Holy Spirit the New Law in
its principal reality. The promise of Jeremiah and Ezekiel is fulfilled: the
one divine will once written on stone is written in the living heart.

The Gospel enacts this doctrine as a lesson in reading. Jesus asks the lawyer,
``What is written? How readest thou?'' The answer from Deuteronomy and
Leviticus is entirely true: love God wholly and the neighbor as oneself. Then
Christ gives the command a body. The Samaritan sees, is moved with compassion,
approaches, binds, pours, sets the wounded man upon his own beast, brings him
to shelter, keeps watch, pays, entrusts further care, and promises to return.
The lawyer asks which persons fall within the boundary of neighbor; Christ
asks which traveller \emph{became} neighbor. The written word reaches its
living perfection when grace makes charity operative.

The Fathers contemplate in the Samaritan both the Lord who saves and the life
he gives his members. Origen preserves an elder's reading of wounded humanity;
Ambrose, Augustine, and Bede identify the compassionate stranger above all
with Christ. Humanity descends from Jerusalem into mortality, is stripped and
wounded by sin, and lies unable to restore itself. The Son becomes near by the
Incarnation, bears our weakness in his flesh, binds the wounds, brings the
patient to the Church, commits care to apostolic ministry, and promises to
return. Bede holds the senses in fruitful union: Christ is Neighbor in the
highest sense, and everyone who performs mercy becomes neighbor. The command
\latin{fac similiter} is therefore the form Christ's own mercy takes in those
he has healed.

The Gospel's opening beatitude also orders the passage's sight. The disciples
are blessed because their eyes see the saving works long desired by prophets
and kings. On the road, priest and Levite likewise see the wounded man, but
their sight does not mature into approach. The Samaritan sees and is moved in
his inward parts; compassion then becomes the long sequence of embodied acts.
The lawyer must finally see himself inside that sequence: not merely defining
the neighbor from outside, but receiving Christ's merciful judgment and going
to enact it. Blessed sight reaches its proper fruit when the person shown
mercy learns to behold another's misery with the heart of Christ.

\subsection*{3. Continual praise contains the whole day and the whole night \cue{Grad., All.}}

The Gradual answers received sufficiency with \latin{Benedícam Dóminum in omni
témpore}. Basil and Augustine hear ``always'' as the consecration of every
season; praise in the Lord becomes common joy because the meek hear and rejoice.
The Alleluia then sounds the opening of Psalm 87, the lament that carries no
easy turn toward visible relief: \latin{Dómine, Deus salútis meæ, in die
clamávi et nocte coram te}. The singers can bless at all times because night
itself remains before the God named salvation.

Patristic exegesis opens that constancy into the mystery of Christ. Augustine
hears the Passion and, in day and night, the Church joined to Christ in
prosperity and adversity. Eusebius reads the sequence from Psalm 86's birth in
Sion to Psalm 87's descent into death as the incarnate Lord's continuous
oblation. Theodoret also lets the afflicted people and suffering humanity
speak. At Mass these voices are not reduced to one historical occasion: they
form the Church's praise in the crucified and risen Christ. The disciples are
about to be called blessed for seeing; the Alleluia teaches them that faith
also addresses God when sight is dark.

\subsection*{4. The Mediator carries sinners from covenant promise to the altar \cue{Off., Sec.}}

At the Offertory Moses stands between threatened judgment and the people. He
does not call the calf harmless. He invokes God's saving name, the deliverance
from Egypt, and the oath to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. First Clement and
Chrysostom admire the pastor willing to be blotted out for his people; Gregory
the Great sees God's ``let me alone'' as the very opening through which the
servant is drawn into bold supplication, and Moses' willingness to die as a
self-offering. Tertullian names the intercession a type of Christ. The figure
is fulfilled, not discarded, in the one Mediator who offers himself and ever
lives to intercede.

Augustine and Theodoret read the scene according to divine constancy. God's
anger is just judgment, not turbulent passion; his eternal providence includes
the prayer by which mercy is bestowed. Theodoret's sequence is especially
important: pardon preserves Israel and the covenantal future, while discipline
and accountability remain. Mercy does not name indifference to evil. It names
the triumph of God's faithful purpose through an intercessor whom God himself
has raised up.

The Secret places that mystery within the Church's Eucharistic offering. Its
grammar asks God to regard the offerings favorably so that, in granting pardon
to us, they may give honor to his name. Justin and Irenaeus praise the pure
offering of the renewed people; Cyril of Jerusalem presents the memorial of
Christ's sacrifice together with intercession for the living and the dead;
Augustine locates every acceptable sacrifice within the one whole Christ,
Head and members. Trent states the doctrinal center without reserve: the Mass
is a true and proper propitiatory sacrifice because the same Christ who offered
himself once on the Cross is offered sacramentally in an unbloody manner. The
human gifts do not purchase mercy; taken into Christ's one oblation, they are
the liturgical action through which the Church truly asks and receives pardon.

\subsection*{5. The Church receives created gifts as sacramental life \cue{Comm., Postcomm.}}

Psalm 103 first sings the Creator's generous order. Rain descends, earth is
filled, grass grows for beasts, and human cultivation brings forth bread,
wine, and oil. Theodoret delights in their bodily purposes; Bellarmine draws
from them gratitude, recognition of fatherly providence, and temperance. Christian spiritual reading
does not empty these goods of their created truth. It discovers that the same
Wisdom who feeds bodily life has ordered creation toward mysteries in which
Christ feeds, gladdens, heals, and consecrates his people.

Eusebius accordingly passes from the vine to the true Vine, from bread to the
mystical Logos, and from oil to light and healing. Augustine sees apostolic
earth bearing Christ as bread, the cup giving sober inebriation, and anointing
extending through the whole Christ. Cassiodorus is explicit: the wine received
in ecclesial mystery is consecrated as Christ's Blood, while oil evokes royal
chrism. Sung at Communion, the triad therefore retains the praise of providence
and reaches its sacramental fullness. Creation supplies what the Church offers;
Christ gives what only his sacrifice and presence can make it become.

The Postcommunion names the fruits for which communicants pray. Participation
in the holy mystery is to vivify; it is to grant expiation and protection.
Ignatius calls the Eucharist medicine of immortality. Aquinas, \work{ST} III,
q.~79, aa.~1--2, 4, 6, 8, explains that Communion nourishes the life of grace,
remits venial sin, strengthens against future sin, unites the faithful to
Christ and one another, and pledges glory; fruitfulness flowers in worthy
reception. The Mass therefore closes where its Gospel command can be obeyed.
Those found half-dead are borne by Christ; those
vivified by his mystery become bearers of another's burden. Those who received
pardon intercede for sinners. Those fed from God's works spend themselves in
mercy. And those guarded through the night run toward the promise of eternal
life, awaiting the Samaritan's return.

\begin{studyframe}
\textbf{Movement in five acts.} Cry for the help without which no course can
begin; receive the Spirit who writes charity within; bless God through day and
night; enter Christ's intercession and offering; receive sacramental life and
go to become a neighbor. This synthesis contemplates the received Roman
formulary as a genuine liturgical whole while respecting the distinct
historical sources from which its elements came.
\end{studyframe}
