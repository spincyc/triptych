\section*{Source-Grounded Synthesis}

\subsection*{A received Roman unity through historical layers}

The formulary did not arrive all at once from one recoverable author. The
Verona collection preserves shorter cognate forms of the Collect and
Postcommunion; the Old Gelasian places the three orations together with
variants; the Hadrianum no.~XXXI gives their later lexical family; the psalm
chants and lessons have their own scriptural and liturgical histories. Yet the
historical layers do not make the 1962 Roman arrangement theologically inert.
Once the Church has received these texts together for this Sunday, their
liturgical sequence is itself a legitimate object of Catholic contemplation.
Schuster's direct commentary on the assembled formulary (III, pp.~128--131)
provides a traditional precedent: he reads Collect and Epistle, Moses and the
Secret, and Communion and Postcommunion as mutually illuminating.

This study therefore distinguishes two sound claims. It does not attribute the
ten-element design to one ancient compiler without evidence. It does affirm a
real received unity: the Church's stable act of proclamation, offering, and
Communion can make texts from several periods speak together in the traditional
senses of Scripture.

\subsection*{Gift does not abolish action; it makes action Christian}

The Introit, Collect, Epistle, Gospel, and Postcommunion form an unusually
strong account of grace-enabled agency. The worshipper first asks God to
attend and hasten. The Collect says that worthy service itself comes from the
divine gift and immediately asks that the faithful may run. Paul excludes
sufficiency from self and immediately says that God has made ministers. Jesus
commands \latin{fac} and \latin{Vade} after the lawyer has correctly read the
Law. The final prayer makes sacramental participation the giver of life and
defense.

Augustine's doctrine of the Spirit writing charity within and Aquinas's account
of the New Law explain why these verbs belong together. Grace neither leaves
the will inert nor permits it to boast as its own first cause. It heals the
person into living action. The Samaritan's many verbs are therefore not a
secular moral appendix to a theology of gift. They are what divine charity
looks like when it has taken flesh in a human agent: compassion approaches,
binds, bears, spends, entrusts, and perseveres. The Postcommunion sends
communicants into that service strengthened by what they have received.

\subsection*{Law, Spirit, and mercy belong to one divine economy}

Paul's contrast is not law against mercy. He calls Moses' ministry glorious;
Jesus sends the lawyer to the written Law and confirms the answer from
Deuteronomy and Leviticus. The distinction lies between command remaining
external to the sinner and command fulfilled through the inward gift of the
Spirit. Chrysostom, Theodoret, Ambrosiaster, Augustine, and Aquinas converge on
this order while preserving distinct emphases: old glory is real, new glory
surpasses it, forgiveness and righteousness are given, and the Spirit supplies
the love by which obedience becomes possible.

The Gospel makes that covenantal doctrine visible. Self-justifying reading
asks for the minimum class to whom love is owed; Spirit-written reading becomes
neighbor through mercy. Moses' Offertory intercession and the Secret's plea for
pardon show why this does not become a new project of self-salvation. The doer
of mercy remains one who needs covenant faithfulness and propitiation. Mercy
does not empty command or judgment. It pardons, renews, and gives the power to
fulfill love's command.

\subsection*{Christ the Neighbor heals a people into neighbor-love}

The Gospel's literal and spiritual senses stand in a fruitful Catholic order.
Literally, Jesus teaches universal embodied mercy through a Samaritan whose
action answers the lawyer's boundary-making question. Allegorically, Origen,
Ambrose, Augustine, Irenaeus, Bede, and the medieval commentators contemplate
fallen humanity in the wounded man and Christ in the compassionate Samaritan.
The Son becomes near by the Incarnation, binds what sin has torn, bears the
patient in his flesh, brings him to the Church, supplies the ministry of care,
and promises to return.

The moral sense proceeds from that saving mystery. The Christian is not asked
to imitate a distant exemplar by unaided resolve. He first receives Christ's
approach and healing; then Christ's charity acts in him. Bede's both-senses
rule is exact: every person who does mercy is neighbor, and Christ is Neighbor
in the fullest sense. Anagogically, the inn is the pilgrim Church, the morrow
opens toward Resurrection, and the promised return toward Parousia and
judgment. Eternal life, the lawyer's first question, becomes the horizon of a
mercy begun now and brought to completion by the returning Lord.

\subsection*{Continual prayer is Paschal rather than triumphalist}

The Introit's urgency, Gradual's continual praise, and Alleluia's day-and-night
cry give the Church a complete grammar of prayer. Augustine and Eusebius hear
Christ and his members in Psalm 69; Basil makes Psalm 33's ``always'' include
loss as well as prosperity; Augustine, Eusebius, Cassiodorus, Theodoret, and
Bellarmine unfold Psalm 87 through the Passion, Israel's affliction, and the
Church's prayer in every condition. Their interpretations are not identical,
but they converge in one theological act: the sufferer continues to address
the God named salvation.

The Mass's Alleluia from so grave a lament is consequently a confession of the
Paschal mystery. Christian praise does not call darkness unreal. It receives
night into Christ's prayer and waits for the Father's answer in Resurrection.
The disciples' eyes are blessed for seeing Christ, but faith continues to cry
when immediate sight is withheld. The Gradual's meek rejoice because their
boast is in the Lord, not because they have made themselves invulnerable.

\subsection*{Covenant intercession reaches propitiatory offering}

Moses pleads for a people who cannot plead innocence. Augustine and Aquinas
teach that immutable providence includes his real intercession; Gregory sees
God open the way to bold supplication and Moses offer himself with the people;
Tertullian receives the servant as a type of Christ. Theodoret keeps pardon
and judgment in their biblical sequence: Israel and the covenantal future are
preserved, while correction remains. Thus the Offertory does not contrast an
angry Old Testament God with a merciful Church. It celebrates the faithful God
who raises an intercessor and fulfills his promise.

The Secret brings this typology to sacramental fulfillment. God regards the
offerings propitiously, and those offerings honor his name by granting pardon.
Justin, Irenaeus, Cyril of Jerusalem, and Augustine place Eucharistic
thanksgiving, created gifts, ecclesial self-offering, and intercession within
Christ's priesthood. Trent gives the definitive Catholic form: the Mass is
truly propitiatory because the same Christ and victim of Calvary is offered in
an unbloody manner. The Church's action is real precisely because it
participates in his one sacrifice. Pardon therefore excludes boasting while
creating worshippers able to honor God's name in praise and obedient charity.

\subsection*{Providence reaches sacramental life without ceasing to be providence}

Psalm 103 first praises the Creator's care for embodied life. Water makes the
earth fruitful; bread strengthens, wine gladdens, and oil brightens. Theodoret
dwells on this literal goodness, and Bellarmine draws gratitude, recognition of
fatherly providence, and temperance from it. Eusebius, Augustine, and Cassiodorus then open the
created triad into Christian spiritual senses: the true Vine and mystical
Logos, Christ preached as bread, the cup's sober inebriation, the anointing of
the whole Christ, consecrated wine, and royal chrism.

Sung at Communion, the psalm receives its sacramental fullness. Creation and
human labor supply gifts that the Church offers; Christ gives himself under
the consecrated species. The Postcommunion asks the holy participation to
vivify, expiate, and guard. Ignatius's medicine of immortality, Aquinas's
account of Eucharistic effects in \work{ST} III, q.~79, aa.~1--2, 4, 6, 8,
and the \work{Catechism} 1391--1395 show the
doctrinal depth of those petitions. The sacrament nourishes the life of grace,
cleanses, strengthens charity, protects against sin, joins the faithful to
Christ and one another, and pledges glory. The proper ends with created
providence elevated into sacramental provision and sacramental provision
ordered toward merciful life in the world.
