\subsection*{The complete appointed text}

The preceding formulary is part of this canonical full edition. Its Latin is
the image-collated text rather than normalized Bible Latin: the Introit retains
\latin{inimíci mei} and \latin{cógitant}; the Alleluia retains the comma after
\latin{Dómine}; the Gospel retains the comma after \latin{iumentum suum}; and
the Offertory and Communion preserve their received adaptations. The English
witnesses are printed without silently repairing those differences.

\subsection*{Help as a lifelong posture \cue{Int.}}

Psalm 69 is short but not contextless. Its cry is substantially the close of
Psalm 39, now presented under a title of remembrance. The antiphon selects the
request for immediate help, the enemies who seek the singer's life, and their
reversal. It is therefore neither a free-standing magical formula nor a warrant
to assign biblical enemy language to contemporary rivals. Its literal force is
the threatened worshipper's appeal to the God on whom life depends.

The reception makes that appeal durable. Cassian, \work{Conference} 10.10,
gives \latin{Deus, in adiutorium} to the monk as a formula able to meet every
condition: danger, distraction, gratitude, and perseverance. The Rule of St
Benedict places it at the opening of the Hours (chs.~17--18) and three times on
the lips of the incoming kitchen server (ch.~35); urgent prayer becomes both
common worship and humble work. Augustine, \work{Enarratio in Psalmum} 69.1--4,
hears the voice of Christ's members and makes perpetual need, not occasional
failure, the reason for perpetual help. Theodoret (PG~80, 1415--1418) instead
keeps the Davidic crisis prominent: calamities surround the speaker, whose own
past sin sharpens the distress. Bellarmine's commentary on Psalm 69 notes both
the Psalm 39 doublet and the verse's daily liturgical use. These are compatible
extensions, not proof that the Mass compiler intended monastic custom or a
single Davidic episode.

Greek reception is broader than Theodoret's historical reading. Eusebius,
\work{Commentary on the Psalms} at Psalm 69 (PG~23, 767--772), treats the cry
as a formula kept ready on the lips, then moves among David, Christ, the
Church, and the Passion--resurrection mystery. The rapid shift of personae is
a feature of his ecclesial exegesis, not a reconstruction of the psalm's date.

\subsection*{Grace precedes the run \cue{Coll.}}

The Collect's syntax protects Catholic teaching from two opposite errors.
Worthy and praiseworthy service is genuinely the faithful people's act, yet
the prayer locates even its coming-to-be in God's gift. The ensuing imperative,
\latin{tríbue}, asks not to replace human running but to make it reach the
promises \latin{sine offensióne}, without stumbling. Grace precedes, accompanies,
and brings the act toward its promised end.

This is the order taught at Trent: preparation for justification is itself
impossible without prevenient grace, while those moved and assisted freely
assent and cooperate (Session VI, chs.~5--6); growth in the received life
remains God's gift and the justified person's action (ch.~16). The
\work{Catechism} 2008--2011 likewise describes merit as arising from God's
initiative and the believer's free collaboration. These sources illuminate the
prayer's grammar; they do not document why this Collect was joined to this
Sunday. Hilary, \work{Tractatus in Psalmum 118}, Daleth 11--12, and Augustine,
\work{Enarratio in Psalmum 118}, sermo 11.1--5, place progress in the path
under divine teaching and help; Augustine, \work{De gratia et libero
arbitrio} 16.32--17.33, and Aquinas, \work{ST} I--II, q.~109, give the
grace-and-cooperation distinction its developed form. Benedict's Prologue
makes running toward the heavenly homeland a monastic reception rather than a
history of this prayer. In the Old Gelasian, all three orations occur together in
Book III, no.~VIII (Wilson, pp.~228--229); the Hadrianum Gregorian family
places them together under its thirteenth Sunday after Pentecost (Wilson,
no.~LXXXI, p.~173). The history establishes a received oration-set, not a
personal authorship claim.

\subsection*{Letter and Spirit within one divine economy \cue{Ep.}}

The pericope begins after Paul has called the Corinthians a letter of Christ,
written not in ink or on stone but by the living God's Spirit on hearts
(2~Cor.~3:3). It ends before his comparison of transient and surpassing glory
has finished (vv.~10--18). Within that frame, \latin{líttera enim occídit}
cannot mean that literal sense, written Scripture, Israel, or moral command is
evil. Paul calls the stone-lettered ministry glorious. The contrast concerns
what external command does when it encounters sin without the inward gift that
forgives and enables obedience; compare Jer.~31:31--34, Ezek.~36:25--27, and
Rom.~7--8.

\begin{receptiontable}
St John Chrysostom & \work{Homilies on 2 Corinthians} 6.2; 7.1, 5 & The
Corinthians themselves are Spirit-written evidence. The minister's confidence
does not arise from self-praise, and the old ministry was truly glorious even
as the new surpasses it.\\
Theodoret of Cyrus & \work{Interpretatio in II Corinthios}, PG~82,
391--394 & The ministers' fitness comes from God; ``letter'' names the law
without the enlivening gift, while the Spirit supplies power for obedience.\\
Ambrosiaster & \work{Commentarius in II Corinthios} 3:4--9, PL~17,
176--177 & Moses' glory is honored, but the Spirit's ministry gives the
forgiveness and righteousness the condemning letter could not confer.\\
St Augustine & \work{De spiritu et littera} 4.6; 14.23--25; 19.34; 26.42 &
Even a good command remains ``letter'' if inward grace is absent; the Spirit
writes charity in the heart. His earlier \work{De doctrina christiana} 3.5.9
also warns against a carnal captivity to signs, but that hermeneutical use is
an application, not Paul's primary covenantal contrast.\\
St Thomas Aquinas & \work{Super II ad Corinthios} 3, lect.~1--2; \work{ST}
I--II, q.~106, aa.~1--2 & New law is principally the grace of the Holy Spirit;
even Gospel writing without inward grace would remain an external letter.
Human ministry remains real because God makes ministers fit.\\
\end{receptiontable}

Catholic reading therefore rejects both self-sufficient activism and a
law-versus-grace caricature directed against the Jewish people. \work{Dei
Verbum} 14--16 and \work{Nostra aetate} 4 require the old covenant and Israel's
received gifts to be honored within salvation history. Paul contrasts modes
and ministries under sin and grace; he does not assign ethnic worth. The
Pontifical Biblical Commission's \work{The Jewish People and Their Sacred
Scriptures in the Christian Bible} 41 further warns that 2~Cor.~3 must be read
within Paul's positive recognition of the old dispensation's permanent value
as revelation.

\subsection*{Praise tested by adversity \cue{Grad.}}

Psalm 33 is alphabetic wisdom praise whose title connects it with David's
escape before a Philistine ruler. Basil (\work{Homilia in Psalmum 33} 1,
PG~29), Theodoret (PG~80, 1101--1109), and Augustine (\work{Enarratio in
Psalmum} 33, sermones 1--2) preserve different solutions to the title's
Abimelech/Achish problem; none converts a difficult superscription into a
secure modern date. On the appointed verses their convergence matters more:
``always'' embraces prosperity and affliction, and praise in the Lord makes
the meek fellow participants rather than spectators.

Augustine's first sermon reads an Old-Latin title addition, ``he was carried in
his own hands,'' Eucharistically: Christ entrusted the sacrament of his body
while carrying that body. The addition is absent from the Clementine title and
from the Douay printed here. It is genuine reception of Augustine's psalter,
not an extra appointed verse. Bellarmine, commenting on vv.~2--3, takes
``always'' as praise at fitting times throughout life and in the Church's
continuing worship. In the Gradual this becomes enacted: the congregation has
heard that sufficiency is from God and answers by making its boast in him.

\subsection*{An Alleluia from the night \cue{All.}}

Psalm 87 is remarkable for ending without an explicit turn to consolation.
The selected verse nevertheless contains its theological fulcrum:
\latin{Deus salútis meæ}. Day and night are not two rival spiritual states but
the entire duration of an appeal addressed to the God named as salvation.

Augustine, \work{Enarratio in Psalmum} 87.1--2, hears Christ's Passion and
reads day and night as prosperity and adversity; Cassiodorus follows that
Christological line, and Bellarmine concretizes it in Gethsemane and Good
Friday. Theodoret (PG~80, 1567--1573) supplies a real counter-reading: Israel's
calamities and, with them, fallen humanity's pains. The Alleluia does not force
one historical hypothesis. Its liturgical work is to confess salvation from
within lament immediately before eyes are blessed for seeing Christ.

Eusebius (PG~23, 1052--1056) reads Psalm 86's birth language followed by
Psalm 87's death language as an incarnational sequence and takes day-and-night
prayer as Christ's continuous oblation. His construction differs both from
Augustine's prosperity/adversity pairing and Theodoret's exilic-human horizon;
their convergence lies in sustained address to God, not in a shared account
of composition.

\subsection*{From correct reading to enacted neighbour-love \cue{Gosp.}}

Luke first blesses the disciples' eyes and ears, then stages a reader of the
law. Jesus neither mocks Torah nor answers in place of the lawyer: he asks
what is written and how it is read. The lawyer joins Deut.~6:5 and Lev.~19:18
correctly. The problem emerges in \latin{volens iustificáre seípsum}. His
question seeks a boundary around obligation; Jesus asks which of three men
\emph{became} neighbour through mercy. The command \latin{Vade, et tu fac
simíliter} prevents admiration from substituting for action.

The road descends sharply through desert terrain from Jerusalem toward
Jericho, so the narrated danger is credible; yet it remains parable, not a
report locating Jesus on that road. Priest and Levite are not proofs about
Judaism as such. The story's shock depends on the Samaritan being the socially
and religiously distrusted outsider who performs the law's demand.

\begin{receptiontable}
Origen & \work{Homiliae in Lucam} 34 & Preserves the ancient Christological
reading: humanity descends and is wounded; Christ draws near; the inn receives
the patient. He attributes the eschatological reading of the promised return
to an elder rather than presenting it as his own invention.\\
Clement of Alexandria & \work{Quis dives salvetur?} 28--29 & Makes the
neighbour one who shares in love and receives love, with Christ as the supreme
neighbour; this is moral-Christological reuse rather than continuous exegesis.\\
St Irenaeus & \work{Against Heresies} 3.17.3 & Reuses the Samaritan's oil as
an image of the Holy Spirit's healing gift; this is a bounded theological
application rather than a continuous exposition of Luke 10.\\
St Cyril of Alexandria & \work{Commentary on Luke}, Sermons 67--68 & Insists
that love of God and neighbour fulfills the law and develops Christ's
pedagogical reversal of the lawyer's boundary-making question.\\
St Ambrose & \work{Expositio Evangelii secundum Lucam} 7.69--84 & Reads the
Samaritan as Christ, the wounds as sins, oil as remission, wine as warning of
judgment, the inn as the Church, and the promised return eschatologically.\\
St Augustine & \work{Quaestiones Evangeliorum} 2.19 & Gives the famous compact
allegorical table from the fall and incarnation to the Church, charity,
apostolic ministry, and return. It does not explicitly map the remedies to
sacraments and does not claim the evangelist's historical intent.\\
St Bede & \work{In Lucae Evangelium expositio} III, Luke 10:23--37,
PL~92, 467C--470D & Holds the two senses together explicitly: every doer of
mercy is neighbour, Christ is the neighbour par excellence, and allegory may
not attenuate the moral institutions of mutual brotherhood.\\
St Albert the Great & \work{Enarrationes in Lucam}, ch.~10 on vv.~25--37,
Borgnet XXIII, pp.~55--70, esp.~66--70 & Leaves the story's literal historicity
undetermined, then insists on neighbour-as-action and enumerates the
Samaritan's deeds before developing spiritual senses.\\
St Bonaventure & \work{Commentarius in Evangelium S. Lucae} 10.44--65,
Quaracchi VII, pp.~266--272 & Develops the road, robbery, clerical duty, oil
and wine, the universality of neighbour-love, and Jesus' pedagogy before
turning to Christological typology.\\
Theophylact of Ohrid & \work{Enarratio in Lucam} 10:25--37, PG~123,
843--852 & Receives the moral question and the Christological Samaritan
together, extending the Greek exegetical tradition into the medieval period.\\
\end{receptiontable}

The later Catholic line remains practical. St John Paul II, \work{Dives in
misericordia} 3, calls the parable a revelation of merciful love through
action; Pope Francis, \work{Fratelli tutti} 56--86, reads every day as a fresh
decision whether to be passer-by or neighbour. These applications are not
substitutes for Christology. They show why Bede's both-senses rule remains
important: Christ heals, and the healed are commanded to act.

\subsection*{Moses between judgment and the people \cue{Off.}}

The Offertory is not a simple excerpt from the Clementine Vulgate. It condenses
Exod.~32:11--14, carries matter from unprinted v.~12, names Jacob where the
Vulgate has Israel, substitutes the land flowing with milk and honey for the
stars-and-land formulation, and closes in an Old-Latin shape. Augustine's
lemma in \work{Quaestiones in Heptateuchum} II.143---\latin{propitiatus est
Dominus de malitia quam dixit facere populo suo}---independently witnesses the
same construction. The text's received difference is therefore part of the
chant, not a corruption to repair from the Douay.

Augustine's questions II.143, 147, and 149--151 hold together divine constancy and real
intercession. ``Evil'' here names threatened punishment, not moral evil in
God; Moses' love for the people is itself within divine providence. In
\work{City of God} 15.25 Augustine adds the doctrinal guardrail that divine
anger is not a perturbation but just judgment. Gregory the Great,
\work{Moralia} 9.16.23, presents God's ``let me alone'' as giving Moses
boldness to supplicate; \work{Moralia} 20.5.14 reads the servant's willingness
to die for the people as self-offering. Tertullian, \work{Adversus Marcionem} 2.26, explicitly
calls Moses' deprecation a type of Christ; Chrysostom, \work{Homily 16 on
Romans}, uses Moses as the intercessor daring all for God's glory.

The Greek and medieval controls add needed limits. Theodoret,
\work{Questions on the Octateuch} 67--68, distinguishes the narrative's
language from changeable passion in God. Question 68 also preserves the
sequence in which mercy postpones and preserves without removing discipline
or judgment. First Clement 53 and Chrysostom,
\work{On the Priesthood} IV.1, receive Moses' willingness to be blotted out as
pastoral intercession. Aquinas, \work{ST} I, q.~19, a.~7, ad~1 and II--II,
q.~83, a.~2, explains that an immutable providential will includes secondary
causes and prayer. The \work{Catechism} 2574--2577 presents Moses as the
intercessor educated in fidelity and mercy. None of this revokes Israel's
covenantal gifts or competes with Christ, the unique mediator.

At the Offertory that history becomes present action: the Church approaches
the altar without claiming the congregation deserves immunity. It pleads the
promise and places a received figure of Christ's intercession beside the gifts.
Typology clarifies the liturgical act; it must not turn biblical Israel into a
mere negative foil or imply mutability in God.

\subsection*{Pardon as the offering's honor \cue{Sec.}}

The Secret's main request is God's favorable regard upon the offerings. Its
purpose clause is striking: the grammatical subject of \latin{dent} remains
the offerings, and \latin{nobis indulgéntiam largiéndo} describes the way they
give honor to God's name---by granting pardon to us. Honor is not an
achievement presented for divine approval. The 1861 English reverses the
Latin clause order and recasts the offerings' grammatical agency, although it
preserves the relation between pardon and honor.

The prayer belongs to the ancient three-oration set found together in the Old
Gelasian and Hadrianum witnesses. The Old Gelasian has a
\latin{largiaris}/\latin{largiendo} variation in the transmission; the 1962
typical reads \latin{largiéndo}. Schuster, \work{The Sacramentary} III,
pp.~128--131, makes pardon the favor to which the other benefits are ordered.
Trent, Session XXII, ch.~2, supplies the doctrinal distinction: the Mass is
the sacramental representation of Christ's one sacrifice and is truly
propitiatory; the prayer does not say that human gifts purchase forgiveness.
Justin, \work{Dialogue with Trypho} 116--117; Irenaeus, \work{Against
Heresies} IV.17.5--18.6; Cyril of Jerusalem, \work{Mystagogical Catechesis}
V.8--10; and Augustine, \work{City of God} X.5--6, 20, provide complementary
ancient controls: the Church offers Eucharistic praise and created gifts, while
Christ and the communion of his body give the offering its acceptable form.

\subsection*{Providence and sacramental reception \cue{Comm.}}

In Psalm 103 the literal sequence is creation's providence: water from God's
upper chambers makes earth fruitful, and cultivation yields bread, wine, and
oil. Theodoret (PG~80, 1693--1707) stays at this horizon: bread nourishes,
wine gladdens, and oil feeds and brightens. Bellarmine adds gratitude,
generosity, and frugality. Eusebius (PG~23, 1273--1280) begins from providence
but also develops spiritual senses: wine evokes the true Vine, bread the
mystical Logos, and oil light and healing. This is Greek Christian
spiritualization of the created triad, but it is not plainly a Eucharistic
exposition. Together these witnesses prevent an exclusively allegorical
reading of created goods.

Augustine, \work{Enarratio in Psalmum} 103, sermo 3.12--14, receives the same
gifts through Christ and his body: bread from the earth becomes Christ preached
through apostolic earth, the cup gives sober inebriation, and oil evokes the
anointing of the whole Christ. Cassiodorus, \work{Expositio Psalmorum}
103.20, is more explicit: wine consecrated as Christ's blood and royal chrism
belong to the verse's ecclesial reception.
That development is especially apt when the 1962 Missal appoints the text at
Communion. It remains reception, not proof of the psalmist's intention.

\subsection*{Participation that vivifies, expiates, and guards \cue{Postcomm.}}

The Postcommunion asks one holy participation to give three related fruits:
life, expiation, and protection. \latin{Páriter} keeps the last two together;
the prayer neither reduces Communion to interior encouragement nor treats the
sacrament as an automatic charm. The communicants ask for what the mystery
effects in rightly disposed recipients.

The Verona sacramentary prints an early form with \latin{participatio tui
sancta mysterii} (Feltoe, p.~60); the Old Gelasian and the Hadrianum transmit
the prayer within the same oration-family used here. The \work{Catechism}
1391--1395 gives a doctrinal vocabulary for its petitions: Communion deepens
union with Christ, preserves and renews the life of grace, separates from sin,
and strengthens charity. Historical transmission does not establish the
prayer's author or a single date, and the doctrinal parallel does not establish
formulary intent.

Ignatius, \work{Letter to the Ephesians} 20, receives Eucharistic communion as
medicine of immortality; Aquinas, \work{ST} III, q.~79, distinguishes its
life-giving, sin-remitting, and protective effects while preserving the need
for worthy reception and the obstacle posed by mortal sin. These are doctrinal
illuminations of the oration's petitions, not translations of
\latin{expiatiónem} or evidence for its original date.
