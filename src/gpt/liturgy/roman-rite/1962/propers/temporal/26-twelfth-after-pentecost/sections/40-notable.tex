\section*{The Propers: Notable and Quotable}

These four afterlives do more than repeat a biblical phrase: each moves it into
a new register and changes what the wording is made to do.

\begin{afterlife}{The neighbour becomes a legal duty --- \work{Donoghue v Stevenson} (1932)}
\textbf{Appointed wording.} The lawyer asks, ``And who is my neighbour?''
(Luke 10:29).

\textbf{Later use and locus.} Lord Atkin's speech in \work{Donoghue v
Stevenson}, [1932] A.C.~562 at 580, moves from the command to love one's
neighbour to a restricted legal duty of care and asks: ``Who then, in law, is
my neighbour?''

\textbf{The turn and its limit.} Jesus refuses the lawyer's attempt to delimit
a class and asks who became neighbour by mercy. Tort law must do the thing the
parable resists: specify foreseeability and legal proximity. Atkin turns a
self-justifying question into a rule for liability; the civil rule is
not presented as the Gospel's whole moral demand.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{The Jerusalem--Jericho road becomes an experiment --- Darley and Batson (1973)}
\textbf{Appointed wording.} A man goes ``from Jerusalem to Jericho''; priest
and Levite see him and pass by (Luke 10:30--32).

\textbf{Later use and locus.} John M. Darley and C. Daniel Batson, ``From
Jerusalem to Jericho: A Study of Situational and Dispositional Variables in
Helping Behavior,'' \work{Journal of Personality and Social Psychology} 27.1
(1973), 100--108, sent seminarians past a slumped confederate while varying
hurry and talk topic. The discussion reports that some ``literally stepped
over the victim'' while going to speak on the parable (p.~107).

\textbf{The turn and its limit.} A narrative about seeing and passing becomes
an experimental protocol. The assigned talk topic was not a significant
predictor of helping, while time pressure had a marked effect. The staged
emergency and small seminary sample do
not exhaust mercy or prove the motives of the parable's priest and Levite.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{The killing letter exposes a marriage contract --- Thomas Hardy (1896)}
\textbf{Appointed wording.} ``For the letter killeth: but the spirit
quickeneth'' (2~Cor.~3:6).

\textbf{Later use and locus.} Thomas Hardy places 2~Cor.~3:6 on the title page
of the 1896 book edition of \work{Jude the Obscure}. In Part VI, ch.~VIII
(ch.~50 in the linked edition), Jude tells Sue inside a church that their
return to socially sanctioned marriages is acting by the letter, and repeats
``the letter killeth!''

\textbf{The turn and its limit.} Paul's contrast between covenant ministries
becomes an indictment of civil and ecclesial marriage convention; the epigraph
also makes the phrase a key for the novel's conflict between legal form and
lived attachment. Jude is a conflicted fictional speaker, not Hardy's
unqualified doctrinal voice, and the novel's use is not Paul's exegetical
meaning.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Paul becomes a canon of statutory construction --- Justice Frankfurter (1950)}
\textbf{Appointed wording.} ``The letter killeth'' (2~Cor.~3:6).

\textbf{Later use and locus.} Justice Felix Frankfurter's dissent in
\work{United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy}, 338 U.S.~537, 548, 550
(1950), applies the phrase to Acts of Congress and invokes it while construing
the War Brides Act in the case of an excluded soldier's wife.

\textbf{The turn and its limit.} A Pauline claim about letter and Spirit is
naturalized as an interpretive maxim: statutory purpose should control a harsh
literal application. The dissent does not cite the verse and supplies no
covenant exegesis. Its force depends on an English idiom descended from Paul,
not on identity between apostolic ministry and judicial method.
\end{afterlife}
