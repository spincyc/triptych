\subsection*{The covenant plea finds its answer in the gaze of the promised Seed\enspace
  \properrefs{Int., Ep., Grad., Gosp.}}

Psalm~73 begins from a sanctuary laid waste and a poor people who appear to
have been abandoned. Its prayer is nevertheless full of theological
intelligence: \latin{Réspice, Dómine, in testaméntum tuum}. Israel does not
first exhibit an achievement but asks God to manifest the fidelity already
pledged. Bellarmine hears the covenant with the fathers; Augustine and
Cassiodorus hear Jeremiah's new covenant and its heavenly inheritance;
Eusebius names the new covenant through Christ and the poor intercessors as
apostles; St~Anthony of Padua contemplates the testament ratified in
Christ's Blood. Their readings name different moments within one economy.
Promise originates in God, passes through Israel's history, and reaches its
christological fulfilment without ceasing to be God's fidelity.

Galatians makes that fulfilment personal: \latin{Et sémini tuo, qui est
Christus}. Jerome, Theodoret, and Aquinas keep Christ himself at the center of
the singular Seed; Augustine joins to the Head those who are gathered into the
one faith. Paul supplies the canonical completion after the lesson: those
baptized into Christ are one in him and therefore Abraham's seed and heirs
(Gal.~3:27--29). The Introit's poor can plead covenant because incorporation
into the Seed places them within the promised inheritance. Their poverty is
not absence of relation but dependence upon a relation God created.

The Gradual repeats \latin{Réspice} after the Epistle and adds the reproach of
God's servants from Psalm~88. That psalm laments the apparent defeat of the
Davidic covenant and immediately names reproach against the footsteps of the
Anointed. Augustine and Cassiodorus accordingly hear the Church bearing
reproach because she bears Christ's name. The repeated chant does not merely
ask for a second look. It places suffering servants inside the cause of the
Seed in whom promise is secure.

Luke then answers the imperative by a verb of sight: \latin{quos ut vidit}.
Ten sufferers stand afar and embody the Psalm's poor; their common cry,
\latin{Iesu praecéptor, miserére nostri}, embodies the Roman Psalter's
\latin{voces quaeréntium te}. Christ's gaze is covenant mercy in act. He does
not set aside Israel's priestly law; he commands the ten to show themselves and
cleanses them while they go. Tertullian therefore sees the one Creator's
economy: Christ honors the Law, manifests himself as true Priest and temple,
and gives what the Law can identify but cannot vivify.

The sequence makes the Gradual's \latin{causam tuam} christological without
making it aggressive. God's cause is vindicated when the Anointed sees the
poor, heals, and draws a stranger into worship. Cassiodorus's gloss is exact:
truth judges its cause by converting those in error until they confess what
they denied. Ruined sanctuary opens toward Christ's Body; reproach borne by
servants joins the Anointed's suffering; the covenant plea is answered by an
incarnate gaze that restores communion.

\subsection*{The good Law names and orders the road; grace gives life and reconciliation\enspace
  \properrefs{Coll., Ep., Gosp., Sec.}}

Paul refuses the false choice between Law and promise: \latin{Absit}. The Law
is God's holy gift to Israel, spiritually good, enduring in moral instruction,
providential in discipline, and rich in figures of Christ (CCC~1961--1964).
Its limitation is not wickedness but incapacity: external precept cannot of
itself communicate the Spirit who justifies and gives life. Chrysostom calls it
a bridle and a probe; Theodoret a guardian of the people from whom the Seed
would arise; Augustine the command that exposes pride and sends the humbled
sinner to grace; Aquinas adds figurative preparation. The Law restrains,
reveals, protects, and promises in figure. Christ heals and vivifies.

The Collect makes this Catholic relation interior. \latin{Da} asks God to give
the increase of faith, hope, and charity; \latin{fac nos amáre} asks him to
cause a love that truly becomes the petitioners' voluntary act. God promises
the end and commands the road, but he also gives the virtues by which the end
is believed, desired, and loved. Trent therefore cites the virtue-increase
petition when teaching growth in justification already received. Grace neither
leaves the will inert nor waits for autonomous achievement. It moves the person
to an obedient act that is genuinely his own.

The ten enact that grammar. Before cleansing is visible, they hear and obey;
only \latin{dum irent} are they cleansed. Faith receives Christ's word, hope
walks, and charity returns to the Giver. The journey does not buy the cure but
is real obedience. Tertullian calls it obedient faith; Cyril says Jesus sends
healing with those sent to the priests. Bonaventure finds all three virtues:
faith begins salvation, hope advances it, charity perfects it.

The tradition's penitential reading takes the same order into the sacramental
life. Augustine sees the priestly showing as a figure of ecclesial ministry;
Bruno says God cleanses while priests judge; St~Anthony, Albert, Bonaventure,
and Denis unfold contrition, confession, satisfaction, and the keys. The
governing axiom, \latin{Deus enim solus emundat peccata}, affirms Christ as
principal forgiver. It does not make absolution a bare medical certificate.
Christ has entrusted power to his Church, and the ordained priest acts as
Christ's minister, truly absolving and reconciling in a judicial act (Trent,
Session~XIV, ch.~6 and can.~9; CCC~1441--1449, 1461). The healed person is restored not to
solitary wellness but to visible communion.

The Secret carries that order to the altar. Scripture has enclosed all under
sin so that promise may be given to believers; therefore the Church places
both people and gifts beneath \latin{propitiáre}. Their offering does not
manufacture pardon from outside Christ. It enters the true and proper
propitiatory sacrifice in which the same Christ who offered himself once on
Calvary is offered sacramentally and unbloodily, and the fruits of his Passion
are applied for pardon and grace (Trent, Session~XXII, ch.~2; CCC~1365--1367).
Law discloses need, grace makes obedience living, Penance reconciles, and the
Eucharistic sacrifice brings sinners beneath the Mediator's oblation.

\subsection*{The one God holds every generation and every hour in the Mediator's hand\enspace
  \properrefs{Ep., All., Off.}}

Ancient commentators differ over Paul's \latin{in manu mediatóris}. Theodoret
and Theodore name Moses, historically standing between God and the people;
Jerome and Chrysostom name Christ, divine giver of the Law; Augustine specifies
Christ in the humanity through which he stands between God and man;
Ambrosiaster contemplates the reconciler. Aquinas holds the levels together:
the Law is in Christ's power, and Moses signifies him. Paul then sets every
ministry beneath \latin{Deus autem unus est}. The God of promise, Law,
Incarnation, and sacrament is one.

The Alleluia gives Moses' ministry a doxological place. Psalm~89's canonical
title calls it his prayer, and its Roman text sings the Lord as refuge
\latin{a generatióne et progénie}. Jerome and Lyra receive Mosaic attribution;
Augustine treats Moses as minister of the old covenant and prophet of the new.
Eusebius populates the beneficiaries with Enoch, Noah, Abraham, the patriarchs,
and Moses, then separately unfolds the refuge as shelter, harbor, wall, and
defense. Theodoret follows remembered divine protection through Egypt,
wilderness, judges, Samuel, and David. Christian hope begins by remembering
benefits.

The allegorical sense makes that refuge Christological. Augustine hears both
every successive age and the two covenant-generations joined by Christ.
Cassiodorus makes the universal Church speak in the plural \latin{nobis};
Arnobius Junior confesses the pre-existent Son and Creator reigning before
mountains, earth, and world. The eternal Word does
not merely watch mortal generations pass. He enters a human generation,
receives them into his Body, and gives participation in abiding life.

The Offertory turns communal memory into personal surrender:
\latin{Tu es Deus meus; in mánibus tuis témpora mea}. The Roman-Psalter
\latin{tempora} differs from the Gallican \latin{sortes}; Theodoret already
knew both, and Cassiodorus comments on the chant's exact reading. Literally,
David moves from threat to trust before deliverance. Theodoret locates the
psalm under Absalom and gives the times their range: joy and sorrow, wealth and
poverty, subjection and rule, peace and conflict are not sovereign over the
servant of God.

The whole psalm supplies the paschal depth: its earlier \latin{In manus tuas
commendo spiritum meum} becomes Christ's word from the Cross. Augustine hears
Head and members speaking as one; Cassiodorus sees the times of Christ's
assumed humanity and the hour held in the Father's power. At the Offertory the
Church places bread and wine upon the altar while placing labor, suffering,
uncertainty, and death within Christ's self-offering. Providence is not
fatalism: the ten still walk, the Samaritan returns, the people offer, and
communicants advance. Trust frees those acts from chance and fear by locating
their final sovereignty in the Father.

The Psalm pair consequently frames Christian time. Passing generations find
refuge in the eternal Word; each person's changing seasons enter the Father's
hand with the crucified Christ. The Postcommunion will carry this entrusted
time toward an eternal term: not an escape from creaturely action, but work
established and perfected in glory.

\subsection*{The cry of need becomes great-voiced thanksgiving, sacrifice, and Communion\enspace
  \properrefs{Int., Grad., Gosp., Off., Sec., Comm.}}

The Roman Introit asks God not to forget \latin{voces quaeréntium te}. The ten
then lift one voice from afar. After the cure, one returns \latin{magna voce}
glorifying God. The relation is more than verbal elegance. Prayer's voice moves
from need to praise because grace has been recognized. Augustine's moral sense
is unyielding: do not claim amendment as self-produced lest one be counted
among the unthankful nine. St~Anthony calls the Samaritan \latin{custos}, the
keeper who guards a gift by returning it to God in praise.

Christ's question, ``Where are the nine?'' remains a real rebuke of
ingratitude. All ten receive common bodily cleansing and all ten obey; the one
does not retroactively buy what the others received freely. Yet gratitude and
worship are owed. Luke binds glorifying God to prostration and thanksgiving at
Jesus' feet, and the tradition rightly hears an implicit confession of divine
dignity. Bede distinguishes the benefit received by all from the saving word
addressed to faith in operation. The mature end of gift is communion with the
Giver.

The traditional one-and-nine reading deepens the same moral and ecclesial
movement. Ten can figure humanity under the Decalogue; nine incompletion; one
the unity in which grace is kept. The stranger can stand for the nations
brought into Abraham's blessing. Theophylact gives the right spiritual law:
foreign origin excludes no one and holy ancestry permits no pride. The figure
does not require an unsupported claim that all nine are Jews. Rather, it
confesses that through Israel's promise and Messiah the nations learn to
glorify Israel's God, while Israel's gifts and calling remain irrevocable.

The Offertory gives thankful return material form. The worshipper confesses
\latin{Tu es Deus meus} and entrusts all times as gifts are brought forward.
The Secret then repeats \latin{propitiáre} over people and offerings. Tertullian
supplies the Gospel theology: the Samaritan offers the true oblation of
thanksgiving before Christ, true Priest and temple. The gift cannot repay the
cure; it becomes a sacrifice of praise within the one propitiatory oblation
that first grants pardon.

Communion brings this return to its summit: \latin{Panem de caelo dedísti nobis,
Dómine}. Wisdom remembers the manna given to Israel; the antiphon addresses the
same Giver in the first person. The Christian \latin{nobis} does not abolish
the biblical ``them.'' It draws the worshipper into the economy in which
Israel's historical bread canonically figures Christ's Eucharistic gift. The
Samaritan is no longer held at a distance: the stranger's thanksgiving becomes
the Church's plural Eucharist.

The tradition follows a continuous ascent. Origen sees the one Logos feeding
souls according to capacity. Gregory of Nyssa joins the unsown bread to the
Virgin-born Word; Gregory the Great sees divine speech condescending to hearers.
Rabanus moves directly from Wisdom through John~6 to Christ's flesh and blood.
Albert names the bread heavenly, life-giving, unitive, viaticum, and a sign of
future fruition. Aquinas makes manna the principal figure of the Eucharist's
every-refreshing effect. Bonaventure holds literal manna and altar mystery in
order: one objective gift bears fruit as grace enlarges devotion. St~John
Paul~II names the resulting sacrament food of pilgrims and a foretaste of
messianic fullness.

Thus the voices of the proper trace one spiritual conversion. The poor cry;
the healed man praises; the offerer confesses; the Church gives thanks and
receives. Petition has not been left behind, for the Secret still asks pardon
and the Postcommunion still asks progress. But the voice that once named lack
has been enlarged into Eucharistic worship.

\subsection*{The virtues and the heavenly bread increase the pilgrim toward redemption\enspace
  \properrefs{Coll., All., Gosp., Comm., Postcomm.}}

\latin{Augméntum} appears at both ends of the prayer cycle. First the Church
asks for the increase of faith, hope, and charity. After Communion she asks
that the recipients may advance \latin{ad redemptiónis aetérnae augméntum}.
The repetition is not accidental to the prayer's grammar, though no claim is
made that every element was composed as one plan. It identifies the same
pilgrim at two points: disposed by infused virtues and nourished by sacrament.

Aquinas explains the first increase as the movement of infused principles
toward supernatural beatitude. Faith receives the revealed end, hope tends
toward it through divine aid, charity unites the will to it. Charity can grow
throughout the wayfaring state because the affections can always draw nearer
to God. The Gospel makes that motion concrete: faith trusts Christ's command,
hope goes before sight, charity recognizes the Giver and returns. The saving
word does not close the road. Jesus says \latin{Surge, vade}.

The Eucharist supplies the pilgrim food for that sending. Aquinas compares its
effect to food that sustains, increases, repairs, and delights spiritual life;
actual reception augments grace and perfects life through union with God. Yet
the sacrament does not immediately introduce the receiver into unveiled glory.
It gives the power to arrive and is therefore viaticum (\emph{ST} III,
q.~79, aa.~1--2). The \emph{Roman Catechism} develops the same effects as
strength, charity, resistance to temptation, and relish for heavenly things.

The Postcommunion's subject must be kept: \latin{proficiámus}, ``may we
advance.'' Christ's objective redemption is complete; the sacrament is not
deficient. Increase belongs to its fruit in those still being conformed to
Christ. Grace's priority does not replace the communicant's movement; it makes
that movement possible and real. The same grammar governed the Collect:
\latin{da}, \latin{fac nos amáre}, \latin{mereámur ássequi}.

The Alleluia now supplies the anagogical horizon. Mortal generations pass;
their years are numbered; works seem fragile. Yet the Lord is refuge and the
Word establishes what grace begins. Bede, Bonaventure, Albert, and Denis
receive \latin{surge, vade} as rising from sin into strong works and advancing
toward neighbor, God, and the heavenly homeland. St~Anthony reads
\latin{surge} as unity rising to upright works and \latin{vade} as humility's
keeper going securely; his surrounding exposition also treats return,
prostration, thanksgiving, and saving faith.
Communion is both
possession and promise: Christ is truly given now, and the gift draws the
receiver toward unveiled communion.

The integrated movement can therefore be stated without reducing the proper to
a scheme. Covenant gives the ground; the Seed gives the inheritance; the Law
names and orders need; grace gives living obedience; mercy heals; gratitude
returns; sacrifice places people and gifts within Christ's oblation; sacrament
feeds the reconciled. Faith came crying, hope walked, charity fell at Jesus'
feet. The Church rises from Communion and goes toward the eternal refuge where
thanksgiving is perpetual and redemption's fruit admits no loss.
