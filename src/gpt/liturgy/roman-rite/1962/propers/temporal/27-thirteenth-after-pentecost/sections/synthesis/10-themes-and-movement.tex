\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\label{triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

\begin{massbox}{Governing thesis: God keeps covenant by giving in Christ, and the fitting human answer is obedient faith returned as thanksgiving and sustained as growth}
The Mass begins with a people whose plea rests on God's covenant. It asks for
the increase of the theological virtues, hears that inheritance is promise and
gift in Christ, watches mercy meet sufferers who obey before they see, and
receives bread from heaven while asking to advance. Grace calls forth love,
movement, thanksgiving, offering, and perseverance.
\end{massbox}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0.1em]
\item \textbf{Promise precedes achievement:} covenant and inheritance are God's acts before they become the worshipper's hope.
\item \textbf{Faith moves under a word:} the ten go before they see; cleansing is given to all, but Luke follows one into thanksgiving and saving faith.
\item \textbf{Gift returns as thanksgiving:} the Samaritan returns, the Secret places people and gifts under mercy, and Wisdom's manna becomes bread given ``to us.''
\item \textbf{Reception opens further growth:} the virtues' increase at the beginning becomes progress toward eternal redemption at the end.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\emph{How to read the movement.} These are converging themes, not a
claim that a compiler designed a four-stage argument. A witness may explain one
text, an older pairing, or a later reception; the detailed commentary marks the
difference.

\subsection*{Covenant, promise, and time}

Psalm~73 speaks from catastrophe: the poor can ask God to remember his flock
and look upon his covenant even when no human achievement carries their case.
Cassiodorus makes that rhetoric explicit. Augustine and the medieval Gloss
hear Jeremiah's new covenant; Bellarmine hears the covenant with the fathers.
The chant does not force their readings into one. Galatians then gives a
defined covenantal field: the promise to Abraham and his seed, Christ, is not
annulled by the later law. The law is good and provisional---exposing and
restraining transgression---but cannot itself impart life. It serves promise;
it does not compete with another god.

The Collect therefore asks for real growth without turning promise into an
independent wage. Trent, Session~VI, chapter~10, excerpts its petition for the
increase of faith, hope, and charity; chapter~16 and canon~32 place Christian
merit wholly within Christ's preceding grace. The Alleluia widens the horizon
to God's refuge ``from generation and progeny,'' and the Offertory entrusts
\latin{témpora mea}---the worshipper's changing times---to God's hands. This
does not excuse passivity; it locates action, suffering, and hope within a
faithfulness larger than one lifetime.

\subsection*{Obedience, cleansing, and thankful faith}

Luke's sequence must remain uneven. All ten call, obey Jesus' command to show
themselves to the priests, and are cleansed while going. One Samaritan sees,
returns, glorifies God, falls at Jesus' feet, and gives thanks; he alone hears
the saving pronouncement. Tertullian emphasizes obedient movement and thanks
offered to Christ; Augustine refuses self-credit; Bede distinguishes common
cleansing from the faith whose living response receives salvation. The
priests certify and restore social and cultic standing; they do not cause the
healing.

The nine are not identified as Jews, and Luke does not state their fate.
Ancient one-Gentile/nine-Jews typologies belong to reception history, not to
the narrative's asserted facts. \emph{Nostra aetate}~4 excludes any account of
the Jewish people as rejected or accursed. Likewise, biblical ``leprosy'' is
not a warrant to diagnose or stigmatize people with Hansen's disease, chronic
illness, disability, visible difference, or contested belief. Moral readings
must turn the examiner toward the self, never make another's body a symbol on
demand.

\subsection*{Bread received, response offered, growth continued}

The Communion adapts Wisdom's third-person manna narrative into direct prayer:
\latin{Panem de caelo dedísti nobis, Dómine}. Christian reception had several
registers. Rabanus and Bonaventure develop Eucharistic readings; John
Paul~II cites Wisdom~16:20--21 for the Eucharist as pilgrim food and messianic
foretaste. Gregory the Great, directly quoting the same sweetness clause,
instead sees divine speech accommodating itself to hearers. His
non-Eucharistic reading disciplines the others: spiritual sweetness need not
mean a sensible feeling, and later sacramental typology does not erase the
verse's original wilderness setting.

The Secret places people and gifts beneath God's merciful
\latin{propitiáre}; thanksgiving and offering answer a gift they cannot buy.
The Postcommunion then repeats the Collect's \latin{augméntum}: those who have
received the heavenly sacraments still ask that \emph{we} may advance toward
eternal redemption. Sacramental grace is not defective, but its recipient
remains a pilgrim. The proper ends neither in self-salvation nor in static
possession, but in grace-enabled progress.

\begin{threestable}{Movement}{Positive form}{Controlling limit}
\textbf{Lack to promise} & The poor plead covenant; inheritance remains gift in Christ. & The law is not evil, Israel is not erased, and merit remains grace-enabled.\\
\textbf{Word to movement} & Faith obeys before sight and discovers mercy on the way. & Obedience neither purchases healing nor blames the sick.\\
\textbf{Gift to thanks} & Cleansing returns as praise, offering, and Eucharistic reception. & Gratitude is response, not price; typology cannot become supersessionism.\\
\textbf{Reception to growth} & Sacrament and virtues carry the pilgrim toward redemption. & Growth is neither self-salvation nor sacramental deficiency.\\
\end{threestable}
