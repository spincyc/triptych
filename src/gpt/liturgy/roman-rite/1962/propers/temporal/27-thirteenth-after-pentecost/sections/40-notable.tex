\section*{The Propers: Notable and Quotable}

\begin{studyframe}
These four cases passed a strict afterlife audit: the appointed wording, or a
clearly identified biblical form behind it, enters a later non-exegetical
register with a documentable change of function. Straight sermons, devotional
reuse, musical settings, and bare mottoes were excluded. Dependence on the
Missal is claimed only where it can be shown; textual convergence is named as
such.
\end{studyframe}

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\subsection*{A sung plea becomes a bull, a satire, and a bonfire\enspace\properrefs{Int., Grad.}}

Leo~X opened the bull \emph{Exsurge Domine}, dated 15 June 1520, with the
psalmic cry \latin{Exsurge, Domine, et iudica causam tuam}. The lament asking
God to defend his own cause had become the juridical opening of proceedings
against Martin Luther. Ulrich von Hutten then republished the bull with mocking
annotations and sent the derisive edition to Luther; Luther publicly burned
the bull with books of canon law in December 1520. One phrase therefore passes
through prayer, papal juridical act, humanist satire, and symbolic destruction.

\textbf{Control.} The bull quotes the psalmic/liturgical form, not this Mass as
a source. Its continuation follows the psalm rather than the Gradual's
inter-psalm ending. The incident is described, not recruited for either side
of a confessional polemic.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\subsection*{Bread from heaven in Joyce's compromised Benediction\enspace\properrefs{Comm.}}

In the ``Nausicaa'' episode of James Joyce's \emph{Ulysses}, the Benediction
versicle \latin{Panem de coelo praestitisti eis} is read amid distracted talk,
commercial detail, and Bloom's voyeuristic attention. The language of bread
bearing every delight becomes an ironized liturgical soundtrack to fractured
and profane appetites. Joyce depends on the Benediction form heard after
\emph{Tantum ergo}, not on the Communion antiphon's adapted
\latin{dedísti nobis}.

\textbf{Control.} The case concerns the wider liturgical life of Wisdom~16:20.
The different pronoun and verb identify the route of dependence and prevent a
false claim that Joyce quotes this Sunday's proper.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\subsection*{A Roman-Psalter phrase becomes a Nestroy family joke\enspace\properrefs{All.}}

The official program of the 32nd International Nestroy Conversations
(Schwechat, 1--5 July 2006) announced Otmar Nestroy's forum contribution
\emph{Familiäres oder: A generatione et progenie aus dem Hause Nestroy}.
The rare Latin form sung in the Alleluia, followed by the German ``from the
house of Nestroy,'' turns a scriptural span of generations into a bilingual
genealogical joke at a scholarly family gathering.

\textbf{Control.} The surviving program supplies title, speaker, duration,
and occasion but no abstract. No argument or intention beyond the title's
plain wordplay is inferred, and the title alone is reproduced under an
unclear modern rights status.

\subsection*{Werfel's homeless times seek a Father's hand\enspace\properrefs{Off.}}

Franz Werfel titled a 1935 poem \latin{Tempora mea in manibus tuis}. The title
reorders the same four words as the Offertory. Friedrich Buchmayr situates the
poem in May 1935 under the pressure of homelessness: the speaker loosens claims
of possession by kin, nation, and even self, and seeks the Father's hand.
The hunted psalmist's trust thus becomes the title and governing address of a
modern Jewish writer deeply engaged with Catholic Christianity while
confronting dislocation.

\textbf{Control.} The biblical allusion is certain; dependence on this Missal
proper is not. Werfel died in 1945 and the poem remains jurisdiction-sensitive,
so only its Latin title is given and the modern poem is paraphrased.
