\subsection*{Introit: the covenant is stronger than the petitioners' case\enspace\properrefs{Int.}}

The antiphon is a liturgical construction from Psalm~73 rather than a continuous
biblical verse. It begins with v.~20, returns to the poor of v.~19, inserts
v.~22 although the Missal's margin does not name it, and closes by changing
v.~23's ``enemies'' into those ``seeking thee.'' It also sings
\latin{derelínquas} where the Clementine has \latin{obliviscáris}. The
appended psalm verse, by contrast, is the lament's first question exactly:
why has God cast off the sheep of his pasture? The chant therefore places a
carefully intensified plea over a biblical catastrophe.

The historical horizon is contested in Christian reception. Augustine sees
the Roman standards of AD~70 and treats the ruin as accomplished history;
Cassiodorus and Theodoret read the same Roman destruction as prophecy. Jerome
briefly points instead to Babylon. Bellarmine argues for the persecution of
Antiochus~IV and even uses the Church's liturgical singing as an argument
against assigning the psalm to an unbelieving voice. Modern Catholic
orientation associates the lament especially with the destruction of the
Jerusalem temple in 587~BC. These judgments cannot all describe the first
historical referent. Their disagreement is more instructive than a false
consensus: the chant receives Israel's lament as the Church's prayer without
settling the psalm's compositional crisis.

The covenant clause likewise admits distinct readings. Augustine asks God to
fulfil the new covenant of Jeremiah~31 and the promised kingdom; Cassiodorus
follows the same line and gives its spiritual logic extraordinary precision:
when the people's merits cannot be recalled, the Lord's promise can. The
\emph{Glossa ordinaria} makes that patristic reading the medieval schoolroom
gloss. Bellarmine, however, hears the older covenant with the fathers and the
land given to them. Guéranger, citing Rupert of Deutz, and Schuster connect
the appeal more generally with the Abrahamic promise. The Sunday does not
require the old and new covenants to be rivals. Galatians will insist that the
later law does not annul the earlier promise, while Christian reception sees
that promise fulfilled in Christ.

At v.~22, textual history changes the spiritual accent. Augustine's lemma
asks God to judge ``my cause,'' and he answers by the risen Christ who will
judge. Cassiodorus has the chant's ``thy cause'' and says God judges his own
cause by converting those in error so that they confess what they denied.
Theodoret expressly defends ``thy'' rather than ``my''; Bellarmine notes
Augustine's variant and rejects it. Thus \latin{causam tuam} need not become a
warrant for religious combat. Its strongest received reading is missionary
and penitential: God's truth is vindicated when error yields to conversion.

The poor and their voices keep the plea ecclesial. Augustine interprets
poverty as abandonment of self-made justice for grace; Cassiodorus names the
faithful remnant and the riches God gives to those who become poor in spirit.
Neither reading permits contempt for materially poor persons. Nor may the
psalm's enemy language be assigned to Jews, political opponents, sick persons,
or any contemporary group. The worshipper enters the desolated community's
question and asks God to remember, hear, judge, and restore.

\subsection*{Collect: grace increases what grace has begun\enspace\properrefs{Coll.}}

The prayer has two linked petitions. First, God is asked to give an increase
of faith, hope, and charity. Second, in order that the petitioners may deserve
to attain what God promises, he is asked to make them love what he commands.
The syntax refuses to separate interior virtue from concrete obedience, or
promise from a transformed will. Even love of the command is requested as
divine action: \latin{fac nos amáre}.

Its transmission is older than its current Sunday. The Verona sacramentary
has the Collect in a July votive series without Sunday assignment. The Old
Gelasian prints it among unnumbered Sunday Masses and again in prayers for
charity. The Gregorian supplement places the prayer set on a differently
numbered Sunday. Those witnesses establish movement through Roman and
Frankish books, not authorship by Leo, Gelasius, Gregory, or Alcuin. The
Sunday number drift is evidence against imagining that every surviving
pairing arose at once.

Trent gives the Collect unusual doctrinal reception. Session~VI, chapter~10,
quotes its request for growth in faith, hope, and charity while teaching the
increase of justification already received. This is not a claim that natural
effort earns first grace. Chapter~16 grounds the merit of the justified in
Christ's grace, and canon~32 protects both that divine source and the genuine
meritorious character of works performed in grace. The prayer is thus fully
Catholic in both directions: the virtues really grow, love and obedience
really matter, and every good movement remains gift.

Aquinas supplies a compatible account without directly quoting this Collect
at the checked loci. Charity on pilgrimage can increase as a person draws
nearer to God; acts of charity dispose toward further increase, and eternal
life is the end toward which grace moves. Guéranger receives the Collect as
the Church's petition for the three foundational virtues and devotedness to
the commandments. The repeated \latin{augméntum} in the Postcommunion will
show that this prayer has no isolated endpoint before glory.

\subsection*{Epistle: one seed, a prior promise, and a law that cannot give life\enspace\properrefs{Ep.}}

Paul's argument begins with Abraham's ``seed'' in the singular and identifies
that seed as Christ. Jerome makes an exhaustive philological claim that
Scripture never uses a plural ``seeds'' in this promise; Theodoret distinguishes
Christ as seed in the proper sense from Abraham's descendants by nature.
Augustine widens the singular christologically to Christ with the Christians
who imitate Abraham's faith: the unity is the unity of faith, not the
exclusion of Christ's members. Aquinas holds the center: Christ alone is the
one in whom all nations can be blessed.

\latin{Testaméntum} carries the argument between covenant and testamentary
inheritance. Jerome observes that Hebrew \emph{berith} means pact or covenant,
while Aquinas explains why Paul can reason from a human will: the divine
promise orders an inheritance and, once confirmed, cannot be displaced by a
later disposition. The 430 years make posteriority visible. Jerome leaves the
chronology unresolved; Aquinas counts from the promise in Genesis~12 to the
Exodus and distinguishes the shorter count from Isaac. The theological point
does not depend on adjudicating every ancient chronology: later law cannot
turn prior gift into no gift.

Paul then asks the difficult question himself: why the law? Chrysostom calls
it a bridle and a probe of wounds that makes the sick seek a physician.
Augustine says it was given to a proud people so that transgression would
humble them into seeking grace. Theodoret describes its care for the people
from whom the seed would come. Aquinas gathers four ends: restraint,
discipline, exposure of weakness, and figurative instruction toward future
grace. The law is therefore neither a rival god's work nor a useless mistake.
Paul says explicitly that it is not against God's promises. It lacks the
power to give life, but it serves the promise by exposing the need that only
gift can answer.

The mediator generated a preserved disagreement. Jerome and Chrysostom name
Christ, the giver of the law; Augustine insists that Christ mediates according
to his humanity; Ambrosiaster presents Christ as arbiter reconciling two
peoples. Marius Victorinus likewise names Christ, though his comments on
vv.~11--19 are lost. Theodoret names Moses. Aquinas bridges the traditions:
the law is in Christ's power, and Moses signifies the Mediator. This is a case
where a patristic matrix is more Catholic than a flattened answer, because it
holds the historical ministry of Moses and the saving mediation of Christ in
their proper relation.

The lesson ends at v.~22, before the famous pedagogue image. Scripture has
``concluded all under sin'' so that promise may be given to believers. Jerome
compares the closure to judicial custody awaiting pardon; Chrysostom and
Theodoret interpret it as conviction; Aquinas calls knowledge of disease and
impotence an inducement to seek Christ the physician. Universal conviction is
ordered to universal need for grace, not to contempt for Israel. The appointed
boundary leaves the hearer inside that need and directs attention to the
covenant plea sung again in the Gradual.

\subsection*{Gradual: God's own cause remembered between promise and mercy\enspace\properrefs{Grad.}}

The Gradual repeats the Introit's covenant plea but reverts from one of its
psalter differences: here the poor are not to be \emph{forgotten}, the
Clementine's verb, rather than not to be forsaken. Its final clause,
\latin{memor esto oppróbrii servórum tuórum}, is not the Vulgate's
Psalm~73:22b; it corresponds to Psalm~88:51 even though the Missal continues
to cite Psalm~73. Fidelity to the liturgical source requires retaining both
the received wording and the printed reference without silently rewriting
one to match the other.

Guéranger explicitly joins this repeated \latin{testaméntum} to the Epistle,
reporting Rupert's interpretation of the ancient people's cry for fulfilment
of the promise made to Abraham. The pairing is therefore documented
liturgical reception, not merely a modern thematic guess. Yet Rupert's
precise older Sunday arrangement was not recovered here, and his witness
cannot establish why the 1962 compiler retained the pair.

The repeated \latin{causam tuam} also deserves discipline. Leo~X used the
same psalmic cry to open \emph{Exsurge Domine}; the phrase consequently entered
one of the defining conflicts of Western Christianity. In the Mass it remains
a prayer addressed to God's judgment, not a transfer of judgment to the
assembly. Cassiodorus's conversion-centered gloss is the safer spiritual
measure: divine truth prevails by bringing persons to know and confess it.

\subsection*{Alleluia: refuge before the journey\enspace\properrefs{All.}}

The Alleluia's Vulgate number is Psalm~89; modern Bibles number it 90. Its
title assigns the prayer to Moses, but Augustine and Bellarmine both deny that
the title establishes Mosaic composition. Augustine still treats Moses as
minister of the old covenant and prophet of the new, while Cassiodorus says
the name directs the reader to the two dispensations the psalm joins. A title
can govern canonical reception without functioning as modern authorship
metadata.

The chant's \latin{a generatióne et progénie} is Cassiodorus's lemma exactly.
He takes the first noun from the beginning of the human race and the second
through its successive renewal to the end. Augustine and Jerome instead read
two generations as old and new covenantal peoples; Augustine also offers the
plain sense of every generation. The refuge is therefore both diachronic and
christological: the God who sheltered Moses' people does not cease to be the
refuge of those brought into Christ's covenant.

Jerome provides a direct cross-chant relation. Explaining why refuge is
needed, he quotes the threatened confessing soul of Psalm~73:19, the verse
from which the Introit and Gradual take the poor. Augustine deepens the
temporal contrast: immutable divine eternity becomes refuge from the
mutability of time. That prepares the Offertory's surrender of ``my times''
without dissolving the literal distress of the Gospel's travelers.

\subsection*{Gospel: mercy on the way and thanksgiving at Jesus' feet\enspace\properrefs{Gosp.}}

Luke locates the encounter on Jesus' journey toward Jerusalem, in the
Samaria--Galilee region, at an unnamed village. The phrase does not permit an
exact route reconstruction, village identification, month, or Passion year.
The ten keep their distance in accordance with the social and ritual facts of
their condition; they make a common appeal to Jesus as Master. Jesus neither
touches them nor pronounces cleansing at once. He commands the action that
would allow priests to examine restoration, and the cleansing occurs on the
way.

Tertullian, in an anti-Marcionite argument, treats their going as obedience
to Christ's word, the priests as witnesses, and the Samaritan's thanksgiving
as an offering rendered to Christ the true priest and temple. Cyril says
Jesus sends healing with those sent to the priests. Augustine reads the
priestly examination as a figure of the Church's ministry in doctrine and
sacrament; Bede preserves this line. Bruno makes the juridical distinction
most plainly: priests judge the person clean; they do not cause the cure.
The later penitential tradition uses the cleansing on the road to defend both
interior contrition before sacramental encounter and embodied confession to
the priest. These are received sacramental applications, not Luke's literal
description of Christian absolution.

All ten receive cleansing; the narrative then narrows to one. Augustine's
\emph{Quaestiones} makes the Samaritan, etymologized as ``keeper,'' the person
who guards unity by giving glory back to God. In \emph{Sermon 176} he applies
the scene against self-attribution: do not claim amendment as one's own lest
one stand among the unthankful nine. Bede distinguishes cleansing from the
salvation spoken to the returning man and associates the difference with
faith in operation rather than bare recognition. Gregory the Great uses the
cleansed lepers for reconciled heretics, while Bruno warns that outward
cleansing alone does not guarantee final perseverance. Together they prevent
the episode from becoming a simple transaction in which cure automatically
equals mature discipleship.

The Samaritan's sequence is bodily and theological: he sees, returns,
glorifies God with a great voice, falls before Jesus, gives thanks, and is
raised by Jesus' word. Luke places glorifying God and prostration before Jesus
in one act without pausing to explain their relation. Theophylact's generous
reception draws the moral conclusion that foreign origin is no barrier to
pleasing God and sacred ancestry no ground for pride. St.~Anthony of Padua,
preaching this Gospel under a different medieval Sunday number, gives the
return, prostration, thanksgiving, and saving faith an extended penitential
and moral reading. St.~John Henry Newman's notes return to the themes of
disease as an image of sin and gratitude as worship; St.~Alphonsus develops
the scene morally, but his inherited medical premises cannot be repeated as
history or medicine.

Several safeguards are non-negotiable. Luke does not call the nine Jews;
Alfred Plummer's \emph{International Critical Commentary} on Luke,
pp.~403--405, explicitly notes that the inference does not follow. Augustine,
Cyril, Guéranger, Schuster, and even Newman at one locus
receive the contrast in forms that can become supersessionist. Their claims
must remain attributed and critically bounded. The Church confesses Israel's
Scriptures as God's word and, under \emph{Nostra aetate}~4, may not portray the
Jews as collectively rejected or accursed. Likewise biblical \latin{lepra}
must not be projected onto modern Hansen's disease or used to shame visible
difference, chronic illness, disability, or unbelief. The Gospel addresses
every Christian first: mercy already received seeks the truthful return of
thanksgiving.

\subsection*{Offertory: changing times in faithful hands\enspace\properrefs{Off.}}

The Offertory is Vulgate Psalm~30:15--16: modern Psalm~31:15--16 when the
title is numbered as verse~1, and 31:14--15 when it is not. Its final
noun is not the Clementine \latin{sortes}, ``lots,'' but \latin{témpora},
``times.'' This is no modern correction. Cassiodorus carries the complete
chant wording and interprets the times of Christ's assumed human life and
death as held in the Father's power. Jerome's Psalter according to the Hebrew
has \latin{tempora}; the medieval Gloss records ``the times of life'' beside
the lot reading; Bellarmine notes both Hebrew and ancient Latin support and
specifically says the Mass frequently sings \latin{tempora mea}.

Augustine's rich exposition belongs instead to \latin{sortes meæ}. He reads
the unknown lot as grace and election without preceding merit, illustrated
by the lot for Christ's seamless garment and by Ephesians~2. Theodoret knows
both Greek readings and treats them together as life's changing circumstances.
The two reception streams can be compared, but Augustine may not be presented
as commenting on the word the Mass actually sings. In either case, the
confession denies chance or persecutors final mastery of life.

The verse is not fatalism. ``My times are in thy hands'' does not identify
every human act with God's positive will, excuse injustice, or abolish
prudence. At the Offertory it entrusts the worshipper's contingent life while
real gifts are prepared and freely offered. The same psalm earlier supplies
the dying Christ's commendation of his spirit; Cassiodorus consequently hears
the whole psalm in Christ's voice. That wider canonical context gives the
short antiphon a paschal depth without pretending that v.~6 is appointed here.

\subsection*{Secret: people and gifts beneath the same mercy\enspace\properrefs{Sec.}}

The prayer turns on a doubled imperative: be propitious to the people; be
propitious to the gifts. Only then does it ask that God, placated by this
oblation, grant pardon and the petitions sought. The syntax neither identifies
the gifts with the people nor separates them. Offerers and offering stand
together under mercy, and pardon precedes the granting of requests in the
prayer's order.

The Old Gelasian preserves both a single-imperative Vatican reading and the
doubled form in Rheinau and St.~Gall witnesses; the Gregorian supplement has
the received double imperative. The ancient witnesses support textual
transmission, not a reconstruction of the prayer's author. A direct check also
corrects a tempting larger claim: Wilson's p.~172 note gives the Ottobonianus
marginal incipits \latin{Respice}, \latin{Respice}, \latin{In te speravi}, and
\latin{Panem de caelo} at Sunday~XII, while the three 1962 orations appear at
Sunday~XIV with different cues on p.~173. These are incipits, not full chants,
and no ninth-century co-location of the whole 1962 formulary has therefore
been shown.

The Preface of the Most Holy Trinity follows. The Secret does not specify how
the oblation placates God and should be read within the Roman Canon's
Christological sacrificial grammar, not as material gifts changing a hostile
divinity. The people ask the Father through Christ to receive what divine gift
first made possible.

\subsection*{Communion: Israel's manna received in Eucharistic thanksgiving\enspace\properrefs{Comm.}}

Wisdom~16 contrasts God's nourishment of Israel with the punishment of
oppressors and praises manna as fitted to every taste. The Missal freely
adapts the verse: third person becomes \latin{nobis}; \latin{dedísti} recalls
Psalm~77's manna verb; laborless preparation disappears; the clauses about
delight and sweetness are rearranged. The antiphon is thus a liturgical
confession, not merely a biblical quotation.

Greek reception is broad but not yet explicitly sacramental. Origen, in a
homily on Psalm~77, echoes Wisdom~16:20--21 almost verbatim and reads manna as
the divine Word feeding souls according to their capacity. Gregory of Nyssa
uses the same verses for the virginal birth and the Logos adapting himself to
the soul. The text often printed under Chrysostom's name in the
\emph{Synopsis Scripturae} is pseudo-Chrysostom and must not be cited as the
saint's. These witnesses establish christological and spiritual reception,
not an assertion that Wisdom's human author described the Eucharist directly.

The Latin medieval line becomes expressly Eucharistic. Rabanus moves from the
literal manna to Christ's declaration, ``I am the bread of life,'' and to
eternal life through participation in that bread. Bonaventure's Eucharistic
use of the every-taste phrase presents sacramental sweetness as the abundance
of the one Christ who answers varied spiritual need. Such language belongs to
devotional and theological reception; it cannot make felt consolation the
measure of sacramental reality. Gregory the Great quotes the same clause in
\emph{Moralia}~VI.16.22 for a non-Eucharistic analogy: divine speech
condescends to the quality of its hearers just as manna answered varied taste.
His distinct use keeps sweetness from becoming a promised sensation. The
Catechism's canonical typology of manna and its Eucharistic teaching provide
the doctrinal frame.

Albert the Great, commenting on Matthew~6:11, directly cites Wisdom's
every-taste manna in a Eucharistic exposition; Aquinas identifies manna as a
figure of this sacrament at \emph{Summa theologiae} III, q.~73, a.~6. Their
claim concerns one objectively given Christ, not a substance that mutates to
match appetite or a promise that every wish will be gratified. Aquinas's
treatment of sacramental fruit in q.~79 preserves the need for worthy
reception, while q.~80, a.~2 distinguishes the angels' spiritual feeding on
Christ from sacramental reception proper to embodied human pilgrims.

St.~John Paul~II explicitly cites Wisdom~16:20--21 in his General Audience of
25 October 2000. Christ illuminates the Exodus gift; the Eucharist nourishes
the pilgrim people and anticipates messianic fulfillment. This later saintly
reception is especially apt for a Postcommunion that still asks progress: the
bread is both present sustenance and pledge of an end not yet possessed.

The Gospel keeps this typology from becoming supersessionist. The antiphon's
``us'' does not replace the biblical ``them'' but makes Israel's remembered
gift the Church's thanksgiving through Christ. The returned Samaritan stands
at Jesus' feet not as evidence of Israel's rejection, but as a stranger whose
faith exposes the wideness of divine mercy and summons every recipient to
gratitude.

\subsection*{Postcommunion: recipients ask to advance\enspace\properrefs{Postcomm.}}

The prayer begins after reception: \latin{Sumptis cæléstibus sacraméntis}.
Its subject is the communicants themselves, \latin{proficiámus}, not the
sacraments: the prayer makes their progress answer redemption's eternal
\latin{augméntum}. Cummiskey's historical English changes the grammatical subject
and omits the noun ``increase''; the Latin must control the theological
reading.

The Old Gelasian places this prayer with the Collect in one unnumbered Mass
and preserves variation between \latin{proficiant}, \latin{proficiat}, and
the received \latin{proficiámus}; the Gregorian supplement has the latter.
The first-person plural makes reception active without making it autonomous.
Those who have received heavenly sacraments are precisely those who ask to
advance.

The shared \latin{augméntum} closes the formulary's arc. Faith, hope, and
charity grow toward what God promises; communicants grow toward redemption's
eternal fullness. The sacrament is not deficient, and the recipient does not
complete Christ's redemption. Rather, an objectively given mystery bears
fruit through a pilgrim person's continuing incorporation, conversion, and
charity. The grateful Samaritan is therefore not the final image of a finished
account: Jesus raises him and sends him onward.
