\section*{Source-Grounded Cross-Proper Synthesis}

\begin{studyframe}
These relations have named textual or reception witnesses. They do not depend
on attributing the final formulary to one compiler. The exploratory proposals
in the following section remain separately classified.
\end{studyframe}

\subsection*{Psalm~73's covenant and Galatians' Abrahamic promise\enspace
  \properrefs{Int., Ep., Grad.}}

Guéranger explicitly interprets the repeated \latin{Réspice} through Rupert of
Deutz as the ancient people's appeal for fulfilment of the covenant promised to
Abraham's faith. The relation is therefore not only a modern observation about
adjacent texts. Augustine supplies an independent and earlier bridge. At the
opening of \emph{Enarratio in Psalmum 73} he applies Galatians~3:29 to the
congregation brought to understanding: those who belong to Christ are
Abraham's seed and heirs according to promise. At the psalm's covenant verse he
hears the heavenly inheritance of Jeremiah's new covenant and says, ``we
possess the tablets and await the inheritance.''

The sources preserve rather than erase the history within that unity.
Bellarmine hears the covenant with the fathers; Augustine and Cassiodorus the
new covenant; Rupert the Abrahamic promise. Paul explains how these can belong
to one divine fidelity: the promise precedes the Mosaic Law, reaches its center
in Christ the Seed, and is not annulled by the later dispensation. The Law is
not contrary to promise; it serves by guarding, instructing, convicting, and
figuring. The chants plead the covenant; the Epistle names its personal
fulfilment; the Gradual returns the plea after Scripture has enclosed all under
sin.

The Psalm~88 insertion in the Gradual extends the documented field. Its
servants' reproach belongs to a lament over the Davidic covenant, and its next
verse names reproach against the Anointed's footsteps. Augustine and
Cassiodorus interpret the servants as Christians suffering under Christ's
name. Thus the liturgy's covenant plea includes not only promise awaiting
fulfilment but the Church's participation in the Anointed's apparent defeat
and final vindication.

\subsection*{The Law written inwardly and the Collect's grace-given love\enspace
  \properrefs{Coll., Ep., Gosp.}}

An expanded Old Gelasian recension gives a direct historical bridge: God
writes his Law in believers' hearts by his finger, then grants increased
faith, hope, and charity. Though not the 1962 Collect, this ancient reception
makes its union with Galatians explicit: external law cannot vivify by itself;
divine action writes obedience within.

Augustine supplies the doctrinal grammar: the Law was given that grace might
be sought, and grace that the Law might be fulfilled. Aquinas maps the three
virtues to the supernatural end: faith knows it, hope tends toward it through
God's aid, and charity joins the will to it. The Collect's \latin{fac nos
amáre} therefore does not ask for an emotion added to command. It asks God to
cause the living charity in which the just requirement becomes the person's
own willing act.

Trent confirms this received reading by excerpting the increase petition in
Session~VI, chapter~10. Its chapters~10 and 16 and canon~32 affirm real growth
and merit inside Christ's prior grace. Luke gives the structure narrative
form. The ten do not purchase healing; Christ's word moves them. Yet they truly
walk before sight. Bonaventure then interprets the complete episode through
faith, hope, and charity. The ancient oration variant, Augustinian theology,
Tridentine reception, and Bonaventuran exegesis establish a source-grounded
continuity: grace writes, virtues move, and love obeys.

\subsection*{The journey to the priests and sacramental reconciliation\enspace
  \properrefs{Ep., Gosp.}}

Tertullian is the earliest strong cross-text witness. Against Marcion he
insists that Jesus' command confirms the Creator's Law and that the journey is
obedient faith. Christ nevertheless stands forth as true Priest and temple,
the giver before whom the Samaritan offers thanks. The Gospel thus enacts
Paul's distinction without denigrating Moses: the Law's priestly office truly
discerns and restores, while life comes through Christ.

Augustine's \emph{Quaestiones evangeliorum}, Bede, Bruno, the anonymous
\emph{De vera et falsa poenitentia}, St~Anthony, Albert, Bonaventure, and Denis
carry the road into the Church's penitential life. Their common sequence is
contrition awakened by grace, intention and actual confession, satisfaction,
and reconciliation through the keys. Bruno and Albert keep efficient causality
clear: God cleanses. Yet the tradition does not reduce priestly action to
observation. Catholic doctrine receives Christ as principal forgiver and the
ordained priest as Christ's minister, truly absolving and reconciling in a
judicial act (Trent, Session~XIV, ch.~6 and can.~9; CCC~1441--1449, 1461).

The Epistle and Gospel therefore illuminate one another in a specifically
Catholic register. The Law neither heals by its external letter nor becomes
irrelevant. It leads the wounded toward Christ within a real priestly economy
that he fulfils and extends sacramentally. The stranger receives health within
the Church's visible communion.

\subsection*{Thanksgiving becomes oblation within Christ's propitiatory sacrifice\enspace
  \properrefs{Gosp., Off., Sec.}}

Tertullian explicitly calls the Samaritan's thanksgiving the true oblation and
identifies Christ as the temple and High Priest to whom it is rendered. That
single witness grounds a relation the ritual sequence then develops. The man
who received mercy returns, glorifies God, prostrates himself before Jesus, and
gives thanks. At the Offertory the assembly confesses \latin{Tu es Deus meus}
and entrusts its times while gifts are prepared. In the Secret it asks God to
be propitious both to people and gifts.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
The return remains grace's answer, never the cure's price. The Secret's double
\latin{propitiáre} makes that dependence grammatical. The Church does not
offer from outside mercy; she and her gifts are placed beneath it. Trent's
Session~XXII, chapter~2 provides the positive doctrinal account: the Mass is a
true and proper propitiatory sacrifice because the same Christ who offered
himself on the Cross is sacramentally and unbloodily offered, and the fruits of
his one oblation are applied for pardon and grace. Aquinas's distinction of
sacrifice as offered and sacrament as received explains why the Secret can
pray over persons, gifts, pardon, and petitions before Communion names the
receiver.

The Gospel's gratitude is thus neither etiquette nor compensation. It is
worship before the divine Giver and a living self-return that Christ gathers
into his own oblation. Augustine's admonition against self-attribution and
St~Anthony's Samaritan as \latin{custos} reinforce the same theology: a gift is
kept by acknowledging its source and offering praise.

\subsection*{Wisdom's manna nourishes the virtues and the road to glory\enspace
  \properrefs{Coll., Comm., Postcomm.}}

The direct Wisdom reception supplies an unusually rich chain. Origen echoes
Wisdom~16:20--21 nearly verbatim and takes manna as the one Logos feeding souls
according to their capacities. Gregory of Nyssa makes the ready, unsown bread
a figure of the Virgin-born Word and of his adaptive nourishment. Gregory the
Great takes the same savor as divine Scripture condescending to hearers. These
readings establish a pattern of one heavenly Wisdom nourishing diverse
receivers toward maturity.

Rabanus makes the line explicitly Eucharistic in his commentary on Wisdom,
moving from Israel's manna through John~6 to Christ's flesh, blood, and eternal
life. Albert cites the Wisdom verses for Eucharistic bread as heavenly,
life-giving, unitive, viaticum, and a sign of fruition. Aquinas says manna
principally figures the Eucharist's effect, since the sacrament's grace
refreshes the mind in every respect. Bonaventure first preserves the literal
manna and then reads dew, sweetness, and desire through grace, Eucharist, and
devotion. St~John Paul~II completes the source chain by citing the verses for
the food of pilgrims and the foretaste of messianic fullness.

This reception documents the link between the two occurrences of
\latin{augméntum}. The Collect asks God to enlarge faith, hope, and charity;
the Communion gives the one Christ whose sacramental grace nourishes and
unites; the Postcommunion asks that those receivers advance toward redemption's
eternal fullness. Aquinas makes the theology exact: Eucharistic food sustains,
increases, repairs, and delights spiritual life, while viaticum gives the power
to reach glory (\emph{ST} III, q.~79, aa.~1--2). The objective gift is one and
complete; its pilgrim fruit grows.

\subsection*{Generations and times pass within one divine refuge\enspace
  \properrefs{All., Off., Postcomm.}}

Jerome provides a direct verbal bridge between the psalm chants. Explaining the
need for refuge in Psalm~89, he invokes the threatened soul of Psalm~73:19,
whose language supplies the poor of the Introit and Gradual. Augustine then
defines refuge as flight from temporal mutability into God's immutable
eternity. Cassiodorus's Roman lemma extends shelter from the first human
generation through every progeny to the world's end.

The Offertory brings that universal horizon into personal confession. Its
\latin{témpora mea} is an ancient Roman-Psalter reading, directly expounded by
Cassiodorus of Christ's human times under the Father's power. Theodoret and
Aquinas record both times and lots; Augustine's exposition of lots develops
grace, election, and the undivided Church. Together the witnesses present
providence as neither chance nor fatalism: the believer acts and offers within
an order whose final sovereignty belongs to God.

\Needspace{3\baselineskip}
The Postcommunion names the direction of those changing times. Recipients ask
to advance toward eternal redemption. Jerome's Psalm~89 exposition moves from
numbered days to resurrection and God establishing human work; Augustine
locates abiding refuge in eternity. The source-grounded synthesis is therefore
anagogical: changing hours, sacramental growth, and successive generations are
drawn into the eternal life in which grace's work is established.

\subsection*{Historical transmission: two inherited streams become one received formulary}

The checked Gregorian edition prevents a fictitious single-origin story.
Wilson's p.~172 note records Ottobonianus~313's marginal incipits
\latin{Respice}, \latin{Respice}, \latin{In te speravi}, and \latin{Panem de
caelo} for Sunday~XII, while the three received orations stand at Sunday~XIV on
p.~173 with different cues. Gellone likewise places the oration incipits at
Sunday~XIV; Savona has all three prayers under Sunday~XIII. Verona and Old
Gelasian witnesses distribute them differently.

The evidence proves transmission, adaptation, and eventual convergence, not a
ninth-century Mass already containing all ten 1962 elements. This historical
plurality strengthens rather than weakens the theological task. The received
Roman formulary is a genuine liturgical whole, while every cross-proper claim
must still be earned from text and reception rather than assigned to an
unverified compiler.
