\section*{Source-Grounded Cross-Proper Synthesis}

\begin{studyframe}
This section retains only relations for which a named witness explicitly joins
the texts, or for which separate direct witnesses establish the same bounded
theological structure. It distinguishes historical pairing from proof of
original design. The following section of exploratory proposals has a lower
evidential status.
\end{studyframe}

\subsection*{The covenant chants and Paul's promise\enspace\properrefs{Int., Ep., Grad.}}

This is the formulary's most securely documented cross-proper relation.
Guéranger explicitly says the Gradual repeats the Introit because the ancient
people, in Rupert of Deutz's account, calls for fulfilment of the covenant
promised to Abraham's faith. Independently, Augustine begins his exposition
of Psalm~73 with Galatians~3:29 and the heirs of Abraham according to promise,
while Jerome and Theodoret explain Paul's \latin{testaméntum} or
\latin{diathēkē} as the promise to Abraham. The sources do not agree whether
Psalm~73:20 itself denotes the old covenant, new covenant, or Abrahamic
promise. What is established is a received theological field in which the
psalm's covenant plea and Paul's non-annullable promise illuminate one another.

\subsection*{Moses, the two covenants, and the one refuge\enspace\properrefs{Ep., All.}}

Augustine, Cassiodorus, and Jerome read Psalm~89's Mosaic title through the
old and new covenants: Moses ministers the one and prophesies the other.
Galatians names a mediated law while insisting that God is one. Aquinas's
Christ-as-Mediator with Moses as figure gives the two passages a controlled
Christian relation: refuge across generations is the fidelity of the one God,
fulfilled through Christ without denying Moses' historical ministry. This is
documented patristic and scholastic reception, not a claim that the Alleluia
was chosen to settle the disputed referent of Galatians~3:19.

\subsection*{Command, road, and continued advance\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Gosp., Postcomm.}}

The Collect and Postcommunion are historically paired in an Old Gelasian Mass
and share \latin{augméntum}; the first asks love of what God commands and the
second asks that recipients advance. The Gospel places cleansing precisely
\latin{dum irent}, while Tertullian, Bede, Bruno, Albert, and Bonaventure
receive the journey as obedient faith with penitential or sacramental
application. The sources support a bounded structure: grace enables obedient
movement, and sacramental reception continues rather than ends the pilgrim's
growth. They do not support a prosperity rule in which obedience guarantees
physical cure.

\subsection*{Gift received, thanks returned, oblation offered\enspace\properrefs{Gosp., Sec., Comm.}}

Tertullian explicitly describes the Samaritan's thanksgiving as an offering
to Christ; Augustine makes true thanksgiving the return to God of what was
received. The Secret places people and gifts under propitiating mercy, and the
Communion turns Wisdom's third-person manna report into a first-person address
to the Lord. Rabanus, Bonaventure, and John Paul~II document Eucharistic
reception of the manna verse. The integrated conclusion is therefore
source-grounded: gratitude and Eucharist answer prior gift. It does not mean
that the one grateful man merited his earlier cleansing or that Christian
reception cancels Israel's place in the manna narrative.

\subsection*{The virtues increase toward redemption\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Postcomm.}}

The exact repeated noun \latin{augméntum} supplies the textual link. Trent
quotes the Collect's virtue-increase petition in its chapter on increased
justification; Aquinas explains charity's increase during pilgrimage. The
Postcommunion makes communicants the subject of continued progress toward
eternal redemption. Together the two prayers give a fully Catholic account
of sacramental fruit: divine grace initiates and sustains real growth in
persons who act, love, receive, and persevere.

\subsection*{Historical pairing: what the ninth-century evidence does and does not show}

The checked Gregorian edition complicates any simple claim of a stable ancient
formulary. Wilson's p.~172 note gives Ottobonianus~313's four marginal
incipits---\latin{Respice}, \latin{Respice}, \latin{In te speravi}, and
\latin{Panem de caelo}---at Gregorian Sunday~XII. The Collect, Secret, and
Postcommunion stand at Sunday~XIV on p.~173 with different chant cues. A later Gellone
witness also assigns the three orations to Sunday~XIV; a fourteenth-century
Savona fragment has them under Sunday~XIII. This proves transmission and
calendar movement, not ninth-century co-location of all ten 1962 elements.
The full formulary is therefore a received historical constellation whose
theological relations must be argued, not presumed from a single ancient
compiler.
