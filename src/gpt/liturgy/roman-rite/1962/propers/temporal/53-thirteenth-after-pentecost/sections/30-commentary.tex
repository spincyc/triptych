\subsection*{Introit: the desolated people plead God's own covenant\enspace\properrefs{Int.}}

The antiphon is a deliberate cento rather than a continuous extract. It joins
Psalm~73:20a, 19b, 22a, and 23a, then appends v.~1 as the psalm verse. It sings
\latin{ne derelínquas} where both Roman and Gallican Psalters have ``do not
forget,'' and \latin{Exsúrge, Dómine} where both have ``Arise, O God.'' Its
closing \latin{voces quaeréntium te}, however, is not an unexplained alteration:
it is the exact Roman-Psalter branch at v.~23, while the Gallican and Clementine
have the voices of God's enemies. The extant Greek speaks of suppliants.
Jerome knew both readings and judged ``enemies'' to fit the immediate context
(\emph{Ep.~106} 46). The lemma printed with Theodoret has the suppliants; the
Schulze/PG apparatus, not Theodoret's exposition, records the hostile variant.
The Roman chant chooses the voices raised \emph{to} God.

In the literal sense, Psalm~73 gives Israel's afflicted sanctuary-community a
voice. Worship has been interrupted, the sanctuary violated, and enemies seem
to triumph over both people and divine name. The ancient Asaphic title places
the lament within the prophetic-liturgical house associated with David, while
received expositors hear different historical fulfilments. Nicholas of Lyra
favors the Babylonian destruction; Bellarmine the persecution under
Antiochus~IV; Theodoret and Cassiodorus the Roman destruction of Jerusalem.
The modern NABRE calls the psalm especially apt to the destruction of
587~BC. These horizons
need not be collapsed: the tradition can distinguish an Asaphic prophetic
voice, the poem's first catastrophe, and later fulfilments in which its prayer
was heard anew.

Bellarmine identifies the covenant as God's pledge to the fathers and the poor
as those who wait daily at the gate of mercy. The antiphon's logic is already
theological: \latin{Réspice} does not ask God to discover information but to
manifest in history the fidelity he has promised. The people have nothing
stronger to present than God's own \latin{testaméntum}. This prepares Paul's
argument that a divine disposition concerning inheritance cannot be annulled
by a later law.

The Fathers unfold the allegorical sense with equal boldness. Augustine and
Cassiodorus read the covenant through Jeremiah~31 and its fulfilment in Christ.
Augustine contrasts this inheritance with land,
prosperity, victory, and bodily health: \latin{tabulas tenemus, haereditatem
exspectamus}, ``we possess the tablets and await the inheritance''
(\emph{Enarr. in Ps.~73} 23). Cassiodorus names eternal life, kingdom, and the
vision of God; when no merits can carry the case, the people appeal to the
unfailing promise (\emph{Exp. Ps.~73} 24). Eusebius identifies the covenant
given through Christ for humanity's salvation and hears the poor disciples
interceding for Israel. St~Anthony of Padua brings the sense directly into
Christian worship: the testament is ratified by Christ's death and Blood;
Christ is the poor soul's inheritance; the soul is Christ's cause because he
stood trial for it; and the seekers' voices become confession and preaching
(\emph{Dominica XII post Pentecosten} II.13--14).

The moral reading begins with poverty. Augustine's poor renounce confidence in
their own righteousness and pray like the publican: \latin{pauperes sumus,
exaudi mendicos tuos}, ``we are poor; hear thy beggars'' (\emph{Enarr. in
Ps.~73} 22). Origen moves from martyrs literally exposed to beasts to the sins
and hostile powers from which truthful confession seeks deliverance
(\emph{Hom. III in Ps.~73} 7--8). Cassiodorus's exact \latin{voces quaerentium te} completes
the movement: the faithful seek in order to find and behold God. Poverty is not
mute; it becomes prayer, confession, and witness.

The anagogical petition reaches beyond relief from one crisis. Augustine joins
\latin{Exsurge} to Christ's Resurrection and final judgment; Cassiodorus sees
truth manifesting itself until error acknowledges what it denied. ``Abandon
not to the end'' asks preservation until seekers behold the One sought. The
chant therefore moves from ruined sanctuary to eternal dwelling, reproach to
vindication, and poverty to inheritance.

Luke will give this prayer a human form. Ten afflicted persons stand afar and
raise their common voice, \latin{Iesu praecéptor, miserére nostri}. The Church
has first asked God not to forget the voices that seek him; the Gospel asks
whether those who receive mercy will remember their benefactor. Divine
remembrance summons human remembrance, praise, and Eucharistic thanksgiving.

\subsection*{Collect: promise becomes love through grace-given increase\enspace\properrefs{Coll.}}

The Collect's grammar is its theology. \latin{Da} governs \latin{augméntum}:
faith, hope, and charity are realities whose increase is given. \latin{Fac}
governs \latin{nos amáre}: even the petitioners' loving obedience is requested
from God. The purpose clause, \latin{ut mereámur ássequi quod promíttis}, names
a genuinely human attaining and merit, while the two imperatives locate its
origin and power in grace. Promise and command are not opposed. God promises
the supernatural end, gives the virtues by which it is believed and desired,
and causes the charity by which his command is loved.

The prayer has a long and differentiated transmission. The Verona sacramentary
contains the Collect in a July series without Sunday assignment. The Old
Gelasian joins it to the Postcommunion in an unnumbered Sunday Mass, and an
expanded charity recension prays to the God who writes the justice of his Law
in believers' hearts by his finger. The Gregorian supplement preserves the
received Collect, Secret, and Postcommunion under Sunday~XIV; Gellone supplies
their incipits under the same number, while a fourteenth-century Savona fragment
has all three under Sunday~XIII. These witnesses establish reception and
movement through Roman and Frankish books, not composition by a pope or one
moment when every chant and prayer was first joined.

The Council of Trent gives the Collect an exceptional place in Catholic
doctrine. Session~VI, chapter~10 introduces the Church's prayer for the
increase of received justification and excerpts: \latin{Da nobis, Dómine,
fidei, spei et caritátis augméntum}. The Council changes the address and does
not quote the whole Collect. Chapter~16 and canon~32 then affirm genuine merit
while locating it within Christ's grace. The liturgical subject truly believes,
hopes, loves, obeys, and advances; none of these movements begins independently
of the gift prayed for.

The prayer's traditional articulation follows its own order: faith apprehends
the revealed end, hope tends toward it with confidence in God's aid, and
charity unites the will to it. These infused gifts can grow throughout the
wayfaring state, yet their increase remains within Christ's grace rather than
an autonomous achievement. Augustine's \latin{Da quod iubes, et iube quod vis}
expresses the same order: God commands a real act and gives what enables it.

The Gospel narrates the Collect. Faith receives Christ's word; hope walks
before cleansing can be seen; charity returns to the Giver. Bonaventure reads
the final saving word through exactly this triad: faith initiates the movement,
hope advances it, charity perfects it (\emph{Comm. in Luc.} XVII.34). The
virtues are not abstract dispositions placed beside the story. They are the
form of obedient travel and grateful return.

\subsection*{Epistle: the promise rests in Christ, and the good Law serves it\enspace\properrefs{Ep.}}

Paul addresses baptized Gentiles being pressed to add circumcision and Mosaic
observance as necessary to their standing in Christ. He reasons from the divine
promise to Abraham, not from contempt for Israel's Law. The lesson's governing
question is his own: ``Is the Law then against the promises of God? God forbid.''
The Mosaic Law is God's revealed gift, holy, spiritual, and good. Its moral
instruction abides; its discipline restrains and discloses sin; its figures
prepare Christ. Its limit is that an external prescription cannot of itself
communicate the Spirit's justifying and vivifying grace (CCC~1961--1964).

The promised Seed is personal. Jerome locates the blessing of the nations in
Christ rather than Isaac taken alone or natural succession as such; Theodoret
calls Christ seed in the proper sense while recognizing the ordinary
genealogical use. Aquinas explains why: Christ is the one through whom and in
whom all nations receive Abraham's blessing (\emph{Super Gal.} III, lect.~6,
Corpus Thomisticum [87761]). Yet the singular does not isolate Christ from his Mystical Body.
Augustine identifies the one seed as Christ together with those who imitate
Abraham's faith, gathered into unity because faith is one (\emph{Exp. Gal.}
23--24). Paul himself completes the claim after the appointed lesson: all who
are baptized into Christ are one in him and therefore Abraham's seed and heirs
(Gal.~3:27--29). Christ remains the personal Head; incorporation into him gives
participation in the inheritance.

\latin{Testaméntum} allows Paul to reason from a confirmed disposition of an
inheritance. Jerome notes that Hebrew \emph{berith} means covenant or pact;
Aquinas explains that even a human will, once confirmed, is not annulled or
supplemented at whim, much less the promise of God. The 430 years make
posteriority visible. Jerome leaves the ancient chronological problem open;
Aquinas counts from Genesis~12 to Exodus and distinguishes a shorter reckoning
from Isaac. The doctrinal point does not depend on resolving every calculation:
the later Law cannot transform prior gift into no gift.

Why, then, the Law? Chrysostom calls it a bridle that checks wrongdoing and a
probe that reveals the wound so that the sick desire the physician. Augustine
emphasizes exposure of pride: the command reveals transgression and teaches
that salvation is not in one's own hand but in the Mediator's. Theodoret sees
providential protection of the people from whom the Seed would arise. Aquinas
gathers four purposes---repression, discipline, manifestation of weakness, and
figurative instruction in the grace to come (\emph{Super Gal.} III, lect.~7,
[87762]). These readings complement rather than cancel one another. The
Law guards, teaches, convicts, and figures; grace heals and gives the interior
power of fulfilment. Augustine's mature formula is exact: the Law was given so
that grace might be sought, and grace so that the Law might be fulfilled
(\emph{De spiritu et littera} 19.33--34).

\enlargethispage{-\baselineskip}
The ``hand of a mediator'' preserves an ancient distinction. Theodoret and
Theodore of Mopsuestia identify Moses; Jerome and Chrysostom identify Christ,
the divine giver. Augustine specifies Christ in his humanity; Ambrosiaster
sees the reconciler. Aquinas preserves both levels: the Law lies in Christ's
power and Moses signifies him. The Alleluia makes this matrix prayer. Moses,
traditional speaker of Psalm~89, confesses refuge through every generation;
Paul adds \latin{Deus autem unus est}. One God gives promise, Law, figure, and
their fulfilment in Christ from beginning to end.

``Scripture concluded all under sin'' describes judicial and medicinal
enclosure. Jerome compares it to custody: the judge declares and confines the
guilty, not as cause of their crime, but so that pardon may be bestowed by
sovereign mercy. Chrysostom and Theodoret emphasize conviction. Aquinas joins
knowledge of disease to knowledge of impotence, both moving the patient to
Christ the physician (lect.~8, [87763]). The purpose is evangelical:
\latin{ut promíssio ex fide Iesu Christi darétur credéntibus}. Promise remains
gift; faith receives the Christ it cannot manufacture. The full letter names
that faith's mature operation as charity (Gal.~5:6).

The other propers make Paul's order visible. The Psalm's poor plead covenant
because they cannot vivify themselves. The Collect asks God to write command
within love. Jesus honors the priestly procedure while giving the cleansing it
can discern but not cause. The Secret approaches the Father through the
Mediator's sacrifice, and the Communion receives the promised Seed as heavenly
bread. Law serves; Christ gives; faith, hope, and charity receive and return.

\subsection*{Gradual: the servants' reproach enters the cause of the Anointed\enspace\properrefs{Grad.}}

The Gradual repeats Psalm~73:20a, 19b, and 22a, but its last clause is verbally
Psalm~88:51a: \latin{memor esto oppróbrii servórum tuórum}. The printed Missal
reference remains Psalm~73. This is not an error to normalize away; it is the
chant's received centonization. Unlike the Introit's \latin{ne derelínquas}, the
Gradual uses/reverts to the Psalter's \latin{ne obliviscáris}. Its repeated
\latin{Réspice} now follows Paul's account of Abraham's covenant and prepares
Luke's \latin{quos ut vidit}: the Church asks God to look, and the Gospel shows
the incarnate Lord's gaze becoming mercy in act.

The Psalm~88 insertion interprets the cause being judged. That psalm laments
the apparent collapse of the Davidic covenant; its next verse names enemies
reproaching the footsteps of God's Anointed, \latin{vestigia Christi tui}.
Augustine reads the servants' reproach as suffering borne by Christians
because of Christ; Cassiodorus says persecutors made the Christian name itself
a crime. The chant therefore moves from reproach uttered against God in
Psalm~73 to reproach borne by God's servants in Psalm~88. The Church's cause is
God's because she belongs to the Anointed and carries his name.

Guéranger, reporting Rupert of Deutz, explicitly joins the repeated covenant
plea to the Abrahamic promise proclaimed in Galatians. This is documented
liturgical reception, though not proof of the formulary's original compilation.
The ritual placement is nevertheless powerful: after Scripture has enclosed
all under sin, the assembly does not plead self-exemption. It asks the one God
to remember covenant, poor, servants, and Anointed before Christ looks upon the
ten who cry for mercy.

\subsection*{Alleluia: the eternal refuge receives passing generations\enspace\properrefs{All.}}

\latin{Dómine, refúgium factus es nobis, a generatióne et progénie} is exact
Roman-Psalter Psalm~89:1. The Gallican has \latin{a generatióne in generatiónem};
the Roman \latin{progenies} emphasizes the living continuity by which one
generation proceeds from another. The canonical title names this the prayer
of Moses, the man of God. Jerome and Lyra receive Mosaic authorship positively;
Augustine treats Moses as Old-Covenant minister and New-Covenant prophet rather
than necessary composer; Theodoret records both traditions. The liturgy can
receive the Mosaic voice without converting one older chronology into dogma.

Eusebius fills the beneficiary list with Enoch, Noah, Lot, Abraham, Isaac,
Jacob, Joseph, and Moses. Separately, he unfolds refuge as shelter, harbor,
wall, defense, and aid in every undertaking. Theodoret extends the memory through
Egypt, wilderness, Joshua, judges, Samuel, and David. Praise rises from
remembered benefit. The God acclaimed now is trusted because he has carried
his people before.

The allegorical sense draws the covenants into Christ. Augustine hears all
generations or the two covenants joined in Christ. Cassiodorus's Roman lemma
makes the universal Church speak in the plural \latin{nobis}: refuge begins
with humanity and reaches the world's end. Arnobius Junior confesses the
pre-existent Son and Creator reigning before mountains, earth, and world. The Word preceding every generation
enters one and gathers mortals into abiding life.

Jerome unfolds the moral image as roof and rock in storm, city walls against an
enemy, shade for a traveler, and escape from a beast. Bellarmine identifies the
fortified dwelling with faith, hope, and charity. Hugh of St~Cher maps refuge
onto conversion: penitents flee sin, the proficient temptation, the perfect
worldly care. The Collect accordingly asks God to enlarge the very dwelling in
which the pilgrim abides.

Anagogically, the psalm contrasts divine eternity with dust, night watch,
fading grass, hidden sin, and numbered years. Augustine finds refuge from
created mutability in the immutable One. Jerome moves toward resurrection,
morning mercy, and God establishing human work. The acclamation is therefore
paschal: generations pass, but eternal Christ receives them and establishes in
glory what grace begins in time. This prepares the Offertory's surrender of
\latin{témpora mea} and the Postcommunion's advance toward eternal redemption.

\subsection*{Gospel: mercy on the road returns as worship and saving thanks\enspace\properrefs{Gosp.}}

Luke sets the encounter inside Jesus' ascent to Jerusalem, through or between
Samaria and Galilee, at an unnamed village. Ten afflicted persons stand afar,
address Jesus as Master, and raise one appeal for mercy. Leviticus~14 assigns
priests the examination and rites by which a healed person is declared clean
and restored to communal and cultic life; it does not make the priest the
healer. Jesus commands all ten to undertake that obedient showing before any
change is visible, and \latin{dum irent, mundáti sunt}.

Luke then marks a theological progression. All ten are cleansed; the Samaritan
sees that he is healed; returning, he glorifies God with a great voice; he falls
on his face at Jesus' feet and gives thanks; Christ says that his faith has
saved him. The Vulgate's \latin{fides tua te salvum fecit} preserves the final
distinction. Common bodily cleansing is gift to all. The narrative follows one
gift into recognition, return, worship, thanksgiving, and saving communion
with the Giver.

Tertullian makes this sequence serve a vigorous anti-Marcionite argument
(\emph{Adv. Marc.} IV.35.9--11). Christ confirms the Creator's Law by sending
the ten to its priests, yet manifests himself as true High Priest and temple;
the Samaritan's thanksgiving becomes an oblation made to Christ, and faith
saves. Cyril says healing travels with those sent to the priests. Augustine's
pastoral inference is severe and fruitful: attribute amendment not to oneself,
lest one be found among the nine who gave no thanks (\emph{Sermo}~176.6). Bede
makes the final distinction explicit: saving faith is faith living and working,
not bare knowledge of a benefit (\emph{In Luc.} V, PL~92:542B--544D).

Christ's question, ``Where are the nine?'' is a genuine rebuke. Gratitude,
praise, and worship are owed responses to gratuitous mercy, even though they do
not purchase it. Luke does not tell the nine's final destiny, and no inference
of damnation is needed to preserve the moral force. The Samaritan glorifies God
precisely by returning, prostrating himself, and thanking Jesus. Tertullian,
Bede, Theophylact, St~Anthony, and the later doctors rightly hear worship and an
implicit manifestation of Christ's divine dignity in that inseparable act.

The ecclesial and penitential tradition grows from the literal order. Augustine
sees variegated leprosy as the mind's mixture of truth and falsehood and the
priestly showing as a figure of the Church's ministry of doctrine and
sacrament (\emph{Quaest. ev.} II.40). Gregory the Great treats leprosy as the
mixture of right and perverse teaching. Bruno states the controlling
distinction: priests judge the clean; God cleanses. Pseudo-Augustine's
\emph{De vera et falsa poenitentia}, Gratian, St~Anthony, Albert, Bonaventure,
and Denis unfold \latin{ite---osténdite---dum irent}
through contrition, confession, satisfaction, and the ministry of the keys.
The heart turns under grace, intends obedient confession, and is restored to
visible communion. Albert preserves the axiom, \latin{Deus enim solus emundat
peccata}; God alone is the principal cause of forgiveness.

That axiom does not reduce the sacrament to certification. Christ entrusted
the keys to the Church: the ordained priest acts as Christ's minister and,
in a judicial act, truly absolves and reconciles the penitent
(Trent, Session~XIV, ch.~6 and can.~9; CCC~1441--1449, 1461). The medieval
reading therefore joins interior conversion to an objective ecclesial act.
Grace can reach the contrite heart before arrival, yet the command to show
oneself is not thereby made dispensable.

St~Anthony and Albert give the moral sense to the Samaritan's verbs. Albert
unfolds an ordered movement from undivided heart and recognition through
return, praise, prostration, humility, and thanksgiving. Anthony calls the
Samaritan \latin{custos}, keeper: a gift is guarded by attributing it to God,
not to self. Newman teaches the same habit through recurrent remembrance and
deeds of thanks; gratitude is itself a form of love. Christ's \latin{Surge,
vade} then sends the worshipper from prostration into strong action.

\Needspace{3\baselineskip}
Traditional number and people typologies belong positively to this spiritual
reception. Bruno, Anthony, Bonaventure, and Richard of Saint Victor see the
ten as humanity transgressing the Decalogue or the universality of sinners
enclosed under sin. Augustine and Bede see the one as unity and the nine as
incompletion outside it. The Samaritan becomes firstfruits of the nations and
an admonition that an outsider may surpass a presumptuous insider.
Theophylact supplies the moral rule: foreign birth prevents no one from
pleasing God, while holy ancestry licenses no pride. This Gentile-inclusion
reading is fulfilled through Israel's Scriptures, Messiah, and promise, not
against Israel. Luke calls only the grateful man a Samaritan and stranger; he
does not identify all nine as Jews or teach collective rejection. Catholic
reception therefore confesses both the traditional type and the irrevocable
gifts and calling of Israel (\emph{Nostra aetate}~4).

The biblical category translated ``leprosy'' includes several ritual skin
conditions and cannot safely be equated with modern Hansen disease. The
traditional spiritual senses of sin, doctrinal mixture, estrangement, and
reconciliation summon the worshipper to self-examination; they never license
stigma toward illness, disability, or visible difference. Rightly ordered, the
literal restoration and spiritual typology alike magnify Christ who sees,
cleanses, reconciles, receives worship, and sends the saved person onward.

\subsection*{Offertory: David's changing times enter Christ's self-offering\enspace\properrefs{Off.}}

The antiphon follows Roman-Psalter Psalm~30:15--16 after omitting the opening
\latin{Ego autem/vero}. The Gallican and Clementine have \latin{sortes meae},
``my lots''; the Roman and chant have \latin{témpora mea}, ``my times.'' Jerome's
Hebrew Psalter supports \latin{tempora}; Theodoret already knew both Greek
readings; Cassiodorus comments on the chant's exact form. The appointed verses
map to modern Catholic 31:15--16 when the title is counted as v.~1, or
31:14--15 when it is not.

Literally the psalm turns from grief, abandonment, hostile speech, and a plot
against life to confidence: \latin{Tu es Deus meus}. Theodoret reads blessed
David under Absalom and gives the times a providential range: joy and sorrow,
wealth and poverty, subjection and rule, peace and war remain in God's
distribution. Bellarmine concludes that life depends not on chance but on the
divine will and power. Trust is spoken before deliverance appears.

The wider psalm opens the allegorical sense. Its \latin{In manus tuas commendo
spiritum meum} becomes Christ's word from the Cross. Augustine hears Christ the
Head and redeemed people as one mystical person; Aquinas, following the Gloss,
reads Christ's tribulation and the Church through David's figure. Cassiodorus takes
\latin{tempora} of Christ's assumed humanity: enemies do not determine the end
of his earthly life, for his hour rests in the Father's power. At the Offertory
the Church takes this confession on her lips. Bread and wine are placed upon
the altar while labor, suffering, circumstance, and final hour are entrusted
within Christ's self-oblation.

The two recensions yield complementary moral senses. \latin{Tempora}: number
and quality of life's seasons belong to God, who changes times and ages.
\latin{Sortes}: Augustine reads grace and election, Matthias, Christ's
undivided garment, and the universal Church; grace cannot be purchased or made
the possession of a faction. Aquinas preserves both: lots are divine gift and
allotment, times the duration and condition of life. Providence therefore frees
from fear, chance, faction, and self-merit for courageous action; it does not
identify injustice with God's positive will or abolish prudence.

Anagogically, ``my times'' tend toward the shining divine face and the sweetness
stored for those who fear God. The God who shelters passing generations also
holds the final hour and establishes his servants' work eternally. The healed
Samaritan has returned life and praise to their source; the Offertory gives that
return sacrificial form for the whole Church.

\subsection*{Secret: the true propitiatory sacrifice places people and gifts under mercy\enspace\properrefs{Sec.}}

The prayer opens with two present imperatives of the deponent \latin{propitior}:
\latin{propitiáre pópulo tuo, propitiáre munéribus}. The datives distinguish
people and gifts while placing both beneath the same divine favor. In the
purpose clause \latin{hac oblatióne} accompanies \latin{placátus}; the prayer
names pardon, \latin{indulgéntiam}, before the petitions sought. Nothing in the
liturgical noun requires the later technical canonical category of an
indulgence, and \latin{postuláta} means petitions ordered within the Church's
worship, not every autonomous desire.

The Mass is Christ's true and proper propitiatory sacrifice. The Lord who
offered himself bloodily on the Cross is sacramentally represented and offered
unbloodily through priests; by this oblation the Church asks grace, pardon, and
the Passion's fruits (Trent, Session~XXII, ch.~2; CCC~1365--1367).
\latin{Placátus} names Christ's one oblation at work, not a gift changing an
unwilling deity or an additional victim beside Christ.

The Gospel supplies a fitting ritual analogue. The ten begin with
\latin{miserére nostri}; the Samaritan returns with glorification and thanks.
Tertullian calls that thanksgiving a true oblation made at the true temple and
before the true High Priest, Christ. The return does not pay for the cure; it
offers grace's living answer. Likewise the people do not leave dependence when
they bring gifts. Their times have first been surrendered to God, and both
offerers and offering remain under \latin{propitiáre}.

Aquinas clarifies the movement by distinguishing aspects rather than dividing
the Eucharist. It has the character of sacrifice insofar as it is offered and
of sacrament insofar as it is received; its sacrificial effect concerns the
offerer and those for whom it is offered, while its sacramental effect is in
the receiver (\emph{ST} III, q.~79, a.~5). The Secret consequently asks mercy
upon people and gifts; the Communion confesses bread given \latin{nobis}; the
Postcommunion makes the receivers the subject of \latin{proficiámus}.

Transmission reinforces, but does not create, this theology. The Old Gelasian
has single- and double-imperative witnesses; the Gregorian supplement has the
received form. On Wilson's p.~172 the marginal Ottobonianus~313 cues
\latin{Respice}, \latin{Respice}, \latin{In te speravi}, and \latin{Panem de
caelo} at Sunday~XII; the three received orations appear at Sunday~XIV on
p.~173 with other cues. The evidence shows two older streams later joined; it
does not show the complete 1962 formulary co-located in one ninth-century Mass.

\subsection*{Communion: the heavenly bread brings promise to sacramental fulfilment\enspace\properrefs{Comm.}}

Wisdom~16 belongs to the book's sustained meditation on Exodus. The chapter
contrasts the afflictions of Israel's oppressors with the Father's provident
nourishment of his children. Manna is food of angels, prepared from heaven
without cultivation, bearing every delight and answering every fitting taste.
Verse~21 interprets this abundance as God's sweetness toward his children;
v.~26 reaches the center: created food serves, but God's word preserves those
who believe. The literal sense already joins historical deliverance, filial
providence, creation's service, the efficacy of the word, and nourishment
ordered to faith.

The Communion makes remembered gift present thanksgiving. Wisdom says that God
gave bread to ``them''; the Church sings \latin{Panem de caelo dedísti nobis,
Dómine}. The antiphon replaces \latin{illis} with \latin{nobis}, chooses
\latin{dedísti} in the orbit of Psalm~77's manna language, omits the laborless
preparation, and condenses the clauses of delight and sweetness. The change to
\latin{nobis} draws worshippers into the one economy whose movement the Mass
has proclaimed. The covenant pleaded in the chants and the promise located in
Christ the Seed now take sacramental form for the pilgrim Church.

Origen gives an early direct Christian unfolding. In \emph{Homilia IV in
Psalmum 77} 9 he reproduces Wisdom~16:20--21 so closely that the ready bread,
every delight, fitting taste, and adaptation to desire are unmistakable. Manna
is the Logos feeding souls according to capacity: milk for one, vegetables for
another, solid food for the mature. The one Wisdom stoops without division,
gives what enables growth, and leads the receiver toward fuller participation
in truth. The Collect's \latin{augméntum} thus finds an ancient spiritual
grammar in the Communion.

Gregory of Nyssa develops the same text in \emph{Vita Moysis} II.137--140. The
ready, unsown bread descending from above first signifies the Word's birth from
the Virgin and then the incarnate Logos adapting his nourishment to the soul.
Heavenly manna discloses condescension, announces Incarnation, and becomes an
image of spiritual feeding. Gregory the Great offers a complementary sense in
\emph{Moralia} VI.16.22: as manna answered differing tastes, divine discourse
\latin{qualitáti audiéntium condescéndit}, speaking fittingly to hearers of
different capacities while remaining one word. Manna's christological,
scriptural, and sacramental receptions arise from the same providential
abundance rather than competing to empty the literal gift.

Rabanus makes the sacramental continuation explicit in his direct commentary
on Wisdom. He first preserves Israel's wilderness manna, then reads it through
John~6: the bread from heaven is Christ, who gives his flesh for the world's
life, and true participation leads to eternal life (\emph{Comm. Sap.} III.9,
PL~109:748B--749A). The Logos contemplated by Origen and Gregory is received as
Christ's life-giving gift. The Gospel's thanksgiving and Communion therefore
interpret one another: mercy recognized becomes \emph{eucharistia} before
Christ and participation in the Giver.

Albert the Great develops the relation while commenting on the daily bread of
Matthew~6:11. He names \latin{panis Eucharistiae}, cites Wisdom~16:20--21 almost
entire, and calls the bread heavenly in origin, deifying in substance,
life-giving in causality, unitive sacramentally, viaticum in effect, and a sign
of eternal fruition. These titles gather the Sunday. Heavenly origin answers
the refuge; life-giving power answers restoration; sacramental union gives
charity ecclesial form; viaticum sustains the entrusted times; and the pledge
anticipates eternal redemption.

Aquinas gives the typology precision in \emph{ST} III, q.~73, a.~6.
Melchisedech principally figures the Eucharistic species, ancient sacrifices
the Passion, and manna principally the sacrament's effect: as manna possessed
every sweetness, the grace of this sacrament refreshes the mind in every
respect. The plenitude is the one Christ objectively given, not a substance
changing with appetite or a promise of sensible consolation. Its fruits unfold
as nourishment, union, charity, and pledge of glory in worthy receivers
(qq.~79--80). Angels feed spiritually upon Christ, but sacramental reception
under signs belongs to embodied human pilgrims (q.~80, a.~2).

Bonaventure preserves the order of letter and mystery. He first explains the
historical manna: no agriculture was required; its created sweetness remained
stable; divine power fitted it to the people's needs. He then reads its mystical
fulfilment in Christ under the altar sacrament. Dew signifies grace;
\latin{Eucharistia} is good grace; correspondence to desire becomes fruit
\latin{pro suo desiderio, id est devotione}. The one gift does not vary in
truth, but grace enlarges faith, hope, charity, and devotion. In \emph{Lignum
vitae} 16 the same Wisdom language surrounds the Last Supper, where Christ's
Body and Blood are food, sacrifice, redemption-price, viaticum, and sustenance.

The \emph{Roman Catechism} draws out the pastoral effect. As natural food
sustains and increases bodily life, the Eucharistic banquet sustains and
invigorates grace, increases relish for heavenly things, inflames charity,
strengthens against temptation, repairs daily losses, and aids the passage to
eternal life. St~John Paul~II gives this inheritance a modern saintly voice:
he cites Wisdom~16:20--21 for Sinai's manna, Christ's illumination of Exodus,
and the Eucharist as food both of pilgrims and of messianic fullness (General
Audience, 25 October 2000, 2).

The complete formulary converges here. Covenant fidelity sends heavenly bread;
the virtues dispose the pilgrim to receive; Christ is promise and food; the
Samaritan's return becomes Eucharistic thanksgiving; the oblation remains
beneath mercy; Communion strengthens the road to glory. The antiphon's
Christian \latin{nobis} receives Israel's gift in fulfilment without canceling
the historical \latin{illis}: the same God feeds one pilgrim people through
figure, promise, and sacrament.

\subsection*{Postcommunion: recipients advance in redemption's fruit\enspace\properrefs{Postcomm.}}

The prayer begins with reception accomplished: \latin{Sumptis cæléstibus
sacraméntis}. Its principal verb remains future-facing, \latin{proficiámus}.
The communicants, not the mysteries, are the grammatical subject. Old Gelasian
variants between \latin{proficiat}, \latin{proficiant}, and the received first
person plural explain why the subject must be stated; Cummiskey's historical
English, which changes that relation, cannot control the Latin.

\latin{Ad redemptiónis aetérnae augméntum} does not imply deficiency in
Christ's objective redemption. The increase belongs to grace's fruit and to
the pilgrim's participation. Aquinas says Eucharistic food sustains, increases,
repairs, and delights spiritual life; actual reception augments grace and
perfects life through union with God (\emph{ST} III, q.~79, a.~1 and ad~1).
The sacrament does not introduce the wayfarer immediately into glory but gives
the power to arrive and is therefore viaticum (a.~2 ad~1).

The repeated \latin{augméntum} closes the prayer-cycle. At the beginning the
theological virtues increase; after the heavenly sacraments their possessors
ask to advance toward eternal redemption. The verbs of the whole formulary now
converge: remember, give, make, go, return, arise, offer, receive, advance. None
competes with grace. Each is the creature's real participation in a gift whose
beginning, efficacy, and end remain God's.

The Gospel's final command becomes the liturgical dismissal in miniature.
Christ raises the grateful man and tells him to go. Bede, Bonaventure, Albert,
and Denis read the rising as passage from sin into strong works and the going
as advance toward neighbor, God, and the heavenly homeland. St~Anthony gives a
narrower gloss: \latin{surge} is unity rising to upright works, while
\latin{vade} is humility's keeper going securely. His surrounding exposition
also dwells on return, prostration, thanksgiving, and saving faith.
Faith came crying, hope walked before sight, and charity returned in thanks.
Fed by heavenly bread, the Church now rises toward the refuge where passing
generations enter eternity and redemption bears its imperishable fruit.
