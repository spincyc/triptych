# Fourth Sunday after Pentecost — Research Scope

**Last updated:** 2026-07-18

This record preserves the source roles, searches, and claim boundaries compressed in the reader-facing guide. Complete appointed texts, checksums, image locations, witness differences, and line-by-line collation results remain in [the verified propers](../propers/verified.md).

## Scriptural and Liturgical Scope

- Appointed and immediate contexts studied: Psalms 9, 12, 17, 26, and 78 in full; Luke 4:31–5:16; and Romans 8:1–30.
- Closely related historical and doctrinal texts actually used: 1 Samuel 16; 2 Samuel 2, 5, and 22; 1 Chronicles 6:31–48, 15:16–19, and 25:1–6; Romans 8:11; Philippians 2:12–13; Ezekiel 18:31–32 and 36:22–27 as treated by Augustine; and John 21:1–14.
- The Collect, Secret, and Postcommunion were analyzed in their complete verified Latin forms. The PDF reproduces only the clauses needed for its argument because it is designed for use beside a hand missal.
- The source-grounded synthesis follows four textual stages: light/order/travail; mercy/judgment/Christ’s command; illuminated eyes/resistant wills; refuge/purification/protection. This is an editorial map of the appointed formulary, not a historical-source claim.

## Historical Coordinates of the Direct Scriptural Propers

The one-page historical orientation inventories seven distinct directly appointed passages in Catholic canonical order: Psalm 9:5 and 10 (Alleluia), Psalm 12:4–5 (Offertory), Psalm 17:3 (Communion), Psalm 26:1–3 (Introit, including its appointed psalm verse), Psalm 78:9–10 (Gradual), Luke 5:1–11 (Gospel), and Romans 8:18–23 (Epistle). Psalm numbering follows the Vulgate and missal; the modern Hebrew-based equivalents are Psalms 9, 13, 18, 27, and 79. Composed orations and their biblical echoes were excluded, as were John 21, Ezekiel, and Philippians, which enter the guide only as related illumination. Each proper row reports a traditional composition location and date under the neutral headings `Location` and `Date`; the spanning prose separates inherited placement from historical judgment. Luke alone adds a citation row for the narrated event so that Jesus' setting is not confused with the Evangelist's.

- **Davidic psalms:** the Greek/Latin and Hebrew titles attribute Psalms 9, 12, 17, and 26 to David. Their rows use `c. 1000 BC` as a broad inherited Davidic orientation and `c. 1010 BC` for Psalm 26's specifically pre-anointing title; neither value claims an autograph date. Psalm 9 and Psalm 12 name no recoverable episode or writing site. Psalm 17 has the strongest biographical superscription, placing David's thanksgiving after deliverance from Saul and all enemies and paralleling 2 Samuel 22, but neither the autograph site nor the final form's date is secure. The Septuagint/Vulgate addition to Psalm 26 says “before he was anointed”; because David was anointed in 1 Samuel 16 and again in 2 Samuel 2 and 5, it does not identify a single moment. The *NABRE* treats Psalms 9–10 as one acrostic, Psalm 12/13 as a typical lament, Psalm 17/18 as royal thanksgiving, and Psalm 26/27 as a two-part psalm; it does not provide demonstrable authorial biographies.
- **Asaphic psalm:** Psalm 78/79's title names Asaph. A strict personal attribution places it with David's Levitical choirmaster in the Jerusalem sanctuary orbit around `c. 1000 BC`, requiring its ruined Jerusalem and profaned Temple to be prophetic. The *Catholic Encyclopedia* notes that “Asaph” can designate the singer's family or collected psalter, while the *NABRE* identifies Babylon's destruction of Jerusalem in 587 BC as the probable historical occasion. The guide therefore reports the Davidic-era value only as traditional metadata and makes the later communal horizon explicit.
- **Luke:** the traditional row combines two inherited judgments rather than claiming a proved itinerary: the ancient *Prefatio vel Argumentum Lucae* places St. Luke's Gospel in Achaia, and an early chronology places it before Acts and the close of Paul's first Roman imprisonment, represented compactly as `c. AD 60`. Luke writes an orderly account to Theophilus and similarly catechized Christians after years as Paul's companion. The *NABRE* records the majority post-70 judgment, commonly AD 80–90, a largely Gentile non-Palestinian audience, and no secure composition city. The event row cites Luke 4:31–5:11 for the early Galilean ministry at Lake Gennesaret; its `c. AD 27` follows the harmonized chronology in the *Catholic Encyclopedia*. Luke names neither a civil year nor the exact shore.
- **Romans:** the *NABRE* introduction and Romans 15–16 support Pauline authorship, likely composition at Corinth in AD 56–58 (represented as `c. AD 57`), the mixed Jewish and Gentile Roman house churches, Paul's nearly completed eastern mission, the Jerusalem collection, and his intended Rome–Spain mission. Tertius is the scribe named in Romans 16:22, not a substitute for Paul as author.

Geographic equivalents remain deliberately broad: Judah and the kingdom of Israel lie in the southern Levant; Jerusalem is in the same region; Achaia is in present-day Greece; Rome is in present-day Italy; and Lake Gennesaret is the Sea of Galilee in present-day northern Israel. These equivalents orient the reader without converting a narrated, sanctuary, or audience location into a proved writing site.

## Direct Reception Retained

- St. Augustine, *Exposition on Psalm 26* §§1–3, on divine light, salvation, protection, and courage amid enemies. The English page is headed [Psalm 27](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1801027.htm).
- St. Augustine, *Exposition on Psalm 78* §§12–13, on God as healer, divine help and human action, forgiveness for God’s name, and the nations’ question. The English page is headed [Psalm 79](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1801079.htm).
- St. Augustine, *Exposition on Psalm 9* §§6, 9–10, on equitable divine judgment, knowledge of hearts, poverty, refuge, and tribulation: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1801009.htm).
- St. Augustine, *Exposition on Psalm 12* §§3–6, on the eyes of the heart, the enemy as devil or entrenched habit, sin’s eclipse, and perseverance as mercy. The English page is headed [Psalm 13](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1801013.htm).
- St. Augustine, *Exposition on Psalm 17* §§2–4, on *totus Christus*, received strength, refuge following deliverance, prior divine help, and non-reliance on self. The English page is headed [Psalm 18](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1801018.htm).
- St. John Chrysostom, *Homily 14 on Romans*, directly on Romans 8:18–23: creation’s personified expectation, subjection in hope, travail toward incorruption, firstfruits, and complete bodily liberation: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/210214.htm).
- St. Cyril of Alexandria, *Commentary on Luke* at 5:2–8, on teaching and sign, Christ’s power, apostolic preaching, partners, and Christ’s continuing gift of the catch: [translation](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/cyril_on_luke_02_sermons_12_25.htm).
- St. Ambrose, *Exposition of Luke* IV.68–79, on Peter’s boat, Christological depth at *Duc in altum*, apostolic discourse as nets, humility after fruitless labor, alternative readings of the boats, confession, and vocation: [Latin text](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Expositio_evangelii_secundum_Lucam/IV).

These witnesses expound appointed biblical passages, not the later assembled Sunday formulary.

## Controlling Distinctions

- The Latin *compelle* is rendered “compel,” not softened into “draw.” The adverbial/predicative *propitius* requires God’s action to be understood as favorable or merciful, but grammar alone does not supply a complete theology of grace.
- *Rebelles voluntates* names the worshippers’ rebellious wills. It does not grammatically assert competing wills within each person; Augustine’s divided will is related illumination.
- The received oblations and rebellious wills belong to coordinated petitions. The prayer does not identify the wills as the oblations.
- Luke does not call Simon rebellious. Using his truthful objection, consent, action, confession, and following to imagine merciful compulsion belongs in `The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities`.
- Ambrose offers alternative interpretations of the two boats; none is presented as the single settled patristic identification.
- Augustine’s contrast between Luke and John is documented canonical reception, not Luke’s literal sense or a claimed universal consensus.
- The Alleluia does not place God’s throne above the lake or identify Christ’s boat-seat as the throne. That juxtaposition remains an exploratory proposal.
- Visible abundance is not treated as proof of sanctity, and present strain is not treated as proof that Christ’s word failed.
- The world’s *cursus*, creation’s unwilling subjection to corruption, and the worshippers’ *rebelles voluntates* name different subjects and relations. Cosmic subjection is not silently redescribed as moral rebellion, and the boat or net is not used as a translation of any oration.
- The Gradual’s liturgical order places *Propitius esto* and the nations’ question before returning in the versicle to *Adiuva nos* and *libera nos*. The guide records that arrangement without presenting it as the psalm’s original sequence.

## Survey and Material Limits

- Surveyed accessible NPNF/ANF and New Advent collections, the Ambrose Latin text, Cyril’s Payne Smith translation, public facsimiles and OCR, and targeted Latin and English source searches.
