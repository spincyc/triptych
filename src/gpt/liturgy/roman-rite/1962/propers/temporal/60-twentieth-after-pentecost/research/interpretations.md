# Proposed interpretations

These are two editorial readings of the complete 1962 formulary. Each joins
compatible, checked arguments from several authors; neither is attributed
as a whole to an ancient commentator. Exact source loci, rights, evidence
states and negative searches are in `scope.md`. The three Maternity orations
contribute only in the Low/conventual branch. Their omission at a
nonconventual sung Mass does not break either Sunday argument.

## Reading A: The word heals and teaches faith

**Controlling claim.** The Mass leads the petitioner from acknowledging need
to entrusting himself to the Lord's word, receiving heavenly medicine, and
asking for the obedience that makes sacramental reception fruitful. Healing
in John is bodily and real; the wider liturgical movement also asks for the
healing of sin, fear and resistance to God's will. The decisive inference is
that the father leaves before seeing the result: Christ's word can direct
conduct before possession of confirming sight. This does not deny the value
of the servants' subsequent report, which leads to household faith.

**Compatible authors and the dispute retained.** Gregory, *Homilies on the
Gospels* II.28.1, says the father believes Christ can heal but thinks bodily
presence necessary. Augustine, *Tractates on John* XVI.3–5, gives a harsher
account of his initial unbelief or lukewarmness and contrasts the Galileans'
signs with the Samaritans' hearing. Both expose an inadequate estimate of
Christ's power and allow the word to educate faith; they do not agree on
whether the petitioner initially possesses faith. The reading can use
Gregory to describe an imperfect believer without making Augustine say the
same thing. Augustine's Psalm 118 §§50–56 adds a second substantive argument:
remembering the promise means asking its fulfilment, and comfort amid
humiliation reaches beyond present relief to resurrection. Augustine's
Psalm 144 §§14–18 develops the physician who gives at the fitting season;
its explicit furnace/food comparison helps join trial and providence.

Schuster III, pp.174–178 supplies later, actual reception of this Sunday's
base elements: the Secret's medicine, Communion's word read as Christ,
and Postcommunion's obedience form a sacramental development. These are not
claims that John intended an encoded commentary on this Secret. Jerome on
Daniel 3:29 prevents corporate confession from becoming an accusation that
the ruler's child deserved illness. Gregory II.28.2 adds the social edge:
Christ's regard does not follow the hierarchy of a ruler and a servant.

**Literal sense.** The John narrative concerns a sick son at Capharnaum,
Jesus at Cana, a father who asks, leaves and learns, and the whole household's
belief. The actual liturgical words ask pardon, nourishment, peace and
obedience. Daniel's speakers confess their people's sins; the Psalmist's
humiliation and the Babylonian exiles have their own histories. The child
is not a cipher for a vice and the other passages are not episodes of his
family's story.

**Allegorical sense.** The incarnate Word, whose power is not confined by
distance, heals and gathers a believing household; the Church receives
Christ as heavenly medicine and the pledged fulfilment of God's promise.
This joins Augustine's and Gregory's Christological exegesis with Schuster's
sacramental reception. The whole-Mass connection is editorial and does not
claim an ancient compositional plan.

**Moral sense.** Trust gives rise to an obedient next step before mastery of
the outcome. Wise walking includes humility toward those of lower rank,
mutual service, repentance and praise. It is not a command to refuse medical
care, suppress lament, or regard verification as sinful. Asking and receiving
remain prior to the obedience for which the Postcommunion itself asks.

**Anagogical sense.** Present healing points beyond temporary bodily relief
to enduring peace, resurrection and the sight of God. Augustine's promise
and physician discussions and Schuster's nourishment-to-vision movement
support this horizon. Neither a particular cure nor every desired earthly
outcome is guaranteed to every petitioner.

**What the integrated reading adds.** A separate treatment of each element
can miss the sequence from the Collect's pardon and peace, through the
Gospel's enacted trust, to the Secret's medicine and Postcommunion's request
for obedience. Its strength is one intelligible argument about trust and
fruitful reception. Its risk is making every text a healing metaphor; the
literal confession, hunger, exile and Trinitarian praise must remain distinct.

## Reading B: Learning thanksgiving in exile

**Controlling claim.** The formulary gives a community under trial a way to
confess, lament and praise together without either approving evil or losing
hope. The distinctive inference begins from the apparent conflict between
Ephesians' thanksgiving and song and the Offertory's weeping beside Babylon's
rivers. Thanksgiving belongs to fidelity toward God amid evil; it does not
require celebrating evil itself. The proper's movement forms communal
conduct: mutual service, generous provision and obedient worship sustain a
pilgrim people who remember their promised home.

**Compatible authors and their reasoning.** Chrysostom, *Homilies on
Ephesians* XIX, locates evil in conduct rather than the substance of time,
requires attentive inward psalmody and ends in reciprocal service. His
strong call to thanksgiving in affliction must remain his claim, distinct
from the NPNF editor's narrower gloss on “all things.” Augustine, Psalm 136
§§1–13, distinguishes Babylon's passing loves from Zion, warns against being
carried away by the currents, and can end by urging mutual song even in
exile. This supplies a substantive account of how lament and song belong
together. Jerome's Daniel 3:29 reads the youths' confession as the people's
voice: solidarity in penitence need not accuse each sufferer of personal
guilt. Together these arguments support the community's worship without
flattening the specific historical exiles into a timeless mood.

Bellarmine on Psalms 136:1–4 and 144:15–16 adds later saintly reception:
interior citizenship can coexist with outward responsibilities, and the
open hand rebukes hoarding. Augustine Psalm 144 §15 explicitly joins the
furnace's confession with praise for provision. Schuster's actual Sunday
commentary brings exile, food, medicine and obedience into liturgical
reception. These are complementary contributions, not an invented dispute
between private faith and public charity.

| Element | Contribution to this reading and its evidential level |
| --- | --- |
| Introit | The community confesses shared sin with Azarias while asking mercy; Jerome distinguishes this representative voice from individual guilt. The Psalm verse sets faithful communal walking before the assembly. |
| Collect | Forgiveness and peace release a people for service. Schuster's treatment makes peace a fruit of pardon; the communal emphasis is the editor's development. |
| Maternity Collect | In the commemorative branch, the Word's birth of Mary grounds the pilgrim people's belonging to Christ in real human history. The prayer's help and Augustine XVI.7 illuminate the extension, not a second Gospel appointment. |
| Epistle | Redeeming opportunity in evil days, inward song, thanksgiving and reciprocal submission define the community's response. Chrysostom refuses to call created time itself evil and requires service across social ranks. |
| Gradual | All creatures depend on the open divine hand. Bellarmine's rebuke of hoarding makes the universal claim ethically demanding: those with food must not praise providence while withholding it. Eucharistic nourishment is Schuster's further liturgical reading. |
| Alleluia | Readiness of heart makes praise possible amid threatened circumstances. Psalm 107's wider dependence prevents the isolated joyful line from promising political invulnerability. The precise liturgical address stays distinct from the biblical genitive. |
| Gospel | Faith comes to embrace the household, not only the petitioner. Gregory's ruler/servant comparison undercuts status-based deference; Schuster's household application supports a communal consequence without claiming that every household conversion follows this pattern. |
| Offertory | Exiles remember Zion in truthful lament. Augustine permits fidelity beside Babylon's currents and song to one another; Bellarmine distinguishes outward responsibilities from inward captivity. Memory redirects worship rather than requiring escape from ordinary duties. |
| Secret | Heavenly medicine expels the vices that deform communal life. Schuster's Eucharistic reading joins the gift received to a healed capacity for charity; it does not reduce sacramental grace to social utility. |
| Maternity Secret | Present and perpetual peace widen the community's hope when the commemoration is admitted. Mary's intercession belongs within Christ's one saving work, not an alternative source of peace. |
| Trinity Preface | The assembly's song is directed to one divine majesty, with equal glory and coeternal Persons. This is a fitting destination of Spirit-filled thanksgiving through Christ to the Father, without claiming the Preface was composed for this Epistle. |
| Communion | Remembering the promise gives a humiliated pilgrim people comfort. Augustine's ecclesial and resurrection reading and Bellarmine's prayer-for-fulfilment explanation distinguish faithful petition from correcting a forgetful deity. |
| Postcommunion | Obedience gives communal thanksgiving durable conduct. The assembly asks for this grace; the sacrament is not earned by a prior successful social program. |
| Maternity Postcommunion | Conditional communion prayer asks cleansing and fellowship with heavenly remedy under Mary's intercession. It adds the purified community's hope, while the Sunday remains complete without it. |

**Literal sense.** Daniel's furnace prayer, Babylon's defeated exiles and
Ephesians' addressed community occupy distinct settings; John tells a
particular household's healing and belief. Their liturgical juxtaposition
does not make them a single historical scene. The appointed Epistle includes
5:21 and the Offertory really weeps. Preserve both, rather than substituting
cheerfulness for thanksgiving.

**Allegorical sense.** Augustine's two cities and pilgrim song let Zion
signify the Church's promised home; the gathered household and sacramental
medicine show Christ forming his people. This Christian reception does not
erase Jewish captivity or assert that the literal text secretly names every
modern institution as Babylon. The violent unappointed end of Psalm 136 is
not a charter against people; Augustine's spiritual reading directs its
force against emerging evil.

**Moral sense.** The faithful resist corrupt conduct in their time through
prudent action, attentive song, mutual service and generosity. Bellarmine's
open-hand application places an obligation on those who can feed others.
Lament can tell the truth about injustice; gratitude does not forbid protest
or declare cruelty good. Neither ancient blame of poverty nor a generic
claim of collective guilt diagnoses a particular victim.

**Anagogical sense.** The pilgrim people's final peace and song belong to
Zion fulfilled, resurrection and the sight of God. Present gifts sustain a
journey whose completion is not yet possessed. This hope sustains earthly
responsibilities instead of making their abandonment a condition of holiness.

**What the integrated reading adds.** Element-by-element commentary can miss
the demanding conversation between the Epistle's thanksgiving, the Gradual's
universal nourishment and the Offertory's tears. Their sequence makes
worship responsible to affliction and to hungry neighbors. Its risk is
turning every word into an account of social exile; actual illness, personal
faith, forgiveness and the transcendence of divine praise retain their force.

## Exegetical comparisons before drafting

| Why believe in John 4:50 and again in 4:53? | Trust acts on Christ's word before the report and is then deepened or enlarged. | The household outcome extends a petitioner's encounter into shared faith. | Augustine's severe estimate of the initial approach and Gregory's imperfect healing-faith differ. Do not turn two narrative moments into a mechanical universal ladder. |
| Are signs rejected? | Jesus corrects dependence on demanded sight or bodily presence; the reported coincidence remains evidential. | The sign serves a household's faith rather than the ruler's status. | John calls it a sign in unappointed v.54. Neither author licenses contempt for reason or medicine. |
| Does confession explain the child's illness? | No: Jerome's representative confession separates communal need from particular blame. | Confession expresses solidarity and need for mercy without convicting each sufferer. | The narratives do not state a causal connection. Do not supply one. |
| How can “all” be fed while the world suffers hunger? | Augustine's fitting-time and final-salvation account qualifies immediate expectations. | Bellarmine's account of hoarding makes human provision and justice a present obligation. | Complementary theological and moral readings; neither explains every instance or blames a sufferer. Psalm 144 is not originally an exclusively Eucharistic text. |
| How can one thank for all things and weep in Babylon? | The promise permits trust and petition through unhealed circumstances. | Inward fidelity, truthful lament and mutual song coexist; evil conduct remains evil. | Chrysostom extends thanksgiving to affliction; NPNF note 404 prefers blessings. Attribute the disagreement, not a unanimous gloss. |
| Whose “glory” is in the Alleluia? | Schuster reads the liturgical address as God, the soul's glory. | The prepared heart and Psalm 107's threatened community join praise with dependence. | The Mass's tibi, gloria mea differs from the received biblical wording; Bellarmine's gloss on Psalm 56:8 does not settle that liturgical address. |
| Does the sacrament require worthiness earned beforehand? | The gifts heal; the Postcommunion asks grace for obedient reception. | The healing gift bears fruit in mutual service and generous conduct. | Schuster's disposition language must be read with the prayer's own petition and Augustine on divine help. |
| How do the Maternity prayers change the reading? | They emphasize the incarnate Word and interceded healing. | They extend shared peace and purified belonging. | A conditional three-prayer commemoration, not the Marian feast's complete Mass; no extra Preface, Gospel or independent saving agent. |
| Do evil days identify a date or an autumn design? | They describe circumstances requiring wise faith. | They expose a moral environment requiring communal fidelity. | Schuster's vintage and aging application is later reception, not demonstrated original composition or the universal calendar's climate. |

Both readings can be developed without suppressing the other. Reading A's
central question is how Christ educates a petitioner's trust; Reading B's is
how a community praises amid shared trial. The first would naturally organize
a homily around the father's departure at the word, the second around the
Epistle's song beside the Offertory's tears. Neither outline is a drafted
homily or a decision that historical compilers intended this unity. The
concise study should compare them at these exact questions rather than run
two disconnected miniature essays. Each must retain its own four senses in
the expansive study.
