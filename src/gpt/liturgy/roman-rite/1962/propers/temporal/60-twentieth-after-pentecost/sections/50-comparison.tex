\section{Trust on the journey, song toward home}
The father's departure and the captives' remembrance answer different
questions. The first shows how a word becomes the ground of action
before its result has been seen. The second shows how desire remains
faithful when present circumstances offer no adequate home. Both reject
the assumption that trust requires possession of the outcome. Their
practical accents nevertheless differ. The first asks what obedient
step Christ's word now makes possible. The second asks what kind of
shared life can continue to praise without abandoning those who weep.

The difference is especially clear in the Gradual. Augustine's physician
addresses the person waiting for relief: a delayed gift need not mean
abandonment, and God's final gift exceeds the benefits first sought.
Bellarmine's open hand addresses the person able to give: praise of
providence carries an obligation to imitate its generosity. The waiting
person and the giving person may be the same person at different times.
Neither answer cancels the other, and neither furnishes a diagnosis of
every particular deprivation.

The Gospel keeps a genuine exegetical disagreement within this common
hope. Gregory sees faith already present but limited by a demand for
bodily presence. Augustine judges the starting point more severely.
Their shared conclusion concerns the sufficiency of Christ's power;
their different accounts of the petitioner remain audible. Similarly,
Chrysostom's thanksgiving in affliction is stronger than the restriction
proposed by the NPNF editor. Gratitude's demanding reach cannot simply
be attributed to every reader of the verse.

The prayers gather the two movements. Pardon and peace prepare a
petitioner for trust and a community for service. Heavenly medicine
heals the heart whose vices injure both its own obedience and its
neighbors. The Communion's promise sustains hope, and the Postcommunion
asks that reception bear fruit in conduct. Where the Marian commemoration
is present, its three prayers confess the incarnate Word and extend the
petitions for peace and cleansing through Mary's intercession. Where
it is absent, the Sunday still carries the whole argument. In both
branches the Trinity receives the assembly's thanksgiving: the journey
of trust and the song of pilgrimage have their end in God.
