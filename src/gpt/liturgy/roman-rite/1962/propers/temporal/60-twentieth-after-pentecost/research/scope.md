## Scripture in its complete immediate contexts

The selected historical English is the registered Douay–Rheims,
Challoner/Gutenberg 1581 text: complete Daniel 3, John 4, Ephesians 4–6,
and Psalms 107, 118, 136 and 144 were read, not just appointed verse matches.
Psalm numbers in this study are Vulgate numbers; NPNF’s Augustine volume uses
the corresponding Hebrew-numbered headings 108, 119, 137 and 145.

Daniel 3 supplies the furnace narrative, Azarias’s communal penitential
prayer, the three youths’ praise and the deliverance. Verses 17–18 already
refuse to make fidelity depend on rescue. The Introit does not simply quote
three verses in order: it rearranges and adapts the confession and mercy
petitions. Azarias speaks in the narrative, not Daniel himself. The children
refuse idolatry yet confess their people’s sins; the Introit is no warrant
for attributing a sick child’s illness to a particular personal offence.
The Psalm 118 verse names the undefiled way, but its complete poem also
prays for teaching, mercy, help, life and recovery of the wandering sheep.

Ephesians 4–6 locates wise walking in renewed communal life, truthful speech,
forgiveness and service. The appointed unit ends with reciprocal submission
at 5:21; it must not stop at inward song or gratitude. The following household
instruction is context, not this Epistle’s appointed text. Its hierarchy and
slavery language must not be smuggled into a claim that Christ endorses
coercion. The end of the letter charges masters to abandon threats because
both stand under one Lord without partiality.

John 4 contrasts mediated testimony, personal hearing and sight of signs.
The Samaritans move from the woman’s report to Christ’s word (39–42); the
Galileans receive him after seeing signs (45). The father initially asks for
bodily presence, believes the word and departs (50), then believes with his
household after the matching report (53). The successive beliefs require
interpretation; they are not a scribal mistake to smooth away. Verse 48
addresses a plural audience through the petitioner. The appointed beginning
omits the first part of 46 naming Cana, but narrative geography still places
Jesus there and the child at Capharnaum. Verse 54 calls this the second sign
in Galilee; it is contextual, outside the appointed ending.

Psalm 144 is royal praise of the Creator’s generosity, mercy, kingdom and
faithfulness, not originally an exclusive statement about Eucharistic food.
Its universal horizon tests any claim that a single cure guarantees immediate
relief for everyone. Psalm 107 combines ready-hearted praise with threatened
communal dependence and the futility of merely human rescue. Its borrowed
parts are recognized by the commentators; a historical occasion for the
finished psalm is not thereby established. Psalm 136 remembers a defeated
Jerusalem in Babylonian captivity. Its withheld music protests coercion and
mockery; the violent close is real context but unappointed. Christian spiritual
readings must not erase either the historical catastrophe or the difference
between remembering violence and commanding it. Psalm 118:49–50 puts hope in
God’s promise amid humiliation; the following stanza maintains obedience
amid derision, and speaks of song in pilgrimage. The Communion stops before
the biblical explanation that the word gives life.

reviewed historical appendix in the expansive study as well as on the concise
study’s second page; do not leave dates orphaned in a derived document.
