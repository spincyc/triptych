\section*{The Propers: Notable and Quotable}

These four checked afterlives move appointed wording into aesthetic,
commercial, military-political, and institutional registers. They are not
offered as exegesis; each depends on an English or ritual transmission whose
distance from the Latin Mass text is stated.

\begin{afterlife}{Epistle, 1 Cor. 15:8 --- William Morris turns apostolic untimeliness into aesthetic distance}
\textbf{Appointed phrase.} Paul says the risen Christ appeared to him
\latin{tamquam abortivo}; the Douay has ``as by one born out of due time.''

\textbf{Later use and locus.} William Morris opens the ``Apology'' to
\work{The Earthly Paradise} (London: F.~S. Ellis, 1868), stanza~4, p.~2:
``Dreamer of dreams, born out of my due time, \textbar\ Why should I strive
to set the crooked straight?''

\textbf{The turn.} Paul's self-abasement occurs while asserting a real, late
apostolic encounter and mission. Morris adds ``my'' and makes untimeliness a
Victorian artist's estrangement from industrial modernity. The apostolic
commission becomes a disclaimer of power to repair the age.

\textbf{Limit.} The 1868 first edition is public domain. Morris does not cite
the Mass; his verbal source is the biblical English idiom shared by the Douay
and King James traditions.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel, Mark 7:34 --- \emph{Effeta} becomes a body deodorant}
\textbf{Appointed phrase.} \latin{Ephphetha, quod est adaperire} enters the
Roman baptismal rite as \latin{Ephpheta}; the rite then speaks of an ``odor of
sweetness.''

\textbf{Later use and locus.} A Willis Pike Co. Laboratories advertisement in
the \work{Perth Amboy Evening News}, 19 June 1912, ed.~2, p.~2, sells
``Effeta, The Body Deodorant'' with the slogan ``Be thou opened unto odor of
sweetness.'' A second dated occurrence is the \work{Norwich Bulletin},
22 June 1912, p.~6.

\textbf{The turn.} The copywriter splices the Gospel command to the baptismal
odor formula. Opening ears and forming confession becomes a twenty-five-cent
promise about perspiration. Sacred language is not merely quoted; its ritual
syntax is redirected into sales copy.

\textbf{Limit.} The newspapers are public domain. The immediate source is the
baptismal rite, which itself receives Mark~7; this is not evidence that the
advertiser knew the Sunday Mass.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Introit verse, Ps. 67:2 --- Cromwell at Dunbar}
\textbf{Appointed phrase.} \latin{Exsurgat Deus, et dissipentur inimici eius};
the Douay reads, ``Let God arise, and let his enemies be scattered.''

\textbf{Later use and locus.} Captain John Hodgson reports that, as the
Scottish line broke at Dunbar on 3 September 1650, Oliver Cromwell exclaimed,
``Now let God arise, and his enemies shall be scattered'' (\work{Original
Memoirs}, Edinburgh, 1806, pp.~147--48). Thomas Carlyle transcribes the scene
in \work{Oliver Cromwell's Letters and Speeches} (1861), II, 332--33; at the
opening of Barebone's Parliament in 1653 Cromwell read Psalm~68 again (III,
171--72).

\textbf{The turn.} A liturgical entrance verse becomes a battlefield
victory-cry and later a political text for a revolutionary assembly. The
speaker moves from petition to reading military success as the verse's
present fulfillment.

\textbf{Limit.} The wording reaches us through Hodgson's recollection,
published a century and a half after the battle; it is absent from Cromwell's
own letter written from Dunbar.
The English is from the psalter/King James stream, not the Douay.
\end{afterlife}

\begin{afterlife}{Gospel, Mark 7:34 --- a healing command becomes an institutional surname}
\textbf{Appointed phrase.} \latin{Ephphetha}, ``Be thou opened.''

\textbf{Later use and locus.} The \work{Scranton Tribune}, 14 November 1895,
p.~3, describes the Ephphatha Guild at St.~Luke's: between fifty and sixty
deaf members, an affiliated Penny Club, services, and fundraising fairs. The
paper itself derives the name from Christ's command. The 1969 \work{Council
Membership Directory} of Organizations Serving the Deaf (ERIC ED032704)
lists an Ephphatha Conference (printed p.~11) and, at printed p.~25,
Ephphatha Services for Deaf and Blind together with its Ephphatha Services
Board.

\textbf{The turn.} A word addressed once to one healed person becomes the
durable name of guilds, boards, services, and conferences. The records
document church-affiliated organizations that place the command in
institutional life.

\textbf{Limit.} These are functioning church-affiliated institutions, not
secular exegesis. The 1895 newspaper wrongly calls the Semitic word Greek;
its claim is retained only as the source's own description. The newspaper is
public domain; the ERIC directory is cited briefly.
\end{afterlife}
