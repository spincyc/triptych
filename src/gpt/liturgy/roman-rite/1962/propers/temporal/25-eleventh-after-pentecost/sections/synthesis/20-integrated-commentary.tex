\subsection*{The risen Christ makes enemies into a household}

The Mass's first and last social images are a house and shared substance. The
Introit's God inhabits his holy place, makes the concordant dwell in a house,
and gives strength to his people; the Communion asks each member of that house
to honor the Lord from substance and firstfruits. The intervening propers show
how such communion is created. It is neither natural affinity nor an exchange
among the already worthy. The Collect places the house beneath mercy that
exceeds merit and request, and the Epistle places within it a man who had
persecuted the Church.

Patristic reception gives this movement its ecclesial depth. Hilary and
Cassiodorus join Psalm~67's house to the believers of one heart and soul in
Acts~4:32. Augustine says that grace builds the divine dwelling; Theodoret
sees persons directed toward the one aim of virtue. The psalm's divine arising
is read by Latin and Greek Fathers through Christ's Resurrection, so the
strength given at the Introit is already paschal power. Paul's history then
discloses a striking form of that victory: the risen Christ appears to an enemy
and grace makes him an apostolic witness. Saint Oscar Romero explicitly joins
the Sunday Gospel with 1~Corinthians~15:9--10 in this way---Christ remakes the
sinner rather than annihilating him.

The house therefore possesses a conversion-shaped unity. Conscience is not
ignored but pardoned; Paul does not conceal persecution but receives a new
identity; the solitary are not absorbed anonymously but given a dwelling. The
same grace that scatters enmity creates concord. When substance is later
offered, it belongs to this paschal household: property is no longer the wall
of private possession but material capable of honoring God and serving his
members.

\subsection*{Reception, opening, and the truth of confession}

The governing relation between Epistle and Gospel is more exact than a general
theme of hearing. Paul defines the Gospel before Mark narrates the opened ears.
It is Christ's death for sins, burial, Resurrection on the third day according
to the Scriptures, and appearances to witnesses. Paul received this and handed
it on; the Corinthians received it, stand in it, are saved through it, and must
hold it fast. Christian hearing therefore has an apostolic object before it
becomes subjective response.

Mark shows the incarnate Lord creating the capacity for that response. Christ
takes the man aside, touches ears and tongue, looks heavenward, groans, and
commands \latin{Ephphetha}. Hearing opens first; then the bond of the tongue is
released and speech becomes right. Gregory the Great's direct exposition,
appointed in the Sunday Office, unfolds the economy: fingers are gifts of the
Spirit, saliva is wisdom from divine speech, the gaze reveals the heavenly
source, the groan teaches prayer, and right speech is confirmed by right
action. Bede gives the same movement a catechetical form: withdrawal from the
crowd, inward hearing, savor of wisdom, and confession of faith. Saint Anthony
hears Old and New Testaments through the two ears and requires deeds to agree
with the confession. These readings inhabit the Gospel's own theological
grammar: the incarnate Word ministers the Spirit and Wisdom through bodily
signs so that a human person may hear and answer God.

The Alleluia supplies a choral analogue. Psalm~80's complete source verse
commands Israel to receive a psalm and give the instruments; Augustine and
Cassiodorus read the interplay as heavenly preaching answered by embodied
works of mercy. The chant condenses that verse, so their instrument exegesis
does not describe every sung word, but its principle fits the whole Mass:
received word and returned deed must sound together. Gregory's Gospel
conclusion says the same thing in another register---do before teaching.

Speech is further disciplined by Christ's command of silence. Augustine uses
the forbidden witnesses' zeal to awaken authorized preachers; Leo~XIV places
the prohibition within Mark's paschal pedagogy, before the mystery of Cross and
Resurrection is fully known. The Mass therefore honors proclamation without
identifying truth with publicity. The ear governs the tongue because the
confession must be received, paschal, ecclesial, and embodied in action.

\subsection*{The cry, the groan, and the word that heals}

The chant dramatizes prayer. Psalm~27 moves from cry to help; the Gradual
reverses it: first the heart trusts, receives help, the flesh flowers, and
praise becomes willing. Then comes \latin{Ad te, Domine, clamavi}. Its exact
Psalm~34:22 phrase, \latin{ne discedas a me}, makes feared silence a plea for
presence: do not be silent; do not depart.

The Gospel answers without discursive abundance. Christ's fingers and saliva
are already a bodily word; his gaze joins the act to heaven; his groan bears
compassion and prayer; one imperative opens what was bound. Gregory teaches
that Christ does not groan from impotence but instructs the supplicant to direct
desire heavenward. Theophylact hears both filial communion and compassion for
wounded nature. Divine eloquence here is efficacious presence: the Word speaks
one word, and hearing and speech come into being.

The Offertory then reverses the Gradual's phrase around the same verb:
\latin{Domine, clamavi ad te, et sanasti me}. The cry feared silence; after
Mark, it remembers healing and becomes extolment while the gifts are prepared.
The relation is a property of the received liturgical sequence rather than a
claim about the psalm's first composition. As prayer, however, it is exact and
fruitful. Divine silence is answered by the incarnate Lord's touch, groan, and
command; the creature's answer is thanksgiving offered at the altar.

This movement also clarifies Christian jubilation. Augustine's reading of
Psalm~80 distinguishes joy in God from the crowd's empty shout and can speak of
a jubilation exceeding articulate language. Mark, meanwhile, presents impaired
speech as a bodily affliction Christ truly heals. The two truths belong
together. Speech and hearing are created goods; the power of verbal fluency is
not the measure of divine praise. Right confession is the fruit of grace, and
its deepest harmony is word joined to deed.

\subsection*{One bodily economy from Resurrection to remedy}

The formulary's bodily vocabulary forms a sustained paschal arc: God gives
strength; flesh flowers again; Christ restores ears and tongue; the singer is
upheld and healed; service becomes help for fragility; barns and presses are
filled; mind and body receive heavenly remedy. In their literal senses these
passages concern real divine help, restored vigor, a concrete healing,
material firstfruits, and sacramental aid. Patristic and liturgical reception
unfolds their allegorical and anagogical depth without dissolving those goods.

Irenaeus and Tertullian use 1~Corinthians~15 to defend the real flesh of the
Christ who died, was buried, and rose. Theodoret reads Psalm~27 as David's
restored bodily vigor after flight and danger; Augustine, Cassiodorus, and
Aquinas receive \latin{refloruit caro mea} through Christ's
Resurrection and the members who will flower in him. The same order appears in
Psalm~29. Theodoret preserves Hezekiah's healing and reads the title through
human nature renewed in Christ; Augustine and Cassiodorus see Christ's body as
the house dedicated by Resurrection; Aquinas sees the Church now under
construction and awaiting final dedication.

Mark gives this paschal pattern a face. Catholic typology does not choose
between the healed man as a real person and as a sign within the Church's
reading: the spiritual sense rests upon the historical healing and extends its
grace-filled pattern to every soul brought to Christ. Pseudo-Jerome can see
humanity in the man; Bede can see catechesis; Ambrose's mystagogy can receive
ears and nostrils into the preparatory \emph{Ephpheta} rite; Lawrence can hear
the Creator's \latin{Bene omnia fecit} renewed in redemption. These show the
fruitfulness of the historical miracle in the Church's sacramental life.

The Postcommunion gives the pattern its doctrinal end. Through sacramental
reception the Church asks help for mind and body, salvation in both, and the
fullness of heavenly remedy. Irenaeus's Eucharistic realism supplies the
patristic grammar: bread and wine become Eucharist, and mortal flesh nourished
by Christ is destined for incorruption. Schuster accordingly relates bodily
contact with Christ's sacramental Body to the body's promised raising. Romero's
integral healing---bodily, spiritual, social, and eternal---expresses the same
refusal to divide the human person. The Gospel manifests compassion for
concrete suffering; the sacrament strengthens the whole person through God's
providence; resurrection completes the remedy.

\subsection*{Firstfruits: grace returned without ceasing to be grace}

Four texts make a theology of fruitful return. The Collect begins with
\latin{abundantia pietatis}: mercy exceeds merit and request and adds what
prayer cannot presume. The Epistle ends with grace that has not become void in
Paul. The Communion sings filled barns and overflowing presses. The
Postcommunion asks to glory in the fullness of heavenly remedy. Between divine
abundance and eschatological fullness stand witness, praise, sacrifice,
firstfruits, and works of mercy.

The Secret states how human action belongs inside grace. The Church names her
worship \latin{servitus} and what she offers \latin{munus}; she nevertheless
asks God to make the gift acceptable and to make it \latin{subsidium} for
fragility. Schuster hears \latin{servitus} as the Church's priestly
\emph{leitourgia}. The offering is not unreal because it depends upon grace.
It is most truly the Church's service when divine favor receives it and returns
it as help.

Proverbs gives this return an economic and moral body. The Origen-attributed
catena fragment directs firstfruits toward Church and poor and extends them to
prayer and virtue. The \emph{Apostolic Constitutions} joins the verses to food,
clothing, and ransom from righteous earnings. Bede's literal sense requires
goods justly possessed and alms directed to God's honor rather than applause;
his spiritual sense returns every good work to grace as source; his anagogical
sense sees the barns as heavenly satiety. Salvian says what was first received
from God is returned in devotion and justice. Aquinas likewise permits only
lawfully held goods to be offered and sees firstfruits as confession that all
increase was first God's gift.

The Communion promise therefore belongs to Wisdom's order of worship, justice,
mercy, and heavenly reward. It is not a prosperity bargain. The filled barn is
already opened toward the neighbor; the winepress flows into praise; the final
abundance is the remedy in which mind and body glory. Grace does not become
private possession when it bears fruit. It becomes communion: the Gospel
received is handed on, hearing becomes right confession, healed life becomes
thanksgiving, property becomes firstfruits, service becomes sacramental help,
and the whole person is returned to God in the risen Christ.
