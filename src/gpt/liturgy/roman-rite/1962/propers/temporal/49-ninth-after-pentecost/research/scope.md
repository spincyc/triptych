## Historical Coordinates of the Direct Scriptural Propers

The reader-facing table gives inherited metadata under neutral `Location` and `Date` fields and qualifies it in prose. Gospel composition, narrated event, recipient geography, and a psalm title's narrated circumstance remain distinct.

- **Psalm 8:** the Hebrew and Greek/Latin title names David, so a broad Davidic kingdom horizon, about 1000 BC, is a responsible traditional orientation. No superscription fixes a writing site or moment in David's life. The hymn moves from the Lord's world-filling name and majesty to human smallness and entrusted dignity; Hebrews 2 later receives it christologically. Modern authorship, date, place, and first performance remain unknown.
- **Psalm 18 (modern 19):** the superscription names David, but no narrative fixes a place or life stage. The psalm joins creation's wordless witness to praise of the Lord's instruction and a servant's prayer for cleansing. A traditional Davidic horizon can orient the table, while modern analysis recognizes the poem's two large movements and cannot securely recover an autograph date or site.
- **Psalm 53 (modern 54):** the title associates the prayer with the Ziphites' report to Saul; 1 Samuel records such reports at 23:19 and 26:1. The traditional horizon is David's pre-royal flight in Judah, broadly about 1010 BC. Neither report supplies the psalm's writing desk, and modern authorship and composition date remain uncertain. The title is received tradition, not independent proof that the words were composed during one recoverable episode.
- **Psalm 58 (modern 59):** the title associates the lament with Saul's agents watching David's house in order to kill him, corresponding to 1 Samuel 19:11. This supplies a traditional pre-royal danger horizon, not a proved date or writing location. Modern historical authorship, date, and place remain unknown.
- **Luke:** ancient ecclesial tradition identifies St. Luke, the Antiochene physician and companion of Paul, and places his writing in Achaia around AD 60. Current Catholic orientation commonly dates the final Gospel about AD 80–90, regards its city as unknown, and understands its first audience as substantially Gentile and outside Palestine. Luke's literary journey reaches Jerusalem at 19:28; the narrated lament occurs on the approach from the Mount of Olives and the action continues in Jerusalem's Temple, traditionally during Jesus' final ministry about AD 29–30.
- **John:** ancient tradition identifies John the Apostle and Ephesus; current Catholic orientation commonly places the Gospel's final form near the end of the first century while preserving debate about stages of composition and the Evangelist's relation to the beloved disciple. The narrated bread-of-life discourse follows the Galilean feeding and is located by John 6:59 in the synagogue at Capernaum, traditionally about AD 29. Composition and event are not collapsed.
- **1 Corinthians:** Pauline authorship is both traditional and the mainstream historical judgment. Paul wrote from Ephesus about AD 56, during his third missionary journey, to the Church he had founded at Corinth. The community included Gentile and Jewish Christians; chapters 8–10 answer disputed participation in idol-associated meals. At 10:1 Paul calls the wilderness generation `our fathers` and applies their history to the baptized Corinthian assembly.

Geographic equivalents remain orienting rather than probative. Judah and Jerusalem are in the southern Levant; Capernaum is by the Sea of Galilee in present-day northern Israel; Ephesus is near present-day Selçuk in Türkiye; Corinth is in present-day Greece; and Achaia is the corresponding Greek regional horizon.

## Direct Patristic, Medieval, and Later Reception Checked

### The appointed Psalms

- **Greek sampling for all four appointed Psalms:** Theodoret of Cyrus, [*Interpretatio in Psalmos*](https://catholiclibrary.org/library/view?chunk.id=00000003&docId=%2FFathers-Synchronized-EN%2FTheodoretus_of_Cyrrhus__Interpretatio_in_Psalmos.en.html), was checked in the direct Greek and synchronized English at Psalms 8 (PG 80, cols. 913–920), 18 (cols. 992–1000), 53 (cols. 1264–1268), and 58 (cols. 1304–1316). At Psalm 8:2 he takes `how admirable` intensively and joins the universal name to recognition of God as Creator; the full exposition then turns to divine care for humanity. At Psalm 18:9–12 he distinguishes precepts that gladden by showing the cause of justification, the command that illuminates the mind's sight, fear that prepares eternal good, and judgments that declare divine decisions; their sweetness belongs to a life ordered as truly human. At Psalm 53:6–8 he reads `behold` as foreknowledge of help not far off and willing praise as the ensuing sacrifice. At Psalm 58:2 he begins with the literal justice of seeking release from unprovoked attackers.
- **Psalm 8, Latin and later reception:** Augustine §4 directly receives the Lord's admirable name throughout the earth through Christ's humiliation and exaltation while the full exposition preserves creaturely wonder: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1801008.htm). Aquinas's [*Super Psalmo 8*](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~Psalm.Ps8) directly treats the divine excellence manifested throughout earth and above the heavens. Bellarmine's Psalm 8 §1 supplies later saintly confirmation that created beauty makes the Creator's name admirable while divine magnificence exceeds the world.
- **Psalm 18, Latin and later reception:** Augustine §§9–12 reads right judgments as freely loved, the command as illumination of the inner sight, chaste fear as enduring, and keeping as itself a reward: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1801019.htm). Aquinas's [*Super Psalmo 18*](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~Psalm.Ps18) directly expounds the same verses; *Summa theologiae* [II–II, q. 19, a. 10](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~ST.II-II.Q19.A10) is illuminating doctrinal reuse rather than direct Psalm commentary. Bellarmine, Psalm 18 §§8–11, joins good conscience, illumination, filial fear, gold and honey, and the servant's experienced keeping in his [1866 commentary](https://www.ecatholic2000.com/bellarmine/commentary-on-psalms.shtml).
- **Psalm 53, Latin and later reception:** Augustine §§4–5 and 8 directly treats prayer by God's name, present interior aid, and willing praise: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1801054.htm). Hilary's direct Psalm 53 exposition supplies confidence under persecution in §§9–10. Bellarmine §§4–6 treats `behold` as sudden assurance, the present tense as confidence, and willing praise. Aquinas's [Matthew commentary I.4.120](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~Matt.C1.L4.n120) invokes the Introit clause as ready divine help amid Joseph's turmoil; it is reuse, not direct Psalm exposition.
- **Psalm 58, Latin and later reception:** Augustine §4 redirects the prayer from human enemies toward the devil, fallen spirits, and temptations: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1801059.htm). Bellarmine, Psalm 58 §§1–2, first keeps David's literal danger and then generalizes the prayer to any just person in mortal danger.

### 1 Corinthians 10:6–17

- **Canonical argument and wilderness map.** The appointed verses depend on 10:1–5, where sea, cloud, food, drink, and Christ the rock address the baptized Corinthians. Verse 6 evokes Numbers 11's craving; verse 7 quotes Exodus 32:6's idol feast; verse 8 evokes Numbers 25; verse 9 the testing and serpents of Numbers 21; and verse 10 murmuring and the destroyer. Paul's twenty-three thousand at verse 8 and Numbers 25:9's twenty-four thousand are reported as an inherited textual difference, not silently harmonized. At verse 14, `therefore` leads directly from divinely aided endurance to flight from idolatry; verses 16–17 then interpret cup and bread as participation in Christ and the many as one body.
- **St. John Chrysostom, Homilies 23–24 — direct exegesis.** [Homily 23](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/220123.htm) applies verses 6–12 to Corinthian craving, idolatry, sexual sin, testing, murmuring, and presumption. [Homily 24 §1](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/220124.htm) reads verse 13 as consolation: endurance depends upon God's gracious aid and supplied outcome, not unaided power. Sections 2–4 then move through Eucharistic participation to communion with Christ and one another and expose division as contradictory to the one bread.
- **Ambrosiaster, Commentary on First Corinthians 10:1–17 — direct Latin exegesis.** The public [Latin text](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Commentaria_in_Epistolam_ad_Corinthios_Primam_(Ambrosiaster)) was checked against Heinrich J. Vogels, ed., *CSEL* 81/2 (1968), pp. 108–113 for the central verses. At verse 12 Ambrosiaster addresses Corinthians who presume on knowledge and lawful food while scandalizing weaker siblings; their supposed doctrinal advance has made them worse. At verse 13 he distinguishes a temptation ending from strength given to endure it; at verses 16–17 the one bread and cup require one mind and action among members.
- **St. Thomas Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians X — direct medieval exegesis.** [Lecture 2, nos. 522–531](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~1Cor.C10.L2) maps the wilderness episodes, discusses the twenty-three/thousand question, insists the events truly occurred and were written for correction, and distinguishes sins of heart, action, and speech. [Lecture 3, nos. 532–535](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~1Cor.C10.L3) treats uncertainty of standing, temptation and consent, ready help, grace, and constancy. [Lecture 4, nos. 537–542](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~1Cor.C10.L4) proceeds from flight from idolatry to Eucharistic participation and ecclesial unity.
- **St. Augustine, *On the Good of Widowhood* §21 — illuminating reuse.** Augustine uses verse 13 against claims that free will can avoid temptation without grace and prayer: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1311.htm). This supports dependence upon God but is not continuous commentary on the pericope.
- Paul begins with `our fathers` at 10:1 and tells those who think they stand to take heed. Romans 9:4–5 and 11:1–2, 11–32 name Israel's gifts and irrevocable calling while warning Gentile Christians against boasting.

### Luke 19:41–47

- **Origen, Homily 38 on Luke — direct Greek-patristic exegesis in Jerome's Latin translation.** The formerly unresolved catena fragment was independently located in [*Homily 38*](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Translatio_XXXIX_Homiliarum), PL 26, cols. 363–366. Origen makes Christ's tears ground the beatitude of mourning; turns the warning toward Christians after Gospel teaching and sacramental initiation; condemns selling Spirit-given teaching; and closes at the Savior's banquet, bread of life, flesh, and blood. This supplies direct Gospel-to-Eucharist reception.
- **St. Cyril of Alexandria, Commentary on Luke, Sermons 131–132 — direct Greek-patristic exegesis in historical English translation.** Sermon 131, printed pages 607–612 in R. Payne Smith's 1859 translation, treats Christ's tears as real pity and desire for Jerusalem's peace; Sermon 132, beginning at page 613, treats Temple prayer and teaching: [text](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/cyril_on_luke_12_sermons_124_134.htm).
- **St. Ambrose, Exposition of Luke IX.17–19 — direct Latin exegesis.** Ambrose reads the Temple as holiness's dwelling rather than a merchant's lodging, priestly ministry as gratuitous service, Scripture as divine coin requiring discernment, and the sale of doves as trafficking in the Spirit's grace: [Latin text](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Expositio_evangelii_secundum_Lucam/IX), PL 15, cols. 1797C–1798B.
- **St. Gregory the Great, Homily 39 on the Gospels — direct Latin exegesis.** Sections 1–2 identify the Roman siege, treat Temple corruption, and observe that Jesus follows discipline by continuing to teach; §§6–7 apply the house of prayer to Christian religious office and the conscience: [Latin text](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Homiliarum_in_Evangelia/XXXIX).
- **St. Bede, In Lucae Evangelium Expositio V.19 — direct early-medieval Latin exegesis.** The Luke 19:41–48 exposition is at PL 92, cols. 570D–573C; an adjacent Paschal synthesis at cols. 573C–574A aligns the five days from Christ's reception near the Mount of Olives to Passover with Exodus 12's keeping of the victim, then joins attentive reception, the gift of Christ's Body and Blood, arrest, and death: [underlying scan](https://www.documentacatholicaomnia.eu/02m/0627-0735,_Beda_Venerabilis,_In_Lucae_Evangelium_Expositio,_MLT.pdf), PDF pp. 137–139. This is a direct Gospel-to-Eucharist-and-Passion bridge.
- **St. Bonaventure, Commentary on Luke XIX §§63–74 — direct medieval Latin exegesis.** Sections 63–69 treat the tears as Christ's priestly compassion on the day of royal honor; §§70–72 interpret the judicial Temple action, retain Isaiah 56's `all peoples` context, and turn Jeremiah's warning toward clerical avarice and simony; §§73–74 stress daily and public teaching: [Latin text](https://catholiclibrary.org/library/view?chunk.id=00000051&docId=%2FMedieval-OR%2FBonaventuraSCommentariusInEvangeliumSLucae.00000165.la.html).
- **St. Anthony of Padua, Sermon for the Tenth Sunday after Pentecost I.3–8 — medieval saintly reception.** Anthony's collection numbers the Sunday differently and pairs the Gospel with other chants and an Epistle. He turns Jerusalem into an image of the soul and Christ's tears into contrition over ruined virtues: [official Italian and Latin](https://www.santantonio.org/it/sermoni/sermoni-domenicali/domenica-x-dopo-pentecoste). This is spiritual reception rather than modern etymology or recovered composition history.
- **Aquinas's Catena aurea — medieval reception map.** At Luke 19:41–44 the catena preserves Origen on Jesus embodying the beatitude of mourners, Cyril on compassion, and Gregory's homily: [Latin/English](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~CaLuke.C19.L3.22). At verses 46–48 it preserves Gregory, Ambrose, Cyril, Augustine, and others on prayer, religious commerce, Christian self-application, and continued teaching: [Latin/English](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~CaLuke.C19.L6.3). The Origen fragment was located in *Homily 38*; Cyril, Gregory, and Ambrose were checked in their direct works.
- **Prophetic contexts actually used.** [Isaiah 56:1–8](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/isaiah/56) joins justice and covenant fidelity to the welcome of foreigners and eunuchs, acceptable sacrifice, a house of prayer for all peoples, and God's gathering of others to gathered Israel. [Jeremiah 7:1–15](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/jeremiah/7) defines the robbers' den through false Temple security amid theft, murder, adultery, perjury, idolatry, oppression of alien, orphan, and widow, and innocent bloodshed. Together they locate Temple prayer within covenant fidelity, inclusive gathering, justice, and care for the vulnerable.
- **Josephus as historical comparator.** *Jewish War* [V.12.1–4](https://www.ccel.org/j/josephus/works/war-5.htm) describes Titus's circumvallation wall, its completion in three days, escape cut off, famine, and households dying; [VII.1.1](https://www.ccel.org/j/josephus/works/war-7.htm) describes demolition of the city and Temple while naming spared towers and part of the western wall. These passages illuminate the siege's material and human reality. They do not prove the oracle, date Luke's composition, or support the claim that literally every stone vanished.
- **Pontifical Biblical Commission no. 74 — later official reception.** [*The Jewish People and Their Sacred Scriptures in the Christian Bible*](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/pcb_documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_20020212_popolo-ebraico_en.html) places Luke 19:41–44 beside 23:28–31: Jesus attends to the coming suffering of Jerusalem's women and children even as his own Passion approaches. The document also stresses Luke's substantially favorable presentation of Israel and Jerusalem.

### John 6:57 / 6:56

- **St. Augustine, Tractates 26–27 on John — direct and adjacent exegesis.** [Tractate 26 §§11–19](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1701026.htm) first quotes 1 Corinthians 10:1–4 to distinguish visible sacrament from fruitful reception, then joins the appointed clause to fellowship, peace, unity from many grains and grapes, the one bread/one body of 1 Corinthians 10:17, mutual indwelling, and participation in Christ's life. [Tractate 27 §§1, 6](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1701027.htm) receives the same clause while commenting on the following verses and stresses incorporation, charity, and unity.
- **St. John Chrysostom, Homily 47 on John — direct exegesis.** Chrysostom treats John 6:53–58, including eating, mutual indwelling, and eternal life: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/240147.htm).
- **St. Cyril of Alexandria, Commentary on John IV.3 — direct exegesis.** At the printed verse-57 discussion Cyril grounds communicant life in Christ's life-giving humanity and divine life from the living Father: [text](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/cyril_on_john_04_book4.htm).
- **St. Thomas Aquinas, Commentary on John VI, lect. 7, nos. 966–977 — direct medieval exegesis.** No. 975 especially treats union, abiding, and eternal life: [text](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~Ioan.C6.L7.n969). The [*Catena aurea* VI, lect. 9](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~CaJohn.C6.L9) mapped Bede, Chrysostom, and Augustine leads but did not replace their underlying works.

## Composed Propers and Doctrinal Illumination

- **Collect:** Augustine, *Letter 130 to Proba* §§15–18 and 21–29, illuminates enlarged and disciplined desire, the Lord's Prayer, and God's merciful refusal of some requested temporal goods: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1102130.htm). It does not directly comment on `Pateant` and is not evidence for the prayer's origin.
- **Secret — direct and documented reception:** Aquinas, [*Summa theologiae* III, q. 83, a. 2](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~ST.III.Q83.A2), objection 5, directly quotes `concede nobis ... haec frequentare mysteria`. His corpus and replies distinguish the once-for-all Passion in Christ the head from daily participation in its fruit and perpetual memorial; the objection's proposed inference—multiple celebrations by one priest in a day—is not Thomas's conclusion. The Second Vatican Council's [*Sacrosanctum Concilium* 2 and note 1](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19631204_sacrosanctum-concilium_lt.html) quotes `opus nostrae Redemptionis exercetur` and explicitly names the Ninth Sunday's super oblata. [*Presbyterorum ordinis* 13 and note 105](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_decree_19651207_presbyterorum-ordinis_lt.html) reuses the clause as `opus nostrae redemptionis continuo exercetur` for Eucharistic sacrifice in priestly ministry and likewise identifies the prayer; the inserted `continuo` makes this a documented reuse, not a verbatim repetition. [*Lumen gentium* 3 and 15](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19641121_lumen-gentium_lt.html) play distinct roles: no. 3 clearly reuses the clause for celebration of the sacrifice of the Cross and immediately joins Eucharistic bread to effected unity through 1 Corinthians 10:17, but supplies no note identifying this Sunday's prayer; no. 15 places the Church's purification and renewal within her prayer and work for Christian unity and a clearer sign of Christ. The former is therefore a clear verbal reuse rather than a separately documented source attribution; the latter controls Smith's account of later Postcommunion reception. Augustine, *City of God* X.5–6 and Aquinas q. 83, a. 1 remain broader controls for sign, sacrifice, memorial, and ecclesial action.
- **Secret — textual history kept distinct from the 1962 text:** Jordi Pinell, OSB, [“I testi liturgici, voci di autorità”](https://www.cultodivino.va/content/dam/cultodivino/rivista-notitiae/1970/notitiae-15-%281979%29/Notitiae-151-1979.pdf), *Notitiae* 15, no. 151 (1979), pp. 92–99, traces the Veronese `frequentata mysteria ... exeritur`, the Old Gelasian move toward `frequentare`, reception through mixed Gregorian sources, and the appearance of `digne` in the mid-eleventh-century Rossianum. Pinell argues that the older `exeritur` presents redemption becoming manifest and operative in Christian life and suggests a Paschal setting. These are attributed scholarly judgments; the guide preserves the appointed 1962 `exercetur`, does not silently emend it, and does not make Pinell's proposed Leonine authorship certain.
- **Communion and Postcommunion:** Augustine's John tractates directly join mutual abiding to 1 Corinthians 10's sacramental warning, fruitful reception, one bread, incorporation, charity, and unity. Chrysostom supplies an independent movement from Christ's self-gift to communicant life; Cyril grounds that life in Christ's life-giving humanity and divine life; Aquinas, *Commentary on John* VI, lect. 7, names union and mutual abiding. *Summa theologiae* III, q. 73, a. 3 and q. 79, aa. 1–4 further illuminates union with Christ and the Church, nourishment, remission of venial sin, and preservation from mortal sin. Guéranger's direct commentary on this Sunday, [*The Liturgical Year*, Time after Pentecost II](https://archive.org/details/liturgicalyear11gura), pp. 255–256, calls the Communion divine union and the Postcommunion's fruits personal sanctification and corporate unity. None supplies a promise of automatic fruit, immediate sensation, or unity without conversion.
- **Later Postcommunion reception:** Innocent Smith, [“United in Praise and in Penance”](https://doi.org/10.1177/00393207261415798), *Studia Liturgica* (online 26 March 2026), §5.3 and notes 80–81, shows that a 1970 Prayer over the Offerings for Christian unity borrowed `purificationem conferat` from this ancient prayer. Read with *Lumen gentium* 15, the later reuse orders ecclesial purification and renewal toward a clearer sign of Christ and restored unity. This is protected modern scholarship, paraphrased as later reception rather than evidence of the 1962 compiler's intent.
- The printed Trinity Preface is liturgical context, not an eleventh proper. Its appointment does not make every source-grounded synthesis an explicit Trinitarian claim.

## Chant Reception and Assignment Boundary

- Dom Dominic Johner, [*The Chants of the Vatican Gradual*](https://archive.ccwatershed.org/media/pdfs/13/08/19/15-40-55_0.pdf) (1940), pp. 279–282, reads the Introit melody as confidence disturbed by the enemy clause and restored at `protector meus`; the Gradual as reverence and joy; the Alleluia's plea as fear turning at `Deus meus` toward contemplation while remaining framed by praise; and the Communion's descending fourth at `in me manet` as answered by a rising fourth at `ego in eo`. These are qualified historical-musical interpretations, not recovered composer intent.
- Assignment history limits intentional claims. The [Gregorian repertory record for `Qui manducat carnem`](https://gregorien.info/chant/id/6714/6/en) reports *Antiphonale Missarum Sextuplex* assignments to Thursday of Lent II and the Fifteenth Sunday after Pentecost, not the Ninth; the [Benevento Gradual 34 witness](https://gregorien.info/manuscript/folio/55/en) associates `Ecce Deus adiuvat` with `Primum quaerite`. The Gospel-to-John-6 turn is therefore real in the received 1962 arrangement but is not presented as the earliest chant-cycle compiler's design.

## Lexical and Cross-Proper Audit

- **Help and action:** `Ecce Deus adiuvat me` and Paul's `fidelis autem Deus` place divine initiative before and within responsible action. Neither text supports passivity; neither makes success evidence of unaided strength.
- **Appetite retrained:** the Collect asks God to cause petitioners to seek what pleases him; the Epistle begins `Non simus concupiscentes malorum`, recalls idolatrous eating and drinking, and proceeds canonically to the Eucharistic cup and bread; the Offertory names judgments sweeter than honey; Communion appoints eating and drinking as mutual abiding. The verbal roots and literary origins differ. The four-proper relation is therefore a labeled editorial proposal, not a lexical derivation or compiler claim.
- **Judgment and sweetness:** Psalm 18 keeps `iudicia`, honey, warning, and keeping within one canonical unit. Luke's Temple action may be compared with that unit; warning and obedient keeping govern the comparison.
- **Warning, one bread, and unity:** the appointed lesson ends at 1 Corinthians 10:13, but the canonical `therefore` at verse 14 proceeds to flee idolatry and then to cup, bread, participation, and one body at verses 16–17. Ambrosiaster makes the warning at verse 12 social—knowledge that scandals a weaker sibling—and the one bread at verses 16–17 a demand for one mind and action. Chrysostom, Aquinas, and Augustine also receive the continuation sacramentally. It supplies a close canonical and reception bridge to the Communion and Postcommunion without proving historical compiler intent.
- **Presumed standing and received abiding:** the Epistle's `qui se existimat stare` names a self-estimate vulnerable to falling; the Communion's `in me manet, et ego in eo` names a reciprocal relation grounded in Christ's gift; the Postcommunion asks that relation to bear purification and unity. Johner's chant reading foregrounds the reciprocal Communion clauses, while Smith documents a later Roman reuse that orders purification toward Christian unity. The sequence supports the governing thesis but is not an etymological link or early chant-assignment claim.
- **Memorial, abiding, unity:** the Secret's repeated sacramental memorial, John's mutual `manet`, and the Postcommunion's `purificationem` and `unitatem` form a strong liturgical progression from enacted gift to abiding and ecclesial fruit. The relation is no longer supported only by broad analogy: *Lumen gentium* 3 uses the Secret's central clause and immediately joins it to Eucharistic unity through 1 Corinthians 10:17. The Council still does not claim to explain the historical assembly of this entire formulary.
- Similar vocabulary, sequential placement, and thematic consonance never prove common human authorship, compositional intent, or historical source dependence. Original multi-proper models remain confined to the globally labeled interpretive section.
