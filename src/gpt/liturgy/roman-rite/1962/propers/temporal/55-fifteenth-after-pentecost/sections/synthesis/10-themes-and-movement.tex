\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\label{triptych:brief-synthesis:start}
\begingroup
\setlength{\parskip}{0.32em}

\noindent
Continuing divine mercy elicits, restores, and sustains a life that becomes
ecclesial action and praise. The formulary moves from the servant's lifted cry
through Spirit-led responsibility and Christ's life-giving word to sacramental
protection, Communion, and the heavenly gift's action upon mind and body.

\subsection*{1. A heard cry becomes restored and communicated life\enspace\properrefs{Int., Gosp., Off., Comm.}}

The Introit opens with need directed toward God: the servant asks the Lord to
incline his ear, hear, save, show mercy, and gladden. Its repeated first-person
grammar is not spiritual enclosure. The soul is lifted toward the Lord, and
the appeal rests on the character of the God whom Psalm~85 goes on to confess
as good, gentle, and rich in mercy. Augustine therefore hears in the needy
voice the prayer of the whole Christ, Head and members; holiness itself is a
gift of grace rather than a title to payment (\emph{Enarratio in Psalmum 85}
§§2, 4--6).

The Gospel answers need before it is voiced. Christ sees the widow, is moved
with mercy, tells her not to weep, touches the bier, commands the young man to
rise, and gives him alive to his mother. Cyril of Alexandria insists upon the
unity of the act: the human touch belongs to the flesh united to the
life-giving Word, while the commanding word manifests divine authority
(\emph{Commentary on Luke}, Sermon XXXVI, pp.~132--136). The miracle is first
the bodily restoration Luke narrates; in Cyril's reception it also becomes a
pledge of the resurrection to come.

At the Offertory, patient waiting becomes divine regard and a new canticle
placed in the mouth. Augustine reads Psalm~39 as the renewed humanity of
Christ's body standing upon the rock and learning a new song
(\emph{Enarratio in Psalmum 39} §§2--5). The Communion then names Christ's
flesh as given for the world's life. A solitary appeal has opened into gift:
the Lord hears, restores speech and life, gives the son to his mother, places
praise in the mouth, and gives himself. Christian hope rests in this divine
initiative, not in a promise that every petition ends in the petitioner's
chosen outcome.

\subsection*{2. Grace conditions responsible action\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Ep., Postcomm.}}

The Collect places the Church beneath continuing mercy because she cannot
stand safely without God. The verbs are active on both sides: mercy cleanses,
fortifies, and governs; the Church is preserved for faithful life rather than
released from it. The Epistle immediately gives the moral form of that life:
those who live by the Spirit must also walk by the Spirit. They restore the
fallen gently, watch themselves under temptation, bear another's burden,
examine their own work, carry their own responsibility, share goods, sow to
the Spirit, persevere, and do good while opportunity remains.

Paul's apparently opposed commands---Challoner's ``Bear ye one another's
burdens'' and the later assignment of each person's own burden---name charity and judgment at
different moments. Augustine reads mutual bearing as the present work of
love and personal bearing as the final account before God (\emph{Sermo}
163B §§1--6). Aquinas develops gentle restoration through patience, material
aid, prayer, and charity while preserving the corrector's vulnerability to
the same temptation (\emph{Super Galatas} 6, lect.~1, Corpus Thomisticum
paragraph [87781]). Dependence
upon mercy therefore excludes neither real agency nor personal
accountability.

The Postcommunion completes the order by asking that the heavenly gift's
operation possess mind and body and continually precede merely self-directed
judgment. The prayer does not erase intellect or will. It asks that the whole
person be claimed and led by grace before private estimation turns inward
upon itself. The Collect rescues moral effort from autonomy; the Epistle
rescues dependence from quietism; the Postcommunion asks sacramental gift to
sustain their ordered union. In this prayer, \latin{praeveniat} states the
gift's priority; the one verb does not by itself define the complete technical
doctrine of prevenient grace.

\subsection*{3. Four registers of life remain distinct\enspace\properrefs{Ep., Gosp., Comm., Postcomm.}}

Across the Epistle, Gospel, Communion, and Postcommunion, the formulary speaks
of life in four coordinated registers. Paul names a moral life led by the Spirit and a harvest of
everlasting life. Luke narrates one young man's bodily return to mortal life.
John names Christ's flesh given for the world's life within a discourse about
abiding, everlasting life, and resurrection on the last day. Their relation
is not identity: moral sowing is not a resurrection technique, the youth at
Nain will die again, and John~6 is not an agrarian allegory of Galatians.

The Fathers preserve the differences while opening the theological horizon.
Cyril treats Nain as a pledge of the general resurrection and John~6 as the
incarnate Word's life-giving self-gift. Augustine reads sacramental power as
inward participation and ecclesial incorporation: the many become one bread
in charity (\emph{Tractate on John} 26 §§12--13). Chrysostom contrasts manna
with Christ's spiritual food while holding together instruction in faith and
the bodily Mystery (\emph{Homily on John} 46 §§1--2). Aquinas joins the real
flesh, the Passion, restored life, and many-grain ecclesial unity
(\emph{Super Ioannem} 6, lect.~6, §87463).

The Postcommunion's explicit pairing of minds and bodies prevents Paul's
``flesh'' from becoming a condemnation of bodily substance. In Galatians the
contrast concerns sowing to disordered selfhood rather than to the Spirit;
in John the flesh of Christ is life-giving because it is the flesh of the
Word. The formulary thus holds sign, sacramental gift, and eschatological
horizon together while refusing both anti-bodily spiritualism and a collapse
of mortal restoration into final resurrection.

\subsection*{4. Mercy enlarges appeal into ecclesial and public praise\enspace\properrefs{Int., Coll., Ep., Grad., All., Gosp., Off., Comm.}}

The scale widens steadily. The Introit's singular servant lifts his soul; the
Collect names the Church; the Epistle orders brethren toward one another,
toward their teachers, toward all, and especially toward the household of
faith. In the Gospel, Christ's disciples and accompanying crowd meet the
widow and the crowd of her city. Fear seizes all, praise rises, and the report
of divine visitation begins. The Alleluia acclaims the great King with
\latin{super omnem terram}, and the Communion names life given for the world.

The Gradual teaches how praise fills time: mercy in the morning and truth by
night. Augustine receives the pair as confession in prosperity and adversity;
Theodoret reads it as unceasing praise of mercy and truthful judgment, within
the psalm's Sabbath rest (Augustine, \emph{Enarratio in Psalmum 91} §§3--4;
Theodoret, \emph{Interpretatio in Psalmos}, PG~80:1615--1618). Augustine's
prosperity-and-adversity reading and Theodoret's continual-praise merism are
distinct patristic developments of the appointed pair.

The Alleluia preserves a further difference. The Clementine Psalm~94:3 ends
\latin{super omnes deos}, and Augustine comments on that form through covenant
and Christ the cornerstone joining Israel and the nations. The Missal and
Theodoret's lemma read \latin{super omnem terram}; Theodoret answers false gods
with universal divine dominion (PG~80:1639--1642). Both forms widen praise,
while Paul's priority toward the household of faith preserves ordered
proximity and the Communion's worldwide scope promises no automatic universal
salvation.

\subsection*{5. Sacramental action guards and transforms the whole person\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Ep., Sec., Comm., Postcomm.}}

The Collect's cleansing, fortification, and governance become more precise in
the two sacramental prayers. The Secret asks that the sacraments guard the
faithful and defend them continually against diabolical assaults. The
Postcommunion asks that the heavenly gift act upon mind and body. Between
them, the Communion names the flesh Christ gives for the world's life. The
sequence holds defense and transformation together: grace protects against
an enemy and also forms the recipient from within.

The Epistle prevents sacramental defense from becoming a talisman. It names
vainglory, envy, temptation, self-deception, corruptible sowing, weariness,
and neglected good as real fields of responsibility. Chrysostom binds
Spirit-led freedom to loving service, and Aquinas reads sacramental reception
as applying Christ's Passion. Augustine's inward participation and ecclesial
incorporation show why protection and charity cannot be separated from union
with Christ.

The prayers neither attribute every fault to demonic causation nor promise
immunity from temptation, suffering, or bodily harm. Nor does divine
possession destroy intellect and agency. The Church asks that mercy cleanse,
defend, govern, and possess precisely so that mind and body may live as one
grace-enabled person. The formulary's movement reaches its sacramental end
when the life received from Christ becomes patient responsibility, guarded
freedom, persevering charity, and praise shared in the Church.
