\section*{Source-Grounded Synthesis Across the Propers}

\subsection*{An enduring prayer group acquires different Sunday settings}

The Collect, Secret and Postcommunion already stand together in Wilson's
\emph{Gelasian Sacramentary}, III.xi, p.~230, under the undated
\emph{Item alia missa}. A second collect accompanies them. The Secret's
\latin{tueantur semper} differs in order from the 1962
\latin{semper tueántur}; the Postcommunion moves \latin{Domine}, and
Wilson's \latin{sed} corrects the manuscript's \latin{sunt}.
His apparatus assigns different numbered Sunday correspondences among
later witnesses. The heading itself supplies no Sunday number.

Wilson's \emph{Gregorian Sacramentary} supplement XXXIIII, p.~174,
places the three prayers in their ritual positions under
\latin{Dominica XVI post Pentecosten}. Its collect, \latin{Super oblata}
and \latin{Ad complendum} preserve the group's structure while differing
from the later Fifteenth-Sunday assignment. Wilson dates the principal
R and O manuscripts to the ninth century, O almost certainly and R
possibly before 850 (introduction, pp.~xvii--xviii); these are manuscript
judgments, not the prayers' composition dates. The manuscripts do not name
the supplement's compiler; Wilson nevertheless argues for Alcuin on the
testimony of the \emph{Micrologus} (1915 introduction, p.~xxi).

The marginal chants make the historical difference concrete. Wilson's O
cues beside this group are \latin{Justus es Domine}, \latin{Unam petii},
\latin{Oravi Deum meum}, and \latin{Vovete et reddite}. The Introit and
Offertory familiar here, \latin{Inclina Domine aurem} and
\latin{Expectans expectavi}, occur with a different prayer group on
p.~173. The O marginal cues, R prayer text and later bracketed additions
have separate origins within Wilson's edition (pp.~xliv--xlv).
The prayers' stability therefore coexists with changed chant associations.

Schuster calls this Sunday \latin{Quinta post Sancti Laurentii} and
explains the succeeding Cyprian and Michael groupings as calendar markers
(III, pp.~139,142). His p.~139--141 account records Lenten reuse of the
Gradual, Gospel, Offertory and Communion: Saturday of Lent~II, Thursday of
Lent~IV, Tuesday of Lent~IV and Thursday of Lent~I respectively.
The three-orational inheritance and
the later complete ensemble belong to different historical questions.

\subsection*{The same Gospel serves postbaptismal restoration}

\emph{The Liturgical Year}'s continuation distinguishes the Naim Gospel's
Lenten setting from its address here to baptized Christians who have fallen
back into sin (1909, pp.~350--351). That received liturgical reading joins
Augustine's public sinner and Bede's reconciliation to Paul's concern for
restoring an overtaken brother. The person restored by mercy becomes a
member able to bear another's frailty. Luke's bodily miracle and the
Church's penitential reception have different literal objects, while the
received interpretation makes restoration to communion central.

The continuation also makes three explicit connections across the Mass:
Galatians' burdens with Eucharistic mutual abiding (p.~348, citing the
contextual John~6:57); Mother Church's restored children with the
Offertory's thanksgiving (p.~354); and the Naim raising with Communion and
the gift's mind-and-body efficacy (p.~355). The appointed Communion remains
John~6:52's last clause. These conjunctions carry the readings into the
assembly's thanksgiving and sacramental life.

\subsection*{The gift sustains persons who still have work to do}

The Collect's dependence, the Epistle's responsibility and the final prayer's
inward efficacy meet in one living community. Aquinas's three forms of
burden-bearing give charity practical expression (\emph{Super Galatas}
6.1); his Eucharistic doctrine joins nourishment to charity aroused in act
and protection that does not remove the possibility of sin
(III.79.1,3,6). The gift sustains the very freedom called to perseverance.

The same relation embraces the body. Christ gives his own life-giving flesh;
Paul condemns disordered sowing, not bodily nature; the Postcommunion asks
for minds and bodies together. The son's present restoration remains
mortal, while Augustine and Aquinas distinguish present participation from
future bodily fulfillment (\emph{John} 26.16; III.79.1--2).
Charity acts within that interval. Good for everyone, particular
responsibility for fellow believers, and praise through changing conditions
are the conduct of a people receiving life and awaiting its consummation.
