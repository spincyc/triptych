\section*{The Propers: Notable and Quotable}

\subsection*{Wilde makes harvest an excuse to withhold charity\enspace\properrefs{Ep.}}

Galatians' sow-and-reap warning becomes Miss Prism's confident dismissal
of a supposed wrongdoer in Act~II of Oscar Wilde's three-act
\emph{The Importance of Being Earnest}. The appointed anchor is
``For what things a man shall sow, those also shall he reap''
(Challoner, American 1899, Gal.~6:8; common modern 6:7).
Prism says, ``As a man sows so let him reap,'' and repeats the maxim after
Jack announces his invented brother's invented death. Chasuble then calls
her to charity. The moral sentence has become a comic refusal to restore
someone, precisely the work Paul's surrounding passage commands.
The two exchanges occur in the identified Gutenberg three-act edition,
Act~II, after Prism's claim that she could have no effect on Ernest and
then immediately before Chasuble's appeal. Her proverbial recasting is
not verbatim Challoner.

\subsection*{Garrison changes the individual sower into a people\enspace\properrefs{Ep.}}

The Epistle's ``God is not mocked'' (American 1899 Challoner, Gal.~6:7)
becomes national judgment in William Lloyd Garrison's ``Guilt of the
North---The National Compact.'' His altered quotation reads:
``Be not deceived: God is not mocked: for whatsoever a people sow, that
shall they also reap'' (\emph{Freedom's Gift}, 1840, p.~38,
paragraph beginning ``Divided, we fall''). The singular moral agent becomes
a political people answerable for complicity in slavery. Garrison turns
the Union's professions of liberty against its actual compact; the change
of pronouns carries his abolitionist indictment. These are Garrison's
words, with national responsibility made their immediate object.

\subsection*{U2 makes a received canticle a shared farewell\enspace\properrefs{Off.}}

The Offertory's \latin{cánticum novum}, Challoner's ``new canticle''
(American 1899, Ps.~39:4), becomes a concert leave-taking in U2's ``40,''
the final track of \emph{War} (1983). The song uses ``new song'' and
asks ``How long to sing this song?'' At the final U2:UV show on
2~March 2024, the musicians left the stage individually while the fans
kept singing, according to U2.com's dated account, ``This is 40''
(5~March 2024, paragraph beginning ``As the band left the stage one by
one''). The same report supplies Bono's identification of the Psalm
source. Received rescue and praise become a communal act of parting whose
voice outlasts the performers. The account identifies no particular Bible
translation or necessarily religious experience among the audience.

\subsection*{A burden-bearing title organizes ideological coexistence\enspace\properrefs{Ep.}}

Galatians~6:2 commands \latin{Alter altérius ónera portáte},
``Bear ye one another's burdens'' (American 1899 Challoner).
Lothar Warneke's \emph{Einer trage des anderen Last\ldots}, produced
in 1987 and premiered on 28~January 1988, makes that imperative a
tragicomic demand for reciprocal help between a communist policeman and a
Lutheran vicar. The DEFA custodians describe the plot's movement toward
tolerance as a church--state parable in the late GDR: Christian
burden-bearing organizes cooperation with an ideological antagonist
(Zengel, October 2021, synopsis and discussion of the characters' equal
weight; UMass DEFA catalog~3523, Synopsis and Commentary).
The title has \emph{anderen}, whereas the checked Lutherbibel~2017
comparison has \emph{andern}. That later Bible is not evidence of the
filmmakers' source edition. The UMass English title's
singular \emph{Burden} is also distinct from Challoner's wording.

\subsection*{Dickens gives bereavement a new mother--son bond\enspace\properrefs{Gosp.}}

Luke says, ``And he gave him to his mother'' (American 1899 Challoner,
7:15). In chapter~II of Dickens's \emph{The Seven Poor Travellers},
Taunton's bereaved mother reads the Naim narrative to herself; Richard
Doubledick, her dead son's former protégé, overhears as he approaches.
Dickens quotes ``Young man, I say unto
thee, arise!'' where Challoner has \emph{to thee}. The narrator explicitly
identifies the Bible; the scene then develops a new filial relation as
Doubledick confesses how Taunton reclaimed him from self-destruction.
Later she nurses Doubledick through his apparent return to life after
Waterloo and receives him as a son. Taunton himself is not resuscitated.
The miracle's language shapes psychological restoration and a new family
bond amid war (``The Story of Richard Doubledick,'' visit at Frome and
subsequent recovery; Gutenberg transcription of the 1894 Chapman and Hall
\emph{Christmas Stories}).
