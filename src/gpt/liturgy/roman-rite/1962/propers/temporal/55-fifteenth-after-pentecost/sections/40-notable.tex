\section*{The Propers: Notable and Quotable}

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\subsection*{Garrison makes ``God is not mocked'' a national judgment\enspace\properrefs{Ep.}}

William Lloyd Garrison's ``Guilt of the North---The National Compact,'' in
Richard S.~Rust's \emph{Freedom's Gift} (Hartford, 1840), turns the warning
from Galatians~6 into an indictment of the United States' political and
religious compact with slavery. Near the top of p.~38, the familiar
singular sower becomes a people who will reap what they have sown. Personal
moral and eschatological consequence becomes abolitionist national judgment.
The grammatical redirection is Garrison's.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\subsection*{Tressell makes the warning recoil upon its speaker\enspace\properrefs{Ep.}}

In chapter~XVII of Robert Tressell's \emph{The Ragged Trousered
Philanthropists} (Grant Richards edition, 1914), pp.~190--193, the evangelical
lantern controlled by the exploitative foreman Hunter displays the appointed
sentence ``God is not mocked.'' Spectators test Hunter's professed literalism
against his conduct and laugh as he retreats. The warning becomes an ironic
visual and class-critical prop whose force falls upon the man displaying it.
The scene belongs to the posthumously abridged Grant Richards edition.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\subsection*{U2's new song becomes a communal leave-taking\enspace\properrefs{Off.}}

U2's ``40,'' track~10 on \emph{War} (Island Records, 1983), directly uses the
Psalm~40 passage corresponding to Vulgate and Douay Psalm~39. The song takes
the biblical ``new song'' rather than Challoner's ``new canticle'' and adds a
recurring unresolved question. At the final U2:UV performance on 2~March
2024, documented by the band's official account, the musicians left one by
one while the audience continued the refrain. Individual testimony of rescue
became a communal concert leave-taking.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\subsection*{Burden-bearing frames coexistence in the late GDR\enspace\properrefs{Ep.}}

Lothar Warneke's DEFA film \emph{Einer trage des anderen Last\ldots}
(produced in 1987; premiered 28~January 1988) takes its title from the
established German wording of Galatians~6:2. A communist police officer and a
Protestant vicar are forced to share a sanatorium room in the early German
Democratic Republic. Their mutual help turns Paul's intra-Christian command
into a tragicomic political parable of church--state tolerance. The film's
synthesis redirects the established German biblical imperative into
cross-ideological coexistence.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\subsection*{Hord redirects harvest toward postwar peace\enspace\properrefs{Ep.}}

Donal Hord's 1950 bronze medal \emph{To Reap Man Must Sow}, commissioned by
the Society of Medallic Arts, reverses the familiar verbal order of
Galatians' sow-and-reap consequence. The Smithsonian American Art Museum
reads its imagined agrarian scene against the aftermath of the Second World
War and the work of the United Nations. Harvest becomes a visual appeal for
labor toward peace rather than only a warning about personal moral
consequence. The scene is the artist's imagined pre-European civilization,
not a historical account of Indigenous societies.
