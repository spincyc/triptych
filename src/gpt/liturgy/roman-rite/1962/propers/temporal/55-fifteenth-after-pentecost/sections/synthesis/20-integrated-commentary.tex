\subsection*{Mercy restores persons to a community of charity\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Ep., Gosp., Comm., Postcomm.}}

The Collect's Church depends on mercy for stability; the Epistle's
community depends on charity for the restoration of its members. The
Gospel's son is given back to his mother. Together these texts give
restoration a relational shape: the fallen person has neighbors, the son
has a mother, and the Church has members whom divine gift preserves for
responsible life. Paul addresses agents who can help and can themselves be
tempted, while Jesus commands a dead person to live.

The Fathers explain that restored relation in distinct ways. Augustine
receives Naim as the raising of public sinners to Mother Church
(\emph{Sermon}~98.4--5, MacMullen's English). Ambrose's continuous Latin
transcription joins the mother's tears, the cross signified by the bier's
wood, and the Church's intercession for each child
(\emph{Expositio in Lucam} V.89--92). Bede's continuous Latin
transcription makes return to ecclesial communion through priestly
judgment explicit (II.7, PL~92:418D). Their spiritual interpretation
preserves Luke's actual bereaved mother; it does not assign the youth a
narrated fault.

Charitable correction belongs to such a community. Chrysostom calls
mildness a spiritual gift and makes reciprocal support compatible with
different gifts and functions (\emph{Galatians}, chs.~5--6,
Alexander's English). Theodoret grounds it in shared mutability and
Christ's law of love (PG~82:499A--502B). Augustine's Latin
\emph{Frangipane V} §§2--4 opening includes neglect of a brother among
one's own responsibilities (Morin, pp.~213--215), while Aquinas
specifies endurance, practical help and intercessory satisfaction
(\emph{Super Galatas} 6.1). Help and personal reckoning strengthen one
another.

\emph{The Liturgical Year}'s 1909 continuation carries that relation into
Eucharistic life: its p.~348 joins Galatians' burdens and Christ's law
to mutual abiding, with a contextual citation of John~6:57.
The appointed Communion is John~6:52's last clause. Augustine's
Tractate~26.13,15,18 explains one body, charity and abiding through the
sacramental gift (Gibb's English). The Postcommunion then asks its operation
to possess the recipients. Life is received within communion and bears
fruit in care of others.

\subsection*{Life-giving flesh restores the body and promises fulfillment\enspace\properrefs{Ep., Gosp., Comm., Postcomm.}}

The flesh sown to corruption in Galatians and the flesh given for life in
John have different referents. Paul's vice-and-virtue context concerns
conduct turned from the Spirit; Christ gives his own flesh. Luke's bodily
raising and the Postcommunion's bodies-and-minds petition place bodily life
within the gift's reach. The opposition to corruption never makes bodily
nature itself evil.

Cyril supplies a direct Gospel--Communion bridge in his continuous
\emph{John} IV.2 (Pusey's English). At pp.~409--414 the flesh gives
life because it belongs to the life-giving Word; at pp.~418--419,
commenting on a following verse, the touch at Naim explains the power of
sacramental participation. His separate \emph{Luke} Sermon~XXXVI
(Payne Smith, pp.~132--135) joins command and touch to that same
christology. That Luke witness includes Greek-catena supplementation of a
missing Syriac folio and further Greek fragments; it is not an uninterrupted
original homily. The continuous John argument independently supplies the
explicit sacramental comparison.

Naim is a present restoration to mortal life, not the general resurrection.
Cyril distinguishes the resurrection of all from the holy person's
beatitude; Augustine distinguishes present abiding from future bodily
fulfillment (\emph{John} 26.15--18). Aquinas joins the present effect on
conduct to future incorruption (III.79.1, ad~3) and distinguishes receiving
capacity for glory from immediate entrance into glory (III.79.2).
\emph{The Liturgical Year}, p.~355, and Schuster, III p.~141,
explicitly carry the Communion's gift into mind-and-body efficacy.
The same person who acts in charity now awaits a fulfillment beyond
present mortal restoration.

\subsection*{Received life perseveres amid dependence and waiting\enspace\properrefs{Int., Coll., Ep., Grad., Gosp., Off., Sec., Comm., Postcomm.}}

The son's rising is immediate. Paul's harvest is future, and his command
to do good concerns present opportunity. The Introit continues to cry,
the Collect asks continuing mercy, and the Offertory gives thanks for
prayer heard. The complete Psalms~85 and 39 nevertheless return to need.
These temporal differences give Christian life room for both decisive
gift and sustained dependence.

Augustine's Psalm~39.2--5 (Tweed's English) holds rescue on Christ the
rock together with a walk still to be made and a crown still to come.
Theodoret joins new benefits to a new song and the hope of resurrection
(PG~80:1151B--1154C); Bellarmine receives the redemption of Christ's
people and their establishment through faith, hope and charity
(O'Sullivan, pp.~118--119). The pit and rock belong to the unsung psalm
context; the Offertory selects hearing and thanksgiving.

The Gradual gives praise both morning and night. Augustine receives
prosperity and adversity (91.1--4); Theodoret reads unceasing praise of
beneficence and just judgment (PG~80:1615C--1618A). Bellarmine first
adds the distinction between mercy visible in creation and judgment praised
in faith's darkness, then explicitly receives Augustine (pp.~289--290).
The explanations differ, and each gives praise substance when circumstances
change. Paul's perseverance likewise joins patient expectation to action
while there is opportunity (Aquinas, \emph{Super Galatas} 6.2).
Sacramental custody and the final prayer's continuing efficacy sustain
that active life through its unfinished course.

\subsection*{Mercy gives particular voices a public horizon\enspace\properrefs{Int., Grad., All., Gosp., Off., Ep., Comm.}}

A servant petitions, the Gradual proclaims mercy and truth, the restored
son speaks, the crowd glorifies God, and the Offertory receives a new song.
Luke gives the son's speech no reported content. The crowd's divine
visitation belongs with the Benedictus and Exodus~4:29--31, where mercy
shown to afflicted people elicits worship. These are several voices and
speech acts within praise of the one Lord.

Augustine's Christ-and-members reading of Psalms~85 and 39 gives singular
prayer an ecclesial voice (85.1--6; 39.2--5, Tweed's English).
\emph{The Liturgical Year}, p.~354, explicitly joins Mother Church's
restored children to the Offertory's thanksgiving. The communal act of
praise responds to a gift received by persons; the mother and son are not
lost in an abstract account of the Church.

The Alleluia's all-earth kingship and the Communion's world-directed gift
extend the horizon. Theodoret's Psalm~94:3 lemma reads all the earth
(PG~80:1641A--1642A); Augustine's Psalm~94 has all gods and develops
the union of circumcision and uncircumcision in the following verse
(94.5--6). His distinct Psalm~46.3 exposition reads the chant's parallel
all-earth clause through Christ crucified and ascended, expressly
including believing Israel and the nations. The psalm traditions widen
praise through different textual forms.

Direct reception of Psalm~46 gives that universal rule the character of
providential care. Bellarmine explains Christ's greatness through his
power and government of the world, giving all nations reason to rejoice
(O'Sullivan's abridged English, Psalm~XLVI, edition v.~2, p.~144).
Aquinas distinguishes universality, eternity and authority over kings,
rejecting a divine transcendence indifferent to earthly affairs
(\emph{Super Psalmo}~46, n.~2, modern Latin transcription [87252]).
Theodoret hears an apostolic summons to the nations in the same psalm
(PG~80:1205D--1208B). Kingship over the whole earth thus embraces the
particular people who receive mercy and raise their voices in praise.

Paul retains both good to all and special care for the household of faith.
Aquinas explains their ordered relation through the common divine image
and intensified practical obligation (\emph{Super Galatas} 6.2).
Universal scope does not imply universal final beatitude: Cyril's John
exposition distinguishes those claims. Mercy's breadth and the particular
person's responsibility remain together.

\subsection*{Sacramental protection strengthens whole-person conduct\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Ep., Sec., Comm., Postcomm.}}

The Collect asks mercy to cleanse and fortify; the Secret asks protection
against demonic assaults; the Postcommunion asks the heavenly gift to
possess minds and bodies. Cleansing, defense and inward efficacy address
the same community whom Paul warns against envy, vainglory and
self-deception. The demonic enemy named by the Secret is distinct from
human opponents and from the neighbor whom charity must correct.

Aquinas's \emph{Summa} III.79.6 explains protection through inward union
with Christ and the Passion's outward defense, while retaining the
possibility of sin. Article~1, ad~2, explains charity aroused to act;
article~3 explains the obstacle presented by knowingly retained mortal sin.
The sacrament's nourishment does not authorize reception while refusing
conversion. Augustine's distinction between visible reception and abiding
(\emph{John} 26.15,18) and Chrysostom's demand for worthy conduct
(\emph{John} 46.3--4, Marriott's English) describe communion as a
living relation whose fruit reaches speech and action.

Schuster's custody, nourishment and faculties under grace give the prayers'
relation a specifically liturgical exposition (III, pp.~139--141).
The heavenly gift precedes and sustains the patient's care, the teacher's
support, the corrector's mildness and the good done while opportunity lasts.
The Church asks to be guarded so that her members may live the charity
which the Epistle commands and the Eucharistic gift nourishes.
