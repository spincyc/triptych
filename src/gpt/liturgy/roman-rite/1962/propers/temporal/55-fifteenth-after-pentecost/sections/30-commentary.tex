\subsection*{The lifted cry of the needy whole Christ\enspace\properrefs{Int.}}

Psalm~85 begins in poverty and trust. The antiphon keeps commands to incline,
hear, save, and show mercy, but omits the petitioner's self-description as
needy, poor, and holy. Its final clause recalls the servant's all-day cry and
is answered by the separate psalm verse: the servant lifts
his soul and asks to be gladdened. The motion is therefore not from distress
to self-possession but from need toward God.

Augustine identifies the speaker as Christ's needy body. The Head is rich,
but he makes the members' need his own; the Church cries through the whole
age figured by the cry throughout the day. Even the psalmist's holiness is received, because
God sanctifies the one who asks to be preserved (\emph{Enarratio in Psalmum
85} §§2, 4--6). Bellarmine transmits that grace-grounded account in his
treatment of the opening verses. Theodoret begins instead from David or
Hezekiah, calls human nature commonly poor, and understands holiness as
innocence relative to unjust enemies. His historical-relative accent and
Augustine's whole-Christ reading remain distinct.

The complete psalm later confesses the Lord as compassionate and abundant in
mercy. The appointed opening rests upon that character even though the
descriptive verses are not sung. The Introit thus
prepares the Gospel not by making the psalmist the widow, but by establishing
mercy as the answer sought from the God who sees need.

\subsection*{Mercy keeps the Church standing\enspace\properrefs{Coll.}}

The Collect's subject is continuing mercy. It cleanses and fortifies the
Church because, without God, she cannot \latin{salva consistere}; the same
divine gift must always govern her. Cleansing concerns what corrupts,
fortifying what threatens, and governance the course of life. The prayer's
compact grammar refuses both self-sufficient stability and passive
dependence.

The Collect, Secret, and Postcommunion already stand together in the Old
Gelasian Sacramentary, Book~III, XI, \emph{Item alia Missa}, pp.~230--231,
where a second collect also appears. Wilson's Gregorian Hadrianum supplement
places the same trio at Sunday~XVI without that additional collect and with
chants different from the 1962 ensemble. The prayer cluster is therefore
attested before the later complete formulary.

\subsection*{The law of Christ joins mutual care and judgment\enspace\properrefs{Ep.}}

The Epistle begins where the preceding exhortation leaves the baptized: if
the faithful live by the Spirit, they must walk by the Spirit. Vainglory,
provocation, and envy immediately show that Spirit-led life is tested in
relationships. When another is overtaken in a fault, the spiritual restore
him in gentleness while considering their own vulnerability. Correction is
neither indifference nor domination.

Chrysostom reads freedom from the Law as freedom for loving service. The
contrast of flesh and Spirit concerns moral choice, not the evil of bodily
substance; gentle restoration protects both the fallen person and the one
correcting him (\emph{Commentary on Galatians}, Homilies~5--6). Aquinas gives
the work concrete form in patience, aid, prayer, and satisfaction
(\emph{Super Galatas} 6, lect.~1 §§343--348). These witnesses keep mercy from
becoming mere softness: charity seeks restoration under the law of Christ.

Paul commands, in Challoner's wording, ``Bear ye one another's burdens,'' and
then assigns each person his own burden. Augustine distinguishes the present
exchange of charity from the personal account at judgment (\emph{Sermo} 163B
§§1--6). Paul likewise joins sharing goods with teachers to testing one's own
work. The household becomes a place where help is real precisely because each
person also stands responsible before God.

The sowing image gives present conduct an eschatological horizon. What is
sown to disordered flesh ripens as corruption; what is sown to the Spirit
ripens as everlasting life. The command not to fail while doing good is
therefore patient rather than frantic. The time still available makes
opportunity morally serious, and Paul's charge to do good to all while giving
particular care to the household of faith joins universal benevolence to
ecclesial priority.

\subsection*{Morning mercy and nocturnal truth\enspace\properrefs{Grad.}}

The Gradual declares confession and praise good, then assigns mercy to the
morning and truth to the night. The complete Psalm~91 is a Sabbath song whose
later verses speak of the downfall of the wicked, the flourishing of the
just, planting in the house of the Lord, and fruitfulness in old age. The
appointed couplet does not print those images, but its morning and night
already make praise a whole-day vocation.

Augustine interprets praise as double confession: acknowledgment of sin and
proclamation of God's glory. Morning and night become prosperity and
adversity, in both of which mercy and truth are confessed
(\emph{Enarratio in Psalmum 91} §§3--4). Theodoret places the psalm within
eschatological Sabbath rest and understands the same pair as unceasing praise
of merciful providence and truthful judgment (PG~80:1615--1618). Bellarmine
develops the relation between visible mercy and judgments whose justice is
not always immediately apparent. Together they form perseverance without
reducing the two times to one identical allegory.

\subsection*{The great King: \latin{super omnem terram}\enspace\properrefs{All.}}

The Alleluia's praise reaches the universal field through its acclamation of
the great Lord and great King with \latin{super omnem terram}. That final
phrase is a liturgical adaptation. The Clementine Psalm~94:3 reads ``above all
gods,'' and Augustine comments upon that form by distinguishing the covenant
Lord from idols and by joining Israel and the nations in Christ the
cornerstone (\emph{Enarratio in Psalmum 94} §§5--6).

Theodoret's lemma reads \latin{super omnem terram}, matching the Missal, and he
opposes universal divine dominion to false gods (PG~80:1639--1642). His
transmitted lemma gives a patristic analogue to the Missal's wording.
Hebrews~3--4 receives the psalm's later warning about hearing God's voice
today and entering rest; it illuminates the whole psalm but does not turn the
Alleluia's single adapted verse into the warning half it does not appoint.

\subsection*{The Lord of life meets the widow at Nain\enspace\properrefs{Gosp.}}

Luke arranges two crowds around one meeting. Jesus approaches Nain with his
disciples and a large crowd; at the city gate a widow accompanies the body of
her only son with many from the city. No request is made. The Lord sees her,
is moved with mercy, tells her not to weep, touches the bier, and speaks to
the dead. The bearers stop, the young man sits up and speaks, and Jesus gives
him to his mother. Fear and praise then seize everyone: a great prophet has
risen, and God has visited his people.

The episode recalls Elias and Eliseus restoring sons to their mothers
(3~Kings~17:17--24; 4~Kings~4:32--37), although Luke names neither prophet.
The canonical resemblance stands beside Luke's own report. The crowd first
names a great prophet and then declares that God has visited his people; Luke
records both acclamations without saying that the crowd explicitly identifies
the prophet as God.

Cyril of Alexandria reads Christ's touch as life-giving because his flesh is
united to the Word. The command manifests divine authority, and the bodily
miracle pledges the general resurrection (\emph{Commentary on Luke}, Sermon
XXXVI, pp.~132--136). Ambrose turns to the ecclesial mystery: the widow is
Mother Church, her tears intercede for a fallen son, the bier figures the
cross, and restored speech becomes renewed confession (\emph{Expositio in
Lucam} V.89--92). Schuster receives this pattern in a penitential setting.

Augustine treats the youth as the middle of three raised figures: hidden,
public, and habitual sin, while never denying the bodily miracle itself
(\emph{Sermo} 98 §§1--7). Cyril's bodily and christological emphasis,
Ambrose's ecclesial reading, and Augustine's moral ordering are distinct
developments in the Church's reception of the episode.

\subsection*{Waiting becomes a new canticle\enspace\properrefs{Off.}}

The Offertory compresses Psalm~39. It retains patient waiting, divine regard,
answered supplication, and a new canticle placed in the mouth. It omits the
pit, the miry clay, the rock, steadied steps, and the many who see, fear, and
hope; it also substitutes several Latin forms for the Clementine verse. Those
omissions matter because the chant's compact arc is hearing-to-song, while
the complete psalm makes deliverance publicly exemplary.

Augustine reads the waiting voice christologically and ecclesially: renewed
humanity is raised from the pit, established upon Christ the rock, and taught
a new song (\emph{Enarratio in Psalmum 39} §§2--5). Theodoret holds Davidic
deliverance and corporate salvation together through repentance, death,
sin's mire, faith, incarnation, and resurrection (PG~80:1151--1154).
Bellarmine develops deliverance from carnal mire onto Christ and the song of
renewal. Hebrews~10 receives the later self-offering portion of the psalm,
with its own textual difference between a body and ears; it does not
erase the Offertory's selected beginning.

\subsection*{Sacramental guarding is joined to moral vigilance\enspace\properrefs{Sec.}}

The Secret asks that the sacraments guard the faithful and defend them always
against diabolical assaults. Its language names a personal enemy without
turning every temptation, fault, sickness, or misfortune into direct demonic
action. The Epistle has already required self-examination, gentleness,
generosity, and perseverance. Sacramental protection strengthens rather than
replaces those duties.

The Trinity Preface is the normal Sunday appointment and supplies the
Eucharistic action with Trinitarian confession. Schuster places the Secret
within a movement from patient prayer through defense toward Eucharistic
nourishment.

\subsection*{The flesh of the Word for the world's life\enspace\properrefs{Comm.}}

The Communion appoints only the final clause of Vulgate John~6:52, modern
6:51b. The chapter has moved from the multiplication and sea crossing through
manna, coming and believing, heavenly teaching, and the promise of
resurrection on the last day. After the appointed clause, the dispute over
eating the flesh, abiding, the hard saying, departure, and Peter's confession
continues. The antiphon must be heard within that full Eucharistic and
eschatological field.

Cyril reads the flesh as life-giving because of its union with the Word and
connects Christ's gift to ransom, sacrifice, and death defeated in Christ's
death (\emph{Commentary on John} IV.2, pp.~408--411). Augustine emphasizes
inward sacramental power, incorporation into Christ, the one bread, and
charity (\emph{Tractate on John} 26 §§12--13). Chrysostom contrasts manna as
type with Christ's food and preserves both teaching-and-faith and bodily
Mystery registers (\emph{Homily on John} 46 §§1--2).

Aquinas coordinates Word-flesh union, the reality of Christ's flesh, the
Passion, restored life, and ecclesial unity figured by many grains in one
bread (\emph{Super Ioannem} 6, lect.~6, §87463). These witnesses differ in
accent but converge upon the life-giving incarnate gift. The Missal's
\latin{pro sæculi vita} differs from the Clementine \latin{pro mundi vita};
the liturgical form governs the antiphon, while the canonical context governs
claims about the Gospel.

\subsection*{The heavenly gift acts upon mind and body\enspace\properrefs{Postcomm.}}

The Postcommunion asks that the operation of the heavenly gift possess minds
and bodies so that not private judgment but its effect may continually
precede within the recipients. Divine action reaches the whole person. Mind
is not discarded, body is not despised, and agency is not annihilated; rather,
the sacrament is asked to govern perception and conduct from within.

The Collect asked continuing mercy to cleanse, fortify, and govern the
Church. The Secret asked sacramental guarding against assaults. The final
prayer gathers both petitions into transformed embodiment. Its
\latin{praeveniat} orders divine gift before self-directed sense, while the
Epistle's imperatives show the corresponding human action: examine, bear,
share, sow, persevere, and do good. Grace's precedence and responsible life
remain one sacramental economy.
