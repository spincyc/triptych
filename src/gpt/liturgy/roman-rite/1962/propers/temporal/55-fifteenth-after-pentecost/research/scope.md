# Research scope: Fifteenth Sunday after Pentecost

## Identity and scope

This brief governs the GPT proper guide for the Fifteenth Sunday after
Pentecost in the Vatican typical edition of the 1962 *Missale Romanum*, printed
pp. 396--397, marginal nos. 1582--1591. The formulary is identified by edition,
heading, incipits, appointments, and boundaries, not by the ordinal alone. Its
ten proper elements are the Introit *Inclina, Domine, aurem tuam* (Ps. 85:1--4),
Collect *Ecclesiam tuam*, Epistle Gal. 5:25--26; 6:1--10, Gradual *Bonum est
confiteri* (Ps. 91:2--3), Alleluia *Quoniam Deus magnus Dominus* (printed as
Ps. 94:3 but materially ending with the wording of Ps. 46:3), Gospel Luke
7:11--16, Offertory *Exspectans exspectavi* (Ps. 39:2--4), Secret *Tua nos,
Domine, sacramenta custodiant*, Communion *Panis, quem ego dedero* (John 6:52),
and Postcommunion *Mentes nostras et corpora possideat*. [COV-001, COV-002,
COV-011; scripture-context, source-citation-coverage]

The 1962 Vatican facsimile controls Latin wording, rubrics, rank, references,
and boundaries. The Benziger 1962 printing is an independent comparison; the
Mame 1922 printing is the declared public-domain Latin antecedent; Cummiskey
1861 and the Douay--Rheims/Challoner supply public-domain English witnesses.
The 1962 images are collation evidence rather than republication licences.
[COV-001; source-citation-coverage]

This is an audit record. It separates the profile's five evidence states:

1. textual observations directly checked in appointed texts and complete
   biblical contexts;
2. documented historical orientation, with competing judgments kept distinct;
3. documented reception in checked ancient, medieval, Doctoral, saintly, and
   liturgical witnesses;
4. source-grounded cross-proper synthesis supported by the first three states;
5. exploratory proposals, confined to the interpretive section and controlled
   by the targeted precedent audit below.

An unverified lead supports no claim. References in square brackets identify
the joined lane findings whose evidence is being integrated.

## Appointed-text controls and complete contexts

### Introit: Psalm 85:1--4 (modern Psalm 86)

The continuous psalm is an afflicted servant's prayer for hearing,
preservation, mercy, guidance, joy, and vindication. The antiphon selects
clauses from vv. 1--3 but omits “needy and poor” and “holy”; the separate verse
is v. 4. The rest of the psalm grounds the appeal in the Lord's mildness and
abundant mercy, joins it to the coming adoration of all nations, asks for a
heart walking in truth, and ends by seeking a visible token of help. Its
language of compassion, patience, mercy, and truth recalls the covenantal
self-proclamation of Ex. 34:5--7 and Num. 14:17--20; Apoc. 15:3--4 is a
canonical analogue for divine kingship, true ways, and the worship of the
nations. These are textual and canonical relationships, not a demonstrated
literary-dependence claim. [SCR-001, SCR-019; scripture-context]

Exact English control: Challoner has “I have cried to thee all the day” at
Ps. 85:3 and “Give joy to the soul of thy servant” at Ps. 85:4. Editorial
forms such as “all day” or “gladden” may be used only as unquoted paraphrase.
[COV-002; source-citation-coverage]

### Epistle: Galatians 5:25--26; 6:1--10

The lection begins at the hinge after the fruit of the Spirit and the claim
that those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh. It turns the
flesh--Spirit contrast into communal conduct: reject rivalry, restore a fallen
person gently and self-watchfully, bear burdens, test one's own work, support
the teacher, sow toward the Spirit, persevere, and do good to all, especially
the household of faith. The surrounding letter keeps the passage within the
dispute over another gospel, circumcision, law, faith, freedom, adoption, the
cross, and the new creature; it is not generic moral counsel detached from
Galatians. [SCR-002; scripture-context]

Gal. 6:2 and 6:5 use different Greek nouns, *ta barē* and *to idion phortion*.
The first concerns mutual aid under another's oppressive burden; the second
closes the warning against comparison with each person's responsibility for
his own work and account. Gal. 5:13--14 and Lev. 19:17--18 ground the law of
Christ in neighbor-love; John 13:34--35 and 1 Cor. 9:19--23 are canonical
comparisons rather than citations made by Galatians. 2 Cor. 9:6--10 supplies a
Pauline parallel for seed, harvest, and every good work. [SCR-003;
scripture-context]

Exact English control: Challoner has “Bear ye one another's burdens,” “For
every one shall bear his own burden,” and “whilst we have time.” Shortened or
modernized forms may not be printed as quotations from that witness.
[COV-002; source-citation-coverage]

### Gradual: Psalm 91:2--3 (modern Psalm 92)

The appointed opening of a Sabbath canticle joins morning mercy and nightly
truth. The continuous psalm proceeds through delight in God's works, the
passing success of the wicked, and the just person's palm- and cedar-like
fruitfulness in God's house and in old age. Psalm 1 is a close canonical
analogue for the planted and seasonably fruitful just person, not an explicit
citation marked by Psalm 91. [SCR-004; scripture-context]

### Alleluia: Psalm 94:3 with a material Psalm 46:3 adaptation

Psalm 94 is a processional summons from joyful confession of the Lord as
creator and king to adoration, covenant identity, and the warning against the
wilderness generation's hardened refusal. Exodus 17 and Numbers 14 supply the
recalled testing and judgment; Heb. 3:7--4:11 explicitly reuses the psalm's
“to-day” and “rest” as a present warning and promise. [SCR-005;
scripture-context]

The Missal prints *Quoniam Deus magnus Dominus, et Rex magnus super omnem
terram*. Clementine Ps. 94:3 has *rex magnus super omnes deos*, while
Clementine Ps. 46:3 has *rex magnus super omnem terram*; Challoner likewise
distinguishes “above all gods” from “over all the earth.” The chant is therefore
a scripturally grounded material adaptation, but the evidence does not identify
the compiler or prove an intentional conflation. No project-made English
translation of the adapted clause may be printed as though Challoner supplied
it; use the verified Latin and describe the difference. [SCR-006, COV-002,
COV-014; scripture-context, source-citation-coverage]

### Gospel: Luke 7:11--16

At Naim, Jesus sees a widow accompanying her only son's bier, acts from
compassion without a reported request, commands the young man to rise, restores
him alive to his mother, and elicits fear, praise, and the crowd's declarations
that a great prophet has arisen and God has visited his people. Luke calls
Jesus “the Lord” at the moment of compassion. The restored man's sitting up and
speaking demonstrates the command's effect; Luke 7:17--23 makes the episode
part of the report reaching John. [SCR-007; scripture-context]

The closest canonical narrative analogue is Elias's restoration of the widow's
son in 3 Kings 17:17--24, especially the return of the living son to his mother.
The Sunamitess's son in 4 Kings 4:8--37 is a second prophetic analogue, though
the mother is not called a widow. Luke has already invoked Elias and the widow
of Sarepta at Luke 4:25--26. Luke does not formally cite either raising; Jesus'
direct command and the crowd's response remain features of Luke's own scene.
[SCR-008; scripture-context]

“God hath visited his people” resumes the visitation and redemption language
of Luke 1:68--79. The repetition supports a Lukan literary relation, while the
crowd's two acclamations remain its reported response rather than a complete
narrator's christological statement. [SCR-009; scripture-context]

### Offertory: Psalm 39:2--4 (modern Psalm 40)

The continuous psalm begins with patient waiting answered by rescue and a new
canticle, then moves through trust, obedient self-offering, public proclamation,
renewed danger, and a final confession that the poor petitioner remains in the
Lord's care. The Offertory retains waiting, divine attention, answered prayer,
and new song but omits the pit, mire, established feet, and the many who see,
fear, and hope. [SCR-010; scripture-context]

Heb. 10:5--10 explicitly reads Ps. 39:7--9 as Christ's speech on entering the
world and the doing of God's will as fulfilled in the one oblation of Christ's
body. The wording difference between the psalm's “ears hast thou pierced for
me” and Hebrews' “a body thou hast fitted to me” must remain visible. Hebrews
does not quote the appointed vv. 2--4 and does not explain why they were chosen
for this Mass. [SCR-011; scripture-context]

### Communion: John 6:52

The appointed final clause stands at the pivot of the bread-of-life discourse:
after the feeding, sea crossing, heavenly-bread teaching, and dispute, Christ's
flesh is promised for the world's life, and the discourse intensifies through
eating, drinking, abiding, eternal life, last-day resurrection, divided
discipleship, and Peter's confession. The Passover notice and manna material
prevent “life” from collapsing into the crowd's earlier physical satisfaction
by loaves. [SCR-012; scripture-context]

John 6 explicitly invokes Israel's manna and Is. 54:13. Matthew 26, Luke 22,
and 1 Cor. 11 supply materially related institution texts in which bread is
identified with Christ's body given or delivered, but John 6 does not narrate
the institution or quote its formula. [SCR-013; scripture-context]

The biblical and patristic lemmata have “life of the world”; the Missal's
*pro saeculi vita* is an adaptation. The checked reception corpus did not
explain that wording change. [PAT-021; patristic-reception]

## Historical orientation of the biblical passages

### Psalms

- **Psalm 85.** The Clementine title has *Oratio ipsi David* and Challoner
  describes a prayer for David. The inspected modern Catholic orientation
  classifies it as an individual lament by a poor, oppressed, devoted servant;
  Briggs proposes a postexilic corporate/public-worship horizon. The PBC
  Responsa of 1910, the Catholic Encyclopedia's *Psalms*, the USCCB NABRE
  Psalms introduction and Psalm 86 page, Briggs, and the exact Clementine and
  Challoner locus establish no precise composition date, place, original human
  audience, or episode in David's life. The first addressee is God. [COV-012;
  source-citation-coverage]
- **Psalm 91.** The title is a canticle for the Sabbath and names no personal
  author. The NABRE Psalm 92 page orients it as praise and thanksgiving by a
  protected speaker; Briggs proposes late-Greek-period temple and synagogue
  use. The same bounded source families establish no specific composition
  date, place, first congregation, or biographical stage. Sabbath, temple, and
  community are use or literary horizons, not recovered provenance.
  [COV-013; source-citation-coverage]
- **Psalms 94 and 46.** The Clementine title attributes Psalm 94 to David;
  Psalm 46 is for the sons of Core. NABRE treats the corresponding modern
  Psalms 95 and 47 as corporate worship or kingship hymns. Briggs proposes
  late-Persian/early-Greek and Persian-period horizons respectively, but the
  checked source families establish no secure target-specific date, place,
  congregation, or authorial life stage. Their titles, speakers, and wording
  remain distinct even where the chant joins phrases from both. [COV-014;
  source-citation-coverage]
- **Psalm 39.** The Clementine and Challoner title is Davidic. NABRE reads the
  opening as thanksgiving later joined to lament; Briggs proposes a postexilic
  composite community prayer. The checked PBC, Catholic Encyclopedia, NABRE,
  Briggs, Clementine, and Challoner set establishes a rescued speaker
  proclaiming in an assembly but no precise date, composition place, first
  audience, or Davidic-life episode. [COV-015;
  source-citation-coverage]

The chronology corpus's typed answer for all four appointed psalm groups is
`undated-in-tradition`; none of these historical or critical orientations may
be substituted for a chronology assertion. [SCR-015, COV-020;
scripture-context, source-citation-coverage]

### Galatians

Paul addresses Gentile converts being pressed toward circumcision. Pauline
authorship is secure, while recipient geography and date remain disputed. The
USCCB NABRE introduction favors northern Galatia around Ancyra and composition
at Ephesus about A.D. 54--55 during Paul's third missionary journey; it
preserves the less likely south-Galatian cities and A.D. 48--50 horizon. The
Catholic Encyclopedia records northern and southern theories and several dates.
The chronology corpus, which alone controls printed dates, carries four
disputed source labels, reproduced exactly in the chronology audit below.
[SCR-026, COV-016; scripture-context, source-citation-coverage]

### Luke 7

Traditional orientation connects the Gospel with Luke the Antiochene physician
and Pauline companion and preserves Achaia/Boeotia and a pre-end-of-Paul's-
Roman-imprisonment account. Luke 1:1--4 presents the evangelist as dependent on
earlier eyewitness traditions rather than as an eyewitness of Jesus. The NABRE
introduction generally places composition after Jerusalem's fall, often A.D.
80--90, for Theophilus and a largely Gentile-Christian, non-Palestinian
readership. Gadenz reports modern proposals including Antioch, Corinth,
Ephesus, Caesarea, and Rome and leaves the place uncertain; his modern majority
horizon is the 70s or 80s, with a minority pre-70 case. [SCR-027, COV-017;
scripture-context, source-citation-coverage]

The narrated event belongs to Jesus' Galilean ministry, after activity at
Capernaum, at Nain. The USCCB Luke 7 note and Gadenz's discussion of Luke
7:11--17 support the narrative sequence, the widow, the death, Jesus'
initiative and compassion, and the Elijah allusion. The chronology corpus
supplies no narrated-event date. The scripture-context lane reports direct
inspection of Gadenz, printed pp. 141--143; the coverage lane independently
verified the public publisher excerpt only through the introduction and does
not use those later pages for the page-2 orientation. The guide must cite the
printed locus accurately if it uses that commentary and must not imply that
the public excerpt contains it. [SCR-028, SCR-031, COV-017, COV-021;
scripture-context, source-citation-coverage]

### John 6

Traditional orientation attributes the Fourth Gospel to the aged apostle John
at Ephesus for later-generation Christians in Asia Minor. The NABRE
introduction describes developed Johannine reflection, probable composition in
the 90s, shaping by editing, and conflict with the synagogue; it does not
establish a modern composition city. Those orientations are alternatives, not
layers to collapse into one claim. [SCR-029, COV-018; scripture-context,
source-citation-coverage]

The narrated sequence lies in Jesus' Galilean ministry near Passover and moves
from the far shore of the Sea of Galilee/Tiberias to boats and a synagogue
discourse at Capernaum. Capernaum is the narrative setting, not the composition
place. The Passover notice is not an absolute date, and the chronology corpus
supplies no narrated-event assertion. [SCR-030, COV-018;
scripture-context, source-citation-coverage]

## Liturgical history and competing judgments

The three presidential prayers have a clustered antecedent in Wilson's Old
Gelasian edition, Book III, section XI, *Item alia Missa*, printed p. 230. It
prints *Ecclesiam tuam*, *Tua nos*, and *Mentes nostras* together but inserts a
second collect. The manuscript's probable seventh- or early-eighth-century
Frankish setting dates the codex, not the prayers' composition. Wilson's
apparatus records later placements at Sunday XVII and XVI and minor variants.
The underlying page image and apparatus were rechecked in the present research
pass. [LIT-001; liturgical-history]

Wilson's Hadrianum supplement, section CXXXIV, printed p. 174, gives the same
three-prayer cluster at Sunday XVI without the extra Gelasian collect. Its
chant cues differ from the target ensemble; another entry at Sunday XV carries
*Inclina* and *Exspectans*. The image and superscript-to-apparatus mapping were
rechecked. The ninth-century manuscripts and Wilson's Alcuin attribution do
not establish a universal early-medieval Sunday number or a transfer rationale.
[LIT-002; liturgical-history]

Three regional medieval sacramentaries continue the prayer trio at Sunday XVI:
Gellone II (about 1000--1025, the editor's dating), Sant Ruf (about 1125--1130,
explicitly hypothetical), and Vilabertran (main body, third quarter of the
twelfth century). A Valldemossa evangeliary-collectary of about 1400 pairs Luke
7 with *Ecclesiam tuam* at Sunday XVI, probably for a joined Matins--Lauds use
according to its editor. These witnesses show durable grouped transmission and
an intermediate Gospel--Collect convergence, not a universal calendar
equivalence. [LIT-007, LIT-009; liturgical-history]

Honorius's *Gemma animae* IV.78--81 divides the later constellation across
adjacent Sundays: Sunday XV has the target Introit, Collect, Galatians
exhortation, and Offertory with different intervening texts; Sunday XVI has
Luke 7 within a different office. His sub-lege/sub-gratia historical typology
is his medieval reception, while the distribution itself is liturgical data.
[LIT-008; liturgical-history]

The directly inspected 1570 Venice Missal prints the complete later sequence at
Sunday XV, in the same proper order as 1962 and with Credo after the Gospel.
The books are substantially continuous, not typographically identical. The
1962 page directs the Trinity Preface; the inspected 1570 target pages do not.
[LIT-003; liturgical-history]

The 1922 Mame and 1962 Vatican formularies retain the same substantive proper,
Credo, and Trinity-Preface direction, but the 1922 page also directs recurring
additional Collects, Secrets, and Postcommunions that the 1962 target page no
longer directs. The page comparison does not identify the legislative act or
complete general-rubric history behind that change. [LIT-010;
liturgical-history]

Schuster calls this Sunday *Quinto post Sancti Laurentii*, the last in an older
feast-relative sequence before a St Cyprian group, and judges the feast names
to be weekly milestones rather than thematic dedications. That is Schuster's
reconstruction, not the 1962 Missal's title. He also observes the continuation
from the preceding Sunday's Ps. 94:1 Alleluia to this Sunday's Ps. 94:3 and the
exact Lenten reuses of the Gradual, Gospel, Offertory, and Communion. Those
relationships do not establish the direction or reason of transfer. [LIT-004,
LIT-005; liturgical-history]

No checked source names the redactor who first assembled the present prayers,
chants, and readings, precisely dates the complete constellation, or explains
the movement from the examined Sunday-XVI witnesses to the later Roman Sunday
XV. Deshusses's critical Gregorian edition, fuller antiphoner and lectionary
stemmata, and more pre-1570 plenary missals were not reached. [LIT-011;
liturgical-history]

## Passage-by-passage reception matrix

The “other appointments” below are only those identified in the joined
1962-registry evidence. For Ps. 85, Galatians, and the Ps. 94/Ps. 46
adaptation, the join identifies the target proper but supplies no additional
appointment; this record makes no corpus-wide uniqueness claim. The registry
is a finding aid rather than textual control. [PRE-003, COV-011;
precedent-search, source-citation-coverage]

### Psalm 85:1--4 — Introit

- **Proper use identified:** Fifteenth Sunday after Pentecost.
- **Ancient direct reception:** Augustine, *Exposition on Psalm 86* §§1--6,
  NPNF 1.8 English, reads the prayer as the one whole Christ, humility receiving
  divine condescension, holiness received by grace, the Church's continuous
  cry, and the soul lifted to God. Theodoret, *Interpretatio in Psalmos*, PG
  80:1553--1556, Greek with facing Latin, reads David and Hezekiah, the poverty
  of human nature, relative innocence against aggressors, continual prayer,
  and the soul turned toward God. Their historical allocation and account of
  holiness materially differ and remain distinct. [PAT-001, PAT-013;
  patristic-reception]
- **Later saintly reception:** Bellarmine, *Commentary on the Book of Psalms*,
  Ps. 85:1--4, 1866 O'Sullivan translation, pp. 259--260, presents poverty as
  humility, holiness as received in justification and baptism, the all-day cry
  as fervent perseverance, and the lifted soul as love's ascent. The English
  edition omits Bellarmine's philological and version discussions. [PAT-018;
  patristic-reception]
- **Use and limits:** Supports the Introit's ecclesial, humble, grace-received,
  and persevering senses. The chant omits the “poor” and “holy” clauses that
  carry part of the commentators' reasoning. The checked reception did not
  explain the formulary's selection history. [PAT-001, PAT-013, PAT-018,
  PAT-021; patristic-reception]

### Galatians 5:25--6:10 — Epistle

- **Proper use identified:** Fifteenth Sunday after Pentecost.
- **Ancient direct reception:** Chrysostom, *Homilies on Galatians* 5--6,
  NPNF 1.13 English, develops humility, gentle correction, mutual endurance,
  Spirit-directed sowing, perseverance, and ordered beneficence. Augustine,
  *Sermo* 163B (Frangipane V) §§2--4, Morin, *Miscellanea Agostiniana* 1
  (1930), pp. 213--215, Latin facsimile, distinguishes infirmities borne in
  patient charity from each person's final account. The Morin facsimile,
  heading, text, and apparatus were directly checked; this supersedes the
  earlier transcription-only state. [PAT-005, PAT-006;
  patristic-reception]
- **Medieval Doctoral reception:** Aquinas, *Super Epistolam ad Galatas*, ch.
  6, lectures 1--2, §§87781--87782, Marietti/Corpus Thomisticum Latin with the
  Larcher English translation, coordinates mercy, correction, mutual aid,
  personal judgment, Spirit-directed work, and ordered charity. [PAT-007;
  patristic-reception]
- **Use and limits:** Supports mutual burden-bearing without erasing personal
  responsibility and action grounded in the Spirit. Theodoret's exact
  Gal. 5:25--6:10 locus remained unopened; the registered Gal. 3:16--22 passage
  cannot be stretched to cover it. Chrysostom is available here through an
  identified historical English translation rather than a fresh Greek
  collation. [COV-008, COV-009; source-citation-coverage]

### Psalm 91:2--3 — Gradual

- **Proper uses identified:** Fifteenth Sunday after Pentecost; Saturday after
  the Second Sunday of Lent. [PRE-003; precedent-search]
- **Ancient direct reception:** Augustine, *Exposition on Psalm 92* §§2--4,
  NPNF 1.8 English, reads the spiritual Sabbath and interprets morning mercy as
  prosperity and nightly truth as acceptance of adversity as just correction.
  Theodoret, PG 80:1615--1618, Greek with facing Latin, reads morning and night
  as a merism for continual praise, mercy as beneficence, and truth as just
  judgment. Their readings differ rather than form one consensus. [PAT-002,
  PAT-014; patristic-reception]
- **Later saintly reception:** Bellarmine, 1866 English, Ps. 91:1--3,
  pp. 289--291, adopts the prosperity/adversity application and credits
  Augustine. [PAT-018; patristic-reception]
- **Use and limits:** Supports continual praise through changing conditions and
  the psalm's Sabbath horizon. No checked reception source explains this
  Gradual's selection or its Lenten reuse. [PAT-021, LIT-005;
  patristic-reception, liturgical-history]

### Psalm 94:3 / Psalm 46:3 — Alleluia adaptation

- **Proper use identified:** Fifteenth Sunday after Pentecost.
- **Ancient direct reception:** Augustine, *Exposition on Psalm 95* §§5--6,
  10, NPNF 1.8 English, expounds “above all gods” in a christological and
  ecclesial account of one people joined in Christ. Theodoret, PG
  80:1639--1642, Greek with facing Latin, transmits the exact “over all the
  earth” lemma and reads universal divine dominion against false gods within a
  Josian and later apostolic/martyr horizon. Theodoret is a material reception
  analogue for the chant, not evidence of its transmission into the chant.
  [PAT-003, PAT-016; patristic-reception]
- **Later saintly reception:** Bellarmine, 1866 English, Ps. 94:1--5,
  pp. 298--299, expounds the biblical “above all gods” wording and God's power
  throughout the world. [PAT-018; patristic-reception]
- **Use and limits:** Supports universal kingship and ecclesial worship while
  requiring exact separation of the biblical and liturgical forms. The direct
  commentaries did not settle the textual history of the adaptation.
  [SCR-006, PAT-021; scripture-context, patristic-reception]

### Luke 7:11--16 — Gospel

- **Proper uses identified:** Fifteenth Sunday after Pentecost; Thursday after
  the Fourth Sunday of Lent; St Monica, Widow. [PRE-003; precedent-search]
- **Ancient direct reception:** Cyril of Alexandria, *Commentary on Luke*,
  Sermon XXXVI, Payne Smith English, pp. 132--136, presents the incarnate
  Word's unsolicited life-giving action, restored speech as evidence of bodily
  life, and the Gospel raisings as pledges of the general resurrection. The
  historic witness mixes Greek-catena and Syriac materials and was not freshly
  collated in either language. Ambrose, *Expositio Evangelii secundum Lucam*
  V.89--92, PL 15 Latin, reads the widow as Mother Church, the bier's wood
  through the Cross, restored speech as life, and return to the mother as
  restored communion. Augustine, *Sermon* 98 §§1--7, NPNF English, preserves
  the bodily miracle while interpreting the youth carried outside as public
  sin and his return as restoration to Mother Church. [PAT-008, PAT-009,
  PAT-019; patristic-reception]
- **Medieval reception:** Bede, *In Evangelium S. Lucae* II.7, PL
  92:417B--419A Latin, develops the Church as widowed mother and the public
  sinner restored by Christ's prior mercy and returned to ecclesial communion.
  This target locus was directly checked and is no longer an unresolved lead.
  [PAT-020, COV-009; patristic-reception, source-citation-coverage]
- **Use and limits:** Supports Christ's compassion and life-giving authority,
  the bodily miracle as pledge of resurrection, and the ecclesial reception of
  return to the mother. The allegorical convergence among Ambrose, Augustine,
  and Bede is reception, not proof that Luke encoded every detail allegorically.
  [PAT-008, PAT-009, PAT-019, PAT-020; patristic-reception]

### Psalm 39:2--4 — Offertory

- **Proper uses identified:** Fifteenth Sunday after Pentecost; Tuesday after
  the Fourth Sunday of Lent. [PRE-003; precedent-search]
- **Ancient direct reception:** Augustine, *Exposition on Psalm 40* §§2--5,
  NPNF 1.8 English, reads deliverance from sinful mire to Christ the rock and
  the renewed person's new song in the voice of Christ, Head and members.
  Theodoret, PG 80:1151--1154, Greek with facing Latin, moves from David's
  deliverance to humanity raised from death and sin through the incarnation
  toward resurrection hope. [PAT-004, PAT-015; patristic-reception]
- **Later saintly reception:** Bellarmine, 1866 English, Ps. 39:1--3,
  pp. 118--120, reads Christ and his people, long-awaited redemption, rescue by
  grace, establishment on Christ, and renewed song. [PAT-018;
  patristic-reception]
- **Use and limits:** Supports answered waiting, rescue, stability, and new
  praise. Augustine's, Theodoret's, and Bellarmine's reasoning depends partly
  on pit, mire, and rock clauses omitted by the Offertory, and none explains
  the chant's textual substitutions. [PAT-004, PAT-015, PAT-018, PAT-021;
  patristic-reception]

### John 6:52 — Communion

- **Proper uses identified:** Fifteenth Sunday after Pentecost; Thursday after
  the First Sunday of Lent. [PRE-003; precedent-search]
- **Ancient direct reception:** Cyril of Alexandria, *Commentary on John*
  IV.2, Payne Smith/Pusey historical English, pp. 408--419, reads the incarnate
  Word's flesh as ransom and life-giving self-gift; his later comparison of
  Christ's flesh with the touch of the bier comments on John 6:53 and can
  illuminate but is not part of the appointed verse. Augustine, *Tractate on
  John* 26 §§10--15, NPNF English, joins interior faith, sacramental reception,
  incorporation into Christ, ecclesial unity, and charity while distinguishing
  outward reception from spiritual effect. Chrysostom, *Homily on John* 46
  §§1--3, NPNF English, retains teaching-and-faith and Eucharistic registers,
  contrasts manna's type with present reality, and joins participation in the
  Mysteries to transformed life. [PAT-010, PAT-011, PAT-012;
  patristic-reception]
- **Medieval Doctoral reception:** Aquinas, *Super Evangelium Ioannis* 6,
  lecture 6, §87463, Marietti/Corpus Thomisticum Latin, teaches life through the
  flesh as instrument of the Word, true Eucharistic gift, ecclesial unity, and
  the universally sufficient effect of Christ's Passion. [PAT-017;

## Source-grounded cross-proper synthesis

### 1. Mercy heard becomes praise voiced

The Introit's all-day plea and lifted soul, the Collect's confession of the
Church's dependence on continuing mercy, Christ's unsolicited compassion at
Nain, the restored youth's speech and the crowd's praise, and the Offertory's
answered waiting and new canticle form the strongest governing movement. The
Gradual places that praise between morning mercy and nightly truth. Augustine
receives the Introit as the continuing cry of the whole Christ and the
Offertory as the renewed person's song; Cyril, Ambrose, Augustine, and Bede
preserve Christ's prior compassion and life-giving action in the Gospel;
Schuster explicitly reads the Gospel's ecclesial intercession, the
Offertory's patient waiting, and the sacramental conclusion as a liturgical
movement. The youth's unreported words are not identified with the assembly's
psalm, and the sequence makes no claim about historical compiler intention.
[SCR-001, SCR-004, SCR-007, SCR-010, SCR-014; PAT-001, PAT-002, PAT-004,
PAT-008, PAT-009, PAT-019, PAT-020; LIT-006; THE-001, THE-007;
scripture-context, patristic-reception, liturgical-history,
theological-synthesis]

### 2. Divine gift grounds responsible action

The Collect's *sine te* confession and the Postcommunion's request that the
heavenly gift's operation take possession of mind and body frame the Epistle's
imperatives. The result holds dependence and action together: walking in the
Spirit, gentle correction, mutual support, self-examination, sowing,
perseverance, and beneficence are neither independent human achievement nor
erased responsibility. Augustine distinguishes present infirmity borne in
charity from the final personal account; Chrysostom and Aquinas coordinate
mercy, discipline, spiritual sowing, and ordered charity. The Secret adds the
defensive petition that the sacraments guard against diabolical assault,
without promising automatic immunity or reducing sacramental grace to a
talisman. [SCR-002, SCR-003; PAT-005, PAT-006, PAT-007; THE-003, THE-004,
THE-006, THE-010; scripture-context, patristic-reception,
theological-synthesis]

### 3. Life is given in distinct but ordered registers

Galatians speaks of walking in the Spirit and reaping eternal life; Luke
narrates the temporal bodily restoration of a dead young man; the Communion
speaks of Christ's flesh given for the world's life; the Postcommunion seeks
the gift's operation in mind and body. Cyril's Lucan sermon makes the bodily
raising a pledge of general resurrection, while Cyril, Augustine, Chrysostom,
and Aquinas read John 6 incarnationally, sacramentally, ecclesially, and
eschatologically. Agricultural, moral-spiritual, biological, sacramental, and
final-resurrection senses remain distinct, and perseverance is not the cause
of Christ's sovereign raising. [SCR-007, SCR-012--SCR-014; PAT-008,
PAT-010--PAT-012, PAT-017; THE-002, THE-006; scripture-context,
patristic-reception, theological-synthesis]

### 4. Personal prayer opens into ecclesial and universal horizons

The singular servant lifts his soul; the Church asks to be cleansed,
protected, and governed; believers bear one another's burdens and do good to
all, with priority toward the household of faith; the crowd recognizes divine
visitation; the Alleluia acclaims the great King with the all-earth adaptation;
and the Communion names Christ's flesh for the world's life. Augustine reads
Psalm 85 through Head and members, Psalm 94 through the one people joined in
Christ, and John 6 through ecclesial incorporation; Aquinas likewise connects
the sacrament's ecclesial unity with the Passion's universal sufficiency. The
crowd's “great prophet” and “God hath visited” remain its own acclamations,
and universal language neither excludes the household of faith nor proves
universal salvation. [SCR-019; PAT-001, PAT-003, PAT-011, PAT-017; THE-008,
THE-011; scripture-context, patristic-reception, theological-synthesis]

### 5. The inherited prayer cluster gathers the action

The Collect begins from inability to stand safely without divine mercy; the
Secret asks sacramental guarding; the Postcommunion asks that the heavenly
gift's operation possess mind and body. Their early clustered transmission is
documented independently of the later chant-and-reading constellation, and
Schuster receives them as a movement into sacramental nourishment and
whole-person governance. In the 1962 sequence they frame the Epistle's moral
action and the Communion's christological center. The evidence establishes a
prayer cluster and an enacted relation in this formulary, not a single date of
composition or an automatic sacramental effect. [LIT-001, LIT-002, LIT-006,
LIT-007; THE-003, THE-006; liturgical-history, theological-synthesis]
