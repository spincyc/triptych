## Organized brief: the cross-proper argument

The strongest argument is the relation between received life and responsible
communal action. Mercy gives and sustains a life its recipients could not
produce, restores persons to relationships, and equips their bodies and
minds for charity, perseverance, and praise. Christ's effective word and
life-giving flesh supply the central ground; the prayers specify continuing
dependence; the Epistle specifies the conduct of those so sustained.

The synthesis commentary should have the following five functional units,
with each substantial argument developed once. Themes and Movement may
introduce the same relations in three to five developed units across its two
pages. The fuller Source-Grounded Synthesis can develop the historical
prayer-group evidence and independent liturgical conjunctions without
repeating the entire commentary. No unit is an abridged procession through
the ten proper headings.

### C1. Mercy restores persons within a community capable of charity

**Class 4; elements:** Collect, Epistle, Gospel, Communion, Postcommunion.
**Joined lanes:** scripture-context SCR-009–SCR-011; patristic-reception
PAT-005–PAT-009, PAT-011, PAT-019–PAT-022; theological-synthesis
THE-001–THE-002; liturgical-history LIT-002; precedent-search PRE-005/PRE-014.

Integration: the Collect's corporate dependence and Luke's return of a
living son to his mother support restoration within relationships. Paul
describes Spirit-given life through gentle correction, shared burdens,
self-examination, teacher support, and good done to all. Develop the tension
between Galatians 6:2 and 6:5 rather than deleting either side. Chrysostom
relates correction to spiritual mildness; Augustine's Frangipane V
distinguishes shared infirmity from each person's account, including failure
to care for a brother; Aquinas specifies endurance, assistance, and
intercession while preserving personal reckoning. Theodoret's Galatians 6
reading gives an additional Greek-tradition account of charity and
restorative care.

Reception supplies the ecclesial interpretation of Naim. Ambrose V.89–92,
Augustine Sermo 98 §§2,4–5, and Bede II.7, PL 92:417B–419A, develop Mother
Church and restored sinners in different ways. Bede explicitly reaches
ecclesial communion and priestly reconciliation. Augustine's John Tractate
26.13,15–18 joins sacramental participation to the unity and charity of
Christ's body. The Liturgical Year p. 348 independently joins Pauline
mutual help to Johannine abiding. These witnesses support an ecclesial and
sacramental argument more substantial than a generic injunction to kindness.

Controlling distinctions: the widow's child is literally dead, with no sin
imputed to him by Luke; the ecclesial moral reading is attributed to its
exegetes. Mutual help neither transfers culpability nor duplicates Christ's
power to raise. Morin's possible marginal gloss on a repeated charity
formula must not become the indispensable premise of the burden argument.
The Gospel's unrequested initiative is an absence within this narrative,
not a universal assertion that faith or petition never accompanies miracles.

### C2. Life-giving flesh restores the body and promises fulfillment

**Class 4; elements:** Epistle, Gospel, Communion, Postcommunion.
**Joined lanes:** SCR-012–SCR-014, SCR-018, SCR-020–SCR-021;
PAT-008, PAT-010–PAT-012, PAT-017; THE-003–THE-004;
LIT-003/LIT-007; PRE-005/PRE-008/PRE-010/PRE-013/PRE-014.

Integration: Galatians' corrupting orientation toward one's own flesh is
read within its vice/fruit contrast; it is not a judgment that bodily nature
is evil. The same formulary presents the young man's bodily restoration,
Christ's flesh for the world's life, and the heavenly gift's action upon
minds and bodies. Aquinas Super Galatas 5.7 expressly rejects destruction
of nature while retaining fasting, vigils, and labor; Summa III.79.1 ad 3
distinguishes present bodily conduct from future bodily incorruption.

Cyril provides the strongest direct Gospel–Communion witness. His John
IV.2 pp. 409–414 treats the appointed bread/flesh clause; pp. 418–419 use
the Naim touch in the exposition of the following verse. His Luke Sermon
XXXVI pp. 133–135 explains life-giving flesh united to the Word. The
transmission boundary is material: the opening unrequested-initiative
paragraph at p. 132 is a Greek-catena supplement to a Syriac lacuna, and
p. 135 note r contains additional fragments. The uninterrupted surviving
argument and the continuous John commentary support the central connection
without disguising the supplements as a complete original sermon.

Augustine Tractate 26, Chrysostom John Homily 46, and Aquinas Super Ioannem
6.6 distinguish outward reception from abiding or worthy participation.
Chrysostom's alternatives for earlier bread language do not negate his
later exposition of Christ's flesh and sacramental incorporation. The
Liturgical Year p. 355 explicitly joins Naim, Communion, and mind/body
efficacy; Schuster p. 141 joins nourishing sacrament, universal expiation,
and the Postcommunion's governed faculties.

Controlling distinctions: Naim's restoration to mortal life, present grace,
and final resurrection are separate. Eternal beatitude is not automatic
for all people or all outward recipients. The Gospel and Communion share
life-giving power without becoming the same event. The Lord touches the
bier, not an explicitly narrated corpse. Elias prays in 3 Kings 17, while
Jesus commands at Naim; John 11 prevents turning that local difference into
a claim that Jesus never prays before raising the dead.

### C3. Life received requires sustained action amid dependence and waiting

**Class 4; elements:** Introit, Collect, Epistle, Gradual, Gospel,
Offertory, Secret, Postcommunion.
**Joined lanes:** SCR-005–SCR-006, SCR-008–SCR-010, SCR-019;
PAT-001–PAT-002, PAT-004–PAT-007, PAT-014–PAT-015, PAT-023/PAT-025;
THE-001/THE-004; LIT-001/LIT-007; PRE-007/PRE-013/PRE-014.

Integration: all-day petition, continuing mercy, morning/night praise,
present opportunity, due-time harvest, expectant prayer, repeated custody,
and the gift's constant action keep the sudden Gospel raising within a
larger account of sustained life. Galatians moves from the life of the
Spirit to walking and unwearied good. Aquinas III.79.2 explains power to
attain glory without immediate admission; Super Galatas 6.2 joins the
farmer's patience to doing good while time remains.

Explain real reception differences. Augustine on Psalm 91 reads
morning/night as prosperity/adversity; Theodoret reads comprehensive praise
by day and night; Bellarmine offers visible mercy versus hidden justice and
then credits Augustine for the prosperity/tribulation account. Psalm 39's
new song follows rescue but the psalm resumes petition. Augustine's Psalm
39 moves from rescue onto Christ the rock to further walking and future
crown. Theodoret and Bellarmine develop redemption and resurrection without
making the appointed antiphon contain its omitted pit, mire, or rock.

Controlling distinctions: sustained dependence is not passivity; the
immediate raising supplies no promise that each mourner receives an
immediate miracle. These unlike times are not a hidden timetable or one
speaker's consecutive biography. Keep P3's practical temporal proposal
distinct from this source-grounded account of perseverance.

### C4. Mercy gives particular voices a public and universal horizon

**Class 4; elements:** Introit, Collect, Epistle, Gradual, Alleluia,
Gospel, Offertory, Communion.
**Joined lanes:** SCR-005–SCR-008, SCR-010–SCR-012, SCR-015–SCR-019,
SCR-022; PAT-001–PAT-004, PAT-013–PAT-016, PAT-024–PAT-029;
THE-006–THE-007; LIT-001/LIT-011; PRE-005/PRE-012/PRE-014.

Integration: hearing and mercy petitioned in the Introit meet praise of
mercy and truth in the Gradual, the crowd's glorification after Naim, and
the received Offertory canticle. The particular servant, Church, son, and
household belong within all-earth kingship, good toward all, and flesh
given for world-life. Aquinas Super Galatas 6.2 grounds beneficence in the
divine image, with practical priority for the faithful and continued care
for even an enemy's human nature. The Liturgical Year p. 354 links restored
children, the Church's desire for worldwide glorification, and Offertory
singing.

The Alleluia needs two distinct reception lines. Psalm 94 supplies the
principal attribution and its creation/flock/wilderness context. Its
Clementine lemma ends above all gods; Theodoret PG 80:1641A/1642A directly
attests an all-earth lemma. Psalm 46 supplies the exact material parallel
and its invitation to nations, kingship, ascent, and gathered peoples.
Augustine Enarratio 46.2–4, Theodoret PG 80:1205D–1208B, Aquinas Super
Psalmo 46 n. 2, and Bellarmine/O'Sullivan p. 144 directly interpret that
parallel: victorious apostolic proclamation, universal dominion, and
providential government. These are positive ancient and later witnesses
for the separately required row; none establishes how the Roman adaptation
arose. Aquinas's three dimensions are universality, eternity, and authority;
Bellarmine emphasizes providence and government. Retain those differences.

Controlling distinctions: the boy's first words are unreported and the
crowd's praise is the crowd's. No source warrants identifying his speech
with the Offertory. The textual visitation parallels in Luke 1/7 and Exodus
4 are not a newly established composition theory. Universal scope is not
universal final salvation, an exclusion of special obligations, or a
political constitution. Do not transplant the ancient witnesses' collective
anti-Jewish polemic into present judgment; their actual readings include
believing Jews, apostles, and gentile incorporation.

### C5. Sacramental protection strengthens whole-person conduct

**Class 4; elements:** Collect, Epistle, Secret, Communion, Postcommunion.
**Joined lanes:** THE-001/THE-003/THE-005;
PAT-007/PAT-011–PAT-012/PAT-017; LIT-003–LIT-004/LIT-007–LIT-009;
PRE-005/PRE-009/PRE-013/PRE-014; COV-001–COV-002/COV-009.

Integration: the Collect asks cleansing, defense, and governance; the
Secret names sacramental custody against demonic assaults; the
Postcommunion asks the heavenly gift's action to possess minds and bodies
and go before human inclination. Galatians requires self-watchfulness and
active charity. Aquinas III.79.6 distinguishes inward strengthening through
union with Christ and protection through the Passion without removal of
the capacity to sin. III.79.1 ad 2 and III.79.3 preserve active charity and
the recipient's disposition. Schuster's pp. 139–141 and The Liturgical Year
p. 355 are documented liturgical interpretations of the prayers' relations.

The prayer-group history gives a second, separately sourced dimension.
Wilson's Gelasian III.xi p. 230 and Gregorian supplement XXXIIII p. 174
place all three prayers together, with differing grouping, numbering, and
word order. The historical argument concerns inherited prayer material and
grouping; it cannot authenticate the full later Mass, since O's marginal
chant cues differ and some cues are later additions. Develop the exact
history in the fuller historical synthesis rather than using it to certify
the theological argument as compiler intention.

Controlling distinctions: the demonic object of the Secret remains real
within the prayer's theological grammar; protection is neither immunity
from temptation nor a merely social metaphor. Grace's priority does not
abolish reason, willing, or bodily goodness. The dead boy is not a license
to receive the Eucharist while knowingly remaining in mortal sin:
Aquinas III.79.3 distinguishes nourishment from first entry into spiritual
life. Rights or catalog repair belongs to its owner and does not negate
the checked prayer and doctrinal evidence in hand.

### The prayer group and the later formulary

LIT-004: H. A. Wilson, *The Gelasian Sacramentary* (Clarendon, 1894),
III.xi, p. 230/PDF 323, prints *Ecclesiam tuam*, an additional *Da
quaesumus Domine hanc mentem*, Secret *Tua nos*, and Postcommunion *Mentes
nostras*. The group is *Item alia missa*, not a named Fifteenth Sunday.
Wilson's note reports seventeenth-Sunday numbering in R/S/Gerb. and Ménard,
sixteenth in Pamelius. Secret word order and Postcommunion Domine/quaesumus
placement differ from the accepted 1962 witness; Wilson's *sed* corrects V's
*sunt*, with Tommasi credited. These are checked edition/apparatus facts.

LIT-008–LIT-009: Wilson, *The Gregorian Sacramentary under Charles the
Great*, HBS 49 (1915), supplement XXXIIII, p. 174/PDF 232, preserves the
three prayers under *Dominica XVI post Pentecosten*. The internal heading
controls, not the page's final-section running head XVII. R's main prayer
text and O's marginal antiphonary cues have distinct provenance. Wilson's
introduction xvii–xviii dates R/O to the ninth century, with qualified
pre-850 judgments; this is manuscript dating, not prayer composition.
The manuscripts do not name the supplement's compiler, but Wilson p. xxi
argues for Alcuin from Micrologus testimony. Preserve both statements and
do not attribute the prayers personally to Gregory.

O's p. 174 cues are *Justus es*, *Unam petii*, *Oravi*, *Vovete*;
p. 173 puts *Inclina*, *Bonum est confidere*, *Expectans*, and *Qui
manducat* beside another prayer group. *Confidere* must not silently become
the current *confiteri*. Brackets mark later additions/substitutions;
parentheses mark loss through binding. Prayer-group continuity therefore
does not establish the full later chants/readings, a first full-formulary
date, the cause of a XV/XVI transfer, or original compiler intent.

dedications. It identifies reuse of the Gradual on Saturday after Lent II,
Gospel on Thursday after Lent IV, Offertory on Tuesday after Lent IV, and
Communion on Thursday after Lent I. These exact cross-formulary claims
remain Schuster's: this lane did not independently collate those Lenten

source, not necessarily the identical antiphon. The Liturgical Year
