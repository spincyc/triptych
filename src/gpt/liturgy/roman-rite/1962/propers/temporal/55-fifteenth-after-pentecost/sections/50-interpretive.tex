\section*{The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities}

\noindent\emph{The following six connections are exploratory editorial and
AI-assisted proposals. They are not sourced historical intent or teaching
attributed to the witnesses cited elsewhere.}

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Carried burdens and the halted bier.\enspace\properrefs{Ep., Gosp.}}
\emph{Precedent result: near analogue located.} Galatians' \latin{onera
portate} and \latin{onus suum portabit} stand beside Luke's bier-bearers,
\latin{qui portabant}, halted before Christ's command. \textbf{Fruit.}
Reciprocal aid is a true work of charity, yet even faithful accompaniment
cannot manufacture the life Christ gives. The shared carrying vocabulary sets
human solidarity beside its limit at the funeral procession.
\textbf{Strongest limit.} Moral fault is not
equated with bodily death; Christ's sovereign act neither erases mutual aid
nor dissolves personal responsibility, and no selection principle is proved.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Speech received, song supplied.\enspace\properrefs{Gosp., Off.}}
\emph{Precedent result: near analogue located.} Luke says that the restored
youth begins to speak but records none of his words; the later Offertory
places a new canticle in the worshipper's mouth. \textbf{Fruit.} The assembly
answers restored but unrecorded speech with appointed praise. The ritual
succession supplies a song after the narrative deliberately leaves the
youth's utterance unheard. \textbf{Strongest
limit.} Luke does not say that the youth praised, and Psalm~39 does not make
him its speaker; the relation belongs to the liturgical sequence rather than
to the Gospel's narrated action.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{The proper's several clocks.\enspace\properrefs{Int., Grad., Ep., Off.}}
\emph{Precedent result: near analogue located.} The Introit's cry lasts all
day, the Gradual appoints morning mercy and nightly truth, the Epistle names
due-season harvest and present opportunity, and the Offertory waits upon the
Lord. \textbf{Fruit.} Daily dependence, continual praise, patient endurance,
and readiness to act can inhabit one Christian discipline. Daily, seasonal,
opportune, and awaited time coexist
without collapsing urgency into impatience. \textbf{Strongest limit.} The
expressions retain their psalmic, Pauline, and ritual genres; they reveal no
hidden timetable and promise no schedule for divine intervention.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Four registers of life.\enspace\properrefs{Ep., Gosp., Comm., Postcomm.}}
\emph{Precedent result: near analogue located.} Galatians joins life in the
Spirit to an everlasting harvest; Luke narrates bodily restoration at Nain;
the Communion names Christ's flesh for the world's life; and the
Postcommunion asks divine operation to possess mind and body. \textbf{Fruit.}
Life is received, restored, cultivated, and directed toward consummation
without turning those acts into synonyms. Agricultural, moral-spiritual,
biological, sacramental, and
eschatological speech correct and deepen one another across the formulary.
\textbf{Strongest limit.} Nain is restoration to mortal life, ethical sowing
does not cause resurrection, and the Eucharistic and eschatological claims
remain those of their own texts.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{From standing to guarding to possession.\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Sec., Postcomm.}}
\emph{Precedent result: near analogue located.} The Collect confesses the
Church's inability to stand safely without God; the Secret asks sacramental
guarding; the Postcommunion asks the gift's operation to possess mind and
body. \textbf{Fruit.} Dependence opens through protection into transformation
of the whole person. The three orations enact a cumulative grammar of
incapacity, custody, and interior
divine action. \textbf{Strongest limit.} Their liturgical progression proves
no joint literary composition; sacramental effect is neither automatic nor
talismanic, and divine possession does not annihilate human faculties.

\Needspace{10\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Ear, eye, touch, and mouth.\enspace\properrefs{Int., Gosp., Off.}}
\emph{Precedent result: near analogue located.} The Introit appeals to the
Lord's ear; Christ sees the widow and touches the bier; the youth begins to
speak; the crowd glorifies God; and the Offertory joins answered prayer to a
new canticle placed in the mouth. \textbf{Fruit.} Prayer heard, misery seen,
death touched, and life voiced form an embodied procession of mercy across
chant and narrative. The senses serve encounter and communion rather than
remaining private possession. \textbf{Strongest limit.} The sequence defines
no fixed psychology of worship, does not identify the narrative speakers with
the liturgical singer, and establishes no historical principle of compilation.
