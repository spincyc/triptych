## Canonical inventory and historical coordinates

### Psalm 7:12 — Alleluia

- The Greek and Latin superscription presents the psalm as David's lament concerning Chus/Cush the Benjaminite. The *Catholic Encyclopedia* groups Psalm 7 among psalms traditionally associated with David's persecution under Saul, but neither Scripture nor the title gives a writing site.
- The table therefore uses `Judah / Israel; exact site unrecorded` and `c. 1010 BC` as approximate traditional metadata. David is heard before secure kingship, appealing from pursuit and accusation to the God who examines hearts and judges peoples; Israel's worshipping community is the inherited audience.
- The identity of Cush and the precise incident are unrecoverable. The NABRE treats Psalm 7 as an individual lament and the Psalms introduction cautions that no psalm can be securely dated from its title.

### Psalm 9:11–13 — Offertory

- The superscription calls Psalm 9 Davidic but gives no life episode or composition site. The table therefore uses `Israel; exact site unrecorded`, `c. 1000 BC`, and explicitly leaves David's biographical stage unspecified.
- Its thanksgiving for judgment and worldwide rule turns toward Zion, the oppressed, and the cry of the afflicted. It first trains Israel's worshippers while opening Israel's praise toward the nations.
- In the Greek and Latin tradition Psalms 9–10 are a single alphabetic poem; the Hebrew tradition transmits them as two. Modern historical author, date, and writing site remain unknown.

### Psalm 24:1–2, 16, 18 (modern Psalm 25) — Introit

- The superscription attributes the acrostic lament to David but supplies no life event or location. The table therefore gives `Israel; exact site unrecorded`, `c. 1000 BC`, and states that no responsible point within David's life can be recovered from the title.
- The prayer joins trust, guidance, remembered mercy, forgiveness, loneliness, and affliction before widening in its final verse to Israel's redemption. The inherited audience is thus Israel at prayer rather than a reconstructed private circle.
- Modern scholarship identifies its acrostic lament form but cannot securely establish author, date, or place. Vulgate/Septuagint numbering is one below the common modern number.

### Psalm 54:17, 19, 23 (modern Psalm 55) — Gradual

- The superscription calls the maskil Davidic but names no incident or site. The table uses `Israel; exact site unrecorded`, `c. 1000 BC`, and does not identify the betraying companion with a particular figure from David's biography.
- The speaker suffers public violence and a trusted companion's betrayal, prays throughout the day, and entrusts his burden to God. The song carries personal crisis into Israel's common worship and supplies language later echoed directly in 1 Peter 5:7.
- Modern author, date, city, and historical betrayer remain unknown; the city imagery does not by itself prove Jerusalem as the place of composition.

### Luke 15:1–10 — Gospel and Communion

- Ancient tradition identifies the Antiochene physician Luke, Paul's companion, as author. The proper row deliberately joins two distinct inherited strands rather than treating them as one itinerary claim: an ancient *Prefatio* says that Luke wrote in Achaia (present-day Greece), while an early chronology places the Gospel before Acts, around AD 60.
- Luke addresses Theophilus and similarly catechized Christians after participation in apostolic mission. The Gospel–Acts project locates Israel's promises, Jesus' saving work, and the Gentile mission within one salvation history.
- The modern majority dates Luke about AD 80–90, treats the precise city as unknown, and describes the author as a non-Palestinian addressing a largely Gentile, non-Palestinian audience. These judgments remain in the explanatory row rather than replacing the requested traditional metadata.
- Luke 9:51–19:27 frames the material as journeying toward Jerusalem; Luke 13:22 supplies the nearer travel notice, but Luke 15 names no town. The narrated-event row therefore says `Journey toward Jerusalem; exact site unrecorded` and does not adopt an unsupported harmonized region. Its c. AD 28–29 range identifies the traditional late-ministry horizon without pretending to recover a town.
- The immediate hearers are tax collectors and sinners drawing near, and Pharisees and scribes murmuring. Jesus defends his welcome by joining the seeker's initiative, repentance, and heaven's joy; the Communion repeats verse 10 from within the same dossier.

### 1 Peter 5:6–11 — Epistle

- Christian tradition identifies Peter as author, writing from Rome—the `Babylon` of 1 Peter 5:13—about the end of AD 63 or beginning of 64. He writes as apostle and fellow elder near the end of his ministry, before the Neronian persecution and his martyrdom, with Silvanus involved in transmission and Mark present.
- The recipients are Christian communities across Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia in Asia Minor (principally present-day Turkey), probably largely Gentile. The appointed close joins humility, God's care, resistance to the devil, worldwide Christian solidarity in suffering, and God's call to eternal glory.
- The NABRE records substantial modern disagreement: some scholars retain Petrine authorship; others propose a later author, and a mediating judgment places a Petrine disciple or circle at Rome about AD 70–90. Cultivated Greek, Septuagint use, Pauline contacts, and the social horizon drive the dispute. The traditional Roman metadata remains in the proper row, while these alternatives are disclosed in its prose.

## Geography and chronology controls

- Composition locations, recipient locations, and narrated-event locations are kept distinct. `Achaia` is the Roman province in present-day Greece; `Rome` is in present-day Italy; and the five recipient provinces of 1 Peter lie in Asia Minor, principally present-day Turkey. Luke's travel narrative supplies direction toward Jerusalem but not a recoverable town or region for Luke 15.
- Traditional Davidic dates are deliberately approximate. The psalm rows use `c.` dates and retain `exact site unrecorded`; none presents poetic setting or later liturgical use as a recovered writing address.
- The Gospel event date follows the traditional chronology used consistently in this project: public ministry beginning c. AD 27 and this Jerusalem-journey discourse late in that ministry, c. AD 28–29. The chronology itself is disputed, so the event row names a ministry phase as well as an approximate range.

## Material limitations and unresolved questions

- The traditional dates for all four Davidic psalms are orientation dates, not demonstrated composition years. Psalm titles may signal authorship, collection, liturgical use, or association; Catholic tradition has not defined David as sole author of the Psalter.
- Psalm 7's Cush, Psalm 54's betrayer and city, and every psalm's writing site remain unidentified. No location was inferred merely from Zion, city, desert, or enemy imagery.
- Luke's Achaian composition place and c. AD 60 date come from separate inherited arguments. The city within Achaia is not named, and the modern c. AD 80–90 dating remains materially different.
- Luke itself supplies only the larger Jerusalem-journey frame and no town for the discourse; no narrower harmonized event location is asserted.
- First Peter's authorship and date remain contested. The table reports Rome and AD 63–64 because the requested metadata is traditional, not because the later Petrine-circle alternative has been resolved.
- No independent theological review is recorded. The working guide remains an aid to study rather than an official liturgical or critical edition.
