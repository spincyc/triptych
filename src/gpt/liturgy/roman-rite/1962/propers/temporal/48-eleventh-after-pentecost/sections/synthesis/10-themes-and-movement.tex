\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\label{triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

\begingroup\footnotesize
\setlength{\parskip}{0.28em}

\begin{massbox}{Governing thesis: the risen Christ opens a people to receive his Gospel, confess it in deed and word, and return his gifts through sacrifice, mercy, and praise}
The Mass moves from God's holy dwelling to the heavenly remedy of mind and
body. Between them, apostolic tradition gives faith its object, Christ opens
hearing and speech, the psalms turn help into thanksgiving, and the altar
receives service and firstfruits. Grace is divine life making a people
concordant, articulate, sacrificial, generous, and destined for resurrection.
\end{massbox}

\subsection*{God makes the house and gives it the Gospel}

Psalm~67 begins with divine agency. The God who arises before his enemies is
Father of orphans, Judge of widows, liberator of captives, guide from Sinai,
and Lord of Zion. The Introit selects the center of that procession: God dwells
in holiness, makes the concordant inhabit one house, and gives strength to his
people. Hilary, Augustine, and Cassiodorus read the house through Acts~4:32,
the believers made one heart and soul. Augustine says grace constructs this
dwelling; Theodoret sees persons directed toward the one aim of virtue. The
Church enters Mass as God's work: a scattered multitude made capable of common
hearing and praise.

The Collect names the principle of that creation. God's abundant goodness
exceeds both merit and request, forgives what conscience fears, and adds what
prayer does not presume. Its verbs move from transcendence through mercy into
pardon and increase. Aquinas's doctrine of divine liberality clarifies the
logic; Schuster hears the added good reach perfected charity and union with
God. Grace does not merely cancel debt. It enlarges the worshipper for holiness
and communion.

First Corinthians~15 then gives this people the word it must receive. Paul
preached a Gospel the Corinthians received, in which they stand, by which they
are saved, and which they must hold. He handed on what he received: Christ died
for sins, was buried, rose on the third day according to the Scriptures, and
appeared to witnesses. Irenaeus and Tertullian defend here the real flesh of
Christ; Chrysostom stresses transmission, burial, cumulative appearances, and
Paul's humility; Theodoret emphasizes living testimony. Grace makes the former
persecutor an apostle. The lesson ends within verse~10 at grace that has not
become void, concentrating the ear on divine victory as conversion and
fruitfulness.

\subsection*{Christ opens hearing into right confession}

Mark shows in the body what the Epistle shows in the Church. Jesus takes aside
a man with deafness and impaired speech, puts fingers in his ears, touches his
tongue with saliva, looks heavenward, groans, and commands \latin{Ephphetha}.
The ears open, the bond of the tongue is loosed, and speech becomes right. The
historical restoration manifests the incarnate Lord's care for the whole
person; because body and soul share one Creator and Redeemer, the act also bears
ecclesial, sacramental, and moral senses.

Gregory the Great's direct exposition is the Sunday's own Matins lesson under
the 1960 Office. Fingers are gifts of the Spirit; saliva is wisdom from divine
speech; the gaze reveals the heavenly source; the groan teaches prayer; right
speech is confirmed by prior action. Bede develops withdrawal from the crowd,
opened interior hearing, savor of wisdom, catechesis, and confession.
Theophylact praises the sanctifying efficacy of Christ's assumed flesh. Anthony
hears Old and New Testaments through the two ears and requires life to agree
with confession. Lawrence of Brindisi hears in \latin{Bene omnia fecit} the
Creator renewing his good work through redemption.

The Alleluia supplies the Church's response. Psalm~80 moves from instruments
and festival joy to covenant hearing and God's promise to fill an opened mouth.
Augustine and Cassiodorus read received preaching and embodied works of mercy
as instruments sounding together; Theodoret preserves the real festal music.
Christ's command of silence then disciplines proclamation. Augustine awakens
authorized preachers through the forbidden witnesses' zeal; Leo~XIV places the
command before Mark's completed paschal confession. Right speech is neither
wordlessness nor publicity: it is received truth, ripened through the Cross and
Resurrection, and made credible in deed.

\subsection*{The cry becomes healing, praise, and sacrifice}

The Gradual changes Psalm~27's order. It first sings the answer---the heart
trusted, received help, flesh flowered again, and praise became willing---then
returns to \latin{Ad te, Domine, clamavi}. Its centonized
\latin{ne discedas a me} asks that silence not become absence. Theodoret
preserves David's restored vigor after flight; Augustine, Cassiodorus, and
Aquinas unfold the flowering through Christ's Resurrection and the members
who will rise in him. Historical restoration grounds the image; Christ gives
it eschatological fullness.

The Gospel answers the feared silence through presence and efficacy: touch,
heavenward gaze, groan, and one opening word. The Offertory then reverses the
Gradual's phrase around the same cry: \latin{Domine, clamavi ad te, et sanasti
me}. Petition becomes thanksgiving while the gifts are prepared. Augustine
and Cassiodorus receive Psalm~29's dedicated house as Christ's body raised from
death; Theodoret sees human nature renewed in him; Aquinas sees Christ and the
Church, now built in conversion and awaiting final dedication. The healed
singer offers praise because the risen body is the form and pledge of every
Christian healing.

The Secret makes this return sacrificial. \latin{Servitus} is the Church's
worship, \latin{munus} the offered gift, and \latin{subsidium} the help that
returns to fragility. The two petitions \latin{sit} ask God to make the oblation
both acceptable and medicinal. Schuster hears the Church's priestly
\emph{leitourgia}. Grace has not made the worshipper self-sufficient; it has
made the weak capable of a real service that divine favor receives and returns
as help.

The order of these texts keeps bodily healing within the Paschal economy.
Theodoret's David and Hezekiah undergo historical deliverance; Mark's man
receives an irreducibly concrete restoration; Augustine and Cassiodorus hear
the same psalmic vocabulary reach its fullness in Christ's risen flesh. These
senses are complementary because the Incarnation and Resurrection establish
their order. Christ does not save humanity by leaving the body behind. His
touch restores a created good, his risen Body dedicates the true temple, and
his sacrifice opens present mercy toward the final resurrection. The Secret
therefore stands between cure and Communion as more than a practical prayer
over gifts. The healed creature enters Christ's offering: thanksgiving becomes
\latin{servitus}, material bread and wine become \latin{munus}, and confessed
fragility is entrusted to the sacrifice from which sacramental help returns.
The proper's movement is not from body to spirit as though the first were
discarded, but from embodied need through Christ's Body toward the glory of
the whole person.

\subsection*{Firstfruits mature into heavenly remedy}

At Communion, Proverbs orders substance and firstfruits toward the Lord. A
Greek catena fragment transmitted under Origen's name extends firstfruits to
virtue and prayer; the \emph{Apostolic Constitutions} directs righteous
earnings to food, clothing, captives, and the persecuted. Bede reads alms from
justly held goods, every good work returned to grace as source, and heavenly
barns filled with eternal satiety. Salvian stresses that possessions first
received from God return in devotion and justice; Aquinas requires lawfully
possessed gifts and sees firstfruits confessing that increase came from God.
The barns and presses therefore signify Wisdom's fruitfulness, not a bargain
for private wealth.

The Communion also completes the Introit's social claim. God first makes the
concordant dwell in one house; at the altar, those household members learn that
substance and increase are gifts capable of return. The
\emph{Apostolic Constitutions} makes the return concrete in food, clothing,
and ransom; Bede requires honest possession before almsgiving and refuses
applause as its measure; Salvian places ownership under the prior fact that
everything was received from God. Thus unanimity is neither sentiment nor
uniformity. It becomes an economy of worship in which goods are purified by
justice, offered in thanksgiving, and opened toward the neighbor. The lesson's
Paul embodies the same law personally: one who once scattered the Church is
received into its apostolic service, and grace bears fruit without ceasing to
be grace. Property, action, and vocation all pass from possession into
communion.

The proper's verbs keep gift and creaturely action in order. God
arises, makes the house, and gives strength; the heart trusts and receives
help; Paul receives the Gospel and hands it on; Christ takes the man aside,
touches him, and opens what was bound. The gathered answer: the flesh flowers,
the tongue speaks rightly, the singer cries
and gives thanks, the Church offers, and the communicant honors God from
substance and firstfruits. These acts are real, yet each follows divine
initiative, and the Secret still asks God to receive the
result and make it fruitful. The sequence therefore gives liturgical form to
Paul's confession that grace was not void: grace restores agency instead of
replacing it, while every good return depends upon the Giver.

The same order joins confession to justice. The opened ear receives a word
that the tongue must speak \latin{recte}; the possessions received under
Providence become firstfruits that must be offered from honest labor and opened
to the neighbor. Anthony and Gregory test speech by conduct, while Bede tests
almsgiving by lawful possession, God's honor, and freedom from applause. These
moral movements share one embodied worship. Voice, work, property, and vocation
are not detachable compartments: each becomes
truthful when what God has given is returned in praise and mercy. Thus the
Gospel's restored communication reaches beyond fluent utterance without
ceasing to honor it, and the Communion's abundance reaches beyond possession
without denying created goods. Both become communion in the house
God has made.

Read through the traditional spiritual senses, the whole sequence retains
this ordered richness. Literally, God protects and strengthens, David is
delivered, Israel praises with instruments, Christ heals a real man, and
firstfruits come from actual labor. Allegorically, the risen Christ constructs
the Church-house, reflowers human flesh, dedicates the temple of his Body, and
opens a people to apostolic testimony. Morally, concord becomes single-hearted
virtue, received teaching becomes deed, thanksgiving becomes acceptable
service, and wealth passes through justice and mercy. Anagogically, strength
becomes incorruption, dedication reaches the completed Church, barns become
heavenly satiety, and remedy reaches mind and body. The Fathers' several voices
do not weaken the proper's unity; they disclose the dimensions of the one
Christological movement enacted by the received Roman formulary.

The Postcommunion gathers the Mass into one anagogical petition. Through
reception of the sacrament the Church asks help for mind and body, salvation in
both, and the fullness of heavenly remedy. Irenaeus's Eucharistic realism and
Schuster's resurrection reading disclose its horizon: Christ nourishes mortal
flesh for incorruption. The Introit's strength, Paul's risen Lord, the
Gradual's flowering flesh, Mark's opened senses, the Offertory's healing, the
Secret's fragility, and the Communion's abundance reach one end. Grace received
becomes communion when it opens persons to true confession, accepted worship,
justice, mercy, and the resurrection of the whole person.
