\weektitle{Eighth Sunday After Pentecost}{Dominica Octava Post Pentecosten}{TLM propers: received mercy, Spirit-led heirs, accountable stewardship, and sacramental restoration}

\begin{properstable}
\properrow{Introit}{\latin{Suscepimus, Deus, misericordiam tuam in medio templi tui}; \latin{Magnus Dominus} (Ps. 47:10--11, 2)}{Mercy and justice received in God's city}{The assembly receives mercy in the Temple, confesses God's right hand full of justice, and joins his worldwide praise from the city and holy mountain.}
\properrow{Collect}{\latin{Largire nobis ... spiritum cogitandi quae recta sunt ... et agendi}}{Right thought, right action, dependent life}{The Church asks God for the constant spirit to think and do what is right because it cannot exist or live according to him without him.}
\properrow{Epistle}{Rom. 8:12--17}{Death or life, Spirit, adoption, and inheritance}{Paul's real death--life alternative leads through mortification and filial witness to coheirship; the printed lesson stops before verse 17's suffering-with-Christ clause.}
\properrow{Gradual}{\latin{Esto mihi in Deum protectorem}; \latin{Deus, in te speravi} (Ps. 30:3; 70:1)}{Refuge and unashamed hope}{The heirs remain petitioners who take God as protector and refuge and place their hope in him forever.}
\properrow{Alleluia}{\latin{Magnus Dominus ... in civitate Dei nostri} (Ps. 47:2)}{Return to the city and mountain}{The Introit's praise of God in his city and holy mountain returns immediately before the Gospel.}
\properrow{Gospel}{Luke 16:1--9}{Unjust prudence, children of light, mammon, and account}{Christ rebukes the children of light through an unjust steward's urgency and commands an eternal use of mammon without commending fraud or a rival master.}
\properrow{Offertory}{\latin{Populum humilem salvum facies}; \latin{quis Deus praeter te?} (Ps. 17:28, 32)}{God saves the humble and humbles proud eyes}{At the offering, God acts as savior and judge: he lowers pride, saves the humble, and alone is God.}

\properrow{Communion}{\latin{Gustate et videte ... suavis est Dominus} (Ps. 33:9)}{Taste, see, and hope}{Sacramental reception appoints the psalm's invitation to taste and see the Lord's sweetness and to hope in him.}
\properrow{Postcommunion}{\latin{reparatio mentis et corporis}; \latin{sentiamus effectum}}{Restoration and experienced effect}{The heavenly mystery is asked to restore mind and body so that enacted worship bears a perceived and lived effect.}
\end{properstable}
\begin{sensestable}
Literal & Mercy and justice are confessed in the sanctuary; Romans sets death against Spirit-given life; Luke's unjust steward acts decisively under an account and exposes the children of light; the orations ask for right action, sanctified conduct, and restoration.\\
Allegorical & Christ is the natural Son who makes adopted coheirs; Augustine reads the holy city and mountain as Christ and the Church, and he reads the Communion psalm of Christ's Flesh and Blood.\\
Moral & Mercy does not suspend justice: think and do what is right, mortify disordered deeds, distinguish prudence from fraud, refuse mammon as master, make restitution, give mercy, and reject proud self-security.\\
Anagogical & Death or life, coheirship through conformity to Christ, the final account, eternal dwellings, everlasting joy, and bodily restoration make present stewardship eschatologically serious.\\
\end{sensestable}

\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}

\begin{massbox}{Governing thesis: mercy and justice form Spirit-led stewardship}
The Mass receives mercy without fleeing God's justice. Grace must form right thought and action in adopted heirs, expose prudence severed from fidelity, and convert received goods into humble offering and embodied life ordered to glory.
\end{massbox}

\noindent\emph{Reading order.} The four stages follow the appointed liturgical sequence. Their architecture is this guide's source-grounded synthesis; no cited Father is presented as the designer of the complete Roman formulary.

\subsection*{1. Receive and ask: mercy stands with justice\enspace\properrefs{Int., Coll.}}

The entrance says \latin{Suscepimus}: mercy has been received before any human account is rendered. Yet the same antiphon says that God's right hand is full of justice. The Collect answers both truths by asking God to \latin{largire} a constant spirit of thinking and doing what is \latin{recta}; its goal is dependent life \latin{secundum te}, not activity detached from the giver's righteousness.

\begin{studybox}{Decisive witness and limit}
Augustine reads Psalm 47's city and mountain ecclesially and insists that received mercy issue in worthy life; Thomas distinguishes ordinary divine motion from healing and elevating grace (\emph{Psalm 47}, 2, 8, 10; \emph{ST} I--II, q.~109, aa.~1--2). Augustine reads \emph{people} at verse 10, not the missal's \emph{Temple}, so he does not supply a Temple allegory. Mercy does not license injustice, and dependence does not erase created agency.
\end{studybox}

\sectionguard
\subsection*{2. Be led and hope: heirs face death, fear, and suffering\enspace\properrefs{Ep., Grad., All., Comm.}}

Romans states an unsparing alternative: life according to the flesh ends in death; mortifying its deeds through the Spirit leads to life. It then moves through adopted children and witness to coheirship. The Gradual keeps heirs dependent upon refuge, the Alleluia returns them to God's city, and the Communion psalm's wider context distinguishes reverent fear from the enslaving fear Paul rejects.

\begin{threestable}{Relation}{Decisive witness}{Essential limit}
\textbf{Death and life} & Chrysostom treats \latin{moriemini} as a real present and final warning, while mortification concerns evil deeds, not bodily substance. & The body remains God's good work and is ordered to restoration.\\
\textbf{Adoption and fear} & Augustine and Thomas distinguish servile terror from filial or chaste fear under charity. & Adoption does not cancel reverence, judgment, or Israel's adoption in Romans 9:4.\\
\textbf{Inheritance and suffering} & The missal stops at \latin{coheredes autem Christi}; biblical verse 17 continues with suffering with Christ unto shared glory. & Suffering neither earns adoption nor becomes good in itself; conformity to Christ is cruciform.\\
\end{threestable}

\newpage
\subsection*{3. Account and offer: the children of light are rebuked\enspace\properrefs{Gosp., Off.}}

The Gospel names the agent a \latin{villicus iniquitatis} and praises that he acted \latin{prudenter}. Christ then says the children of this age are more prudent than the children of light: disciples are made uncomfortable by worldly resolve for temporary security. The Offertory answers with God's action---he saves the humble, humbles proud eyes, and alone is God.

\begin{threestable}{Gospel question}{Decisive control}{Liturgical answer}
\textbf{What is praised?} & Jerome separates prudent provision from fraud; Cyril emphasizes accountable use of entrusted goods for mercy. & Adopted heirs need the steward's urgency without his injustice.\\
\textbf{What is forbidden?} & Augustine rejects theft followed by almsgiving and requires amendment and restitution. & An offering cannot bribe the Judge, purchase heaven, or excuse continuing injustice.\\
\textbf{What controls it?} & Luke 16:10--13 demands fidelity and personifies mammon as a rival master. & Eternal urgency governs administration; justice governs its means; hope remains in God.\\
\end{threestable}

\sectionguard
\subsection*{4. Return and receive: grace reaches conduct and body\enspace\properrefs{Sec., Comm., Postcomm.}}

The Secret's \latin{Suscipe} answers the Introit's \latin{Suscepimus}: those who received mercy ask God to receive gifts that remain his by origin. Operative grace must sanctify conduct and lead to everlasting joy. Communion restores the worshipper to receptive hope; the Postcommunion seeks restoration of mind and body and an effect deeper than passing sensation.

\begin{threestable}{Proper}{Decisive witness}{Guardrail}
\textbf{Secret --- gift returned} & Irenaeus says the Church offers what is already God's gift and that outward sacrifice cannot conceal injustice. & Stewardship neither originates the gift nor permits worship to launder sin.\\
\textbf{Communion --- taste and hope} & Augustine reads Psalm 33:9 directly of Christ's Flesh and Blood and hope in the Lord. & Sensory language does not reduce faith to bodily taste or private feeling.\\
\textbf{Postcommunion --- restoration} & Thomas joins Eucharistic nourishment and charity to glory; his partial-obstacle distinction concerns actual venial sin. & It does not excuse unworthy reception or make emotion the measure of grace.\\
\end{threestable}

\begin{studybox}{Limits governing the complete movement}
\textbf{Moral:} prudence serves justice; almsgiving never licenses fraud. \textbf{Anthropological:} mortification opposes disordered deeds, not the body. \textbf{Sacramental:} grace acts without making reception mechanical. \textbf{Historical:} the four stages analyze the appointed order; they do not recover its composition.
\end{studybox}

\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

\subsection*{Mercy and justice: what makes action right?\enspace\properrefs{Int., Coll., Ep., Gosp., Sec., Postcomm.}}

The Introit does not oppose mercy to judgment. The assembly has received mercy in God's Temple, yet the same antiphon declares his right hand full of justice. The Collect therefore asks not merely for activity but for a constant spirit of thinking and doing what is right. Romans places responsible action under the leading of God's Spirit; the Gospel then exhibits vigorous deliberation and decisive action that remain unjust. The Secret and Postcommunion carry the question into worship: grace must sanctify conduct, and enacted worship must bear a real effect.

\begin{fourstable}{Movement}{Appointed wording}{Theological work}{Guardrail}
\textbf{Mercy and justice} & \latin{Suscepimus ... misericordiam}; \latin{iustitia plena est dextera tua} & Mercy received places life under God's justice rather than outside it. & Justice is not reduced to punishment, nor mercy to indulgence.\\
\textbf{Thought and deed} & \latin{cogitandi quae recta sunt ... et agendi} & The Collect asks grace to govern both deliberation and execution. & Good intention does not make an unjust means good.\\
\textbf{Led yet acting} & \latin{mortificaveritis}; \latin{spiritu Dei aguntur} & Divine leading enables responsible human action. & Grace is neither coercion nor permission for passivity.\\
\textbf{Prudent yet unjust} & \latin{Quid faciam?}; \latin{prudenter}; \latin{villicum iniquitatis} & The steward shows that foresight and effectiveness can be severed from justice. & Christ commends urgency, not fraud.\\
\textbf{Conduct and effect} & \latin{operante virtute}; \latin{conversatio}; \latin{exsequimur ... sentiamus effectum} & Sacramental grace is asked to reach conduct and lived fruit. & External performance or felt consolation alone does not prove fidelity.\\
\end{fourstable}

Augustine's \emph{Exposition on Psalm 47} directly joins mercy received to worthy life and worldwide praise. The appointed antiphon adds its own judicial pressure: mercy is received from the God whose right hand is full of justice. Thus the Collect's \latin{recta}, the unjust steward, and the Secret's sanctified \latin{conversatio} do not introduce a moral theme foreign to the entrance; they unfold the relation already sung there.

\begin{sourcecard}{Textual control --- \latin{populi tui} and \latin{templi tui}}
At Psalm 47:10 Augustine reads \emph{in the midst of your people}, while the missal follows the Vulgate \emph{in the midst of your Temple}. His moral treatment of received mercy is direct reception of the verse, but he does not supply an exegesis of the Temple noun. The appointed Temple, city, mountain, mercy, justice, and worldwide praise remain first the psalm's own vocabulary.
\end{sourcecard}

\sectionguard
\subsection*{Sons, heirs, and stewards: why do children of light keep accounts?\enspace\properrefs{Ep., Grad., Gosp., Comm.}}

The verbal bridge is exact. Romans names those led by the Spirit \latin{filii Dei}; the Gospel contrasts \latin{filii huius saeculi} with \latin{filiis lucis}. The first passage grants adopted identity and inheritance, while the second rebukes the children of light for lacking purposeful foresight. Their juxtaposition does not make adoption a financial contract. It asks whether those who know the final good as heirs will administer present life with an urgency purified of the steward's injustice.

\begin{threestable}{Pauline movement}{Direct reception}{Necessary distinction}
\textbf{Death or life} & Chrysostom treats \latin{moriemini} as a real present and eschatological warning; the Spirit mortifies evil deeds unto life. & Paul condemns life according to the flesh, not the substance or ordinary care of the body.\\
\textbf{Led and acting} & Augustine says the Spirit acts in the person who also truly acts; grace is necessary for even the struggle, not merely easier victory. & Divine motion does not abolish the will or excuse inertia.\\
\textbf{Adoption and fear} & Augustine lets punitive fear serve until charity matures; Thomas distinguishes worldly, servile, initial, and filial or chaste fear. & Paul rejects servile domination, not judgment, repentance, awe, or the holy fear of separation from God.\\
\textbf{Children and heirs} & The Spirit gives filial prayer and witness; Thomas identifies God himself as the inheritance. & Christ is Son by nature; Christians are sons by adoption, which does not erase Israel's adoption in Romans 9:4.\\
\end{threestable}

The Gradual gives adopted freedom the posture of dependence: the heirs still seek refuge and hope in God. Augustine reads Psalm 70 as abandoning self-confidence for Christ and Psalm 30's refuge as divine condescension toward the threatened. The wider context of the Communion psalm commands fear of the Lord together with truthful speech, departure from evil, good action, and peace. That fear is compatible with filial hope; it is not the enslaving terror Paul rejects.

\begin{sourcecard}{A hard liturgical boundary --- Romans 8:17a and 17b}
The missal cites Romans 8:12--17 but stops after \latin{coheredes autem Christi}. The biblical verse continues \latin{si tamen compatimur, ut et conglorificemur}; verse 18 begins Paul's comparison of present sufferings with future glory. Augustine's \emph{Sermon 156} stops with the appointed text. Thomas, \emph{Commentary on Romans} VIII, lect.~3, nos.~646--649, treats that coheirship; nos.~650--651 treat the unappointed continuation. Coheirship is therefore not detached from cruciform conformity, but suffering neither earns adoption nor becomes a good sought for itself.
\end{sourcecard}

\subsection*{Mammon, the poor, and the unbribable Judge\enspace\properrefs{Gosp., Off., Sec.}}

The parable's discomfort cannot be solved by making the steward innocent or wealth neutral. Luke says that goods were squandered, an account is required, documents are reduced for the steward's future shelter, and the agent remains \latin{iniquitatis} even when his foresight is praised. Christ's comparison then turns on the children of light: worldly agents often pursue temporary security more purposefully than disciples pursue the kingdom.

\begin{threestable}{Narrative pressure}{What the text establishes}{What it does not establish}
\textbf{Charge and account} & The steward is accused of squandering and loses administration. & The accusation need not be reconstructed as a misunderstanding.\\
\textbf{Reductions and praise} & He changes debt records; the master praises prudence while still naming him unjust. & Praise of foresight is not approval of fraud. A surrendered-commission theory is possible, not certain.\\
\textbf{Children and mammon} & Christ rebukes the children of light and commands an eternal use of \latin{mammona iniquitatis}. & Mammon is not merely harmless matter, and the steward's injustice is not transferred into the life of light.\\
\end{threestable}

Cyril says the children of light are illumined by Christ for upright works, generosity, mercy, and charity; their rebuke concerns deficient purpose, not permission to imitate injustice. He treats possessions as goods entrusted for the poor and death as the approaching end of administration. Sermon 109 on Luke 16:10--13 supplies the controlling test: fidelity in what belongs to another precedes true riches, and mammon cannot share God's mastery.

The phrase \latin{mammona iniquitatis} presses beyond ``transient wealth.'' The biblical note explains mammon as that in which one trusts, wealth's tendency toward dishonesty, and its personification as a rival god in verse 13. Luke then names the Pharisees lovers of money and ends the chapter with the rich man and Lazarus: neglected poverty at the gate is followed by irreversible judgment. Those verses are not appointed, but the chapter's canonical continuation refuses to let eternal dwellings become an abstraction from the poor.

Augustine preserves two readings. Wealth actually gained by wrong requires that the wrong cease and that restitution precede beneficence, as with Zacchaeus. More broadly, even honestly acquired wealth can be called mammon of iniquity when a disordered world mistakes it for true riches. Under neither reading may one plunder another, distribute part as alms, and imagine Christ the Judge bribed. Jerome likewise isolates the commendable foresight from the steward's fraud.

The Offertory is more than a subjective posture: God \emph{saves} the humble people and \emph{humbles} proud eyes. Augustine reads the humble as those who confess dependence rather than establish their own righteousness, and the sole God as the sons' inheritance. Irenaeus adds a sacrificial control: correct outward offering cannot deceive God while injustice remains within. The Secret's gifts from divine bounty cannot launder theft, substitute for restitution, or purchase the eternal home.

\begin{studybox}{Limits that must remain uncomfortable}
Urgency is commanded; fraud, usury, squandering, proud control, and service to mammon are not. The poor are not autonomous gatekeepers who sell heaven, yet mercy toward Christ in the needy belongs to conversion under judgment. The Offertory does not promise that a ritual gift cancels an unjust account; it places offerer and goods under the God who saves the lowly and actively brings pride down.
\end{studybox}

\subsection*{Receive, render, and return: whose gifts and whose account?\enspace\properrefs{Int., Ep., Gosp., Sec., Comm., Postcomm.}}

A family of receiving verbs changes subjects and objects across the Mass. The Introit's \latin{Suscepimus} and the Secret's \latin{Suscipe} are forms of the same verb. Romans' \latin{accepistis}, the Gospel's \latin{Accipe}, and its \latin{recipiant} are distinct prefixed compounds within the \latin{capio} family, as the Lewis--Short entries confirm. The relation is lexical, not one undifferentiated transaction: mercy, adoption, altered documents, shelter, offered gifts, sacramental reception, and eternal welcome retain different agents and moral qualities.

\begin{fourstable}{Form or act}{Receiver}{Object}{Control}
\latin{Suscepimus} & The assembled Church & God's mercy & Gift precedes account and offering.\\
\latin{accepistis} & Adopted children & The Spirit of adoption & Sonship is gift, not wages or acquired possession.\\
\latin{Accipe} / \latin{recipiant} & Debtors / the steward & Altered records / temporary shelter & A compromised exchange is narrated, not blessed.\\
\latin{Suscipe} & God & Gifts brought from his bounty & Offering is thanksgiving, not payment of divine need.\\
\latin{Gustate} / \latin{sentiamus} & Communicants & The Lord's gift / its effect & These complete the ritual movement but are not further forms of \latin{capio}.\\
\end{fourstable}

Another verb sharpens the contrast. The Spirit \latin{testimonium reddit}---renders witness---to true filial identity; the master commands the steward \latin{redde rationem}---render an account---of administration. The objects and speakers differ, and no spiritual ledger is implied. Still, the repeated \latin{reddere} makes truth and accountability audible together: sonship is witnessed by the Spirit, while stewardship is exposed to judgment. Altering papers cannot alter either truth.

The Secret's grammar turns reception into truthful return without barter:

\begin{fourstable}{Clause}{Grammatical force}{Doctrinal illumination}{Boundary}
\latin{munera ... deferimus} & Worshippers truly bring gifts. & Human agency and material creation enter thanksgiving. & Offerers are not first owners or sources.\\
\latin{de tua largitate} & The same gifts originate in God's bounty. & Irenaeus says the Church offers God what is already his. & God receives no remedy for need.\\
\latin{gratiae tuae operante virtute} & Grace's power is the requested operative cause. & The Collect's dependence and Romans' leading reach sacramental expression. & Grace does not support passive reception without conversion.\\
\latin{praesentis vitae ... gaudia sempiterna} & Present conduct is sanctified and led to everlasting joy. & Stewardship and sacrament share an eschatological horizon. & Eternity gives earthly conduct weight rather than bypassing it.\\
\end{fourstable}

Irenaeus is doctrinal illumination, not direct comment on the Roman prayer. His argument nevertheless joins its terms: the Church offers created things that came from God, gives thanks rather than supplying a divine lack, and must offer with truthful conscience and neighbor-love. Communion then restores the worshipper to the receptive place, so the movement ends neither in ownership nor in a balanced account but in gift received and life changed.

\subsection*{Created goods and embodied life: what dies, and what is restored?\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Ep., Gosp., Sec., Comm., Postcomm.}}

Romans does not identify bodily substance with the flesh's evil deeds. Chrysostom and Augustine insist that the body is God's work, while life governed by disordered desire must be mortified. Luke makes a parallel moral distinction about possessions: oil, grain, documents, and wealth belong to created and social life; squandering, fraud, extortion, hoarding, and service to mammon disorder their use. Thus created gifts can enter the Secret, bodily tasting can accompany Communion, and the Postcommunion can ask restoration of mind and body without contradiction.

\begin{threestable}{Created reality}{Disorder rejected}{Graced order}
\textbf{Body} & Life according to the flesh and its evil deeds lead to death. & The Spirit leads embodied persons, and the mystery orders the body to resurrection.\\
\textbf{Temporal goods} & Squandering, dishonest accounts, proud ownership, and mammon as master. & Entrusted goods serve mercy and become thankful offering from God's bounty.\\
\textbf{Human agency} & Prudence detached from justice and worship detached from conversion. & Grace enables action, sanctifies conduct, and strengthens charity.\\
\textbf{Sensation} & Physical taste or felt consolation treated as the measure of grace. & Sacramental reception is real, while its fruit concerns disposition and faithful life.\\
\end{threestable}

The death language must remain literal in its moral and eschatological force. The Collect asks to \latin{vivere} according to God; Paul says \latin{moriemini} and \latin{vivetis}; the Gospel's stewardship ends and its application looks to the moment \latin{cum defeceritis}; the Secret joins present conduct to everlasting joy. Finally, the Postcommunion asks restoration of the body whose evil deeds must be mortified. The Mass does not despise mortal life: it makes the use of body and goods answerable to resurrection and judgment.

Augustine reads \latin{Gustate et videte} Eucharistically: Christ bears himself in his hands, and blessedness rests in hope in the Lord. Irenaeus says Eucharistic bread and cup nourish the flesh and argues that bodies so nourished, though buried and dissolved, are raised by the Word to immortality and incorruption (\emph{Against Heresies} V.2.2--3). This is a resurrection horizon for \latin{reparatio corporis}, not a promise of immediate physical cure.

\begin{fourstable}{Question}{Doctrinal distinction}{What follows}{What does not follow}
\textbf{Does the sacrament act?} & Christ acts efficaciously; fruitful reception also concerns the recipient's disposition (CCC 1127--1129). & \latin{effectum} is objective before it is felt. & Reception is not mechanical or independent of conversion.\\
\textbf{What of unworthy reception?} & Thomas treats mortal sin and sacrilegious reception as a distinct obstacle (\emph{ST} III, q.~79, a.~3; cf. 1~Cor. 11:27--29). & The Eucharist is not a license to approach while refusing grave repentance. & Article 8's habitual-fruit distinction is no assurance for every bad disposition.\\
\textbf{What of venial sin?} & Actual venial sin that distracts the mind can hinder actual refreshment without removing increase of habitual grace or charity (a.~8). & Felt sweetness is not the only fruit. & Ordinary involuntary distraction is not what Thomas specifies.\\
\textbf{Mind and body?} & Present grace orders embodied action; future incorruption is its bodily consummation. & Restoration concerns the whole person and final glory. & The prayer does not promise instant relief from every illness or burden.\\
\end{fourstable}

Thomas also links Eucharistic passage to glory with Romans 8:17: the sacrament gives the power of coming to glory, not glory without the path of suffering with Christ (q.~79, a.~2). The unappointed half-verse therefore prevents sacramental restoration from becoming triumphalism, while the Postcommunion prevents mortification from becoming contempt for the body.

\sectionguard
\subsection*{The chants as one ecclesial response: where does the assembly stand?\enspace\properrefs{Int., Grad., All., Off., Comm.}}

The chants do more than separate readings and prayers. They give the assembly a continuous response: mercy and justice within God's city; refuge and hope after the promise of inheritance; praise on the holy mountain before the steward's crisis; humility under God's saving judgment at the offering; and Eucharistic tasting joined again to hope. This is a liturgical reading of the appointed order, not a claim about one human compositional plan.

\begin{threestable}{Chant movement}{Augustinian witness}{Function here}
\textbf{Temple, city, mountain} & Psalm 47 joins Christ and the Church, mercy received unto worthy life, and praise reaching earth's ends. & Private stewardship is heard inside the worship and justice of God's people.\\
\textbf{Refuge and hope} & Psalms 30 and 70 move confidence away from self toward God as refuge and healer. & Adopted heirs remain petitioners, not autonomous possessors.\\
\textbf{Humility and sole God} & Psalm 17 opposes confessed dependence to proud self-righteousness and names God as inheritance. & God, not the steward's control or mammon, saves and judges.\\
\textbf{Taste, fear, and trust} & Psalm 33 receives Augustine's Eucharistic reading; its context joins fear, truthful speech, goodness, and peace. & Communion makes dependent, truthful life sacramental and embodied.\\
\end{threestable}

Two textual limits remain. Augustine reads ``people'' where the Introit says ``Temple,'' and ``house of refuge'' where the Gradual says ``place of refuge.'' His direct moral and christological exegesis remains useful without erasing those differences. The concordance's force lies not in pretending identical textual forms, but in letting the appointed psalms answer the difficult readings with justice, refuge, humility, truthful conduct, hope, and sacramental trust.

\section*{The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities}

\noindent\emph{These exploratory AI-generated syntheses are attributed to none of the cited authorities and claim no historical compositional intent; each exposes its principal textual anchors and limit.}

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{From Temple and city to houses, altar, and tabernacles.\enspace\properrefs{Int., All., Gosp., Sec., Comm.}}
The entrance and Alleluia locate praise in Temple, city, and holy mountain. The steward seeks temporary reception in human houses, while Christ directs attention to eternal tabernacles. At the altar those who received mercy ask God to receive gifts from his bounty, and Communion makes them recipients again. This architecture may ask which dwelling governs present administration: shelter secured by manipulation, or lasting communion approached through mercy and truthful offering. Yet these are distinct images, and no cited source is claimed to have designed one architectural allegory of the formulary.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{From altered letters to an embodied effect.\enspace\properrefs{Gosp., Sec., Comm., Postcomm.}}
Luke's steward changes written debt records under the pressure of an account. The Spirit instead renders witness to filial truth; the Secret asks grace to sanctify the \latin{conversatio} of present life; Communion commands tasting and seeing; and the Postcommunion seeks restoration of mind and body with a perceived effect. The sequence may contrast a manipulated account with truth embodied in conduct: no revised ledger can replace conversion. This remains an original meditation; the Spirit's witness is not an accounting document, the orations do not mention ledgers, and sacramental fruit is not measurable by sensation alone.
