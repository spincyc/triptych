\section{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}
\subsection{Introit: the victory retains the wound}
The opening antiphon draws together Apocalypse 5:12 and 1:6. In the fifth chapter, John sees the Lamb standing as slain within the heavenly court. The slaughter is not an unfortunate prelude now forgotten by victorious heaven: it identifies the one who receives worship. The chapter's movement from the closed book to the Lamb's ability to open it locates royal worthiness in redemptive victory. Apocalypse 1 places kingdom and priesthood within an account of Christ's faithful witness and saving blood. The composite chant brings those claims into one act of praise.

The received commentary of Victorinus on Apocalypse 5 connects the opening of the testament with the Lamb's overcoming death and bringing humanity into life. It illuminates the chapter's redemptive movement rather than explaining every term of the chant's selected verse. The ANF delivery carries attribution and interpolation cautions; its contextual argument is useful without making that received text the sole authority for the interpretation.\footnote{Received Victorinus, \emph{Commentary on the Apocalypse}, ch. 5, in \emph{Ante-Nicene Fathers} VII, with its editorial elucidations; see also ch. 1 on vv. 4--7.}

The term \emph{divinitatem} in the Latin acclamation must be heard with the Son's identity in Colossians, not as an acquisition of divine nature after death. The acclamation celebrates the Lamb; the Epistle identifies him as the one through whom all creatures exist. The psalm verse then asks for judgment and justice. It makes right rule, rather than power considered alone, part of the assembly's first approach to the King.

\subsection{Collect: the wound beneath division}
The Collect diagnoses the scattering of peoples through sin. The movement from a universal horizon to the concrete wound of human division gives restoration a personal and social content. People cannot be healed simply by enlarging the territory under one administration. The prayer asks that their relation to Christ change, and describes his dominion by its gentleness. Its royal vocabulary therefore concerns the manner of rule as well as its extent.

The contrast becomes more exact in the Gospel: a king whose servants do not fight to prevent his delivery is heard, believed and obeyed. The Collect's request prepares for that account of allegiance. Augustine's explanation of regeneration and Chrysostom's distinction between rescue and transfer show why the prayer needs a divine gift. A new allegiance is not produced merely by a better label for an unchanged captivity.\footnote{Augustine, \emph{Tractates on John} 115.2,4; Chrysostom, \emph{Homilies on Colossians} II, on Col 1:12--15. These witnesses explain the biblical passages, not the composition of the Collect.}

\subsection{Epistle: rescue, creation and reconciliation}
The thanksgiving of Colossians 1:12--20 begins with the recipients. They have been made fit for an inheritance and transferred from darkness into the beloved Son's kingdom. It then turns toward the identity of that Son. The sequence matters: the person who saves them is not one more member of the invisible world through whom they must climb to God. Visible and invisible creatures alike depend on him. The inventory of heavenly powers removes the possibility of reserving an exempt spiritual territory beyond his lordship.

The passage's two uses of \emph{firstborn} organize its account of primacy: the Son's relation to creation and his relation to the dead. Creation, the Church and resurrection converge upon him. The latter part then names the Cross as the means of peace. The God who sustains beings does not reconcile them by withdrawing from their condition. Chrysostom emphasizes both the Creator's continuing preservation of creatures and the extraordinary condescension of his joining himself to the Church as its head.\footnote{Chrysostom, \emph{Homilies on Colossians} III, on Col 1:15--20, NPNF I.13.}

The next verses, Colossians 1:21--23, identify the recipients as former enemies and call them to perseverance. They prevent a careless inference from cosmic language to automatic salvation irrespective of response. The passage reaches all creation in its account of the Son's authority; personal reconciliation remains a gift to be received and lived. The second interpretation develops Chrysostom's answer to the most demanding phrase: what can reconciliation of things in heaven mean if holy angels have not sinned?

\subsection{Gradual: the reach of justice}
Psalm 71:8 and 11 are two selected verses, not a continuous reading of verses 8--11. They extend dominion across seas and toward every nation's rulers. The intervening and surrounding psalm describes homage and deliverance; verses 12--14 especially give the royal work its character in the rescue of the needy. Worldwide reach and care for the helpless belong to the same portrait.

Augustine begins his exposition with Christ as the true Solomon, the peacemaker whose kingdom exceeds that of the historical king. Bellarmine permits an immediate petition by David for Solomon's just government before unfolding its fulfillment in Christ. The difference affects how the opening petition is heard: one begins directly with the fulfillment, the other gives the historical royal prayer its own place within it. Both understand the reach and endurance of the psalm to surpass Solomon.\footnote{Augustine, \emph{Expositions of the Psalms}, Ps 71.1--2,13--16 (NPNF heading: Psalm 72); Bellarmine, \emph{Commentary on the Book of Psalms}, Ps 71, opening and comments 10--13 in the O'Sullivan delivery. Comment 10 treats biblical v. 11.}

\subsection{Alleluia: a kingdom that does not pass away}
Daniel 7 first displays beastly kingdoms, then the heavenly court and the human figure who receives dominion. The selected concluding clauses of verse 14 celebrate that dominion's endurance. The longer verse and the chapter explain whose dominion it is and how the saints belong to its future. The Mass does not sing the entire vision, but its unending kingdom has its meaning within that contrast between what rises from the earth and what God gives.

Jerome takes the figure's humanity seriously. Received power does not make the eternal Son a lesser divinity; it is spoken of him according to the human nature he assumed and the humiliation described in Philippians 2. The same commentary denies that the saints' inheritance is exhausted by a temporary earthly victory. Its endurance gives the Postcommunion's hope of shared reign a horizon larger than this life's political fortunes.\footnote{Jerome, \emph{Commentary on Daniel}, VII, on 7:13--14 and 17--18; PL 25, cols. 670--671.}

\subsection{Gospel: the prisoner questions the judge}
The appointed scene is inside the governor's hall. Pilate asks whether Jesus is King of the Jews; Jesus asks where that description comes from. The exchange discloses the accusation and exposes the questioner's assumptions. Pilate is investigating a threat to authority. Jesus answers by identifying the source of his kingdom and the purpose of his coming into the world.

John 18 has already included Peter's sword and Jesus' refusal of it. In the trial, the absence of a fight to prevent his delivery becomes evidence for the kingdom's different origin. Jesus then speaks of testimony and hearing. The appointment ends there, at verse 37. Pilate's subsequent question about truth and his departure belong to the surrounding narrative, not to the appointed Gospel's final sentence. The assembly is left with a call to hear. The narrative identifies particular speakers and authorities; its conflicts do not assign guilt to Jewish people across generations.\footnote{John 18:10--11,28,33--40; 19:1--22; Augustine, \emph{Tractates on John} 115.1--5.}

\subsection{Offertory: intercession for the nations}
Psalm 2's promise of the nations follows rebellion against the Lord and his anointed. At verse 8 the Son asks and the Father grants. Augustine relates this asking to the Incarnation, sacrifice and intercession. The Son's eternal dignity and his temporal work for humanity belong to one person. At the offering, the nations are consequently the horizon of priestly petition, not a possession the congregation wins for itself.\footnote{Augustine, \emph{Expositions of the Psalms}, Ps 2.7; Bellarmine, \emph{Commentary on Psalms}, Ps 2, comments on vv. 7--9.}

Verse 9's rod belongs to the psalm's context, not the Offertory's sung words. Bellarmine's account includes correction of sinful habits and final judgment. The passage demands accountability before the Son; it supplies no commission for worshippers to impose conversion by violence.

\subsection{Secret: the sacrifice gives what division lacks}
The Secret names human reconciliation and asks the Christ offered in sacrifice for unity and peace among the nations. Its logic joins the altar to the need named in the Collect. A divided human family cannot make its unity merely by declaring itself united. The petition places its hope in the personal agency of the Son.

The relation to the Epistle is especially close: peace through the blood of the Cross becomes a sacrificial petition. Chrysostom's insistence on the manner of reconciliation makes that connection intelligible. The giver does not remain outside the cost of the gift. The community asks for a peace purchased through his self-giving, which places its own enmities under judgment.

\subsection{Preface: the royal priest}
The Preface names the eternal Priest and universal King together and places his self-offering on the Cross at the center of their unity. Its account of the kingdom proceeds through truth and life, holiness and grace, justice, love and peace. These qualities identify the life of the kingdom, rather than a second kingdom added to a purely political one. The royal offering reaches the Father and draws the assembly into thanksgiving with the heavenly host.

The sequence deepens the Epistle's movement from cosmic authority to the Church and reconciliation. The Son who sustains creation is the priest who offers himself. His subjection of creatures and the kingdom's presentation to the Father belong within this priestly work. Pius XI's feast teaching likewise joins kingship to redemption and priesthood, while extending its moral claims to public life.\footnote{Pius XI, \emph{Quas primas} (11 December 1925), nos. 12--20.}

\subsection{Communion and Postcommunion: peace that perseveres}
The Communion takes selected clauses from Psalm 28:10--11. The psalm's storm, powerful divine voice and disrupted landscape precede its conclusion in royal permanence and peace. Augustine reads the Church as exposed to the world's flood while receiving peace in Christ. The antiphon therefore promises something stronger than an agreeable interval between troubles: a blessing grounded in the King's enduring rule. Bellarmine preserves both natural and ecclesial readings of the psalm and relates present strength to final blessing.\footnote{Augustine, \emph{Expositions of the Psalms}, Ps 28.10--11 (NPNF heading: Psalm 29); Bellarmine, \emph{Commentary on Psalms}, Ps 28, conclusion.}

The Postcommunion brings sacramental nourishment, active service and heavenly reign together. Its military image is governed by the King's conduct in the Gospel and by the peace requested at the altar. Fidelity includes struggle, but the battle that would prevent his self-offering has already been refused. The final petition looks toward lasting fellowship with him. Receiving the sacrament opens into persevering service; service reaches its end in the gift of shared reign.
