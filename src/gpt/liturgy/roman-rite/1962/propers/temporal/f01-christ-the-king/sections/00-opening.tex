\section{A King Who Gives His Life}
Christ stands before a governor who can order his death, and the Mass calls him King of the universe. That encounter is the feast's sharpest question. How can the prisoner who declines armed rescue be the Lord to whom every ruler owes allegiance? The answer unfolds through a sacrifice that makes a people, a truth that gives them ears to hear, and a peace that reaches beyond the boundaries they have made for themselves. The King's apparent helplessness in the Gospel belongs beside the Epistle's account of his creative power and its proclamation of reconciliation through the Cross.

The Introit has already joined royal acclaim to the Lamb's death. The assembly therefore approaches the trial with a different understanding of victory from Pilate's. It does not have to choose between a successful king and a suffering victim. Colossians identifies the same Son as the origin and sustainer of creation, the head of the Church, and the mediator of reconciliation. The proper Preface gathers priesthood and kingship around his self-offering. The Mass's prayers ask that this victory become effective in divided human families and in the fidelity of those receiving the sacrament.

Two readings develop that movement. In the first, the King makes a people capable of hearing him. Augustine explains the kingdom's different origin through regeneration and identifies hearing with obedient belief; Jerome explains the Son of Man's received dominion through the Incarnation and the saints' enduring inheritance. In the second, the Creator restores communion by giving himself. Chrysostom follows the Epistle from the Son's creative primacy to reconciliation through his blood; Bellarmine explains the Psalter's universal royal claims through justice, conversion, and final judgment. Each reading accounts for the whole formulary, but their questions differ: how does a person become a willing subject of this King, and what does his universal rule accomplish among the creatures he reconciles?

The feast celebrated here is \emph{D. N. Iesu Christi Regis}, I class, in the Sanctorale of the 1962 \emph{Missale Romanum}. Its appointed place is the last Sunday of October, which falls on 25 October in 2026. The Gloria, Creed and proper Preface belong to this occurrence. The otherwise occurring Sunday supplies no additional prayers. The eleven active elements appear below in their liturgical order. Psalm numbers follow the Missal's Vulgate system; the usual Hebrew number appears in parentheses when different.\footnote{\emph{Missale Romanum}, Vatican typical edition, 1962, pp. 697--698, nos. 3938--3949; p. 291, no. 1080; \emph{Rubricae generales}, 17(d), 111(a), 115(c). Communion & Ps 28:10--11 (29), selected clauses & The eternal King blesses his people with peace.\\
