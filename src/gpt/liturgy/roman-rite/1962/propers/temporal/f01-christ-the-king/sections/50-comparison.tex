\section{One Kingship, Two Questions}
The first interpretation distinguishes the grace by which a person enters the kingdom from the inheritance toward which that person perseveres. The second distinguishes the Son's authority over all creatures from their willing obedience and their reconciliation with God and one another. The first explains why belief and obedience begin with a gift; the second explains why the recipient of peace must seek reconciliation with others. Gratitude for grace and the refusal of a feud are different consequences of belonging to the same King.

Their different starting points also explain how they approach the kingdom's universality. The account of regenerated hearing concerns the new life of people who remain in the world and can be gathered from every nation. The account of creative lordship concerns Christ's authority even over those who resist him. Augustine's distinction between origin and presence leaves room for the public responsibilities Bellarmine describes; Bellarmine's distinction between authority and conversion leaves the need for grace intact. Both anticipate a fulfillment beyond present political fortunes, but one emphasizes the saints' inheritance and the other the complete acknowledgment of Christ at judgment.

Their agreement includes a narrower exegetical difference. Augustine introduces Christ as the true Solomon in Psalm 71; Bellarmine allows the opening prayer its immediate Davidic petition for Solomon's just rule before tracing its fulfillment beyond him. Augustine begins with the promised peacemaker, while Bellarmine explains how a prayer within Israel's royal life exceeds its historical king. This difference concerns the opening petition. On universal homage, Bellarmine's three alternatives still distinguish present conversion, final acknowledgment and the authority Christ already possesses over unwilling subjects.
