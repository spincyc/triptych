\section{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\zlabel{triptych:concise:themes:start}
Christ's kingship gives what it commands: a people able to hear him and a peace they must learn to live. The Mass places the prisoner before Pilate beside the Creator of every power. His self-offering joins those two scenes. The King who declines armed rescue is accomplishing the reconciliation for which the peoples pray.

\subsection{The slain Lamb and the divided family}
The Introit's heavenly acclaim begins with the Lamb as slain. Apocalypse 5 places his victory within the opening of the sealed book; the accompanying words from Apocalypse 1 bring kingdom and priesthood into the same redemptive praise. The wound belongs to the identity of the victorious one. The assembly consequently begins by receiving the meaning of victory from Christ, before the Gospel exposes Pilate's assumptions about it.

The Collect names the corresponding wound in humanity: sin scatters human families. The royal prayer concerns their healing. Its description of Christ's dominion as gentle belongs beside its universal reach. An enlarged territory cannot by itself cure hostility within the people who inhabit it. The Introit's Psalm petition already makes judgment and justice part of royal power; the Collect asks that this rule become effective in persons divided by sin.

Two questions emerge. How do people become subjects willing to hear this King? Augustine answers from the kingdom's origin in regeneration; Jerome explains its enduring inheritance through the incarnate Son. What does the King accomplish for the creation subject to him? Chrysostom answers from the Creator's sacrificial reconciliation; Bellarmine unfolds the justice and varied recognition of his universal authority. The first question concerns entry and perseverance; the second concerns the communion into which the saved are brought.

\subsection{The Creator stands before a governor}
Colossians moves from thanksgiving for deliverance into an account of the deliverer's identity. The Son through whom all things exist is head of the Church, firstborn from the dead and maker of peace through the Cross. Chrysostom follows that movement from power to condescension: the one above all joins himself to those below. His authority reaches the invisible powers as fully as the visible ones. No creature offers an independent source of salvation beyond him.\footnote{Chrysostom, \emph{Homilies on Colossians} II, on 1:12--15; III, on 1:15--20.}

The Gospel presents the same King's testimony under accusation. His kingdom has another origin, and his servants do not fight to prevent his delivery. Augustine understands the distinction as a people living in this world whose new life comes from grace. He expressly relates it to the Epistle's transfer out of darkness. The two texts thus meet in the question of belonging: what changes when someone becomes Christ's subject?\footnote{Augustine, \emph{Tractates on John} 115.2,4.}

The Gradual and Alleluia widen the answer. Psalm 71 reaches every nation and ruler; Daniel 7 announces an enduring kingdom. Its subjects cannot retreat into a private religious sentiment. Nor can they identify its permanence with the fortunes of any earthly power. The human figure who receives dominion belongs to a vision in which beastly kingdoms pass and the saints receive a lasting inheritance.

\subsection{The nations enter the horizon of sacrifice}
The Offertory's Psalm 2:8 places the Son's asking at the center of the Father's promise of the nations. Augustine reads that petition through incarnate sacrifice and intercession. The peoples for whom the Collect seeks healing enter the Son's priestly concern. Their salvation cannot be reduced to the congregation's expansion or achievement: the one who asks is the one whose self-giving makes the inheritance possible.\footnote{Augustine, \emph{Expositions of the Psalms}, Ps 2.7.}

The Secret makes the relation between sacrifice and peace explicit. The Father is asked that the offered Son bestow unity and peace upon the nations. The prayer reaches farther than those assembled while exposing their own continuing dependence. Being together in a church does not make its members reconciled simply by proximity. The Epistle identifies the source of the needed gift: peace is accomplished through the blood of the Cross.

The proper Preface unites eternal priesthood and universal kingship in the Son's offering of himself. Its truth, life, holiness, grace, justice, love and peace describe the kingdom's character. They are the life his rule gives, rather than ornaments attached to power. Thanksgiving then joins the angels. Chrysostom's explanation of heavenly reconciliation makes that company intelligible: humanity's estrangement from heaven is healed through the Son, and human praise enters a communion it could not create for itself.\footnote{Chrysostom, \emph{Homilies on Colossians} III, on reconciliation of things in heaven.}

Universal lordship and universal willing obedience remain different claims. Bellarmine gives the Gradual's homage three possible reaches: widespread conversion, complete recognition at judgment, and present authority even over those who resist. The Secret's continued petition belongs within that distinction. The King has authority over all; the peace of voluntary communion still calls for conversion.\footnote{Bellarmine, \emph{Commentary on Psalms}, Ps 71, numbered comment 10 on biblical v. 11.}

\subsection{A blessing carried into conflict}
The Communion receives the King's peace at the close of a psalm filled with the power of his voice amid a storm. Augustine reads its conclusion as the Church's peace in Christ while the world's disturbances continue. Such peace does not depend on an untroubled week. Bellarmine similarly relates strength in the present journey to the blessing of its completion.\footnote{Augustine, \emph{Expositions of the Psalms}, Ps 28.10--11; Bellarmine, Ps 28, conclusion.}

The Postcommunion therefore joins nourishment to continuing service and heavenly reign. Augustine's hearer still needs sustenance and perseverance. Chrysostom's recipient of peace must carry it into ordinary relationships, including the relationship marked by a chosen feud. The gift is neither exhausted by inward recognition nor completed by a spoken greeting. It changes how a person obeys and how persons live together.

The first reading follows the making of a willing subject toward lasting fellowship with the King. The second follows the Creator's reconciling work toward communion across human and heavenly divisions. They converge in the final petition without becoming interchangeable. Gratitude answers the gift of hearing; concord answers the gift of peace. Both depend on the slain Lamb whose kingship the Mass has acclaimed from the beginning.
