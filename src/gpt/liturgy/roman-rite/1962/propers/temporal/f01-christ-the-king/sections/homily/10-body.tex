There are two men in the governor's hall. One can order an execution. The other has been delivered into his hands. If we were looking for the man with power, the answer would seem obvious. Yet today the Church asks us to recognize our King in the prisoner.

Pilate wants to know whether Jesus is a king. It is a practical question. A king might have followers willing to fight for him. He might threaten the authority Pilate represents. Jesus points to something Pilate can see: his servants are not fighting to prevent his being handed over. Then he speaks about truth, and about those who hear his voice.

Jesus describes people who hear his voice and respond to truth. Their allegiance involves more than the recognition that he has power over them. They believe him and obey him.

Saint Augustine explains Jesus' words about his kingdom's origin. Jesus says that it is not of this world. He does not say that it is absent from this world. The King's people live here, among people who believe and people who do not. Augustine recalls the wheat and the tares growing together until the harvest. The kingdom's heavenly origin is consistent with the evil its members still encounter and the final separation they await.

Belonging to this kingdom therefore concerns our present conduct. Those who remain in the world need to receive a life that comes from Christ. They still have responsibilities to other people, including people who resist him. Augustine explains the change in the people themselves: through a new birth, they become subjects of this King.

The Epistle begins by giving thanks for that change. The Father has rescued us from darkness and brought us into the kingdom of his beloved Son. Augustine uses this very passage to explain the Gospel. The people transferred into the Son's kingdom owe their new life to God. Thanksgiving acknowledges both their rescue and the life into which they have been brought.

Saint John Chrysostom draws attention to how generous that gift is. God gives the inheritance, and he also makes us fit to receive it. Someone can be given an office without having the ability to carry it out. God reaches more deeply. His grace changes the recipient. The King gives us the life from which obedience can grow.

Obedience can seem beyond us even when we recognize what is right. Someone may know that a correction is deserved and still resent the person who gives it. Understanding the fault has not yet made him willing to acknowledge it. The Epistle gives thanks for what God has done for people who needed rescue. The one who makes them fit for their inheritance can change the unwilling recipient too. Our weakness is a reason to turn toward him for the grace to respond.

At the Gospel's close, Jesus connects belonging to truth with hearing his voice. Augustine explains that hearing means inward belief and obedience. Pilate can hear every sound Jesus makes. The hearing Christ asks of us reaches further: it allows his truth to judge our choices and change them.

Augustine also notices which comes first. Belonging to truth gives rise to hearing. We do not earn God's first gift by proving how good we already are at listening. Grace enables the response. That is why Christian obedience can be both humble and active. We really must respond, and we can give thanks for the very possibility of responding.

Consider what happens when someone corrects us. We may listen closely, but only to prepare our defense. We can remember every word and remain determined that none of it will change us. Hearing the King asks something more costly. It asks us to let truth reach the place we are protecting. Perhaps there is a real fault beneath the excuse. Perhaps the person we resent has told us something we needed to hear.

The King who asks this of us has given himself for us. The Epistle identifies him as the one through whom all things were created and in whom they continue to exist. It also says that he makes peace through the blood of his Cross. The same Son sustains his creatures and gives himself for their reconciliation. His servants do not fight to prevent his delivery, and the Passion proceeds. The prisoner before Pilate is moving toward the sacrifice through which enemies can be reconciled.

We can therefore trust his correction. He seeks the healing of the person he calls to obedience. The Mass's opening prayer names sin as the wound that divides the human family and asks that its divided peoples be gathered under Christ's gentle dominion. His grace changes people whose sin has estranged them. The change in the hearer concerns the people with whom he lives as well.

At the offering, the prayer called the Secret asks the Father that his Son give unity and peace among the nations. The one offered in sacrifice is himself the giver of the peace being sought. We bring to the altar the division we need him to heal. The Son whose voice calls us to obedience gives himself for the reconciliation of the people he gathers.

Chrysostom presses the demands of this peace upon his congregation. They hear it proclaimed to all in worship, yet someone keeps a feud alive with a fellow member of the Church. Chrysostom compares their condition to a body with some members healthy and others disordered. Its healthy part does not make the whole body well. The Son came to reconcile heaven and earth; those receiving his peace have to seek reconciliation with one another.

If you have recognized your own fault in a correction you resent, acknowledge it to the person concerned. Say plainly what you did wrong, without turning the acknowledgment into another accusation. The truth you have heard then changes your conduct toward someone from whom you have been estranged. Christ's grace makes that response possible, and his peace calls for it.

Acknowledging your fault leaves the other person's responsibility intact. Continuing wrongdoing still has to be resisted truthfully, and another person's response remains beyond your control. Reconciliation requires truth about what each person has done. It also requires giving up hatred of the person who has wronged us. Christ's authority reaches both people; each needs the conversion the Mass's prayers seek.

The Communion antiphon sings the King's blessing of peace at the end of a psalm filled with the force of God's voice amid a storm. Augustine reads its conclusion as the Church's peace in Christ while the world's troubles continue. The blessing can be received during conflict, while the people receiving it still need to persevere. A relationship may take time to mend, even when someone has begun to acknowledge his fault.

The prayer after Communion asks that those already nourished by the sacrament, who glory in serving under Christ's standard, may reign with him in heaven. Their present service has that lasting fellowship as its hope. The people who first received the capacity to hear still depend on the King as they continue in obedience. We receive nourishment from him as people who need grace to persevere.

The hope of shared reign extends beyond the conflict in which that obedience is learned. Christ gathers people who can hear him and live together in his peace, and he calls them to lasting life with him. The prisoner whose testimony we hear in the Gospel is the Son who gives his life for the people who will share his kingdom.
