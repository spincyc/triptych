\section{Hearing the King and Receiving His Peace}
\zlabel{triptych:concise:commentary:start}
\subsection{Who becomes a subject of the King?}
Pilate's question concerns royal identity, but Jesus' answer also reveals the character of his subjects. A ruler defended by armed followers would fit the governor's expectations. Jesus instead points to the absence of a fight to prevent his delivery, then to the hearing of his voice. The appointed Gospel ends at John 18:37, before Pilate's question about truth. Its final movement therefore concerns a response still open to those who hear the passage: whether they will receive the King's testimony as an obedient people.

Augustine's distinction between the kingdom's place and its origin prevents two misunderstandings. Jesus says that his kingdom is not \emph{of} this world; he does not say it is absent from the world. Its members remain within human relationships and responsibilities. Augustine recalls both Christ's prayer for disciples who remain in the world and the wheat and tares that coexist until the harvest. Present membership and final purification cannot be made identical. The Church's mixture does not make grace worldly, and grace's heavenly source does not remove its recipients from history.\footnote{Augustine, \emph{Tractates on John} 115.1--2.}

His decisive explanation concerns regeneration. The people once belonged to the prince of this world; they become Christ's kingdom through a new birth. Augustine himself invokes Colossians 1:13 to explain the change. The Epistle's transfer into the beloved Son's kingdom therefore names more than a change of ruler over otherwise unchanged subjects. The Collect's wounded human family needs this kind of healing. External organization can restrain conflict, but it cannot by itself turn hostility into the willing allegiance the prayer seeks.

The next difficulty lies in the order of Jesus' words about truth and hearing. Does someone first hear correctly and earn the right to belong to truth? Augustine answers that belonging is the prior gift from which obedient hearing follows. He distinguishes natural creaturehood from the grace at issue. All human beings exist through the Word, yet not all believe. The hearing Jesus names must therefore be more than the universal relation of creature to Creator. It is inward belief and obedience, enabled by the truth to which the hearer has been given.\footnote{Augustine, \emph{Tractates on John} 115.4.}

Chrysostom brings the same problem into view from the Epistle's opening thanksgiving. An inheritance may be offered to someone unable to enjoy it; here the Father also makes its recipients fit to share it. Rescue itself does not exhaust the generosity. Deliverance from darkness is followed by entrance into the Son's kingdom. The gift includes both freedom from captivity and a new royal relation. Augustine's account of hearing explains the willing response within that relation; Chrysostom's account of fitness explains why its recipients give thanks rather than claim an achievement.\footnote{Chrysostom, \emph{Homilies on Colossians} II, exposition of Col 1:12--15.}

This distinction also clarifies the relation between the two readings of the Mass. The Creator has authority over every creature, including the person who resists him. Yet that authority is not already the obedient hearing Augustine describes. Bellarmine's distinction between rightful rule and willing conversion will keep the two from collapsing into one. The first reading asks how a person receives new life as a subject; the second begins with the Lord's wider claim over the world and asks how estranged creatures enter communion.

The moral consequence follows from the account of hearing itself. Correction is part of listening when the speaker is Lord; refusal of every correction contradicts the allegiance professed. At the same time, another person's conversion cannot be treated as the trophy of one's own skill, since the capacity to hear is received. The Introit's praise and the Epistle's thanksgiving give the appropriate form to both truths. The hearer acts, obeys and perseveres, while acknowledging that the life from which those acts proceed is a gift.

\subsection{How can the universal Lord receive a kingdom?}
The Mass speaks repeatedly of reception: the Lamb receives acclaim, Daniel's human figure receives dominion, and the Son asks the Father for the nations. These expressions can seem inconsistent with the Epistle's claim that everything exists through the Son. If his authority is universal, what could he still receive? Jerome and Chrysostom approach that difficulty from different texts, and their answers explain different parts of the same identity.

In Daniel 7, the human figure approaches the Ancient of Days after the vision of beastly kingdoms and judgment. Jerome reads the Son of Man through the reality of the Incarnation. The Son of God assumes human flesh; the gift of power and kingdom concerns that assumed condition. Philippians 2 supplies the movement of humiliation and exaltation. The one who eternally possesses divine dignity truly enters the human condition in which obedience and exaltation are predicated of him. Humanity is therefore neither an appearance discarded at victory nor a rival person beside the eternal Son.\footnote{Jerome, \emph{Commentary on Daniel}, VII, on 7:13--14, PL 25, cols. 670--671.}

That reasoning matters to the first reading's promised inheritance. Daniel 7:18 and 27 associate the saints with the kingdom; the Postcommunion asks for lasting reign with Christ. Their hope depends on more than a divine ruler remaining invulnerable above them. Jerome's incarnate King carries human nature into the enduring dominion. He rejects fulfillment exhausted by the Maccabean victory, since it cannot account for the promise's scope and permanence, and rejects an earthly millennium as the saints' final inheritance. Regeneration has a destination beyond another cycle of earthly rise and collapse.\footnote{Jerome, \emph{Commentary on Daniel}, VII, on 7:17--18,27.}

Chrysostom meets a related difficulty in Colossians 1:15. Calling Christ firstborn of creation might appear to place him first within a created series. He reads the explanatory clauses instead of isolating that title. All things, visible and invisible, are created through the Son. The source of the entire created order cannot be assigned to that order as its earliest member. The named spiritual powers reinforce the point: invisible dignity does not confer an exemption from creaturehood.\footnote{Chrysostom, \emph{Homilies on Colossians} III, on Col 1:15--17.}

He then makes the dependence continuous. The Son does not merely initiate beings that thereafter subsist independently; he sustains them. His lordship is consequently different in kind from the superiority of one created ruler over another. This explains why the second reading begins with creation. No earthly or angelic power can stand outside the authority of the King, or supply a saving route independent of him. The head of the Church is the one on whom its members already depend for existence.

Jerome protects the reality of received human glory; Chrysostom protects the Son's uncreated primacy. Neither makes kingship an acquisition of divine nature. The Introit's \emph{divinitatem} occurs within the Lamb's acclamation, and Colossians identifies the one acclaimed. His death manifests redemptive victory; it does not promote a creature into God. The received Victorinus commentary gives supporting context by connecting the Lamb's victory over death with the opening of the testament and humanity's passage into life. Its received text carries attribution and interpolation cautions and offers a chapter-level argument, not a separate explanation of every term of the antiphon.\footnote{Received Victorinus, \emph{Commentary on the Apocalypse}, ch. 5, ANF VII, with the edition's elucidations.}

The Preface brings these identities into one action: the eternal Priest and universal King offers himself. Augustine's treatment of Psalm 2:8 likewise understands the Son's asking through incarnate sacrifice and intercession. A petition spoken within the saving work of the Son does not imply deficient divine power. It reveals what that power does for the peoples. The first reading sees their human inheritance opened in him; the second sees the Creator himself entering the cost of their reconciliation.\footnote{Augustine, \emph{Expositions of the Psalms}, Ps 2.7.}

\subsection{What breach does the Cross heal?}
The Collect's wound is sin's dispersion of human families. The Secret asks the sacrificial Christ for unity and peace among the nations. Their diagnosis makes the Epistle's reconciliation concrete: the needed gift is not simply orderly coexistence among parties whose inward hostility remains untouched. Yet Colossians extends reconciliation even to things in heaven. That extension raises the second reading's most difficult question. What could holy angels need to have reconciled?

Chrysostom begins with the apparent contradiction. Heaven is the model of obedience invoked in the Lord's Prayer; war there seems inconsistent with that obedience. He resolves the difficulty by locating the breach between holy angels and sinful humanity. The angels oppose the dishonor human beings have done to their Lord. Their alienation concerns our wickedness, not a sin of their own for which they require redemption. Christ removes the estrangement by bringing humanity into their company.\footnote{Chrysostom, \emph{Homilies on Colossians} III, exposition of Col 1:20, discussion of ``things in the heavens.''}

He points to angels appearing joyfully among human beings and to humanity received in heaven. The reconciling work reaches two directions: human beings are brought into peace, and their relation to heavenly society is restored. This is a stronger claim than relief from individual distress. The Creator gathers a communion whose divisions no created intermediary can heal by independent power. The angels have a real ministry within that order; they do not provide an alternative salvation outside Christ.

The contrast with Augustine's account of hearing is instructive. Augustine asks why the individual can respond inwardly when mere creaturehood does not explain faith. Chrysostom asks what that saving relation brings the person into. A converted hearer is received into a communion reaching beyond the limits of one household, people or earthly congregation. The first answer identifies the gift that transforms allegiance; the second reveals the breadth of the shared life that results. Neither can be reduced to private religious experience.

The Preface's conclusion gives that breadth a liturgical expression. After proclaiming the priestly King's offering, the assembly joins the angels in thanksgiving. The Introit has already situated praise in the heavenly court around the Lamb. Earthly worship belongs within the reconciliation proclaimed by the Epistle: humanity enters praise through the Son, while remaining creaturely. The human worshipper has not become an independent source of glory or a heavenly being by nature.

The manner of reconciliation is as consequential for Chrysostom as its reach. God reconciles through the Son himself, through his death, and through the Cross. Each step makes the condescension more exact. The giver does not stand apart from the cost; the one through whom all things exist bears the estrangement's cost personally. This is why the King's delivery in John 18 can belong to his royal work. Preventing it by force would not accomplish the reconciliation Colossians proclaims. The Introit's slain Lamb and the Preface's self-offering make sacrifice central to the feast's victory.\footnote{Chrysostom, \emph{Homilies on Colossians} III, on Col 1:19--20; John 18:10--11,33--37.}

The universal language nevertheless leaves personal response intact. Colossians 1:21--23 turns to former enemies and calls for perseverance in faith. Chrysostom's holy angels are not fallen spirits universally pardoned, and his account does not announce saving friendship already enjoyed by every person. Augustine's obedience and Bellarmine's distinctions about homage remain necessary. The Creator's reconciling work supplies the possibility and source of peace; its beneficiaries are summoned to live from it.

The moral demand follows without minimizing the breach. Reconciliation matters because wrongdoing has wounded relationships. Pretending otherwise would empty the prayers' diagnosis of meaning. Equally, treating a wrongdoer as beyond every possibility of conversion would close the very horizon toward which the King's self-giving is directed. The Secret joins truthful recognition of division to petition for its healing. The gift it seeks is deeper than denial of injury and wider than peace confined to one's allies.

\subsection{What does the homage of all nations mean?}
The Gradual selects Psalm 71:8 and 11, joining worldwide dominion to homage from rulers and peoples. The surrounding verses describe deliverance of the needy. Royal justice therefore determines the character of the dominion: the same king who receives universal homage rescues those unable to free themselves. An interpretation that retained only the scale of power would miss the reason the psalm gives for hope in that power.

Augustine and Bellarmine genuinely differ over the opening petition used by the Introit. Augustine begins with Christ as the true Solomon, the peacemaker whose kingdom surpasses that of the historical king. Bellarmine allows an immediate prayer by David for Solomon's just government. That prayer has an intelligible historical object before the psalm's scope and endurance lead beyond Solomon to Christ. Both affirm Christological fulfillment; they differ over how directly the opening speaks of it.\footnote{Augustine, \emph{Expositions of the Psalms}, Ps 71.1--2; Bellarmine, \emph{Commentary on Psalms}, Ps 71, opening and comments 4--7.}

The difference changes the path of understanding. Augustine brings the definitive peacemaker directly before the reader. Bellarmine first gives the charge to govern justly its place within Israel's royal life, then shows why that historical reign cannot exhaust the promise. The literal setting and the allegorical fulfillment remain connected in his argument. Neither account makes the universal homage evidence that every ruler now freely obeys Christ.

Bellarmine addresses precisely that difficulty at biblical verse 11, treated in his numbered comment 10. He distinguishes three possible senses. Scriptural generalization can describe the widespread conversion of rulers and nations while allowing exceptions. The complete recognition may belong to final judgment. Or the words may concern Christ's present authority, which holds even over those who do not consent. These answers differ in subject and time: conversion concerns a willing change; judgment concerns unavoidable acknowledgment; authority concerns the King's actual right.\footnote{Bellarmine, \emph{Commentary on Psalms}, Ps 71, comment 10.}

These distinctions connect the two readings without making them say the same thing. Augustine's hearer belongs to the order of willing allegiance. Chrysostom's Creator has authority over every creature before any creature consents. Bellarmine explains how universal rule can be confessed without pretending that the personal transformation has already occurred everywhere. The moral challenge accordingly runs both ways. Resistance does not disprove Christ's authority, while reciting that authority does not prove one's own conversion.

Bellarmine then interprets the psalm's rescue of the poor through liberation from the devil's captivity, forgiveness and restored grace. The helpless become recipients rather than objects of royal contempt. His reasoning supplies a concrete bridge to Colossians' transfer from darkness. The King's power is attractive because it frees; its justice includes the person who cannot secure his own deliverance. Human and public responsibilities belong within that saving rule, not outside the scope of a merely private faith.\footnote{Bellarmine, \emph{Commentary on Psalms}, Ps 71, comments 11--13; Pius XI, \emph{Quas primas}, nos. 12--20.}

The Offertory's Psalm 2:8 reaches the same peoples through the Son's petition. Augustine emphasizes incarnate intercession for their salvation; Bellarmine relates their inheritance to the Son's rightful possession. The following rod-of-iron verse belongs to the psalm's context, not the appointed Offertory. Bellarmine's treatment includes correction of sinful habits and final accountability. The royal image cannot simply become a commission for worshippers to coerce conversion. The Gospel's King gives himself, and his subjects are called to hear.\footnote{Augustine, \emph{Expositions of the Psalms}, Ps 2.7; Bellarmine, \emph{Commentary on Psalms}, Ps 2, comments on vv. 7--10.}

\subsection{How does received peace become a way of life?}
The Communion's blessing follows the storm and powerful voice of Psalm 28. Augustine reads the conclusion as the Church's peace in Christ amid the world's flood, reaching toward final reign in his saints. The setting prevents peace from being measured solely by outward calm. A sufferer has not disproved the King's blessing by remaining exposed to adversity. The first reading's hope concerns perseverance through that condition, sustained by the King whose reign outlasts it.\footnote{Augustine, \emph{Expositions of the Psalms}, Ps 28.10--11.}

Bellarmine preserves natural and ecclesial readings of the psalm and does not settle all alternatives concerning the flood. At its conclusion, present strength and final blessing belong to different stages of the journey. This distinction gives the Postcommunion's nourishment a continuing purpose. The hearer has not become self-sufficient by first believing. The sacrament sustains service whose completion remains a gift of fellowship with Christ. The prayer's military image is governed by the King who refuses a battle to prevent his Passion and by the peace sought at the altar.\footnote{Bellarmine, \emph{Commentary on Psalms}, Ps 28, opening and conclusion.}

Chrysostom approaches the same blessing from the relation between the recipients. His exposition of heavenly reconciliation turns toward the Church's repeated exchange of peace. A greeting addressed to all cannot be made true by peace with one neighbor while a chosen feud is preserved with another. He compares that partial concord to a body with some members healthy and others disordered. The health of the whole cannot be established by pointing only to its healthy part.\footnote{Chrysostom, \emph{Homilies on Colossians} III, practical application following the exposition of heavenly reconciliation.}

The comparison is rooted in his exegesis. If Christ's self-giving restores communion across the breach between heaven and humanity, deliberate enmity within his body contradicts the gift received. The immense reach of reconciliation becomes exacting in a particular relationship. The faithful cannot secure peace everywhere by their own effort, but neither may they use its immense scale to excuse the feud they choose to maintain. The Secret's petition for nations and the Communion's blessing for the people meet in that ordinary obligation.

Here the readings make their different moral demands most clear. Augustine's account exposes pride in the origin of faith: the hearer owes his very capacity to respond to grace. Chrysostom exposes selectivity in the life of communion: received peace cannot be restricted to those one already likes. Humility before the gift and concord among its recipients support each other. A grateful hearer can still need reconciliation with a neighbor; a worker for peace still needs to receive truth and correction.

Their final hopes are likewise related without being identical. Jerome's exposition of Daniel protects the enduring inheritance of the saints from reduction to a temporary victory or an earthly final kingdom. Bellarmine's final acknowledgment protects universal authority from being judged by its present incomplete recognition. The former directs the pilgrim toward life with the incarnate King; the latter places every power before his judgment. Saving communion and unavoidable acknowledgment remain distinct.

The literal texts have led through accusation, rescue, royal justice and sacrificial petition. Their allegorical fulfillment centers on the one Son who becomes man, gathers the Church and restores heavenly communion. Their moral summons reaches obedient hearing and the refusal of cherished enmity. Their anagogical horizon is the shared reign sought after Communion. The first reading follows persons made capable of belonging; the second follows the communion into which they are brought. Both end with dependence on the King who gives his life and continues to nourish the people he has made.
