\properlane{reconciled-creation}{The Creator Restores Communion by Giving Himself}
The authority celebrated by the Mass is the authority of the one through whom the world exists. Yet the Epistle directs that cosmic claim toward the blood of the Cross. The Creator restores communion by entering the cost of reconciliation himself. Chrysostom explains the identity and action of this reconciling Son; Bellarmine explains how the universal royal claims of the Psalter concern justice, conversion and a recognition still awaiting completion. Together their arguments make peace the content of royal victory, rather than a pleasant consequence added after it.

\subsection{The firstborn and the origin of every power}
Colossians names the Son the firstborn of all creation. The phrase can appear to place him at the head of a sequence of creatures. Chrysostom refuses that inference by following the clauses that explain it: all things were created through him, and all continue to depend on him. The Son cannot be assigned to the class whose existence he causes. The vocabulary of birth must be read within the passage's account of creative agency.\footnote{Chrysostom, \emph{Homilies on Colossians} III, on Col 1:15--17, NPNF I.13.}

Chrysostom's exposition develops the point through the distinction between visible and invisible creatures. Invisible powers might seem to offer a higher route to God than the human Christ. Paul names them within creation precisely to exclude that exemption. Thrones, dominations, principalities and powers are not rival sources of being. Their greatness remains received. The Son's primacy is therefore more radical than superiority in rank within a common order.

Nor does his work stop with an initial act of creation. Chrysostom explains that creatures would cease to stand if separated from the Son's sustaining providence. Their present existence is dependent. The ruler who resists him, the angel who serves him and the ordinary believer who prays to him share this creaturely dependence, though their moral relations to him differ. This distinction will matter when Bellarmine distinguishes universal authority from willing obedience.

The Epistle next names the Son head of the Church. Chrysostom hears the astonishing generosity of the transition: the one above all joins himself to those below. His account of divine dignity becomes an account of love. The firstborn from the dead gives resurrection the same personal center as creation. The Creator does not discard the world that needs reconciliation; the risen head establishes a new life for his members.

The Preface places that identity within priestly thanksgiving. The universal King offers himself on the Cross. His dominion is not secured by obtaining something outside him that could supply a power he lacks. The giver of being gives himself for those whose enmity cannot be cured by the fact of existence alone. Creation explains why all are his; redemption explains how enemies can come to live with him in peace.

\subsection{Why the blood of the Cross matters to the argument}
It is possible to acknowledge that reconciliation happened and pass too quickly over its manner. Chrysostom makes the manner decisive. He distinguishes the greatness of reconciliation itself from the further wonder that God accomplishes it through the Son himself, through death, and through the Cross. The one who makes peace bears its cost personally. The Cross is not an independent instrument with a saving power detached from the one who gives himself upon it.\footnote{Chrysostom, \emph{Homilies on Colossians} III, exposition of Col 1:19--20.}

This is the point at which the feast's royal claims become most demanding. A king might pacify a territory by suppressing everyone who resists. Christ reconciles the estranged through his own self-giving. His conduct before Pilate belongs to that work. The absence of a fight to prevent delivery is not evidence that his reconciling mission has failed. It permits the Passion through which the Epistle says peace is made. The Gospel's truth and the Epistle's Cross belong to the life of the same person.

The Introit has made the relation audible from the first moment. Heavenly worship recognizes the slain Lamb. Victory has not erased his sacrificial identity. The Collect then names the wounded human families who need restoration. The Secret brings both sides into one petition: the offered Christ is asked to give the peoples unity and peace. The prayer does not treat unity as something the congregation produces by assembling in one place. The sacrificial giver is its source.

Such peace has a moral shape. It does not require pretending that sin caused no wound or that hostility is a trivial misunderstanding. The Epistle names reconciliation because enmity is real. Its generosity is measured by the actual breach it heals. A community that refuses to name wrongdoing cannot recognize the need for reconciliation; one that treats wrongdoers as beyond every possibility of conversion denies the purpose of the King's self-giving. The proper prayers hold the wound and the hoped-for healing together.

\subsection{What is reconciled in heaven?}
The last phrase of the Epistle raises a problem deeper than the scale of earthly conflict. Things in heaven also belong to reconciliation. Chrysostom asks how there can be war among heavenly beings when heaven is the model of obedience invoked in the Lord's Prayer. His answer does not make the holy angels sinners. The breach lies between heaven and sinful humanity: the angels oppose the dishonor humanity has done to their Lord.\footnote{Chrysostom, \emph{Homilies on Colossians} III, discussion beginning with the question about ``things in the heavens.''}

The solution is correspondingly relational. Christ brings humanity into heavenly company and removes the estrangement. Chrysostom points to the angels' joyful appearance among human beings and to humanity's reception in heaven. Earth is reconciled both internally and with heaven; heaven receives the human beings formerly alienated from it. The result is communion across a divide that no created intermediary could heal by independent power.

This explains why Chrysostom turns against reliance upon angels as substitutes for Christ. Their ministry is real, but they are themselves servants within the order the Son reconciles. To make them alternative saviors would mistake the beneficiaries and witnesses of his peace for its independent source. The Son's creative primacy and reconciling action thus answer one connected concern: no invisible power can offer a saving route that bypasses him.

The interpretation also gives the Preface's heavenly company particular force. The assembly's thanksgiving joins angelic worship after proclaiming the priestly King's self-offering. Human beings do not enter that praise by claiming they have become divine or ceased to be creatures. They enter through the reconciliation accomplished by the Son. The Introit's heavenly court and the Communion's blessing of peace are two moments within this shared life.

The universality of the claim must retain the Epistle's immediate context. Colossians 1:21--23 turns from the cosmic horizon to former enemies and their perseverance in faith. Chrysostom's explanation of heavenly reconciliation concerns holy angels and humanity, not universal pardon for every rebellious spiritual being. Nor does the scale of Christ's work imply that every human person already enjoys saving friendship with him. The kingdom's reach and the response of its subjects remain distinct.

\subsection{All kings, and the recognition still to come}
The Gradual declares a dominion embracing all nations and rulers. Bellarmine asks how such language can be true when willing acknowledgment is visibly incomplete. His commentary on Psalm 71:11 presents three ways the universal claim bears upon Christ. It can describe widespread conversion in the scriptural manner of speaking generally while allowing exceptions; it can look toward final judgment, when acknowledgment will be complete; and it can name the actual authority Christ already has over all, with or without their consent.\footnote{Bellarmine, \emph{Commentary on Psalms}, Ps 71, numbered comment 10 on biblical v. 11, O'Sullivan translation.}

The alternatives are not interchangeable. Conversion describes a change in the subject. Final acknowledgment describes an encounter with judgment that cannot be evaded. Present authority describes the King's right and power even where the subject resists. Confusing these claims produces two opposite errors: announcing that everyone already obeys willingly, or deciding that resistance proves Christ lacks universal authority. Bellarmine avoids both by explaining what each sense asserts.

His reading of the psalm's beginning is equally important. The prayer for the king's judgment may concern David and Solomon in its immediate historical reference. Solomon can receive a responsibility to govern justly; the request is intelligible within Israel's royal life. Yet the psalm's endurance and scope carry the interpretation beyond him. Bellarmine moves from the historical king to Christ rather than forcing every promise to be exhausted by Solomon's reign. Augustine begins instead from Christ as true Solomon and peacemaker. Their disagreement concerns the immediate scope of the opening, while both find its fulfillment in Christ.\footnote{Bellarmine, \emph{Commentary on Psalms}, Ps 71, opening and comments 4--7; Augustine, \emph{Expositions of the Psalms}, Ps 71.1--2.}

The verses following universal homage explain why people turn to this King. He rescues the helpless. Bellarmine develops that rescue as liberation from the devil's captivity through forgiveness and restoration of grace. Human poverty is not a reason for the King to despise his subjects; it is the condition in which they discover their need of him. Bellarmine's exposition therefore joins the Psalm's royal scope to the Epistle's transfer from darkness. He supplies more than another assertion that Christ is powerful: he explains the saving attraction and justice of his rule.\footnote{Bellarmine, \emph{Commentary on Psalms}, Ps 71, comments 11--13, on the deliverance passage following biblical v. 11.}

At the offering, Psalm 2:8 extends the same horizon to the ends of the earth. Augustine relates the Son's asking to priestly intercession; Bellarmine relates the inheritance to the Son's identity and rightful possession. The nations are not marginal to Christian prayer. Neither are they already indistinguishable from a fully obedient people. The Collect and Secret keep asking for a healing that the universal King can give and that divided humanity continues to need.

\subsection{The Church's peace and the feud at its door}
Chrysostom does not allow reconciliation to remain a grand picture remote from ordinary conduct. His homily turns to the Church's repeated exchange of peace. The greeting is directed to all. A person who maintains peace with one neighbor and a feud with another cannot treat partial agreement as the health of the whole body. Chrysostom compares such selective peace to a body in which some members are at rest while others remain disordered. The body is not healthy merely because the disorder is localized.\footnote{Chrysostom, \emph{Homilies on Colossians} III, practical application following the exposition of heavenly reconciliation.}

The comparison changes the meaning of the liturgical greeting. It is not a decorative word laid over unchanged relationships. The Church asks for a gift whose demands reach the person one would prefer to exclude. Chrysostom's conclusion grows directly from his exegesis: the Son came to reconcile heaven and earth, so a recipient of that peace contradicts the gift by deliberately preserving enmity. The universality that appeared immense in the Gradual becomes exacting in one ordinary relationship.

The Communion and Postcommunion place this challenge within grace. Peace is the King's blessing, and nourishment sustains the service that follows. A Christian is not asked to invent reconciliation out of personal resources, but to act from a gift received. Augustine's reading of Psalm 28 also keeps peace from being confused with immunity to opposition. Perseverance can require truthfulness, patient correction and resistance to wrongdoing; it cannot make hatred the principle of Christian fidelity.

The Alleluia finally protects this work from despair. The kingdom outlasts the unstable powers of Daniel's vision. Present failures of willing acknowledgment do not determine its final horizon. Jerome's account of the Son's received human dominion and the saints' heavenly inheritance agrees with the hope of the Postcommunion. What the Creator begins through the Cross leads toward communion that death and political change cannot dissolve.

\begin{fourSenses}
\item[Literal.] Colossians joins creation, headship and the Cross; the royal Psalms join dominion to justice; Jesus' trial discloses a kingship whose witness proceeds through his delivery; the prayers seek reconciliation and peace.
\item[Allegorical.] The one Son is Creator, incarnate head, priestly victim and King. His self-giving restores humanity to communion with God and heavenly worship, and gathers his Church from the nations.
\item[Moral.] Those who receive peace must oppose their own feuds and care for the helpless. Royal justice requires conversion and public responsibility; voluntary allegiance is more than unavoidable subjection to authority.
\item[Anagogical.] Universal authority will receive complete acknowledgment at judgment. Saving communion reaches the eternal kingdom, while present resistance and imperfect recognition neither exhaust nor annul Christ's reign.
\end{fourSenses}
