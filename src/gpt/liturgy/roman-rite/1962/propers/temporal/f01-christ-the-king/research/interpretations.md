# Two defensible whole-formulary readings

Research proposals, 19 October 2026. The arguments below are editorial
constructions from independently inspected sources. The Fathers and saints did
not comment on this 1925 formulary as a whole. The exact-text record remains
pending; the elemental assignments and biblical boundaries are those verified
against the 1962 page images and recorded in `scope.md`.

## A. The King makes a people capable of hearing him

**Controlling claim.** Christ's kingdom has a different origin from worldly
kingdoms because its people are created anew by grace. The Mass begins by praising
the slain Lamb and ends by praying for perseverance and shared reign: its subject
is not simply royal status but how sinners become the King's obedient people.

**Principal compatible authors and actual reasoning.** Augustine, *Tractate on
John* 115.2–4, expressly reads John 18 alongside Colossians 1:13. The kingdom
exists in the world, including a mixed present condition, but its origin is the
regeneration of people formerly under the prince of this world. He locates hearing
in inward obedience and belief. The order of the truth/hearing clause matters:
grace makes the hearer belong to truth; hearing is not an independent merit that
earns that belonging. His Psalm 28.10–11 distinguishes the Church's present
exposure to storms from the peace Christ gives and the future reign in his saints.

Jerome, *Commentary on Daniel* VII.13–14,17–18, contributes a different necessary
step. The Son of Man's reception of power belongs to the assumed human condition
and the economy of self-emptying, not to an inferior divine nature. The saints'
kingdom is heavenly and enduring. His argument against an exhausted Maccabean
fulfillment and an earthly millennium prevents this reading from reducing grace
to either a merely past political victory or a future worldly empire. Augustine
explains entry into the kingdom; Jerome explains why the incarnate recipient and
his saints can receive an enduring kingdom without compromising the Son's divinity.
Both are substantial carriers, not a list of supporting names.

Bellarmine on Psalms 2 and 28 provides later reception: divine possession can
break sinful habits rather than authorize the faithful to break enemies, and
present strength is ordered to final blessing. These compatible arguments
strengthen the reading without pretending that every modern application is his.

| Element | Contribution to this reading |
| --- | --- |
| Introit | The congregation praises the Lamb as slain: entry into royal life begins with redemption, not self-exaltation. Its Psalm petition asks for just judgment rather than congratulating human power. |
| Collect | The wound of sin explains dispersion; the petition asks that peoples be gathered under a dominion characterized by gentleness. The prayer is for healing allegiance. |
| Epistle | Rescue from darkness and transfer to the beloved Son's kingdom supply Augustine's own bridge to the Gospel. The giver makes recipients fit for their inheritance; citizenship is a gift with moral consequences. |
| Gradual | The kingdom's reach refuses a private or regional church. Its worldwide scope gathers hearers from all peoples; the surrounding Psalm explains why their king rescues the poor. |
| Alleluia | Received yet indestructible dominion secures the people's hope. Jerome's humanity/divinity distinction makes reception intelligible without making the Son a promoted creature. |
| Gospel | Belonging to truth and hearing the King's voice defines the passage's decisive response. The absence of a fight to prevent his delivery identifies a kingdom not produced by that kind of force. |
| Offertory | Augustine Psalm 2.7 places the Son's asking within incarnate sacrifice and intercession. Peoples become his inheritance for their salvation; offering is therefore missionary and intercessory. |

| Preface | Truth, holiness and grace describe the quality of the kingdom into which people are brought. The Cross is the means by which its priestly King gives this life. |

**Four senses within A.** Literal: a royal interrogation in Jerusalem, the Pauline
claim of deliverance, and the precise petitions and acclamations of this feast.
Allegorical: the incarnate King gathers his Church through sacrificial redemption
and grace, with the Son of Man's enduring kingdom embracing the saints. Moral:
hear inwardly by obedient faith, renounce domination and the habits that fracture
communion, and persevere in the received gift. Anagogical: the Church's present
pilgrimage and conflict reach peace in Christ and a shared heavenly reign.

**Limits and payoff.** This reading must not turn the kingdom into an invisible
private feeling. Augustine's kingdom is a people in the world, and the Gradual,
Offertory and Collect expressly reach peoples and rulers. Nor does grace eliminate
response: hearing is obedient believing, and Colossians 1:23's perseverance remains
in view. This construction especially explains why a Mass celebrating dominion
places a nonresisting prisoner and petitions for transformation at its center.

## B. The Creator restores communion by giving himself

**Controlling claim.** The King's universal authority and his sacrificial
self-giving belong to one work: the Creator and head of the Church reconciles the
creation he sustains. The Mass moves from the heavenly praise of the sacrificed
Lamb through the peace made by his blood to the assembly's participation in that
peace. Its universality is tested by the healing of enmity, not merely by the
extension of power.

**Principal compatible authors and actual reasoning.** Chrysostom, *Homilies on
Colossians* II–III, first distinguishes rescue from darkness from the greater gift
of transfer into the beloved Son's kingdom. His exposition of 1:15–17 refuses to
put the Son among creatures: all angelic and earthly beings are created and held
in being through him. His treatment of 1:20 then asks the difficult question of
what reconciliation in heaven can mean. He identifies the breach between angels
and sinful humanity; the Son's work brings humanity into their company. He counts
the manner of reconciliation as decisive: through Christ himself, his death and
the Cross. His practical conclusion exposes the contradiction of repeatedly
exchanging the Church's peace while continuing a feud with one's brother.

Bellarmine, *Commentary on Psalms* 2 and 71, supplies the compatible but distinct
argument about universal rule. At Psalm 71's opening he allows a literal Davidic
prayer for Solomon's just government, then shows why the promise exceeds that
king. At the universal-homage verse he distinguishes widespread present conversion,
the complete recognition of final judgment, and Christ's present authority even
over those who do not recognize him. The following verses explain the attractive
reason for homage: rescue of the helpless, forgiveness and healing. Christ's right
over all peoples and their voluntary conversion are therefore related but not
identical claims. Chrysostom explains the reconciling means; Bellarmine explains
the extension, justice and unfinished recognition of the reign.

Augustine Psalm 71.1,13–16 adds the true Solomon as peacemaker and the humility
of kings who acknowledge their own need. The received Victorinus commentary on
Apocalypse 5 adds qualified support for the Lamb's death opening the testament
and bringing humanity from death into life. Neither secondary support is needed
to meet the two-carrier threshold; the interpolation cautions in ANF remain visible.

| Element | Contribution to this reading |
| --- | --- |
| Introit | Heavenly acclamation identifies the one worthy of dominion as the slain Lamb; Apocalypse 5's context places universal thanksgiving around redemptive victory. |
| Collect | Restoration of all things is prayed for as the healing of sin's division among human families. Universality must be read with this diagnosis, not as acquisition of territory. |
| Epistle | Creation, sustaining providence, headship, resurrection and reconciliation form one argument. The crucified King is the maker of the powers, not a rival power who happens to defeat them. |
| Gradual | Universal homage is bounded by the Psalm's justice for the helpless. Bellarmine's alternatives prevent a false claim that willing submission has already become universal. |
| Alleluia | An imperishable dominion contrasts with the beasts' unstable kingdoms. The Son of Man's reception does not make the divine Son a late entrant into creation. |
| Gospel | The king's refusal of a fight to prevent delivery makes his self-giving consistent with, rather than a defeat of, his reconciling mission. His testimony requires a response; Pilate's categories do not exhaust kingship. |
| Offertory | The Father's grant of the nations belongs to the Son's priestly asking and rightful inheritance. The whole earth is the horizon of intercession. |
| Secret | The prayer most explicitly joins sacrifice, reconciliation, nations, unity and peace; it asks the present victim to give what the community cannot manufacture. |
| Preface | Eternal priesthood and universal kingship converge on the Cross and the kingdom offered to the Father. Its sequence describes truth, life, holiness, grace, justice, love and peace as the content of royal victory. |
| Communion | The people receive the King's blessing of peace after praying for the nations' reconciliation. Reception calls for the concord Chrysostom demands rather than ritual words detached from conduct. |
| Postcommunion | Sacramental nourishment and active fidelity lead toward shared reign. Service is participation in the King's self-giving work and cannot justify renewed enmity among his members. |

**Four senses within B.** Literal: the Pauline relation of Creator, Church and
Cross, the Psalms' justice and dominion, the trial narrative, and the feast's
actual sacrificial petitions. Allegorical: the one Christ is Creator, priestly
victim and King, reconciling earthly humanity with heavenly communion. Moral:
refuse feuds, protect the helpless and let the peace exchanged in worship govern
ordinary relationships and public responsibility. Anagogical: all authority
will stand before his judgment, and reconciled communion reaches the eternal
kingdom; current recognition remains incomplete.

**Limits and payoff.** Do not infer universal individual salvation from the
scope of reconciliation. Chrysostom's angels are alienated from human wickedness,
not themselves fallen recipients of redemption. Bellarmine's final compulsory
recognition differs from saving friendship. This reading explains the formulated
connection between cosmic lordship and the Secret's concrete request for unity
and peace better than an isolated sequence of royal proof texts would.

## Consequential difficulties and actual comparisons

1. **John 18:36, the kingdom's origin and location.** The appointed contrast is
   between a kingdom originating from this world and one that does not. Augustine
   115.2 explicitly refuses the inference that it is absent from the world; his
   temporal qualification does not postpone all kingship to a later earthly
   regime. A uses this to explain regenerated membership. B asks how the same
   King's refusal of armed rescue serves reconciliation. B's connection to the
   sacrifice is editorial synthesis, supported by John 18–19 and Chrysostom on
   Colossians, not an assertion that Chrysostom comments on this trial here.
   Bellarmine's public and universal authority is complementary to Augustine's
   account of origin, not evidence that Augustine denies obligations in society.

2. **John 18:37, belonging to truth and hearing.** Augustine 115.4 distinguishes
   grace-enabled obedience from natural creaturehood or independently earned
   merit. A makes this distinction its decisive account of how one enters.
   B retains it as the condition of willing response while beginning instead
   with Christ's authority over creatures. The concrete difference is pastoral:
   A asks how a hearer is transformed; B asks whether a recipient of peace can
   persist in enmity. Neither can reduce testimony to mere assent to a slogan.

3. **Colossians 1:15–17, firstborn and creation.** Chrysostom III argues from
   the creation and continuing dependence of all things through the Son against
   treating him as the first creature. Jerome on Daniel 7:13–14 handles the
   analogous problem of bestowed power through the incarnation and humiliation.
   These are compatible answers to different difficulties, not rival Christologies.
   A deploys Jerome to protect the believer's enduring inheritance in the incarnate
   Son; B deploys Chrysostom to identify why no created intermediary can replace him.
   The NPNF editorial discussions and its homily's variant at 1:18 must stay
   separate from the appointed Latin and from the Father's argument.

4. **Colossians 1:20, things in heaven.** Chrysostom III does not solve the
   phrase by announcing universal forgiveness of all heavenly and infernal beings.
   He describes the restored relation of holy angels and humanity and the ascent
   of human nature. B makes this a decisive argument for communion across the
   heavenly/earthly divide. A begins with rescued persons and their hearing but
   must retain this larger horizon; it cannot make reconciliation merely private.
   Colossians 1:21–23 is a necessary contextual control on both readings.

5. **Psalm 71:1,8,11, Solomon and universal homage.** Augustine 71.1–2 begins
   with the true peacemaker Christ because the Psalm exceeds the historical
   Solomon. Bellarmine's opening admits an immediate literal petition by David
   for Solomon and then moves beyond him. This is a real difference of immediate
   exegetical scope, not a dispute over Christ's fulfillment. Bellarmine further
   gives distinct present-conversion, final-judgment and present-authority readings
   of universal homage. A foregrounds the converted people; B preserves the
   difference between universal authority and incomplete voluntary recognition.
   Both must retain the poor and the oppressor in the Psalm's surrounding verses.

6. **Psalm 2:8 and the prayer for the nations.** Augustine 2.7 relates the Son's
   asking to incarnate sacrifice and intercession; Bellarmine 2.7–9 grounds his
   rightful possession in sonship and treats judgment as both merciful correction
   and real final accountability. A emphasizes possession for salvation and the
   breaking of sinful habits. B emphasizes the world's relation to its rightful
   Lord and the difference between authority and saving obedience. The Offertory
   itself appoints verse 8, not the rod-of-iron verse 9; the latter remains context,
   never an additional sung text or a mandate for human violence.

7. **Psalm 28:10–11, peace amid storms.** Augustine 28.10–11 places the saints
   within the world's flood before final reign and distinguishes peace in Christ
   from unpromised worldly calm. Bellarmine preserves natural and ecclesial
   readings and several flood interpretations without settling all of them.
   A stresses perseverance of the hearer amid conflict; B stresses shared peace
   made real in the body. Neither may infer a guaranteed absence of adversity.

8. **The Introit's receiving and the Preface's royal priest.** The Lamb receives
   acclamation in Apocalypse 5's redemptive drama; the selected term for divinity
   must not be made to say that Christ first becomes God after being killed.
   Jerome's assumed-humanity distinction and Chrysostom's uncreated Creator
   argument constrain both readings. The received Victorinus text interprets the
   chapter through overcoming death and inheritance, not acquisition of a newly
   divine essence. This doctrinal coordination is editorial and must be named so.
   The Preface joins priesthood and kingship in self-offering; no Father is
   alleged to have written its sevenfold kingdom description.

These readings are complementary but noninterchangeable. A's decisive inference
is that the kingdom's different origin transforms how a person hears and belongs.
B's is that the Creator's universal lordship takes sacrificial form and requires
actual reconciliation among its beneficiaries. A cannot be reduced to an inward
kingdom, and B cannot be reduced to a successful earthly monarchy. The comparison
belongs at the relevant texts in the concise study, not only in terminal labels.
