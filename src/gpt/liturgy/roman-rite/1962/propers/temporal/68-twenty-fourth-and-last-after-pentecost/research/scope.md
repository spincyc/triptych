## 3. Complete literary contexts and geography

Read directly in the registered per-book Gutenberg Challoner artifacts:
Jer 28–30; complete Psalms 43, 84, 129; the whole four-chapter Colossians;
Mt 23–25; and Mk 11. Native verse addresses, not generated study page
numbers, govern citations. The Latin comparison read Jer 29 in its
surroundings, the three complete Psalms, Col 1 and Mt 24:1–35, and
Mk 11:20–29 in the registered eBible Clementine artifacts. The English
whole-context reading is not replaced by that more limited Latin comparison.

* **Jeremiah:** a letter from Jerusalem to the deportees in Babylon
  after Jechonias's departure (29:1–3). They must build, plant, raise
  families, seek and pray for their captor city's peace, and reject false
  promises of an early return. Chapter 28's two-year promise and 29's
  seventy-year horizon matter to *cogitationes pacis*. Chapter 30 retains
  judgment and promised healing: peace is not immediate immunity.
  The liturgical abbreviation does not cancel the omitted whole-heart
  seeking of 29:13 or the historical addressees.
* **Ps 43(44):** title assigns it to the sons of Core/Korah; it recalls
  ancestral deliverance and possession of the land, denies self-saving
  military power, and then laments faithful suffering and apparent
  abandonment. The Gradual's praise cannot be made evidence that believers
  never suffer. An ancestral remembered event is not the date or exact
  occasion of the poem.
* **Ps 84(85):** sons-of-Korah title. Restored captivity/remitted guilt
  is followed by a fresh plea for conversion and life, an attentive
  hearing of God's peace, and fruitfulness. Its movement explains why
  the Mass can sing restoration and still ask for healing. No exact
  topographical place of composition is supplied by the title.
* **Colossians:** the writer prays for recipients at Colossae (1:2),
  already commended for faith and love; knowledge must bear fruit,
  endure joyfully and thank the Father for rescue. The hymn of the
  Son (1:15–20), reconciliation and perseverance (1:21–23), the
  anti-deception warning of chapter 2, and the putting off/on and
  forgiveness of chapter 3 constrain “filled,” “worthy” and “translated.”
  Captivity belongs to the writer (4:3, 10, 18), not automatically to
  the recipients; Rome is the historical composition tradition, not
  a place named in this pericope.
* **Matthew:** Jesus leaves the Temple and sits on the Mount of Olives
  (24:1–3). The disciples ask about the buildings, his coming and the
  consummation. “Not yet the end” precedes the appointment; unknown
  day/hour and watchfulness follow it; chapter 25 develops responsible
  waiting, judgment and mercy. Judea, mountains, housetop, field, desert
  and inner rooms belong to the warning's scene and contrasts. They
  are not all places at which the discourse was spoken.
* **Mark:** entry into Jerusalem, fig tree and Temple action frame the
  withered-tree instruction; it occurs on the return journey from
  Bethany before the next Jerusalem entrance (11:12, 19–20, 27).
  No precise tree location is claimed. *Habete fidem Dei*, mountain,
  prayer and mutual forgiveness form the instruction (22–26).
  Communion is the liturgical application of v. 24, not the original
  narrative setting of Jesus' words.

Intertexts were checked separately, not added to the appointed inventory:
Dan 7:12–14; 9:20–27; 11–12 (including 11:31 and 12:11).
Desecration, persecution, saving the people, resurrection and Daniel's
own difficulty understanding belong to these contexts. No calculation of
a modern end date is drawn from their numbers. The “Son of man ... clouds”
of Mt 24:30 has the concrete Daniel 7 royal/judicial background. Other
cosmic/prophetic allusions may be mentioned only at the precision supported
by the checked commentators, not as newly researched independent dates.

### Actual close readings and disagreements

**Theophylact on the Communion.** The 1525 Basel printing by Andreas
Cratander, *Enarrationes in quatuor Evangelia*, in Johannes Oecolampadius's
Latin translation, was checked at *In Marcum* XI, fol. 69r–v. The Mark
11:24–25 lemma begins at the bottom of fol. 69r and the commentary ends
on 69v before the lemma for the return to Jerusalem. Theophylact reads
faith as reaching toward God with the whole heart. The petitioner,
united to God, prays ardently and entrusts himself wholly to him. In the
next verse he includes forgiving the brother who has offended the petitioner.
Bede explains the mountain and forgiveness from the surrounding verses,
and Aquinas explains what prayer does in the petitioner. Theophylact
adds an explanation from the appointed verse itself. His standing is
ecclesiastical writer under the author's standing inventory.

**Jerome.** PL 24 cols. 893–896, on 29:10–14, keeps the long historical
waiting against false prophecy; only God knows his peace-intent fully.
He explicitly asks why prayer is needed if the return is promised: it
makes the recipients disposed/worthy to receive, rather than supplying
information God lacks. The further spiritual application to final rest
is distinguished from the letter's own Babylonian reference. It supports
R1's patient hope and R2's changed recipients, not a prosperity guarantee.

**Augustine's Psalms.** Ps 43 §§5–8 is a genuine perfect-tense inference:
the future is so certain that the prophet says it as past, and the
following future praise confirms this. Past ancestral deeds and future
kingdom leave the martyrs' present affliction intact. Ps 84 §§2–4 likewise
reads the perfect tense prophetically: *Converte nos* shows the restoration
had not happened at the prophet's standpoint. Do not attribute the distinct
Christian present application of initial forgiveness and future consummation
to that temporal argument. Captivity is finally overcome when mortality is
overcome. Ps 129 asks
the hearer to cry rather than sink into despair; Christ is propitiation,
and the watch's morning can be read through his resurrection. Later
verses clarify the first two, but are not added to either chant.

**Chrysostom.** Colossians homily 2 explains why Paul prays for people
already praised: genuine fidelity is not exhaustive understanding;
practical fruit and enduring strength are still needed. The Father not
only gives an inheritance but makes people fit for it; rescue and transfer
are distinct benefits, not a mere change of label. The Matthew homilies
distinguish the Temple catastrophe and the final coming, explain urgent
flight by real vulnerability (including pregnancy/nursing), then use
public lightning, the cross and gathering to exclude secret claimants.
“This generation” is the generation of believers for the larger
eschatological reach, not a permission to schedule the end within a
single contemporary lifespan.

**Aquinas.** Col 1 lectiones 2–3 (native 87847–87848) expound 1:3–14:
the preceding thanksgiving and the appointed prayer respectively.
Lectio 4 (native 87849) expounds 1:15–17, beyond the appointed Epistle;
its account of the invisible Son, creation and conservation of all things
is used separately as doctrinal illumination of the Trinity Preface.
Col 1 lectio 3 says prayer raises us to God, not bends God's
will; knowledge, virtuous action and further knowledge belong together.
Patience bears adversity; longanimity bears delay; joy prevents their
reduction to sad necessity. On *dignos fecit* the worthiness itself is
God's gift, so no prior worthiness explains the first grace. “Lot”
excludes self-selection; transfer is understood with present deliverance
and future inheritance, not the elimination of remaining moral effort.
Matthew 24's lectiones record literal Roman/desecration explanations
alongside reported Antichrist/heresy and spiritual applications.
“Then” does not force immediate temporal succession. In lectio 3,
native 87397 (decoded derivative lines 1068–1072), Aquinas reports
Origen's distinction between a human's vulnerability considered in
himself and preservation by divine election, then offers those elect
only in appearance as an alternative. R2's warning against presuming
personal immunity is an editorial application of that argument.
Cosmic signs receive literal and mystical
explanations; heaven/earth's passing is change of condition, not simply
annihilation. The Church-generation reading is distinguished from a
contemporary-cohort reading limited to Jerusalem.

**Bellarmine.** Ps 43 native vv. 7–9 really says remembered rescues:
“as often as I have been rescued.” It must not be quoted as endorsing
Augustine's exclusively future force for that clause. His ensuing
persecution of the faithful prevents a simplistic temporal-success
interpretation. Ps 129's two depths are misery and heartfelt
acknowledgment; awareness of misery without mercy yields despair,
mercy without recognizing the wound neglects medicine. His
morning-to-night watch concerns every age and condition of life; this
does not deny Augustine's resurrection application. Final redemption
also removes punishment and danger of relapse, not merely one present
danger. His Davidic language is reception, not independent proof of
Psalm 129's authorship.

**Bede.** Native book III, not “book I” from the derivative's `{1.11}`
prefix. He does not deny God's power to move a mountain when need
requires; he also reads the mountain spiritually as demonic pride.
