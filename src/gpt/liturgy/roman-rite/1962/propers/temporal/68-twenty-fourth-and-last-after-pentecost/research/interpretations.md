# Two proposed readings of the complete Last-Sunday Mass

These are research arguments, not a finished study or homily. The object is
the universal 1962 formulary resolved for 22 November 2026 in `context.md`,
including its prescribed Trinity Preface. `propers/verified.md` gives the
complete appointed wording and the collation audit for its historical
English witnesses. `scope.md` and the bindings record the
checked editions, reading extents and access limits. Vulgate Psalm numbers
precede modern numbers.

Both constructions below are **editorial whole-formulary synthesis**.
Augustine, Chrysostom, Aquinas, Bede, Theophylact, Jerome and Bellarmine
interpret the particular Scriptures or doctrine cited. Fromage's 1883
chapter explains several parts of the received Mass. The five printed
chants, including the Introit on p. 503, supply wording evidence. The
opening note on p. 501 describes the recurrence of the chants. The
paragraph following the Introit expounds the Collect's petition for
grace-assisted action. Fromage's preceding chapter transmits Rupert's
reading of four shared chants. Theophylact, Rupert and Fromage contribute
as ecclesiastical writers. The carrying authors are named in each reading.

## R1. Deliverance is certain without being complete in the present

### Governing question and distinguishable answer

How can the Introit promise peace, the Epistle say that God has **already**
rescued and transferred us, and the Gradual sing **“Thou hast delivered
us,”** when the Gospel warns of such severe distress and the Mass ends
asking for a cure? Are destruction and delay evidence that the promise
has failed?

This reading answers with the distinction between a real present rescue
and its final consummation, not by denying either distress or deliverance.
Its controlling movement is through time: promise and accomplished
redemption sustain a still afflicted people until Christ publicly gathers
them. R2 instead asks what becomes of the praying person's will and
desires. R1's first practical consequence is **patient discrimination in
trial**, not the claim that the faithful can eliminate trial by praying
with sufficient intensity.

**Carrying eligible authors:** Augustine of Hippo (Father, canonized
Doctor) and John Chrysostom (Father, canonized Doctor). Each supplies an
indispensable inference below. Aquinas, Jerome and Bellarmine add checked
corroboration or a precise alternative, not honorary names. Fromage supplies
the direct whole-Mass liturgical reception.

### The consequential words and the Fathers' actual inferences

1. **Appointed difficulty: *Liberasti nos ... in Deo laudabimur tota die*
   (Gradual).** The completed rescue and future praise are followed, in
   the whole Ps 43(44), by lament over faithful people suffering defeat.
   Augustine, *Enarrationes*, Ps 43, §§5–8 (English NPNF heading XLIV), notices the
   shift of tense. The prophet can speak the future as already accomplished
   because God's promise makes it certain; the subsequent future praise
   shows that its complete realization is still to come. Present persecution
   is therefore not a contradiction of the Psalm or proof of divine
   fickleness. This is a claim about prophetic temporal force, not a
   denial that God acted for the ancestors.

   Bellarmine, Ps 43, native vv. 7–9, instead takes this clause as praise
   for **remembered rescues**. He still recognizes the faithful's ensuing
   present affliction. Preserve the disagreement: his inference grounds
   confidence by analogy with what God has done; Augustine grounds it in
   the certainty of what God will do. R1's thesis accommodates both grounds,
   but the author must not say they identify the same tense in the same way.
   If Bellarmine is followed here, change “the Gradual prophesies our future
   deliverance in the perfect tense” to “the Gradual recalls God's rescue;
   Augustine also hears prophetic certainty in its tense.”

2. **Appointed difficulty: *tribulatio magna ... propter electos
   breviabuntur dies illi* and *non praeteribit generatio haec, donec omnia
   haec fiant* (Mt 24:21–22,34).** Jesus is answering the disciples'
   connected questions after leaving the Temple; “then” does not force
   all the answers into a single consecutive episode.

   Chrysostom, *Homilies on Matthew* 75–77, first keeps the concrete
   Judean catastrophe and need of flight: the housetop, field and burdens
   of pregnancy/nursing make escape difficult. He does not make those
   afflicted morally worse because they have children. He then distinguishes
   the later coming from the earlier siege; Mt 24:23's “then” is not a
   requirement of immediate chronological succession. The final coming
   is public as lightning. In homily 76, §§2–3 on Mt 24:23–27, even
   impressive signs can come from deceivers who direct people to a
   particular place. Christ's glory will be seen everywhere at once
   and needs no herald.
   The hearer can refuse the summons to go looking for him.

   Augustine, *Quaestiones euangeliorum* I.38 on 24:23–27, takes east and
   west as the whole world reached by the Gospel from Jerusalem. The
   lightning is the Church's manifest authority throughout that world.
   “Here” and “there” are schisms that claim Christ for a region, and the
   chambers and desert are hidden gatherings of heretics. Augustine's
   hearer tests a claim to possess Christ against the Church's worldwide,
   public communion. Chrysostom's hearer tests a purported final advent
   against the universal, immediate visibility of Christ himself. R1
   follows Chrysostom for the depiction of the final coming. Augustine's
   answer treats this set of verses and supplies a different ground for
   discernment during the waiting.

   In homily 77, on 24:34, Chrysostom interprets
   “generation” as the generation of believers: the Church is not extinguished
   by the catastrophes he has just described. Its continuance is the
   relevant pledge, not a computed final date.

   Aquinas, *Super Matthaeum* 24, lectiones 1–3, reports the alternatives
   with care. Reading every clause solely as Jerusalem's destruction
   leaves too much unexplained; a contemporary-cohort meaning of
   “generation” is appropriate only within that restricted reference.
   The enduring believing generation explains the broader reach.
   Shortened days can concern the curtailing of disasters, not literally
   reducing the number of hours in a day. These are specific exegetical
   answers, not scientific forecasts. Their consequence is that the
   Lord promises preservation/gathering without promising that each
   disciple avoids suffering or death.

3. **Appointed difficulty: *eripuit ... transtulit ... in quo habemus
   redemptionem* (Col 1:13–14), yet strength for *omnem patientiam et
   longanimitatem* is still requested (1:11).** Chrysostom, *Colossians*
   homily 2, reads rescue **and** transfer as a double divine benefit,
   not simply escape from one oppressor into neutrality. The Father has
   made the recipients fit for the saints' inheritance and brought them
   into the Son's kingdom. Paul's continuing prayer does not deny this:
   grace received by faithful people is not a reason to stop asking for
   growth and strength. Aquinas, *Super Colossenses* 1, lectio 3, separates
   patience under adversity from longanimity during delay and notes the
   accompanying joy. R1 consequently refuses both despair (“nothing
   has happened”) and realized triumphalism (“nothing remains to endure”).

### Whole-Mass argument: promise, affliction, gathering and medicine

**Promise does not mean immediate return.** The Introit's *cogitationes
pacis ... reducam captivitatem* is spoken by the Lord, not an assurance
the congregation invents for itself. In Jer 29 it opposes false promises
of a quick return; the omitted verses require patient life and prayer in
Babylon and whole-hearted seeking. Jerome, *In Ieremiam* V, reads the
seventy-year wait in the unit beginning at 29:10 (PL 24 cols. 893–894).
He explains the gathering on that timetable in the opening of the next
unit, which begins within v. 14 (col. 894). The Introit compresses this
promise made to people who still had years to wait.

The attached *Benedixisti ... avertisti captivitatem Iacob* speaks restoration
in the completed tense. Augustine, Ps 84(85), especially §§2–4 within §§1–13,
does not infer that restoration had already happened for the prophet:
prophetic certainty speaks the future as past, and the continued
*Converte nos* proves that it remained future from that standpoint.
His account of captivity as sin and mortality also reaches final freedom
from death. The Christian assembly's application to forgiveness already
received and consummation still awaited is this reading's editorial
whole-Mass inference, supported directly by the Epistle's rescue and
transfer, not Augustine's explanation of the prophet's temporal standpoint.
Thus the **Collect's** prayer
*Excita ... fructum ... remedia maiora* belongs to R1's temporal movement.
People already called “faithful” need renewed wills to pursue fruit and
receive further remedies. It is not a request to earn the first grace
by their unassisted future performance. Fromage's direct Collect
commentary on p. 503 (PDF 529) makes this grace-assisted growth explicit.

**Thanksgiving and a cry can coexist.** The **Epistle's** already-given
rescue is followed by a **Gradual** of certain/remembered rescue and an
**Alleluia** which cries *De profundis*. Their sequence need not be
ironic: the already redeemed can still cry out from mortality and sin's
consequences. Augustine, Ps 129(130), §§1–8, says that the still-crying
person has not given up; not crying is worse. The appointed two verses
do not yet name the watch or abundant redemption, but his whole-Psalm
reasoning shows why the depths are compatible with hope. The Church
sings the cry with Alleluias without declaring affliction unreal.

The **Gospel** makes the danger concrete before promising its end.
Augustine I.37 reads the winter weather and sabbath travel restriction
in v. 20 as real hindrances to flight and offers a further reading
about being caught up in temporal grief or pleasure on that day. For
Chrysostom the lightning depicts the visibility of the final coming.
At v. 28 he takes the eagles as the company
of angels, martyrs and all the saints. In *Super Matthaeum* 24, lectio 3,
Aquinas reads the gathering as a further sign of the manifest coming
and connects the body with the signs of Christ's Passion.

Augustine I.42 locates the body in heaven, where Christ took the human
body he had assumed. Christ speaks of a corpse because he is about to
die. The eagles are spiritual people fed as from his body when they
imitate his Passion and humility. Fromage, p. 510 (PDF 536), connects
reception of Christ's Blood with the renewal of the eagles' youth, and
takes their hunger after exile as desire for the Body they will see at
the eternal Pasch. On the same page he also speaks of sharing Christ's
suffering and humiliation. R1 follows Chrysostom on the gathering and
preserves the difference between Augustine's explanation of the feeding
image through imitation and Fromage's Eucharistic application. The
Gospel ends with Christ's assurance that his words endure when heaven
and earth pass away.

**The still-waiting offer and receive.** The **Offertory** returns to
*De profundis* and actually repeats its opening. Now the cry accompanies
the offering; the **Secret** asks that accepted offerings and prayers
lead to hearts turned toward God and passage from earthly cravings
to heavenly desires. The pastoral application is not a ban on responsible
earthly work. It is release from treating present possessions or survival
as the final good—the kind of attachment the Gospel's urgent flight
exposes. Fromage prints the Offertory's repetition on p. 513 and directs
his following comment to the Secret's turning of desire. The connection
between the chant, the prayer and the Gospel's warning is the editor's.

The prescribed **Trinity Preface** identifies the final good as the one
living God: three persons, one essence, equal majesty. The earthly
congregation already joins angels and archangels in praise through
Christ, while not yet seeing as the blessed see. Augustine,
*De Trinitate* III preface §3 and III.10.21/III.11, distinguishes the
one unchangeable God from mutable visible manifestations and rejects
inferiority from the Son's/Spirit's mission. This doctrinal analogue
prevents R1 from treating cosmic instability as instability in God or
the Son as a lesser final refuge. The prayer's actual angelic conclusion
and Sanctus junction support present participation in unending praise;
no special historical origin for this Preface is asserted.

The **Communion's** *credite quia accipietis, et fiet vobis* can thus
encourage confidence before final sight without promising immediate
fulfilment of every temporal preference. The specific contextual check
belongs more fully to R2: Bede's faith working by love and mutual
forgiveness, and Aquinas's purpose of prayer, bar that distortion.
The **Postcommunion's** *medicationis dono curetur* says why hope
remains an active dependence after receiving: remaining defects need
cure. Augustine, *De Trinitate* III.4.10, names Eucharistic reception
for spiritual health; Fromage p. 513 (PDF 539) interprets this actual prayer as
continuing medicine. Sacramental nourishment is real without being the
immediate abolition of suffering, concupiscence or mortality.

### R1's own four senses

* **Literal:** Jeremiah promises the deportees eventual return;
  the Psalms alternate rescue, praise and petition; Paul prays for
  already-believing Colossians; Jesus warns the disciples of concrete
  danger and distinguishes a public gathering. The actual prayers ask
  renewed fruit, converted desire and sacramental cure.
* **Allegorical:** the Father's transfer into the Son's kingdom and
  redemption through his Blood inaugurate the deliverance to which
  captivity/return can be spiritually related. Christ's gathered body,
  not a secret counterfeit messiah, receives the promise. This fulfilment
  does not erase Israel's historical return.
* **Moral:** persevere joyfully through delay, refuse seductive private
  assurances, take prudent action in danger, and keep praying/receiving
  healing instead of treating trial as divine abandonment.
* **Anagogical:** final gathering, resurrection and enduring praise
  complete what grace begins. Christ's words outlast the mutable world;
  do not turn the consummation into a date or a guaranteed escape from
  personal death.

### Strongest alternative and boundary

A Jerusalem-only reading can explain flight, pregnancy, winter and
Sabbath very concretely; it must be taught before widening the horizon.
It does not, in the principal checked Fathers' judgment, exhaust public
universal coming, final gathering and the enduring believing generation.
Conversely, an all-future allegory loses the historical force of flight.
R1 holds the distinction rather than collapsing it. It also preserves
Augustine/Bellarmine's real tense difference instead of manufacturing a
single patristic reading of the Gradual.

## R2. Confident prayer asks God to change its own petitioner

### Governing question and distinguishable answer

The Communion says *quidquid orantes petitis ... fiet vobis*. Does faith
give the petitioner control over events? Why then ask God to stir the
wills of his own faithful, turn hearts already making offerings, and
cure defects after Communion? Does *dignos nos fecit* mean a prior
worthiness explains the gift?

This reading follows agency and the object of desire, not primarily the
timeline of catastrophe. Prayer's assurance is not the crowning of an
unchanged, self-sufficient will. God rescues, makes fit and enables
action; prayer raises the recipient to receive; faith acts in love
and forgiveness. The concrete consequence is that a prayer for another
person's good cannot be separated from forgiving him. Asking for cure
after Communion confesses the need for transformation, not the failure
of the sacrament.

**Carrying eligible authors:** Thomas Aquinas (canonized Doctor) and
Bede the Venerable (Father, canonized Doctor). Aquinas supplies the
causality of grace and prayer. Bede explains effective faith and
forgiveness in the Markan context. Theophylact's direct reading of
v. 24 is supporting evidence from an ecclesiastical writer. Chrysostom
contributes a distinct confirmation on *dignos fecit*. Augustine and
Bellarmine further clarify captivity and medicine.

### The consequential words and two principal inferences

1. **Appointed difficulty: *qui dignos nos fecit in partem sortis
   sanctorum* (Col 1:12).** Did God find worthy people and then reward
   their prior natural qualification? Aquinas, *Super Colossenses* 1,
   lectio 3 (native 87848), distinguishes worthiness as the very effect
   of grace. The text says **made** worthy. Therefore the prior cause
   of grace cannot be that same recipient's already-established
   worthiness. He also explains the lot: the inheritance is not
   self-allotted by an individual's choice, although God is not
   acting at random.

   Chrysostom, Colossians homily 2 on 1:12–14, observes that giving
   a royal place to someone not fitted for it would not be the whole
   benefit. God both fits and grants, both rescues and transfers.
   The consequence is stronger than “God helps good people”: the gift
   includes a new capacity and standing. Yet the preceding *ut ambuletis
   digne Deo ... fructificantes* shows a real ensuing life, not an
   inert label. Grace's priority and fruitful cooperation are not
   alternatives. This inference distinguishes R2's account of changed
   persons from R1's account of delayed consummation.

2. **Appointed difficulty: *quidquid ... credite quia accipietis,
   et fiet vobis* (Communion).** Detached from Mk 11:22–26, this can
   sound like a technique for obtaining whatever one strongly imagines.
   Bede, *In Marci evangelium expositio* III, Mk 11 (registered derivative
   lines 338–339), does not separately quote v. 24; he interprets its
   immediate instruction about faith, mountain and forgiving while
   praying. That exact native scope must survive the citation.

   Bede allows an actual miracle when necessary, adducing the report
   about Gregory Thaumaturgus. He reads the mountain spiritually as
   the devil who exalts himself in pride, and identifies effective
   faith as *quae per dilectionem operatur*. Those still short of such
   perfection should ask for pardon. In Bede's reading of v. 25,
   they can be confident of receiving it if they first forgive
   those who have offended them.

   Theophylact, *In Marcum* XI, Basel 1525, fol. 69r–v, interprets the
   appointed v. 24 directly. Faith extends the whole heart toward God.
   In ardent prayer the petitioner is united to God and gives himself
   wholly to him. Theophylact then brings in pardoning others from v. 25.
   Bede identifies a concrete consequence of charity in the
   surrounding context. Theophylact's direct reading explains what
   gives the petitioner's confidence its shape in v. 24 itself.
   Both locate the explicit injunction to forgive in the next verse.

   Aquinas, Col 1 lectio 3, explains why petition matters even though
   God knows what is needed. Prayer raises the petitioner to God.
   Jerome, *In Ieremiam* V, in the unit headed “Vers. 10 seqq.”
   (PL 24 cols. 893–894), likewise finds that prayer disposes the exiles
   to receive a return already promised. R2 reads confidence as
   participation in God's good purpose. Forgiving the
   neighbor is one concrete act of that participation.

3. **Appointed difficulty: *Excita ... voluntates; ad te corda
   converte; quidquid ... vitiosum ... curetur*.** These are addressed
   to God by believers, offerers and communicants, not simply by
   people outside the Church. Their grammar attributes the stirring,
   turning and curing to divine action while asking for the faithful's
   active fruit and changed desire. Fromage's Collect comment on
   p. 503 concerns grace-assisted fruit. His separate remarks on
   p. 513 ask for the Secret's change of desire and describe the
   Postcommunion as cure of what remains faulty. The editor brings
   those three discussions into one argument about divine agency
   and the petitioner, with Aquinas and Bede supplying the carrying
   exegesis.

### Whole-Mass argument: hearing, willing, offering and being cured

**God's peaceful purpose elicits a real petition.** The **Introit**
does not say God will acquiesce in every existing desire. He declares
his own thoughts of peace, invites invocation and promises restoration.
Jeremiah's omitted 29:13 asks whole-hearted seeking; its inclusion
in the context, not an invented addition to the chant, protects
the promise from becoming a formula indifferent to the petitioner.
Jerome's question about why a promised gift must be prayed for has
particular force here. The Psalm verse *avertisti captivitatem Iacob*
then receives Augustine's spiritual captivity reading: the captivity
from which people need freedom includes sin, not only an external
constraint on their preferences.

The **Collect's** stirred wills produce *fructum*, with further
*remedia* still needed. This is a fitting prayer before Paul's
**Epistle**, in which knowledge of God's will leads to walking
worthily, pleasing him, bearing fruit and growing in knowledge.
Aquinas's sequence knowledge–action–further knowledge prevents
“being filled” from meaning sterile possession of information.
Chrysostom's explanation of continued prayer for the already-faithful
likewise prevents complacency. Neither author erases personal
responsibility; both locate its fruitful possibility within divine
benefaction. *Patientia* and *longanimitas cum gaudio* name the
form this new life can take when circumstances do not oblige it.

The **Gradual** promises *et in nomine tuo confitebimur in saecula*.
In R2, praise for God's rescue is thanksgiving rather than self-credit.
Bellarmine's remembered-rescue
reading makes this especially straightforward: what happened through
God calls for God's praise, not confidence in one's own bow or sword
(the whole Psalm's preceding contrast). Augustine's prophetic
reading is retained as R1's fuller account, not contradicted here.
The **Alleluia** cries *De profundis clamavi ad te, Domine:
Domine, exaudi orationem meam*. It ends there, without the
continuous Psalm's ears clause. R2 understands this petition from
the depths as dependence on mercy, not bargaining strength.
Bellarmine, Ps 129, links acknowledgment of one's misery to trust in
mercy; awareness of the wound without mercy despairs, while trust
that refuses to acknowledge the wound declines the medicine.
This inference illuminates why the Collect asks remedies and the
Postcommunion asks healing, though Bellarmine does not comment on
those orations.

**Truthful faith does not follow every attractive claim.** The
**Gospel's** false Christs and false prophets, even with great
signs, test whether the will wants Christ or merely reassurance.
Aquinas, *Super Matthaeum* 24, lectio 3, native 87397
(decoded derivative lines 1068–1072), reports Origen's distinction
between human vulnerability to deception and preservation by divine
election; he also gives the alternative of those elect only in
appearance. R2 infers that apparent members must not presume
immunity to deception. This does not replace literal Judean danger
with a moral allegory. R1 gives the historical/final distinction its
fullest home. Augustine I.38 supplies a distinct reason for R2's present
discernment. A group that claims Christ for its own territory or hides its
teaching from the Church's public life contradicts the worldwide reach
Augustine sees in the lightning. Aquinas in the same lectio explicitly
attributes to Augustine the criteria of consistency across places and
public teaching, and also reads the lightning as Christ's manifest coming.
Christ's abiding *verba mea* give the disciple grounds to judge what the
claimant teaches. In v. 32 Augustine I.39 reads the tender fig branch as
human beings advancing through faith to spiritual fruit and manifesting
their adoption. That gives R2 an analogy for the Collect's prayer for
fruitfulness.

**An accepted offering is accompanied by the conversion of its
offerer.** At the **Offertory**, *De profundis* is cried again and
its opening repeated. The editorial inference is persistence in
dependence rather than acquisition of bargaining power by bringing
gifts. It becomes explicit in the **Secret's** own words:
*oblationibus precibusque susceptis ... ad te corda converte*.
Acceptance and conversion belong in one petition. *A terrenis
cupiditatibus liberati, ad caelestia desideria transeamus* asks
not simply that existing cravings be fulfilled in a more luxurious
place but that the direction of desire change. L's historical
“enjoyments” is not permission to obscure the Latin *desideria*.
Fromage p. 513 directly discusses this passage from earthly to
heavenly desire.

The **Trinity Preface** gives a concrete answer to “toward whom?”
The prayer is thanksgiving through Christ to the holy Father,
confessing Father, only-begotten Son and Holy Spirit in one divine
essence and equal majesty. It does not distribute one part of
salvation to three unequal beings. Chrysostom's Colossians homily
2 describes the Father's action and the Son's kingdom without
making their beneficence competitive. Aquinas, *Super Colossenses* 1,
lectio 4/native 87849, expounds Col 1:15–17, beyond the appointed
Epistle: the Son is the Father's invisible image, and all things are
created in and through him and conserved in him. His account is
checked doctrinal illumination of the Preface, not direct commentary
on it. Augustine, *De Trinitate* III preface §3, rules out
inferiority from being sent. The mind's conversion is therefore
toward the real triune God, not a vague spiritual satisfaction;
joining the angels' praise is an act of grateful worship.

At **Communion**, confidence follows this offered prayer of
conversion. Theophylact reads the verse's believing petitioner as
giving himself wholly to God. Bede's account of forgiving a neighbor
shows what the surrounding Gospel asks of a person praying for pardon.
The **Postcommunion** then asks that whatever remains *vitiosum*
in the mind be cured by the received mysteries' medicinal gift.
It does not claim that the worshipper must have no defect left
before asking for medicine, or that receiving mechanically
endorses an unchanged will.

Augustine, *De Trinitate* III.4.10, explicitly relates the sacramental
food/drink to the Lord's Passion and spiritual health through God's
invisible operation. Fromage p. 513 (PDF 539) relates the actual concluding
prayer to the cure of remaining faults. Together with the Latin,
these give a concrete conclusion: the gift one confidently asks
for includes release from one's own defects. No diagnosis of an
individual's moral state, assurance of automatic perseverance, or
claim about Communion without repentance follows.

### R2's own four senses

* **Literal:** divine peace invites prayer; Paul asks knowledge to
  bear fruit and thanks God for making recipients worthy; Jesus'
  Markan instruction links faith, prayer and forgiveness. The
  actual three orations ask God to stir, turn and heal the faithful.
* **Allegorical:** the Father's transfer into the Son's kingdom
  frees from sin's power; redemption through the Son's Blood makes
  fruitful membership possible. The Church offers and receives in
  Christ, confessing the one Trinity rather than its own sufficiency.
* **Moral:** ask confidently while seeking God's will, forgive the
  person against whom one prays, resist flattering counterfeits,
  and submit one's cravings and remaining faults to conversion.
  Do not make another person's affliction proof of faithlessness.
* **Anagogical:** heavenly desire tends to the saints' inheritance
  and unending triune praise. Full healing and vision, not
  unlimited possession of earthly wishes, complete the prayer's
  movement toward God.

### Strongest alternative and boundary

The bare Communion wording can appear to promise any requested temporal
outcome. A merely psychological reinterpretation would evade its
assertion that God really acts; Bede's allowance of a needed miracle
prevents that evasion. The verse's actual setting nevertheless
requires faith in God and forgiveness. Aquinas locates prayer's
change in us without denying the providential gift. Neither saint
gives this study a general algorithm specifying which temporal
requests must be granted on demand. Their complementary arguments
support a moral/theological reading, not clinical explanations of
unanswered prayer.

## Concrete comparison for the future studies

### Rupert's reading of the shared chants

Rupert of Deutz, *De divinis officiis* XII.23 (PL 170, cols. 329–330),
anticipates at the entrance what he expects Christ to do for the remnant
of Israel at the end of the world. The peace clause from Jer 29:11
promises welcome at Christ's table. The opening of Ps 43:8 supplies the
thanksgiving of those freed from spiritual captivity. The opening of
Ps 129:1 voices Joseph's brothers' plea in Gen 50:17. The opening of
Mk 11:24 answers in the voice of Joseph, who reassures and promises to
feed them (Gen 50:19–21). At the end the speakers and addressees matter.
Penitents ask for pardon, and the one they wronged answers with care.

**R1** also connects the peaceful intention, thanks, distress and
reassurance. Rupert's Church gives thanks now for a deliverance whose
recipients will give thanks in the future. He makes the Gradual the
future remnant's thanksgiving. Augustine explains how a prophet can
speak of future rescue as past. Bellarmine reads the same past tense
as a memory of deliverance. These are three distinct inferences. R1
sets the acclamation and renewed Offertory cry in the time of a Church
already rescued and still awaiting the gathering described in this
Mass's Gospel. Rupert's specific eschatological subject is Israel's
future reception at Christ's table. He finds it prefigured in Joseph
and signified by his Gospel's five loaves, which he reads as the five
books of Moses opened to Israel. The Jeremiah lesson he pairs with that
Gospel is Jer 23:7–8, about a new exodus. This gives a parallel way of
joining promise and thanksgiving across time, with its own lesson and
Gospel (John 6).

**R2** also hears the last two chants as related petition and answer.
Rupert makes a particular pardon and reconciliation legible through
Joseph and his brothers. In R2 the Secret itself asks that the
offerers' hearts turn toward God. Theophylact explains the faith of
the one praying in Mk 11:24. Bede follows the injunction in Mk 11:25
to the petitioner's obligation to forgive. Rupert's narrative gives
the pardon-seekers a voice and puts the forgiving answer in the mouth
of the one petitioned. This complements the question of what needs to
change in the person who prays. It supplies neither R2's account of
the Secret nor its inference from the Marcan context.

Fromage's Twenty-third-Sunday quotation in Shepherd's English (Duffy
XI, pp. 487–489) transmits this sequence, and his Last-Sunday p. 501
names the earlier explanation. The study's two arguments keep their
own carrying authors and their appointed Colossians and Matthew. The
Joseph comparison stays in research because the older lessons and
Rupert's particular eschatological application are needed to account
for its force and limits. The printed Introit on p. 503 and Fromage's
following Collect commentary retain their separate roles.

Aquinas reads *fecit* causally, Chrysostom says God fits as well as gives. Thanksgiving displaces self-sufficiency and enables responsible fruit. |
| *Liberasti* followed by *De profundis* | The change from praise to lament preserves the interval before full deliverance; Augustine's prophetic use of the perfect tense and Bellarmine's remembered rescue remain distinct options. Endure without calling God unfaithful. | Rescue elicits praise of God; misery honestly confessed remains capable of mercy. Ask for medicine rather than claiming that gratitude eliminates the need for it. |
| *fulgur / generatio haec / verba mea non praeteribunt* | How will the faithful recognize Christ's final coming while enduring deception? For Chrysostom, Christ's glory will appear to all at once. The believing generation endures through catastrophe. | How can believers judge competing claims to possess Christ now? For Augustine I.38, the worldwide Church's public authority exposes claims that confine Christ to a region or a hidden group. Discernment is an aspect of converted willing. |
| *quidquid ... fiet vobis* | Confidence is compatible with waiting and incomplete visible fulfilment. Present nourishment is not a guarantee of temporal security. | Bede's faith working through love and forgiveness, with Aquinas's prayer raising us to God, prevents a technique of control. Forgive concretely; ask for the healing of the petitioner. |
| *remedia / curetur* | Why medicine after an accomplished rescue? Grace is real but its consummation is future. Do not infer abandonment from remaining weakness. | What does God grant? A will capable of fruit, a heart turned from cravings, a mind cured of defects. Do not defend those defects as conditions God must accommodate. |

The readings share God's prior action, Christ's saving Blood, truthful
prayer and final hope. Their explanations are not interchangeable:
R1 would lose its argument if suffering necessarily proved that
deliverance had never begun; R2 would lose its argument if efficacy
meant leaving the petitioner's will and relationships unchanged.
Neither entailment is required by the checked texts.

For the concise study, interleave these answers at the actual paired
questions rather than summarize two sets of generic headings. Give the
siege/final-coming distinction, Gradual tense dispute and “this
generation” their fullest home in R1. Compare the referents of the
lightning and the implications for discernment where that image first
appears. Give *dignos fecit*, the faith and forgiveness context of
Communion and the three divine-action verbs their fullest home in R2.
Cross-reference shared doctrinal
explanations instead of duplicating them.

For the later homily, a defensible single argument for this adult
assembly is R2's: the confidence to ask includes the courage to be
changed, made concrete by forgiving a person one resents and asking
for sacramental healing. This is a research proposal, not drafted
speech or an instruction to combine the two homiletic arcs. R1 remains
available if authoring chooses patient hope under deceptive assurances
instead. In either case, never make private eschatological predictions,
attribute collective guilt to Jews, or diagnose another sufferer's faith.
