\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}

\begin{massbox}{Governing thesis: mercy opens the ear by reforming the petitioner}
The formulary does not oppose mercy to judgment. It asks mercy to train desire, receives Israel's history as Christian admonition, meets Christ's warning inside his tears, and moves through sweet instruction and Eucharistic memorial toward purification and unity.
\end{massbox}

\noindent\emph{Reading order.} These stages follow the appointed sequence. They are the guide's source-grounded synthesis, not a claim that one historical author designed the complete formulary.

\subsection*{Mercy forms the prayer it hears\enspace\properrefs{Int., Coll., All.}}

Psalm 53 confesses God as helper while danger remains. The Collect then makes a demanding request: if petitioners are to receive what they desire, God must first make them ask what pleases him. Mercy is not divine indulgence toward every impulse; it is the grace by which desire learns truth. The Alleluia returns this dependence to God rather than licensing private vengeance.

\subsection*{Israel's history warns the baptized Church\enspace\properrefs{Ep., Grad.}}

Paul says the wilderness events were written \emph{for our correction}. His grammatical target is the Christian assembly at Corinth, not Jews as a condemned out-group. The Gradual's wonder before God's name and majesty answers the warning, ``let anyone who thinks that he stands take heed,'' with creaturely praise rather than superiority.

\newpage

\subsection*{The visitor weeps before he judges\enspace\properrefs{Gosp., Off.}}

Jesus approaches his own people's city as a Jewish teacher, weeps, names hidden peace, foretells siege, and calls the Temple back to prayer. Cyril stresses Christ's saving compassion; Gregory turns the warning toward complacent Christian hearers. Both authors also develop anti-Jewish or replacement claims. Those historical polemics are named and rejected rather than allowed to control the Gospel. The Offertory's sweet judgments train the hearer under judgment; they do not furnish a weapon against another people.

\subsection*{Memorial bears fruit as abiding, cleansing, and unity\enspace\properrefs{Sec., Comm., Postcomm.}}

The Secret's \latin{quoties} says that whenever the memorial of the sacrifice is celebrated, redemption's work is enacted. The historical Passion is not repeated; Christ acts sacramentally. John joins eating and drinking to mutual abiding, while the final prayer supplies an ecclesial test of reception: purification and unity, not a private experience alone.

\begin{threestable}{Movement}{Gift received}{Controlling limit}
\textbf{Instruction} & God's judgments delight and warn the servant. & Sweetness is not flattery, and correction does not authorize coercion.\\
\textbf{Memorial} & The once-for-all saving work becomes sacramentally present and operative. & The historical crucifixion is not repeated.\\
\textbf{Communion} & Christ abides in the recipient and forms one people. & Reception is neither magic nor a guarantee of felt consolation.\\
\end{threestable}

\begin{studybox}{A necessary historical and theological correction}
Luke's destruction oracle has often been recruited into anti-Jewish and supersessionist polemic. The text places Jesus within Israel, weeping over Jerusalem and teaching in its Temple; Paul expressly turns Israel's history into correction of baptized Christians. The guide rejects inherited claims of collective, hereditary, or uniquely Jewish guilt and refuses to make the Church immune from the same summons to repentance.
\end{studybox}

\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

\subsection*{A prayer against distorted prayer\enspace\properrefs{Int., Coll., All.}}

The Introit's speaker does not pretend self-sufficiency: God helps, sustains the soul, judges in truth, and saves by his name. Augustine reads the psalm's helper as one who works within the servant rather than replacing willing action (\emph{Exposition on Psalm 54}, 4--5, 8--9). The Collect makes the same relation interior. It does not merely add a correct object to an unchanged appetite; it asks God to make the petitioners seek what is pleasing.

Augustine's \emph{Letter 130 to Proba}, \S\S~15--18, 21--29, supplies doctrinal illumination of the Collect, not evidence for its origin or reception. He uses the Lord's Prayer to discipline desire and gather right petitions within the form Christ gave; he also treats God's refusal of a harmful request, or delay in granting a good one, as mercy that corrects and enlarges desire. Read beside the Collect, this makes asking what pleases God a prayer for desire itself to be schooled, not a claim that every sincerely wanted object should be granted.

Bellarmine reads the Introit's ``behold'' as a turn from plea to sudden, present assurance, then carries that confidence into willing praise. Aquinas later reuses the same clause while Joseph is troubled in Matthew 1: divine help is already ready before the human servant can see the resolution. Bellarmine begins Psalm 58 with David's literal mortal peril and then widens its rescue petition to any just person in grave danger. These later moves preserve action within dependence; Aquinas's reuse does not become direct Psalm commentary, and Bellarmine's generalization names no modern enemy.

That order disciplines two opposite errors. Prayer is not self-authentication, as though intensity made any desire good. Nor is dependence passivity, as though the one helped no longer asks or acts. Psalm 58's rescue petition likewise entrusts the enemy and judgment to God. Augustine's exposition contains historically anti-Jewish speculation in its later development; that polemic is rejected. The usable point is narrower: rescue is requested from pride and violence without the sufferer assuming God's judgment as a private possession.

\begin{threestable}{Phrase}{Source-grounded force}{Limit}
\textbf{God helps me} & Divine aid sustains an acting, praying servant. & Grace is not a substitute for responsible action.\\
\textbf{Ask what pleases God} & Mercy reforms the desiring subject before granting the object. & The prayer does not promise every request.\\
\textbf{Deliver me} & Judgment and rescue are entrusted to God. & Lament does not license retaliation or identify a modern enemy.\\
\end{threestable}

\subsection*{A warning addressed to ``us''\enspace\properrefs{Ep., Grad.}}

Paul recalls craving, idolatry, sexual sin, testing, and murmuring after calling the sea, cloud, food, and drink figures for the Corinthian baptized. Chrysostom stresses that the catalogue destroys complacency: one who thinks he stands must take heed (\emph{Homily 23 on First Corinthians}). In the next homily he reads verse 13 as consolation within real struggle: temptation remains human, God is faithful, and endurance has a divinely supplied outcome or way through.

Aquinas insists that the wilderness events truly occurred and were written for Christian correction; he distinguishes failures of heart, action, and speech, then treats temptation, consent, ready divine help, grace, and constancy rather than autonomous endurance (\emph{Super I Corinthios} X, lects.~2--3). On the Gradual, his \emph{Super Psalmo 8} reads the Lord's admirable name as divine excellence manifested throughout the earth while majesty rises above the heavens. Bellarmine similarly moves from creation's beauty to the Creator's admirable name and insists that divine magnificence exceeds the created world. The Psalm therefore answers presumption with creaturely praise, not worthlessness.

This is not the aphorism that God never gives a person more suffering than that person can handle. Paul's subject is temptation to sin; the promise rests on God's fidelity, not autonomous strength. It neither measures every trauma against an individual's resources nor makes failure proof that the sufferer lacked faith. Psalm 8 intensifies the correction by holding human smallness and entrusted dignity beneath God's majestic name.

\begin{studybox}{Israel, figure, and the Church}
Paul does not erase Israel or declare the Church naturally superior. He tells Christians that Israel's wilderness failures occurred as figures and were written for \emph{our} correction. Christian figural reading is valid only while it preserves the historical people and lets the warning judge the reader. It becomes supersessionist when Israel is reduced to failure and the Church imagines itself beyond the same sins.
\end{studybox}

\subsection*{Tears, visitation, and the house of prayer\enspace\properrefs{Gosp.}}

Luke holds warning and compassion in one scene. Jesus sees Jerusalem and weeps before speaking of siege. Cyril takes the tears as disclosure of compassion and the will to save; Gregory turns the city's blindness into a warning for hearers who rest complacently in present ease. These bounded insights were checked in their direct works. Their anti-Jewish and replacement developments are historical reception evidence, not conclusions retained by this guide.

Anthony of Padua makes a specifically medieval spiritual move: he reads Jerusalem as an image of the soul and Christ's tears as contrition over virtues brought to ruin. That application turns the warning toward the Christian hearer, but it supplements rather than replaces the historical city, its Jewish people, or their suffering.

The Temple action also requires precision. Jesus cites Isaiah's house of prayer and Jeremiah's robbers' den, expels sellers and buyers, and then teaches daily. The critique is prophetic and intramural, spoken by a Jew within Israel's sanctuary. Christians may receive it as judgment on worship corrupted by exploitation, but not as proof that Israel was discarded or that violence in sacred spaces is authorized.

The word \emph{visitation} carries both gift and reckoning. Failure to recognize peace is grave precisely because the visitor comes near enough to weep and teach. The Christian liturgy cannot repeat the oracle as a triumph over Jerusalem; it must ask where Christ's peace, poor, prayer, and teaching remain unrecognized within the Church.

\subsection*{Judgments sweeter than honey\enspace\properrefs{Off.}}

Psalm 18 moves from the heavens' witness to the Lord's instruction. Augustine reads its right judgments as ordering the inner person, and their sweetness as a delight exceeding bodily sweetness (\emph{Exposition on Psalm 19}, 9--12). Yet the appointed span also includes warning and the servant's keeping. Delight is not approval of whatever the hearer already wants; it is appetite schooled by truth.

Aquinas's \emph{Super Psalmo 18} holds the sequence together: right judgments direct action, the command enlightens, chaste filial fear endures, and honeyed sweetness remains joined to warning and observance. Bellarmine likewise moves through good conscience, illumination, filial fear, value beyond gold, sweetness beyond honey, and the servant's experienced fruit in keeping the judgments. Neither lets the honey image float free of the word that searches the servant.

Placed after the Temple scene, the Offertory prevents two simplifications. Judgment is not merely punitive, since the servant delights in it; sweetness is not merely comforting, since the same word searches and warns. The juxtaposition can support patient reform of worship and economic life, but it cannot establish that the psalmist predicted Luke's scene or that liturgical sweetness excuses coercive correction.

\subsection*{The enacted memorial and the unity it seeks\enspace\properrefs{Sec., Comm., Postcomm.}}

The Secret's syntax is causal: the Church asks worthily to frequent these mysteries \emph{because} whenever this sacrifice is commemorated, the work of redemption is enacted. The prayer gives sacramental density to memorial without multiplying Calvary. Its subject is God's saving action celebrated in the Church, not the community manufacturing redemption by repetition.

Augustine's \emph{City of God} X.5--6 supplies a doctrinal grammar for the Secret: visible sacrifice is the sign of interior sacrifice, true sacrifice orders the person to God, and the whole redeemed city is offered ecclesially through its high priest. Aquinas's \emph{Summa theologiae} III, q.~83, a.~1, then distinguishes the once-for-all Passion from its sacramental representation: the Eucharistic celebration memorializes that one historical sacrifice and applies its fruit to those who celebrate it; it is not a second historical immolation. These are doctrinal illuminations of the Secret's sign, memorial, and ecclesial action, not evidence for the prayer's origin or reception.

Augustine's \emph{Tractate 26 on John}, 17--19, insists that eating and drinking are inseparable from abiding; he also connects the sacrament's sign of unity to incorporation in Christ. Chrysostom's \emph{Homily 47 on John} emphasizes that Christ gives himself and draws communicants into life. Aquinas's \emph{Super Ioannem} VI, lect.~7, makes the later scholastic move explicit: sacramental reception is ordered to union with Christ, mutual abiding, and the life received from him, not to a momentary bodily transaction. The Postcommunion states the formulary's fruit with unusual economy: purification and unity. Personal cleansing that hardens division contradicts the prayer's direction; institutional unity without conversion empties the other petition.

\textbf{Communion} & Christ's flesh and blood are received. & Mutual abiding. & Not a momentary transaction or private possession.\\

\subsection*{Saint Anne's admitted prayer triad on 26 July 2026\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Sec., Postcomm.}}

This branch is an ordinary commemoration, not a second Mass formulary. At Low Mass and the conventual Mass, Saint Anne's Collect recalls the grace by which she became mother of the Mother of God's only-begotten Son and asks the aid of her patronage. Her Secret asks that the present sacrifices, through her intercession, profit both devotion and salvation. After Communion, her prayer begins from nourishment by the heavenly sacraments and asks arrival at eternal salvation. Each petition remains subordinate to the Sunday prayer in its liturgical position.

At a nonconventual sung Mass the triad is omitted. The feast is not transferred, and neither Saint Anne's Matthew~13 Gospel nor her chants, color, or other proper texts enter the Sunday celebration. These limits belong to the actual 1962 occurrence; they are not optional editorial preferences.

\item \textbf{Gospel, Luke~19:42 --- Constance Holme, \emph{The Things Which Belong---} (1925).} Holme's title lifts the unfinished cadence of ``the things which belong unto thy peace.'' Within the novel, domestic and marital conflict eventually turns the phrase toward what truly belongs to one woman's peace. The biblical lament becomes a literary test of costly recognition rather than a decorative title.

\item \textbf{Gospel, Luke~19:44 --- Washburn ``A'' Mill memorial, Minneapolis.} Documentation for the mill records the 1878 flour-dust explosion and a memorial inscription adapting ``not one stone upon another.'' Siege language is redirected to industrial catastrophe and public remembrance. The inscription does not interpret Jerusalem or identify industrial death with divine punishment.

A prophetic Temple accusation becomes a dangerous parliamentary insult; the anecdote is political afterlife, not an endorsement of Jenkins's cause.

\section*{The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities}

\noindent\emph{These exploratory proposals arose in an AI-assisted editorial process. They are attributed to none of the cited authorities and claim no historical compositional intent. Each identifies its anchors and limit.}

\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{The way through temptation may begin as a changed desire.\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Ep.}}
\emph{Classification: near analogue located.} The Collect asks God to form what petitioners seek; Paul promises divine fidelity and a sustainable outcome within temptation. Read together, grace does not merely open an exit outside the desiring person but can reform the desire that made the temptation persuasive. The fruit is hopeful moral agency without self-reliance. Paul does not identify his \latin{proventum} with the Collect, and psychological change is neither instant nor the only form of help.

\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Majesty makes dependence dignified.\enspace\properrefs{Ep., Grad.}}
\emph{Classification: near analogue located.} Paul warns the one who thinks he stands; Psalm 8 places fragile humanity beneath a name majestic throughout the earth. The juxtaposition makes humility neither humiliation nor self-erasure: dependence belongs to creatures entrusted with dignity. Its fruit is confidence in God without presumption. The Gradual quotes only the psalm's opening, and the full psalm must not be used to authorize domination.

\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{A visitation recognized through lament becomes merciful presence.\enspace\properrefs{Gosp., Sec.}}
\emph{Classification: near analogue located.} Jesus recognizes catastrophe through tears and remains to teach; the Secret celebrates the memorial in which redemption's work is enacted. Their conjunction suggests that Christian recognition of a wounded place should become truthful mourning and costly, sacramental solidarity rather than triumph over ruins. Its fruit is compassion disciplined by truth. The proposal neither equates Jerusalem's siege with Calvary nor turns social action into a sacrament.

\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Sweet judgment cleanses worship without sweetening violence.\enspace\properrefs{Gosp., Off.}}
\emph{Classification: not located in the checked corpus.} Temple correction is followed by instruction sweeter than honey. Together they suggest reform whose end is restored prayer and delight in truth, not destruction of opponents. Its fruit is patient examination of religious commerce and power. The sequence does not prove a compositional link, authorize physical expulsion by modern readers, or deny the Gospel action's severity.

\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{The way of escape can be ecclesial.\enspace\properrefs{Ep., Comm., Postcomm.}}
\emph{Classification: near analogue located.} Paul promises endurance in the plural; John describes mutual abiding; the final prayer asks unity. Their liturgical relation resists the solitary slogan that a sufferer must handle everything alone: God may supply endurance through a body whose members bear one another. Its fruit is help-seeking and shared responsibility. The texts do not guarantee that every community responds faithfully, and unity never requires concealing abuse.
