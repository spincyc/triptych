## Scriptural and Liturgical Scope

- Appointed and immediate contexts studied: Psalms 8, 18, 53, and 58 in full (modern Psalms 8, 19, 54, and 59); 1 Samuel 19:8–18, 23:14–29, and 26:1–5; Exodus 32:1–6; Numbers 11, 21:4–9, and 25; 1 Corinthians 8:1–11:1; Luke 19:28–48; Isaiah 56:1–8; Jeremiah 7:1–15; John 6; and Hebrews 2:5–13.
- Closely related controls actually used: Exodus 16 and 32; Numbers 14, 21, and 25; Romans 9:4–5 and 11:1–2, 11–32; 1 Corinthians 11:27–29; and 2 Corinthians 1:8–10. The last passage controls the modern expansion of 1 Corinthians 10:13 from temptation to every kind of suffering.
- The Collect, Secret, and Postcommunion were analyzed in their complete verified Latin forms. Reader-facing quotation is limited to the clauses necessary for analysis because the guide is a companion to, not a substitute for, a hand missal.
- Seven distinct directly appointed biblical passages are inventoried in Catholic canonical order and then verse order: Psalm 8:2 (Gradual); Psalm 18:9–12 (Offertory); Psalm 53:3, 6–7 (Introit); Psalm 58:2 (Alleluia); Luke 19:41–47 (Gospel); John 6:57 under the missal's numbering (Communion; modern verse 56 in many editions); and 1 Corinthians 10:6–13 (Epistle). Composed orations and their biblical resonances were not mislabeled as direct quotations.
- The rebuilt movement—received help, relinquished presumption, visitation through tears and reform, and Eucharistic abiding in one body—is editorial synthesis of the assembled formulary. It rests especially on Paul's immediate continuation from temptation to flight from idolatry and one bread/one body (1 Cor. 10:14–17), and on documented reception of the Secret in *Sacrosanctum Concilium* 2, *Lumen gentium* 3, and *Presbyterorum ordinis* 13. It is not attributed to a recoverable historical compiler.

## Historical Coordinates of the Direct Scriptural Propers

The reader-facing table gives inherited metadata under neutral `Location` and `Date` fields and qualifies it in prose. Gospel composition, narrated event, recipient geography, and a psalm title's narrated circumstance remain distinct.

- **Psalm 8:** the Hebrew and Greek/Latin title names David, so a broad Davidic kingdom horizon, about 1000 BC, is a responsible traditional orientation. No superscription fixes a writing site or moment in David's life. The hymn moves from the Lord's world-filling name and majesty to human smallness and entrusted dignity; Hebrews 2 later receives it christologically. Modern authorship, date, place, and first performance remain unknown.
- **Psalm 18 (modern 19):** the superscription names David, but no narrative fixes a place or life stage. The psalm joins creation's wordless witness to praise of the Lord's instruction and a servant's prayer for cleansing. A traditional Davidic horizon can orient the table, while modern analysis recognizes the poem's two large movements and cannot securely recover an autograph date or site.
- **Psalm 53 (modern 54):** the title associates the prayer with the Ziphites' report to Saul; 1 Samuel records such reports at 23:19 and 26:1. The traditional horizon is David's pre-royal flight in Judah, broadly about 1010 BC. Neither report supplies the psalm's writing desk, and modern authorship and composition date remain uncertain. The title is received tradition, not independent proof that the words were composed during one recoverable episode.
- **Psalm 58 (modern 59):** the title associates the lament with Saul's agents watching David's house in order to kill him, corresponding to 1 Samuel 19:11. This supplies a traditional pre-royal danger horizon, not a proved date or writing location. Modern historical authorship, date, and place remain unknown.
- **Luke:** ancient ecclesial tradition identifies St. Luke, the Antiochene physician and companion of Paul, and places his writing in Achaia around AD 60. Current Catholic orientation commonly dates the final Gospel about AD 80–90, regards its city as unknown, and understands its first audience as substantially Gentile and outside Palestine. Luke's literary journey reaches Jerusalem at 19:28; the narrated lament occurs on the approach from the Mount of Olives and the action continues in Jerusalem's Temple, traditionally during Jesus' final ministry about AD 29–30.
- **John:** ancient tradition identifies John the Apostle and Ephesus; current Catholic orientation commonly places the Gospel's final form near the end of the first century while preserving debate about stages of composition and the Evangelist's relation to the beloved disciple. The narrated bread-of-life discourse follows the Galilean feeding and is located by John 6:59 in the synagogue at Capernaum, traditionally about AD 29. Composition and event are not collapsed.
- **1 Corinthians:** Pauline authorship is both traditional and the mainstream historical judgment. Paul wrote from Ephesus about AD 56, during his third missionary journey, to the Church he had founded at Corinth. The community included Gentile and Jewish Christians; chapters 8–10 answer disputed participation in idol-associated meals. At 10:1 Paul calls the wilderness generation `our fathers` and applies their history to the baptized Corinthian assembly.

Geographic equivalents remain orienting rather than probative. Judah and Jerusalem are in the southern Levant; Capernaum is by the Sea of Galilee in present-day northern Israel; Ephesus is near present-day Selçuk in Türkiye; Corinth is in present-day Greece; and Achaia is the corresponding Greek regional horizon.

## Direct Patristic, Medieval, and Later Reception Checked

### The appointed Psalms

- **Psalm 8:** Augustine §4 directly receives the Lord's admirable name throughout the earth through Christ's humiliation and exaltation while the full exposition preserves creaturely wonder: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1801008.htm). Aquinas's [*Super Psalmo 8*](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~Psalm.Ps8) directly treats the divine excellence manifested throughout earth and above the heavens. Bellarmine's Psalm 8 §1 supplies later saintly confirmation that created beauty makes the Creator's name admirable while divine magnificence exceeds the world.
- **Psalm 18:** Augustine §§9–12 reads right judgments as freely loved, the command as illumination of the inner sight, chaste fear as enduring, and keeping as itself a reward: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1801019.htm). Aquinas's [*Super Psalmo 18*](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~Psalm.Ps18) directly expounds the same verses; *Summa theologiae* [II–II, q. 19, a. 10](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~ST.II-II.Q19.A10) is illuminating doctrinal reuse rather than direct Psalm commentary. Bellarmine, Psalm 18 §§8–11, joins good conscience, illumination, filial fear, gold and honey, and the servant's experienced keeping in his [1866 commentary](https://www.ecatholic2000.com/bellarmine/commentary-on-psalms.shtml).
- **Psalm 53:** Augustine §§4–5 and 8 directly treats prayer by God's name and present interior aid; §9's retributive development was not needed: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1801054.htm). Hilary's direct Psalm 53 exposition was checked, but §§8 and 11 contain collective anti-Jewish and punitive rhetoric; only the bounded confidence under persecution in §§9–10 was considered. Bellarmine §§4–6 treats `behold` as sudden assurance, the present tense as confidence, and willing praise; his imprecation-as-prediction remains historical reception, not a rule for naming modern enemies. Aquinas's [Matthew commentary I.4.120](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~Matt.C1.L4.n120) invokes the Introit clause as ready divine help amid Joseph's turmoil; it is reuse, not direct Psalm exposition.
- **Psalm 58:** Augustine §4 redirects the prayer from human enemies toward the devil, fallen spirits, and temptations: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1801059.htm). His §§5 and 18–19 develop collective guilt and witness-people speculation about Jews; those sections were checked and rejected. Bellarmine, Psalm 58 §§1–2, first keeps David's literal danger and then generalizes the prayer to any just person in mortal danger. The English translator states that he substituted simple Douay headings for Bellarmine's original prefaces; the chapter heading `Christ and His Enemies the Jews` therefore must not be silently attributed to Bellarmine and is excluded in any event.

### 1 Corinthians 10:6–17

- **Canonical argument and wilderness map.** The appointed verses depend on 10:1–5, where sea, cloud, food, drink, and Christ the rock address the baptized Corinthians. Verse 6 evokes Numbers 11's craving; verse 7 quotes Exodus 32:6's idol feast; verse 8 evokes Numbers 25; verse 9 the testing and serpents of Numbers 21; and verse 10 murmuring and the destroyer. Paul's twenty-three thousand at verse 8 and Numbers 25:9's twenty-four thousand are reported as an inherited textual difference, not silently harmonized. At verse 14, `therefore` leads directly from divinely aided endurance to flight from idolatry; verses 16–17 then interpret cup and bread as participation in Christ and the many as one body.
- **St. John Chrysostom, Homilies 23–24 — direct exegesis.** [Homily 23](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/220123.htm) applies verses 6–12 to Corinthian craving, idolatry, sexual sin, testing, murmuring, and presumption. [Homily 24 §1](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/220124.htm) reads verse 13 as consolation: endurance depends upon God's gracious aid and supplied outcome, not unaided power. Sections 2–4 then move through Eucharistic participation to communion with Christ and one another and expose division as contradictory to the one bread. Anti-Jewish old/new comparisons were not imported.
- **St. Thomas Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians X — direct medieval exegesis.** [Lecture 2, nos. 522–531](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~1Cor.C10.L2) maps the wilderness episodes, discusses the twenty-three/thousand question, insists the events truly occurred and were written for correction, and distinguishes sins of heart, action, and speech. [Lecture 3, nos. 532–535](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~1Cor.C10.L3) treats uncertainty of standing, temptation and consent, ready help, grace, and constancy. [Lecture 4, nos. 537–542](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~1Cor.C10.L4) proceeds from flight from idolatry to Eucharistic participation and ecclesial unity.
- **St. Augustine, *On the Good of Widowhood* §21 — illuminating reuse.** Augustine uses verse 13 against claims that free will can avoid temptation without grace and prayer: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1311.htm). This supports dependence upon God but is not continuous commentary on the pericope.
- Paul begins with `our fathers` at 10:1 and tells those who think they stand to take heed. Romans 9:4–5 and 11:1–2, 11–32 prevent a reading in which Israel is merely a failed racial or religious foil and the Church is naturally immune from the same sins.

### Luke 19:41–47

- **St. Cyril of Alexandria, Commentary on Luke, Sermons 131–132 — direct Greek-patristic exegesis in historical English translation.** Sermon 131, printed pages 607–612 in R. Payne Smith's 1859 translation, treats Christ's tears as real pity and desire for Jerusalem's peace; Sermon 132, beginning at page 613, treats Temple prayer and teaching: [text](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/cyril_on_luke_12_sermons_124_134.htm). The same sermon attributes the Roman disaster to collective Jewish rejection and treats Israel's legal service as abandoned; that supersessionist and retributive frame is not repeated unqualified.
- **St. Gregory the Great, Homily 39 on the Gospels — direct Latin exegesis.** Sections 1–2 identify the Roman siege, treat Temple corruption, and observe that Jesus follows discipline by continuing to teach; §§6–7 apply the house of prayer to Christian religious office and the conscience: [Latin text](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Homiliarum_in_Evangelia/XXXIX). Gregory also calls Jerusalem faithless and says its overthrow changes it for the better. Those claims are historical reception, not the guide's judgment, and require explicit correction.
- **St. Bonaventure, Commentary on Luke XIX §§63–74 — direct medieval Latin exegesis.** Sections 63–69 treat the tears as Christ's priestly compassion on the day of royal honor; §§70–72 interpret the judicial Temple action, retain Isaiah 56's `all peoples` context, and turn Jeremiah's warning toward clerical avarice and simony; §§73–74 stress daily and public teaching: [Latin text](https://catholiclibrary.org/library/view?chunk.id=00000051&docId=%2FMedieval-OR%2FBonaventuraSCommentariusInEvangeliumSLucae.00000165.la.html). His reprobation and replacement claims in §§68–69 were checked and rejected.
- **St. Anthony of Padua, Sermon for the Tenth Sunday after Pentecost I.3–8 — medieval saintly reception.** Anthony's collection numbers the Sunday differently and pairs the Gospel with other chants and an Epistle. He turns Jerusalem into an image of the soul and Christ's tears into contrition over ruined virtues: [official Italian and Latin](https://www.santantonio.org/it/sermoni/sermoni-domenicali/domenica-x-dopo-pentecoste). This is spiritual reception, not modern etymology, recovered composition history, or denial of the literal city's suffering.
- **Aquinas's Catena aurea — medieval reception map.** At Luke 19:41–44 the catena preserves an Origen fragment on Jesus embodying the beatitude of mourners, Cyril on compassion, and Gregory's homily: [Latin/English](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~CaLuke.C19.L3.22). At verses 46–48 it preserves Gregory, Ambrose, Cyril, Augustine, and others on prayer, religious commerce, Christian self-application, and continued teaching: [Latin/English](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~CaLuke.C19.L6.3). Cyril and Gregory were checked in direct works. No independent public locus was found for the exact Origen fragment, so it remains a catena-preserved attribution rather than a separately verified homily.
- **Prophetic contexts actually used.** [Isaiah 56:1–8](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/isaiah/56) joins justice and covenant fidelity to the welcome of foreigners and eunuchs, acceptable sacrifice, a house of prayer for all peoples, and God's gathering of others to gathered Israel. [Jeremiah 7:1–15](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/jeremiah/7) defines the robbers' den through false Temple security amid theft, murder, adultery, perjury, idolatry, oppression of alien, orphan, and widow, and innocent bloodshed. These contexts prevent reduction of Luke 19:46 to commerce alone and prevent Isaiah's inclusion from being recast as replacement of Israel.
- **Josephus as historical comparator.** *Jewish War* [V.12.1–4](https://www.ccel.org/j/josephus/works/war-5.htm) describes Titus's circumvallation wall, its completion in three days, escape cut off, famine, and households dying; [VII.1.1](https://www.ccel.org/j/josephus/works/war-7.htm) describes demolition of the city and Temple while naming spared towers and part of the western wall. These passages illuminate the siege's material and human reality. They do not prove the oracle, date Luke's composition, make Josephus's blame theology normative, or support the claim that literally every stone vanished.

### John 6:57 / 6:56

- **St. Augustine, Tractates 26–27 on John — direct and adjacent exegesis.** [Tractate 26 §§11–19](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1701026.htm) first quotes 1 Corinthians 10:1–4 to distinguish visible sacrament from fruitful reception, then joins the appointed clause to fellowship, peace, unity from many grains and grapes, the one bread/one body of 1 Corinthians 10:17, mutual indwelling, and participation in Christ's life. [Tractate 27 §§1, 6](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1701027.htm) receives the same clause while commenting on the following verses and stresses incorporation, charity, and unity. Polemical fathers/sons and `Jews` language in Tractate 26 is not used positively.
- **St. John Chrysostom, Homily 47 on John — direct exegesis.** Chrysostom treats John 6:53–58, including eating, mutual indwelling, and eternal life: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/240147.htm). Its hostile generalizations at the outset were excluded.
- **St. Cyril of Alexandria, Commentary on John IV.3 — direct exegesis.** At the printed verse-57 discussion Cyril grounds communicant life in Christ's life-giving humanity and divine life from the living Father: [text](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/cyril_on_john_04_book4.htm). Anti-Jewish polemic on the preceding printed pages was not retained.
- **St. Thomas Aquinas, Commentary on John VI, lect. 7, nos. 966–977 — direct medieval exegesis.** No. 975 especially treats union, abiding, and eternal life: [text](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~Ioan.C6.L7.n969). The [*Catena aurea* VI, lect. 9](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~CaJohn.C6.L9) mapped Bede, Chrysostom, and Augustine leads but did not replace their underlying works.

## Composed Propers and Doctrinal Illumination

- **Collect:** Augustine, *Letter 130 to Proba* §§15–18 and 21–29, illuminates enlarged and disciplined desire, the Lord's Prayer, and God's merciful refusal of some requested temporal goods: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1102130.htm). It does not directly comment on `Pateant` and is not evidence for the prayer's origin.
- **Secret — direct and documented reception:** Aquinas, [*Summa theologiae* III, q. 83, a. 2](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~ST.III.Q83.A2), objection 5, directly quotes `concede nobis ... haec frequentare mysteria`. His corpus and replies distinguish the once-for-all Passion in Christ the head from daily participation in its fruit and perpetual memorial; the objection's proposed inference—multiple celebrations by one priest in a day—is not Thomas's conclusion. The Second Vatican Council's [*Sacrosanctum Concilium* 2 and note 1](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19631204_sacrosanctum-concilium_lt.html) quotes `opus nostrae Redemptionis exercetur` and explicitly names the Ninth Sunday's super oblata. [*Presbyterorum ordinis* 13 and note 105](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_decree_19651207_presbyterorum-ordinis_lt.html) does the same for Eucharistic sacrifice in priestly ministry. [*Lumen gentium* 3](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_const_19641121_lumen-gentium_lt.html) adapts the clause to celebration of the sacrifice of the Cross on the altar and immediately joins Eucharistic bread to the represented and effected unity of the faithful through 1 Corinthians 10:17. This establishes direct Thomistic and conciliar reception, not merely doctrinal analogy. Augustine, *City of God* X.5–6 and Aquinas q. 83, a. 1 remain broader controls for sign, sacrifice, memorial, and ecclesial action.
- **Communion and Postcommunion:** Augustine's John tractates directly join mutual abiding to 1 Corinthians 10's sacramental warning, fruitful reception, one bread, incorporation, charity, and unity. Chrysostom supplies an independent movement from Christ's self-gift to communicant life; Cyril grounds that life in Christ's life-giving humanity and divine life; Aquinas, *Commentary on John* VI, lect. 7, names union and mutual abiding. *Summa theologiae* III, q. 73, a. 3 and q. 79, aa. 1–4 further illuminates union with Christ and the Church, nourishment, remission of venial sin, and preservation from mortal sin. None supplies a promise of automatic fruit, immediate sensation, or unity without conversion.
- The printed Trinity Preface is liturgical context, not an eleventh proper. Its appointment does not make every source-grounded synthesis an explicit Trinitarian claim.

## Corrective Framing of Anti-Jewish and Supersessionist Reception

- Jesus is Jewish, approaching and lamenting a Jewish city and teaching in Israel's Temple. Luke 19:46 joins Isaiah 56:7 to Jeremiah 7:11; it is prophetic, intra-Jewish critique before it is Christian self-application. It cannot authorize contempt for Jews, a theory of collective hereditary guilt, or violence against a religious or ethnic group.
- The Roman destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70 is a historical event. Luke's final literary form and Jesus' narrated oracle are distinct historical questions. The guide does not claim independent knowledge that a modern disaster, the siege itself, or any later suffering is a directly assigned divine punishment.
- Paul identifies Israel's wilderness generation as `our fathers` and directs the warning toward baptized Christians. Israel remains named with gifts, covenants, worship, promises, and irrevocable divine calling in Romans 9 and 11. Figural reading becomes distortion if it makes Israel only failure and exempts the Church from correction.
- The USCCB's *God's Mercy Endures Forever* no. 7 addresses Luke 19:44 specifically and, following *Nostra aetate* 4, rejects presenting Jews as rejected or accursed by God as though Scripture taught it: [official source](https://www.usccb.org/resources/god-s-mercy-endures-forever-guidelines-presentation-jews-and-judaism-catholic-preaching). This modern protected source is paraphrased, not reproduced.
- Material historical exclusions are named rather than silently sanitized: Cyril's collective rejection and legal-service claims in Luke Sermon 131; Gregory's `faithless city` and replacement language in Homily 39; Bonaventure's reprobation and replacement claims in Luke XIX §§68–69; Augustine's collective-guilt and witness-people speculation in Psalm 59 and polemic in Tractate 26; Hilary's Psalm 53 polemic; the translator-supplied Bellarmine heading; Chrysostom's old/new polemic around 1 Corinthians 10; and anti-Jewish excerpts preserved elsewhere in the *Catena aurea*. The guide uses only bounded positive reasoning and states the correction.

## Lexical and Cross-Proper Audit

- **Help and action:** `Ecce Deus adiuvat me` and Paul's `fidelis autem Deus` place divine initiative before and within responsible action. Neither text supports passivity; neither makes success evidence of unaided strength.
- **Desire and temptation:** the Collect asks God to cause petitioners to seek what pleases him; the Epistle begins `Non simus concupiscentes malorum`. The verbal roots differ, so the relation is conceptual rather than an exact Latin echo. Their juxtaposition may support disciplined desire only as editorial synthesis.
- **Judgment and sweetness:** Psalm 18 keeps `iudicia`, honey, warning, and keeping within one canonical unit. Luke's Temple action may be compared with that unit, but the Psalm does not predict the Gospel scene and sweetness cannot be used to soften violence or declare the hearer righteous.
- **Warning, one bread, and unity:** the appointed lesson ends at 1 Corinthians 10:13, but the canonical `therefore` at verse 14 proceeds to flee idolatry and then to cup, bread, participation, and one body at verses 16–17. Chrysostom, Aquinas, and Augustine all receive this continuation sacramentally. It supplies a close canonical and reception bridge to the Communion and Postcommunion without proving historical compiler intent.
- **Memorial, abiding, unity:** the Secret's repeated sacramental memorial, John's mutual `manet`, and the Postcommunion's `purificationem` and `unitatem` form a strong liturgical progression from enacted gift to abiding and ecclesial fruit. The relation is no longer supported only by broad analogy: *Lumen gentium* 3 uses the Secret's central clause and immediately joins it to Eucharistic unity through 1 Corinthians 10:17. The Council still does not claim to explain the historical assembly of this entire formulary.
- Similar vocabulary, sequential placement, and thematic consonance never prove common human authorship, compositional intent, or historical source dependence. Original multi-proper models remain confined to the globally labeled interpretive section.
