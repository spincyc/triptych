# Proposed readings of the whole formulary

These are editorial constructions for the ten appointed elements of the
1962 Nineteenth Sunday after Pentecost. Each rests on compatible, directly
checked reasoning from more than two distinct Fathers or saints, but no
Father is credited with composing or expounding this exact ten-element
sequence unless the cited work actually does so. The text, source and
rights audit is `scope.md`; these readings provide alternatives for the
expansive study, comparison in the concise study, and a bounded choice for
the homily. Neither a research proposal nor this record is a review verdict.

The historical account in `scope.md`, revised in research iteration 2,
distinguishes received Pauline attribution, a secretary under Paul's direction,
and the conditional later-disciple hypothesis. Neither reading below depends
on deciding between them. Attributing the Epistle to Paul in inherited
theological exposition must not imply that its modern authorship question
has been settled; its historical alternatives belong in the cited dossier.

## A. The invited people clothed in charity

**Controlling claim.** The Lord who declares himself the people's salvation
does more than fill a room: he forms a people whose common life is the
charity appropriate to his Son's feast. The Mass's prayers prevent the
garment from becoming self-produced merit. Its judgment prevents welcome
from becoming complacency. The decisive question is what it means to belong
fruitfully to the assembly one has entered.

**Compatible author reasoning.** Augustine, Sermon90 §§4–10, argues from
things shared by good and bad Christians—baptism, sacramental attendance,
fasting and spiritual gifts—to the distinguishing garment of charity.
He then makes that distinction demanding: love must extend beyond one's
family and friends to strangers and enemies, desiring the evil person's
healing. Gregory, Gospel Homily38 §§9–13, explains why the garment is
charity from the Incarnation itself: love brought the Bridegroom to his
Church. His weaving analogy prevents separating love of God from concrete
compassion; his bound hands and feet expose withheld help. Section12
distinguishes incomplete charity from its total absence and forbids
despair. These arguments support one another without making every
imperfection exclusion from the feast.

Augustine's Psalm77 §§1–3 supplies a third precise contribution: reception
of common gifts does not automatically yield the same fruit in everyone,
and the old people's faithful hearers also received grace. Schuster's exact
Sunday commentary on the Secret provides the sacramental articulation:
the gift has saving power, while the prayer asks that the participants
receive it fruitfully. This author has read the same ten elements; his
argument can be distinguished from the editor's patristic cross-application.

| Appointed element | Necessary contribution to this reading |
| --- | --- |
| Introit | God's promise precedes the people's achievement. The appended call to hear makes salvation a relationship of attentive belonging; Augustine's context resists boasting about mere possession of gifts. |
| Collect | The assembly asks for obstacles to be removed so that body and mind can serve. Charity's garment is a graced freedom for God's service, not a purchase of invitation. |
| Epistle | “Members one of another” gives the invited people a corporate ethic: truth instead of deception, anger brought under restraint, hands sharing with need. These are practices by which charity becomes visible. |
| Gradual | One praying body asks that its raised hands and voice be received. Augustine's Christological interpretation relates its offering to the Head's Passion; Gregory's neglected hands supply a moral test of the worshippers, not a different literal meaning of the chant. |
| Alleluia | Praise is announced among nations, enlarging the horizon of the invitation. Augustine Ps104 §1 and Bellarmine104:1 supply direct reception; the psalm's covenant history remains its literal ground. |
| Gospel | The feast is prepared before response, the invitation reaches beyond the first invitees, the hall includes good and bad, and the king judges the garment. Gregory and Augustine explain how invitation, mixed membership and final accountability coexist. |
| Offertory | The welcomed people still meet hostility. Divine saving power permits enemy-love without pretending that enemies cannot harm or that material adversity has vanished. Augustine137 §§10–13 expressly moves confidence beyond temporal immunity. |
| Secret | Gifts are offered before majesty, with a request for saving benefit to the participants. Schuster's distinction between the gift and its fruit makes the garment question sacramental rather than merely social. |
| Communion | God's command is answered by a wish for directed ways. Augustine118:5 interprets the wish as prayer for enabling grace. Communion therefore does not authorize self-certification that one is already perfected. |
| Postcommunion | God's medicine releases perversity and enables lasting adherence. This returns Augustine's prayer for an enemy's healing to the worshipper's own need of healing; it prevents the critic of the undressed guest from exempting himself. |

**Four senses specific to A.**

- **Literal:** A royal wedding invitation is repeatedly refused, then widely
  extended; reception into the hall does not end accountability. Alongside
  it, the Epistle describes truthful, restrained and generous conduct in
  one body, and the prayers request freedom and healing for obedience.
- **Allegorical:** In Augustine and Gregory, the Son's wedding manifests
  Christ's union with the Church. Charity is the garment appropriate to
  that union; the Church prays and offers in her crucified Head. The
  Incarnation is one Person in two natures, never two persons married.
- **Moral:** Receive invitation without entitlement; test charity in truthful
  speech, restraint of resentment, generous work and love of the difficult
  neighbour. Ask for the grace to do so and distinguish imperfect growth
  from refusal to love.
- **Anagogical:** The present mixed assembly looks toward the final feast
  where charity is perfected and judgment is real. God's enduring lordship
  promises more than survival, while the many/few saying summons
  perseverance rather than a calculation of the number saved.

**Limits and pressure points.** The wedding garment is not a sermon about
modern dress codes or the expense of clothing. No historical proof was
found for a universal custom of a host supplying garments. Do not make
charity independent of faith, sacraments or grace. Do not say a bad recipient
makes Christ's sacrament unreal. The servants' murder and the city's
destruction belong to a judgment parable; its reception is not permission
for anti-Jewish collective blame or human retaliation. Augustine explicitly
acknowledges faithful ancient Israelites. The one guest is a representative
figure, not proof that exactly one of a multitude will be condemned.

**What the whole-Mass reading adds.** An element-by-element account can
leave the Gospel as a frightening dress inspection and the Postcommunion
as a routine ending. Their relation makes the assembly's response to
judgment a request for divine healing. The movement is invitation, truthful
participation, saving gift and continuing transformation; it remains
editorial synthesis of the attested texts and reception.

## B. The renewal of hands, words and desires

**Controlling claim.** God's saving work restores the person's actual powers
of action—mind, speech, anger, work, prayer and desire—so that the new person
can live in the community of the Son. Worship gathers these activities and
sends them back into life healed. The decisive question is how received
grace changes what a person does today without reducing grace to a
self-improvement programme.

**Compatible author reasoning.** Chrysostom, Ephesians XIII–XIV, joins
renewal as God's creation and baptismal clothing to detailed practical
changes. Mutual membership makes lying irrationally destructive; sunset
stops anger from becoming a nightly occupation; honest work reaches its
goal in giving. Bellarmine, Psalms118:4–8 and140:1–5, supplies a necessary
counterweight to voluntarism: commanded obedience requires grace, initial
justification is not earned by law, and acceptable prayer rests on Christ
and pure intention. His account of the mouth's guarding includes cooperation
that itself comes from grace. These are substantive compatible arguments
from two distinct saintly authors, not names attached to a general moral.

Augustine140:2–4 adds the Christological centre: the evening sacrifice is
Christ's voluntary Passion, and the Church's prayer is the Body's prayer.
Gregory38:13 provides a searching Gospel application: hands which refuse
the needy are already bound by culpable inaction before the judicial
binding. Aquinas Super Ephesios4 lectio7 confirms renewal through the
Spirit, with holiness in heart, truth in mouth and justice in deed, while
preserving several expositions of “spirit of your mind.” Schuster's
commentary on the Collect and Postcommunion provides a direct liturgical
account of ordered body/soul and healed desires. No one of these authors
is made to speak all the links by himself.

| Appointed element | Necessary contribution to this reading |
| --- | --- |
| Introit | The physician-like promise begins with God's saving identity and hearing of distress. The call to incline the ear makes receptivity, rather than sheer effort, the first human action. |
| Collect | Body and mind are freed together, for service with an unencumbered mind. Schuster reads this as ordered human powers directed to God, not flight from embodiment. |
| Epistle | This is the explanatory centre: renewal becomes truthful mouth, bounded anger, honest working hands and actual gifts to need. Chrysostom's positive requirement goes beyond abstaining from wrongdoing. |
| Gradual | Raised hands and prayer are offered together. Augustine's evening-sacrifice reading puts the work under Christ's self-offering, while Bellarmine tests intention and the surrounding psalm guards the mouth. |
| Alleluia | Speech moves outward as praise and witness, the positive counterpart to renounced lying. Bellarmine connects proclamation to love's desire that others know God and to the help needed even to praise. |
| Gospel | The feast prevents virtue from being self-enclosed productivity: renewed action belongs to relationship with the Son. Gregory's bound hands test whether professed belonging actually helps a needy person; they are not a condemnation of inability, disability or poverty. |
| Offertory | Human hands are neither the sole agent nor the final security. God's right hand sustains life in tribulation; Bellarmine137:7–8 and Augustine137:13 exclude self-sufficient boasting about one's works. |
| Secret | The offering asks saving efficacy for us. Honest work's gift is not identified simplistically with the Eucharistic offering; the latter is God's means of healing the participants' action. |
| Communion | A command becomes a petition for directed ways. Bellarmine's distinction of initial grace and growth in justice prevents both passivity and self-justification. Augustine's reading of *utinam* gives the request its exact force. |
| Postcommunion | The requested medicine has a double action: freedom from perversity and persevering attachment to God's commands. Desire itself needs healing, so this is more than compliance enforced from outside. |

**Four senses specific to B.**

- **Literal:** Paul orders a renewed community's speech, anger and labour;
  the psalms ask for accepted prayer, preservation and directed ways; the
  orations ask for unhindered service and medicinal change. The king's
  scrutiny makes response consequential.
- **Allegorical:** The new person participates in Christ, source of renewal;
  his evening sacrifice enables the Body's prayer and life. The Bridegroom's
  charity supplies the form of the community's restored action.
- **Moral:** Interrupt resentment, tell the truth to the person who depends
  on it, and work so that someone else can receive help. Prayer and honest
  action cannot be partitioned into unrelated parts of the week. Genuine
  inability is not culpable refusal, and poverty is not failure to deserve
  the invitation.
- **Anagogical:** Renewal now tends toward the perfected human life of the
  final feast. The evening of trial is not the last word: in Augustine's
  Christological reading, Passion leads to resurrection morning. Final
  judgment prevents indefinite postponement of conversion.

**Limits and pressure points.** This is not a claim that the Roman compiler
deliberately chose a hand motif, nor that external activity alone constitutes
the garment. Charity's source and relation to God remain decisive. The
bound hands are Gregory's moral exposition, not the literal cause stated
in Matthew. The Gradual does not prescribe the celebrant's particular
gesture here, and the Mass need not occur at sunset. The Epistle's anger
instruction is not a command to recover emotional calm on a clock while
ignoring justice. No equation of human work, Eucharistic sacrifice and
Christ's unique Passion is permissible; their relation is participation.

**What the whole-Mass reading adds.** It makes the physical vocabulary
mutually illuminating: attentive ear, renewed mind, truthful mouth,
working and praying hands, directed ways, God's saving right hand and
medicinal operation. Each image retains its original context. What emerges
is a practical account of grace, not a concordance claiming identical
meanings wherever a body part occurs.

## Concrete comparison and choice

| Point of comparison | Reading A | Reading B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Governing question | What makes belonging fruitful in the mixed assembly? | How does grace renew everyday powers of action? |
| Main exegetical pair | Augustine90 and Gregory38: garment, charity, mixed feast and enemy-love | Chrysostom Eph XIII–XIV and Bellarmine Psalms118/140: renewed conduct and enabling grace |
| Gospel emphasis | Welcome and accountable participation | Belonging tested through real helping action |
| Epistle emphasis | Social form of the charity appropriate to Christ's members | Concrete transformations that define renewal |
| Communion/Postcommunion | Invitation is answered by a request for the garment's growth and healing | Command becomes prayer, and medicine changes desire and habit |
| Principal danger avoided | Presumption from membership, or despair from imperfect charity | Moral self-sufficiency, or a separation of worship and life |
| Important omission if used alone | The precise economic demand to work for sharing can become too general | The royal Son, common feast and final judgment can become a practical-life illustration |

These are complementary emphases, not rival doctrines. Actual reception
differences remain: Augustine and Bellarmine identify the Psalm77 speaker
differently; Gregory leaves room for more than one relation of the Matthew
and Luke banquet accounts; Augustine stresses dependent petition in the
Communion, while Schuster stresses delight. None is flattened into a
fabricated consensus. A homily for the stated adult assembly can choose A
as its one governing argument and use B's honest-work/almsgiving example
as a test of the garment. It should not deliver both research plans or
all four proposals as a speech.

## Four exploratory proposals, with prior-art and limits

The following are offered for testing, not advertised as unprecedented.
Their source search is the bounded corpus and conjunction search recorded
in `scope.md`. Each states what would strengthen or defeat the conjecture.

### P1. Two evenings: resentment and offering

**Proposal:** Reading the Epistle's sunset boundary beside the Gradual's
evening sacrifice can give the day's ending a double function: stop feeding
resentment and entrust one's life to God. The textual triggers are Eph4:26
and Ps140:2; Chrysostom XIV explains the danger of night-time rumination,
while Augustine140:3 places the evening sacrifice in Christ's Passion.

**Prior art and residual contribution:** Generic evening-prayer discussion
already joins anger and prayer; the bounded web search returned such
examples. No priority claim survives. The proposed contribution is a
comparison of the two appointed moments under Christ's sacrifice, not the
discovery of evening reconciliation. **Limit/test:** Neither source by
itself teaches that this Sunday was historically assembled for that motif.
Documented ancient formulary commentary making that precise relation would
strengthen an historical claim; a mere modern co-occurrence would not.
This remains pastoral synthesis and cannot date the celebration's hour.

### P2. Four kinds of hands, without one flattened meaning

**Proposal:** The Epistle's working hands, Gradual's raised hands, Gospel's
bound hands and Offertory's saving divine hand allow a structured contrast
between enabled service, prayer, culpable refusal and God's initiative.

**Prior art and residual contribution:** Rupert XII.19 explicitly joins
working, giving and raised hands in a different Gospel arrangement;
Gregory38:13 already joins bound hands to withheld alms. Those connections
are inherited, not new. The residual proposal is their careful comparison
in this exact Matthew22/1962 inventory, including the distinct agency of
God's hand. **Limit/test:** A reading that treats literal prison bonds,
human work and divine power as synonyms defeats the proposal. A historical
design claim would need an actual assembly witness. The proposal excludes
blame directed at physical incapacity or dependence on others.

### P3. Commands become petitions at the table

**Proposal:** The passage from the Communion's stated command to its wish,
then the Postcommunion's request for healing and adherence, makes reception
the place where moral demand becomes a petition for its accomplishment.
It complements the Gospel's scrutiny without deleting it.

**Prior art and residual contribution:** Augustine118:5 directly teaches
praying for what God commands; Bellarmine118:5,8 distinguishes grace and
obedient growth; Schuster directly reads the Postcommunion as healing grace.
The proposed sequence adds the explicit connection between these appointed
positions and the preceding garment judgment. **Limit/test:** It fails if
it turns God's enabling into absence of human response, or if the Eucharist
is treated as automatically fruitful regardless of disposition. An early
commentary explicitly combining these three precise moments would narrow
the residual editorial contribution further.

### P4. A full feast against a scarcity reading of election

**Proposal:** Place Haeckel's scarce-resources use of the called/chosen
formula beside the Gospel's prepared feast and the Epistle's giving to
need. This exposes how reception can relocate “chosen” from accountable
response to survival competition, and why the parable should not be
reduced to competition for a limited number of places.

**Prior art and residual contribution:** Haeckel's actual chapterVII gives
the naturalistic appropriation; Augustine90 §4 reads the one guest as a
class, not a literal headcount. The comparative juxtaposition is editorial;
no historical line of influence between Haeckel and this Mass is claimed.
**Limit/test:** It cannot prove a numerical doctrine of universal salvation
or settle the theological relation of calling and election. If the
literary comparison obscures the king's real judgment, it has failed. Its
use belongs in a cultural gallery or expansive exploratory section, not
as a necessary homily detour.
