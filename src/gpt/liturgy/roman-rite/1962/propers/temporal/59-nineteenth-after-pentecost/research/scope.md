## Direct reception and competing emphases

**Augustine, Sermon 90, §§1–10** (NPNF I.6, Sermon XL in its English
sequence, Benedictine XC; full sermon read, not only an anthology extract).
He distinguishes the present mixed feast from the final feast of the good;
the one improperly dressed guest represents a class, not a census of one.
In §§4–6 baptism, attendance at the altar, fasting and impressive gifts do
not by themselves distinguish the garment: charity does. This is not a
denial of the sacraments but an argument about their fruit in their recipients.
Sections7–10 test love by strangers and enemies; he asks for the healing of
the evil person rather than his destruction and cites Christ and Stephen.
God's image is renewed as charity widens. This directly supports the Gospel
reading and supplies an ethical explanation compatible with Eph4; it is not
an exposition of this precise ten-element Roman formulary.

**Gregory the Great, Homilies on the Gospels II.38, §§1–14**, Latin historical
text inspected, especially §§1–7,9–14. Section1 distinguishes Matthew's
present feast with mixed guests from Luke's final banquet, while explicitly
allowing another harmonization without injury to faith. Section3 joins the
Incarnation and the Church's union with Christ, carefully denying that the
Incarnation unites two persons. Sections9–11 identify the garment as charity,
join love of God and neighbour through the woven-cloth analogy, and insist
that enemy-love tests its quality. Section12 consoles those whose charity
is real but imperfect: imperfection is not the same as having no garment.
Section13 applies the bound hands and feet to refusing alms and visits to
the sick; §14 turns the unknown outcome toward humble perseverance. The
heading names a sermon in St Clement's basilica; it does **not** prove that
Gregory preached this later-numbered Sunday or established its present pairing.
The transcription's pericope label ends at13 while the quoted text includes
the saying now numbered14: cite the actual text and homily sections.

**John Chrysostom, Homilies on Ephesians XIII–XIV**, NPNF I.13, the actual
commentary surrounding 4:23–28 inspected. XIII on4:23–24 explains renewal
as change of conduct, not replacement of human nature: the garment conferred
in baptism is to remain visible in life, and creation language denies
self-sufficiency. XIV on4:25–28 argues from the body: members cannot safely
lie to each other. Sunset limits anger before night nourishes it; hostility
opens a place for the devil. Cessation of stealing is insufficient: work
must yield what can be shared. His lay audience need not become monastics to
obey these demands. These are specific Greek patristic arguments, not a
generic injunction to be kind. XIII's treatment is preparatory; XIV carries
the appointed social particulars.

**Augustine, Enarrationes in Psalmos**, NPNF I.8, directly inspected:
Ps77 §§1–3 (English heading78), Ps104 §§1–3 (105), Ps118 opening Aleph
§§1–8 (119), Ps137 §§10–13 (138), Ps140 §§1–7 (141). Ps77 begins with
the Christian congregation learning from the old people's gifts and failures;
Augustine includes faithful recipients of grace within ancient Israel.
He hears God speaking through the prophet in v.1 and stresses humble hearing.
Ps104:1 is praise rather than confession of sin; he reads the Gentile
announcement as prophetic of evangelists and couples voice and hands in v.2.
Ps118:4 explains *nimis* as great diligence, and v.5's wish as prayer for
God to give what he commands. Ps137 locates secure life beyond the power
of enemies and forbids proud reliance on one's own works. Ps140:2 reads
the evening sacrifice as Christ's voluntary Passion and the Church praying
in its Head; the following lips passage joins confession and restraint.
His anti-Manichaean application to refusing bread to the hungry belongs to
§6's historical polemic, not to the literal psalm or a modern adversary.
These are bounded readings of the relevant sections, not a claim to have
read every sermon on all five psalms.

**Robert Bellarmine, Commentary on the Psalms**, John O'Sullivan's historical
English (1866), retained text, Ps77:1–8;104:1–2;118:1–8;137:7–8;140:1–5
inspected. Psalm104's announcement follows love's desire that others know
the beloved, and even praise requires help. Ps118 distinguishes initial
justification by grace from growth in justice through obedient action, so
his strong language about command is not independent self-justification.
Ps140 links acceptable prayer to Christ the high priest, pure intention,
faith, hope, love and humility; lips need both grace and cooperation, itself
enabled by grace. Ps137's right hand receives a Christological reading and
its completion of God's work excludes boasting. Material differences:
Bellarmine's Ps77 exposition assigns the immediate voice to David, whereas
Augustine hears God speaking through the prophet; Bellarmine argues from
the later mention of 'our fathers'. Neither author's exposition establishes
a canonical composition date. Bellarmine's strong phrase in118:4 about the
legislator who 'can neither forgive nor forget' cannot be isolated from the
following account of grace and forgiveness. No fresh printed-edition
collation of the 1866 translation is claimed; paraphrase the checked sense.

**Thomas Aquinas, Super Ephesios 4, lectio7**, the Dessain 1857 Latin OCR
around printed329, lines34632–34747: the Spirit of the mind admits several
readings—Holy Spirit dwelling in the mind, rational spirit, or mind made
spiritual. Adam names oldness; Christ initiates newness; the renewed person
has holiness in heart, truth in mouth, justice in action. The opening of
lectio8 was located but is not used for a detailed claim. OCR is imperfect;
this is inspected Latin reception, not a facsimile-verified quotation or a
fresh critical edition. Chrysostom independently carries the main argument.

**Blessed Ildefonso Schuster, The Sacramentary III**, English1927,
“Nineteenth Sunday after Pentecost,” printed171–174, complete unit read in
the retained OCR (lines10707–10901). This is direct later liturgical

He reads bodily health as ordered to the soul's service, the new garment
as Christ, the body's members as bearing reciprocal obligations, the
Gradual as evening pilgrimage before resurrection morning, the Alleluia as
evangelization, and the Secret as a request for dispositions that permit
sacramental fruit. The Postcommunion concerns healing grace and changed

Gregory does not date the sermon to this Sunday.

## Cultural afterlives: three checked entries

These belong to a clearly marked gallery, not doctrinal proof or an extension
of the appointed readings. The historical passage was read in each complete
acquired work; no claim of direct influence from this Sunday Mass is made.
The called/chosen formula also occurs in Matthew20:16 in the historical
Bible tradition. Arnold and Haeckel do not identify a verse number in these
loci, so their wording is reception of the shared formula, not proof that
they borrowed specifically from Matthew22:14.

1. **Francis Bacon, Essays, “Of Anger,” opening and ensuing argument**,
   Gutenberg575, lines4745–4813. Bacon prefers scriptural limits to the
   Stoic ambition of extinguishing anger; he then examines injury, perceived
   contempt, reputation and delaying action. This takes Eph4:26 into a
   statesman's practical moral psychology. The same essay advises reserving
   revenge in imagination to gain time: that counsel is not Paul's
   ecclesial goal or Augustine's enemy-love. The contrast makes the borrowing
   more instructive than a detached familiar quotation.
2. **Matthew Arnold, “Heinrich Heine,” closing paragraphs**, in William
   Savage Johnson's *Selections from the Prose Works*,1913, Gutenberg12628,
   lines4911–4953. Arnold balances intellectual brilliance against moral
   balance and character; he closes the comparison of Byron and Heine with
   the called/chosen formula. The divine banquet's judgment becomes a
   criterion of fulfilled literary promise. It remains an ethically charged
   borrowing, not a claim to know either poet's eternal destiny. Arnold's
   judgment is reported as Arnold's, not adopted as an exhaustive biography.
3. **Ernst Haeckel, History of Creation I, chapterVII**, English1880,
   translation revised by E. Ray Lankester, Gutenberg40472,
   lines5140–5204; compare printed164 in the1876 edition available from
   Darwin Online. The formula describes competition for sunlight, moisture
   and subsistence and is extended to human labour. This striking change
   of register replaces a king's invitation and accountable response with
   differential survival under limited resources. It is an appropriation
   in a naturalistic explanation, not evidence for the parable's meaning
   or an endorsement of Haeckel's social conclusions. The image of scarcity
   also contrasts sharply with the Gospel's already prepared feast and
   the Epistle's demand to give to someone in need.
