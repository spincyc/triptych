The feast is ready before anyone accepts the invitation. Yet the Gospel's
last question concerns a guest already inside. The Nineteenth Sunday after
Pentecost holds these two truths together: God opens the feast of his Son,
and participation in that feast calls for a transformed life. Neither the
repeated refusal outside the hall nor the unworthy presence within it
overthrows the king's purpose. His generosity fills the room; his judgment
searches the people who have entered.

The Epistle makes the transformation concrete. The new person speaks truth,
refuses to cultivate anger, ceases stealing, and works to give to someone
in need. These actions belong to a common life: Christians are members of
one another. The prayers ask God to make such a life possible. The Collect
seeks freedom of body and mind for his service; the Secret asks that the
offering benefit its participants; the Postcommunion asks for healing that
frees them from perversity and keeps them attached to his commands. Divine
gift and human response meet throughout the Mass.

Two readings disclose different depths of this movement. Augustine and
Gregory the Great explain the wedding garment as charity, the love that
honours the Bridegroom and gives truthful substance to membership in his
Church. Chrysostom and Bellarmine explain how renewal reaches a person's
actual powers: thought, speech, work, prayer, and desire. The first asks
what makes belonging fruitful. The second asks what grace changes in daily
life. They converge at the Communion, where the command to keep God's law
is answered by a petition for directed ways.

Secret & \textit{Haec munera} & The offerings before divine majesty are petitioned to be saving for their participants.\\
Communion & Ps 118(119):4--5 & God's command awakens a wish for ways directed toward obedience.\\
Postcommunion & \textit{Tua nos, Domine} & Healing frees from perversity and enables continuing adherence to God's commands.\\
