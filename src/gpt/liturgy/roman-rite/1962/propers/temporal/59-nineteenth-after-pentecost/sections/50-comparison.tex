\section{Belonging That Becomes a Life}

The garment of charity and the renewal of action answer different
questions about the same grace. Augustine and Gregory ask what
distinguishes fruitful presence at the feast from presence without
love. Chrysostom and Bellarmine ask how renewal becomes the use
of a mouth, a will, and working hands. The first exposes the
presumption that membership settles everything. The second
exposes the distance between a general wish to be good and
the particular good another person needs.

Each also corrects a possible thinning of the other. Charity
can become an attractive general word if it is never required
to speak truth, interrupt resentment, or give. Conversely,
practical improvement can become self-enclosed if the Son's
feast, Christ's gift, and the final judgment disappear from
view. The worker remains a guest. The guest is called to a
life whose generosity can be tested.

The Communion holds the two emphases together. Augustine's
dependence, Bellarmine's obedient growth, and Schuster's
delight all concern a person who loves God's command and
asks for directed ways. They do not erase their different
accents. Dependence answers the temptation to self-sufficiency;
growth answers passivity; delight answers the thought that
God's service can only be endured. The Postcommunion asks
that the medicine of the feast transform desire and sustain
this life.

The last request is therefore a fitting answer to the
Gospel's searching ending. The assembly does not escape
judgment by pretending that charity is unimportant, and it
does not escape fear by announcing its own perfection.
It asks the Lord who first promised salvation to free it,
heal it, and keep it faithful. The feast becomes fruitful
in a people whose received love becomes a life.
