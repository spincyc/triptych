# Proposed whole-formulary interpretations

These two arguments are editorial syntheses over the verified1962 resumed
Fifth-Sunday compilation. Each is carried by at least two distinct Fathers
or canonized saints actually read at precise loci; attribution below concerns
their own passages, not authorship of the whole-Mass construction. The
eligible standing inventory supplies Chrysostom, Augustine, Jerome, Bede,
Bellarmine and Aquinas; Fromage is not counted toward that requirement.
The source roles and qualification boundaries are in scope.md.

## A. Charity preserves the people who may yet become wheat

Stable proposed key: patience-and-conversion.

Controlling claim: the Lord's refusal to let the servants uproot the tares
forms a community that pursues conversion with mercy, rather than claiming
the last judgement for itself. The argument rests first on Chrysostom,
Matthew Homily46.1, and Augustine, Sermon23 [Benedictine73] §§1–4; Chrysostom's
Colossians Homily8 on3:12–15 explains the community's positive discipline.

### Consequential difficulty and checked inferences

The appointed words oppose the servants' proposal colligimus ea to the
master's ne forte ... eradicetis simul ... et triticum, then distinguish
sinite utraque crescere usque ad messem from final binding and burning.
Why does a good master permit the enemy's crop to remain? Why does restraint
now not nullify judgement later? Jesus's explanation in Matthew13:36–43
identifies the field as the world, the good seed as children of the kingdom,
the enemy as the devil, the harvest as the world's consummation and the
reapers as angels. It does not make the enemy another creator or assign the
servants the angels' office.

Chrysostom reads the tares as heretics imitating truth after it is sown. Sleep
can signify the negligence that exposes hearers; the servants show concern
but insufficient discretion. His inference from possible injury to the wheat
is that killing heretics can destroy saints along with them. He also asks
whether some tares may become wheat, making preservation an opportunity for
repentance rather than a declaration that error is good. His conclusion
forbids killing in this argument while permitting stopping speech and breaking
up assemblies. An author who turns him into a spokesman for unrestricted
modern toleration would suppress a material part of his answer.

Augustine's sermon addresses wicked Christians and warns both bad and good.
Tares can be found among clergy and laypeople, and today's tares can become
wheat. Humans may take wheat for tares; the angels who make the final
separation do not err. The consequence is double: no hearer may presume
himself permanently wheat, and no impatient human judgement has the harvest's
certainty. This complements Chrysostom's collateral-damage and conversion
arguments while broadening their immediate referent beyond doctrinal heresy.
The two referents must remain attributed, not fused into one historical
explanation.

Chrysostom on Colossians supplies an active practice for the interval. Mercy
is the inward affection of a parent, not merely an outward concession;
forgiveness imitates Christ even after serious injury. Charity binds the
other virtues together. Peace acts as an umpire: when grievance proposes
revenge and patience proposes endurance, the Christian awards the contest
to patience because Christ has called the members into one body. Thus
sinite does not mean neglecting moral formation or ceasing to teach.

### This interpretation's four senses

- Literal: the good sower opposes an enemy and postpones separation because
  uprooting can injure the wheat; Paul commands a forgiven people to bear
  with, forgive and admonish one another. The prayers confess need for grace.
- Allegorical: in Christ's kingdom the people called into one body receive
  mercy from the Lord whose seed is good; Augustine's changing tares and
  Chrysostom's spared potential wheat place conversion within his present
  work. The Church's mixed present does not falsify Christ's holiness.
- Moral: forgo retaliatory destruction, forgive concrete grievances, teach
  the word, examine one's own instability and seek another's conversion.
  Discernment and correction remain real; no claim is made that these loci
  settle every question of civil force or ecclesiastical discipline.
- Anagogical: Christ's harvest finally separates with a certainty human
  judgement lacks; the barn and the effect of salvation promised after the
  sacramental pledge give patience its end. Patience is time for conversion,
  not an erasure of the fire or an automatic universal acquittal.

Important limit: Jerome's actual exiles are not simply relabelled heretics,
and the Psalms' enemies are not identified with present parish opponents.
This argument concerns a Christian's manner of acting before the last
judgement. It is strongest where the Gospel and Colossians explain that
action. It needs the second reading to explain more fully why God commands
petition for a gift already promised.

## B. The promised salvation is received through prayer and mercy

Stable proposed key: promise-prayer-and-pledge.

Controlling claim: God's promise produces prayer, repentance and thanksgiving
while its saving effect is awaited; confidence rests on his mercy, not on
the command of the petitioner over the outcome. Jerome, In Ieremiam5 on
29:10–14, and Augustine, Enarratio129 §§1–8, carry the argument. They are
distinct Fathers with substantive, compatible contributions. Bellarmine
Ps129, Bede onMark11:25 and Aquinas STII-IIq83a15–16 sharpen its account of
the asking subject and the promised good. Aquinas onCol3:16–17 supports the
role of thankful instruction and song.

### Consequential difficulty and checked inferences

The Introit promises exaudiam vos and reducam captivitatem; the Communion
says quidquid orantes petitis, credite quia accipietis; yet the Collect asks
for continuing protection, the Secret for pardon and stable hearts, and the
Postcommunion for the effect of a salvation whose pignus has already been
received. Why ask and wait if reception is promised? Does quidquid make
whatever the hearer presently wants the measure of God's action?

Jerome's sequence is decisive. The context rejects the false prophets'
quick return and locates God's promised good beyond their timetable.
God could grant the promised good without prayer, yet urges his people to
call and seek with the whole heart. Therefore prayer is a commanded mode
of receiving the promise, not evidence that the promise is doubtful or an
attempt to inform an ignorant God. Jerome then reads the ultimate return
anagogically for God's people. The historical exiles and their promised
restoration remain the literal starting point.

Augustine asks how a sinner in the depths can cry at all. The cry is already
hope, and the psalm's acknowledgement that no one could stand under strict
reckoning leads to propitiation with God. In §3 he relates this propitiation
to Christ's sacrifice; in §§4–8 the rising Head and the waiting body explain
hope for resurrection and complete redemption. Accepting his inference
changes the petitioner's confidence: it is confidence in the redeeming Lord
while acknowledging sin, not confidence in personal innocence or immediate
escape from every suffering. Bellarmine's two depths—misery and inward
attention—likewise oppose both despair and careless self-satisfaction.

Bede onMark11:20–26 reads powerful faith as faith working through charity.
The less perfect may ask for their sins to be forgiven and eternal life,
provided that they forgive offenders; the adjoining forgiveness command is
part of the explanation. No new claim about Bede's manuscript reading or
book number is made. His support remains subject to the recorded fragment
admission deferral for the work's present locus grammar.

Aquinas q83a15 distinguishes the merit of prayer, rooted in charity, from
its impetration, rooted in God's gracious gift. His four conditions concern
asking for oneself, for what is necessary to salvation, piously and
perseveringly. Reply2 allows a delay conducive to the petitioner rather than
treating every delayed gift as refusal. Article16 distinguishes a sinner's
evil request from a good natural desire God can hear from mercy. These
qualify the desired good and the manner of asking; they are not a device
for proving that every ungranted temporal request reflects insufficient
faith. His checked Colossians paragraph further shows how Scripture's
indwelling becomes instruction, heartfelt praise and habitual direction of
ordinary work toward God.

| Gospel | The owner allows a real interval before harvest. This gives a narrated form to waiting for the final act; it does not itself teach Aquinas's four conditions of prayer. The future barn and fire preserve the seriousness of the good requested. |
| Offertory | Repetition of the depths at the offering leads into the Secret's actual request for propitiation. Augustine's Ps129§3 supplies a Christological account of propitiation; connecting that exposition with this liturgical sequence is the editor's synthesis. |
| Secret | Offerers ask that the sacrifice bring merciful absolution and direction of wavering hearts. The saving object includes conversion of the demander, not merely a favourable change of circumstances. |
| Trinity Preface | The thanksgiving addresses the Father with the Son and Holy Spirit, confessing equal glory and distinct Persons. The received good leads to adoration of God himself. The same text's common opening gives thanks always and everywhere, consistent with Col3:17. |
| Communion | The promise of reception is heard within faith in God and forgiveness of others. Bede's charitable faith and Aquinas's saving-good/perseverance distinctions give precise content to confidence; no mechanical prosperity promise follows. |
| Postcommunion | Pledge already received and saving effect still requested are both explicit. This is the clearest appointed articulation of the difference between present gift and final fulfilment; it supports Augustine's body awaiting the risen Head without denying the present sacramental gift. |

### This interpretation's four senses

- Literal: God promises restoration to exiles who must call and seek; the
  Psalmist cries, acknowledges sin and waits; Christ commands faith and
  forgiveness, while the orations ask protection, pardon and salvation's effect.
- Allegorical: Christ is the propitiation by whose sacrifice the depths can
  become hopeful prayer; the risen Head gives the waiting body its hope.
  The Mass's gift and thanksgiving belong to this life received through him.
- Moral: ask piously and perseveringly for saving good, forgive an actual
  offender, acknowledge dependence, let the word instruct the heart, and
  give thanks in ordinary action. Do not substitute either despair or the
  manipulation of God for petition.
- Anagogical: promised restoration reaches complete redemption and bodily
  resurrection. The pledge anticipates the effect of salvation; the harvest
  marks the consummation in which delay ends and Christ's judgement is fulfilled.

Important limit: the Introit's historical promise is not a timetable for an
individual's temporal wishes. Augustine's spiritual depths are his Christian
reception of the whole psalm, not a claim that every sufferer caused his
misfortune by a specific sin. The Secret's sacrifice and the Postcommunion's
pledge are their literal liturgical assertions; no Father is credited with
having commented on those exact composed prayers at the checked loci.

## Concrete comparison before authoring

| Test | Reading A | Reading B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exegetical question | Why may the servants not uproot what is demonstrably evil before harvest? | Why do promised hearing and reception coexist with commands to pray, repent and await the effect? |
| Distinguishing inference | Collateral harm, possible conversion and fallible human judgement limit destructive action toward others. | God makes prayer a means of promised mercy; the sinner receives saving good from grace while its complete effect is awaited. |
| Consequence for the hearer | Do not claim the unerring reaper's office; forgive, teach and seek the other's conversion. | Ask with charitable faith and perseverance; acknowledge need and give thanks for the gift already received. |
| Principal textual difficulty | Ne forte ... et triticum alongside the certainty of the fire and barn. | Quidquid ... accipietis alongside pignus accepimus and capiamus effectum. |
| What would be lost if reduced to the other | The Gospel's reason for prohibiting a particular destructive action, including harm to innocent and reformable persons. | The relation between divine promise, human petition, propitiation and present sacramental gift. |

The readings agree about grace, conversion, charity and last judgement. They
are complementary emphases, not competing doctrines. A parish application
about a grievance belongs chiefly to A; an application about persistent
prayer after an apparently unanswered petition belongs chiefly to B. Neither
should end merely “be patient and kind”: A must preserve the master's reason
for restraint and B must preserve the promised gift's gracious manner and
saving object. Their common material should receive one full development
and a precise cross-reference in the eventual expansive study.
