\section*{The Propers: Notable and Quotable}

\Needspace{13\baselineskip}
\subsection*{The kingdom becomes political independence\enspace\properrefs{Gosp., Comm.}}

\textbf{Biblical wording.} ``Seek ye therefore first the kingdom of God''
(Matt.~6:33, Douay--Rheims--Challoner). The Communion repeats the kingdom's
priority in its shorter liturgical form.

\textbf{Later wording.} ``Seek ye first the political kingdom'' (Kwame
Nkrumah, \emph{Africa Must Unite}, 1963, ch.~6, ``Freedom First,'' p.~50,
second paragraph).

Nkrumah makes the Convention People's Party's demand for independence the
condition for social and economic advance. Substituting \emph{political}
changes the first object of seeking: the phrase orders a program of national
liberation and development. Its concise English form is closer to the
Authorized Version's cadence than to the Douay wording printed here.
Nkrumah's political priority is his historical argument; Matthew's kingdom
cannot simply be identified with a nation's independence.

\Needspace{13\baselineskip}
\subsection*{A critic claims the lilies' exemption from work\enspace\properrefs{Gosp.}}

\textbf{Biblical wording.} ``they labour not, neither do they spin''
(Matt.~6:28, Douay--Rheims--Challoner).

\textbf{Later wording.} ``I toil not, neither do I spin'' (Joseph L.
Mankiewicz, \emph{All About Eve}, revised final screenplay, April~5, 1950,
p.~3, scene~5, ``CLOSE ADDISON,'' opening voice-over).

Addison DeWitt describes the critic's relation to the theatre by placing
himself outside the labor of its makers and performers. His surrounding
insect imagery makes the exemption parasitic and potentially harmful. The
lilies' creaturely dependence becomes a cultivated profession of detached
power. The screenplay's first person and \emph{toil} reshape the familiar
English saying. This locus belongs to the dated script, not to a verified
point in the finished film; Matthew's lily saying also has a parallel in
Luke~12.

\Needspace{13\baselineskip}
\subsection*{Two masters become incompatible political principles\enspace\properrefs{Gosp.}}

\textbf{Biblical wording.} ``You cannot serve God and mammon''
(Matt.~6:24, Douay--Rheims--Challoner).

\textbf{Later wording.} ``as opposite as God and mammon'' (Abraham Lincoln,
Peoria speech, October~16, 1854, paragraph beginning ``But NOW it is to be
transformed into a `sacred right.'\,'' in the National Park Service
transcription).

Lincoln opposes the Declaration's equality principle to the proposed right
to enslave another person in his argument against the Kansas--Nebraska
policy. The paragraph's language of holding to one principle and despising
the other carries the biblical antithesis into political judgment. God and
mammon name an incompatibility that compromise cannot reconcile. This is
the Peoria speech, not the later ``House Divided'' speech. The biblical
saying also occurs in Luke~16:13, so the verbal echo does not single out
Matthew as Lincoln's source.

\Needspace{13\baselineskip}
\subsection*{The lily becomes the poet's desired release\enspace\properrefs{Gosp.}}

\textbf{Biblical wording.} ``they labour not, neither do they spin''
(Matt.~6:28, Douay--Rheims--Challoner).

\textbf{Later wording.} ``They toil not, neither do they spin.'' (John Keats,
\emph{Ode on Indolence}, epigraph; text in G.~Kim Blank, \emph{Mapping Keats's
Progress}, ed.~3.27, University of Victoria).

The epigraph frames a poem in which Love, Ambition, and Poetry pass before
the speaker. Stanzas~III--IV, lines~25--40, name those figures and prefer
indolence to their claims; stanza~VI, lines~51--60, dismisses the visions while
Poetry's attraction remains. The lilies' release from labor becomes a wish
for freedom from demands upon the self, including artistic striving. The
carefully made poem sustains that tension rather than simply renouncing
poetry. Its epigraph follows the familiar Authorized Version wording;
the saying's Lukan parallel remains relevant.

\Needspace{13\baselineskip}
\subsection*{Spinning becomes an examination result\enspace\properrefs{Gosp.}}

\textbf{Biblical wording.} ``they labour not, neither do they spin''
(Matt.~6:28, Douay--Rheims--Challoner).

\textbf{Later wording.} ``For though I toil not, neither do I spin, / I shall
be spun'' (R.~F. Murray, ``The Waster's Presentiment,'' \emph{The Scarlet
Gown}, 1891, p.~50, lines~3--4).

A student who has delayed his reading anticipates the April examinations,
an uncle's displeasure, and the absence of a degree. The collection's own
note defines \emph{spin} as failure in an examination (p.~122). The Gospel's
image is turned through grammatical voice: the speaker's failure to spin
actively ends in his being spun passively. The full eleven-line poem makes
the pun a judgment on neglected study, not a claim that Christ commends
idleness. Its \emph{toil} wording shares the Authorized Version's cadence,
and the biblical saying has a parallel in Luke~12.
