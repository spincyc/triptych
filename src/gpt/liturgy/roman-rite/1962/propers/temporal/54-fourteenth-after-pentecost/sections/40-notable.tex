\section*{The Propers: Notable and Quotable}

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\subsection*{``No man can serve two masters'' as conflict-of-interest law\enspace\properrefs{Gosp.}}

In \emph{United States v. Mississippi Valley Generating Co.}, 364
U.S.~520, 549--550 and n.~14 (1961), the Supreme Court invokes Matthew~6:24
while applying the federal conflict-of-interest statute. The opinion first
changes ``can'' to ``may,'' then its footnote reproduces the appointed phrase
``No man can serve two masters.'' The Gospel's undivided service becomes an
institutional rule against divided economic loyalty in federal contracting,
without requiring proof of actual corruption or financial loss. The verse
supplies moral foundation and analogy; the statute and the Court's reasoning,
not the citation alone, supply positive law.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\subsection*{``God and Mammon united'' as political reversal\enspace\properrefs{Gosp.}}

Upton Sinclair titles chapter~XIV of \emph{The Goslings} (1924) ``God and
Mammon'' and closes it by asserting their union (pp.~62--66, especially
p.~66). Matthew's mutually exclusive names are deliberately reversed as an
accusation that church influence, municipal politics, and business interests
collude in public schools. The new force is polemical and institutional:
Sinclair makes the impossibility of joint service into an ironic description
of an alliance he condemns. His anti-clerical allegations remain his argument,
not findings of this study.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\subsection*{``Neither do they spin'' as a science-fiction joke\enspace\properrefs{Gosp.}}

In David Gerrold's ``The Trouble with Tribbles,'' \emph{Star Trek}, season~2,
episode~15, Spock applies ``neither do they spin'' to creatures that eat and
multiply while doing no useful work; McCoy answers from affection for them.
The exchange occurs in the sickbay dialogue before the cut to the bridge and
also appears in Gerrold's published screenplay, scene p.~177. The Authorized
Version supplies ``toil,'' while the brief overlap is exact in the appointed
Douay phrase. Providential consolation becomes a deadpan labor complaint, and
the joke depends upon the scene's contrast between practical usefulness and
affection rather than upon the isolated quotation.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\subsection*{``Consider the lilies'' against an advertising machine\enspace\properrefs{Gosp.}}

Ray Bradbury's \emph{Fahrenheit 451}, part~2, ``The Sieve and the Sand,''
places ``Consider the lilies'' in Montag's attempted memorization while the
Denham's Dentifrice jingle repeatedly breaks into his attention. The Library
of America edition prints the scene at pp.~294--296, especially p.~295, and
its textual note at p.~877 identifies Matthew~6:28. The Gospel phrase becomes
the contested object of memory inside an invasive commercial soundscape. The
verse remains sympathetically invoked; interruption and medium, rather than a
new theological assertion, give it literary and commercial force.

\subsection*{``O taste and see'' as an urban poetics of incorporation\enspace\properrefs{Off.}}

Denise Levertov's ``O Taste and See,'' in the 1964 collection of that name,
begins from a subway Bible poster and redirects the phrase ``O taste and see''
into a sensory catalogue of grief, mercy, language, weather, fruit,
embodiment, and mortality. \emph{The Norton Anthology of Poetry}, 5th ed.,
p.~1678, notes~1--2, identifies Psalm~34:8 in Hebrew numbering. The
Offertory's invitation expands into an urban poetics of incorporation rather
than remaining a detached religious slogan. The poem's widening of the object
does not necessarily exclude God; its ambiguity resists a simply secular
classification.
