\section*{The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities}

\begin{studyframe}
The following six proposals are exploratory editorial syntheses developed in
this AI-assisted guide. Their anchors are appointed words and inspected
reception, but the proposed combinations are not claims about a compiler's
intention. Local precedents identify earlier repository proposals; external
near analogues identify only the specific relationship each source supplies.
\end{studyframe}

\begin{proposal}{P1. Helpers beneath the one Master\enspace\properrefs{Grad., Gosp., Off.}}

\pfield{Anchors}{The Gradual prefers the Lord to humans and princes;
the Offertory promises the Lord's angelic camp; Matthew~6:24 excludes two
masters.}

\pfield{Mechanism}{Reading these together distinguishes mediation from
mastery. A helper can act under God without occupying the place of God.}

\pfield{Fruit}{Receive human and angelic assistance gratefully while
resisting the demand that a benefactor become the final object of loyalty.}

\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses}{The juxtaposition answers
a possible apparent contradiction between mistrusting princes and welcoming
an angel. Both can serve dependence on the Lord.}

\pfield{Precedent}{Earlier GPT work on this proper and Claude's proposal~P5
supply local precedents. An external near analogue is located in Augustine's
Psalm~117.4: good humans and angels help through God. His Psalm~33, second
exposition, §10, separately identifies the angel with Christ. Neither locus
joins these chants to Matthew's two-master saying.}

\pfield{Strongest limit}{The angel's received identifications differ, and
the proposal cannot turn every mediator into the same kind of being or office.
The complete three-element arrangement remains an exploratory joining.}

\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{P2. Protection makes pilgrimage possible\enspace\properrefs{Int., Gosp., Off.}}

\pfield{Anchors}{The Introit calls God protector and longs for his courts;
the Offertory sets an angelic camp around the reverent; the Gospel addresses
anxious concern for necessities.}

\pfield{Mechanism}{Protection supports movement toward God's house and
kingdom. Security can be received for faithful living instead of made into
a demand for exhaustive control.}

\pfield{Fruit}{Accept enough protection to act and worship without treating
a fully secured future as the condition for obedience.}

\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses}{The desired courts and
the surrounding camp become complementary spatial images: a destination
and protection on the way.}

\pfield{Precedent}{Earlier GPT work on this proper supplies a local
precedent. External near analogues occur in Augustine's Psalm~83.8, where God
is the house's wall, and in his Psalm~33, second exposition, §10, on the
protecting angel. These passages do not explicitly combine the courts and
camp or connect them with Matthew's anxiety discourse.}

\pfield{Strongest limit}{Protection is not physical immunity. The metaphor
cannot promise that a believer will escape poverty, attack, or death, and the
proposed spatial joining remains exploratory.}

\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{P3. What is added serves where one is led\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Gosp., Comm., Postcomm.}}

\pfield{Anchors}{The Gospel and Communion promise \latin{adiciéntur}; the
Collect asks \latin{dirigátur}; the Postcommunion asks \latin{ducant}.}

\pfield{Mechanism}{Addition names provision along a directed life, while
the prayers name its saving destination. The end supplies the measure for
the use of present goods.}

\pfield{Fruit}{Judge a possession or opportunity by its service to faithful
living and final salvation, rather than by accumulation alone.}

\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses}{The repeated promise
of addition acquires a destination from prayers that do not use its verb.}

\pfield{Precedent}{Earlier GPT work on this proper supplies a local
precedent. An external near analogue is located in Augustine,
\emph{De sermone Domini}, II.17.58: the divine physician gives or withholds
with perpetual rest in view. That passage does not join the Gospel to these
Collect and Postcommunion petitions.}

\pfield{Strongest limit}{Matthew's added things have the stated antecedent
of food, drink, and clothing. The proposal cannot expand them into every
wanted good or make earthly prosperity proof of saving direction.}

\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{P4. Tasting goodness loosens wealth's mastery\enspace\properrefs{Gosp., Off., Sec., Comm.}}

\pfield{Anchors}{Mammon can become a master; the Offertory invites tasting;
the Secret presents a saving victim; the Communion puts the kingdom first.}

\pfield{Mechanism}{The sequence can be read as an education of receiving:
goodness is welcomed as gift, and goods are used and shared under the
kingdom's priority instead of possessed as final security.}

\pfield{Fruit}{Let grateful reception redirect desire toward charity and
release the demand to secure happiness through ownership.}

\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses}{The Gospel's negative
alternative and the Offertory's positive invitation address the same
receiver's attachments on either side of the liturgy's movement toward
Communion.}

\pfield{Precedent}{Local precedents occur in earlier GPT work on this proper
and an analogous proposal for the Eighth Sunday, whose ritual association
differs. External near analogues occur in Augustine's Psalm~33, second
exposition, §§8--14, especially the Body and Blood at §11, and his
Psalm~117.6, which connects tasting with Christ's Passion at verse~12.
That latter verse is not appointed in this Gradual. Neither source supplies
the full four-element joining.}

\pfield{Strongest limit}{Food, inward taste, and \latin{hóstia} are not
interchangeable terms. Sacramental fruit is not measured by pleasant feeling;
the proposed education of desire remains a theological possibility.}

\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{P5. Desire is directed toward its object\enspace\properrefs{Int., Ep.}}

\pfield{Anchors}{The Introit's soul \latin{concupíscit} for the Lord's
courts. The Epistle uses \latin{concupíscit} in the flesh--Spirit conflict and
ends with the noun \latin{concupiscéntiis} among what belongs to crucified
passions.}

\pfield{Mechanism}{The related vocabulary places a holy object of longing
beside desires that oppose the Spirit. Moral transformation orders desire;
it does not require the extinction of all desire.}

\pfield{Fruit}{Examine what is desired and how its pursuit serves God,
rather than treating intensity itself as proof of either holiness or sin.}

\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses}{Words that sound hostile
in Paul's conflict become words of devotion in the opening chant.}

\pfield{Precedent}{Claude's earlier proposal~P6 on this proper supplies a
local precedent. An external near analogue is positively located:
Chrysostom actually quotes the soul's longing in Psalm~83:3 while explaining
Galatians~5:17 (\emph{In Gal.}, ch.~5). Augustine's Psalm~83.3, 7, 9--11
also develops the soul's desire and its struggle. Chrysostom establishes a
Psalm--Epistle link, without expounding this Roman appointment as a unit.}

\pfield{Strongest limit}{The lexical echo does not make every occurrence
of the word morally equivalent. The different desired objects and Paul's
specific oppositions govern the comparison.}

\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{P6. The creature cannot add what the Father can\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Gosp., Comm.}}

\pfield{Anchors}{Matthew~6:27 asks who can \latin{adícere} a cubit to
stature through anxious thought; 6:33 promises \latin{adiciéntur}, repeated
at Communion. The Collect confesses mortal instability without God.}

\pfield{Mechanism}{The paired forms of addition contrast the reach of
anxious human agency with the Father's provision. This concerns who can give,
where P3 concerns the end to which gifts are directed.}

\pfield{Fruit}{Work within creaturely responsibility and receive what cannot
be produced by worry as gift.}

\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses}{The question within
the Gospel is answered by a later promise whose verb returns in the
Communion, and the Collect makes dependence a common petition.}

\pfield{Precedent}{Claude's earlier proposal~P2 on this proper supplies a
local precedent. An external near analogue is located in Augustine,
\emph{De sermone Domini}, II.15--17, §§51--58, which explains stature,
necessary goods, labor, and providence. It does not make the exact joining
with these Roman prayers and chant.}

\pfield{Strongest limit}{The appointed Latin and English speak of stature;
the comparison must not silently replace that object with lifespan. The
promise of provision remains compatible with labor and with the faithful
person's experience of need.}

\end{proposal}
