\section*{The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities}

\noindent\emph{The following six connections are exploratory editorial and
AI-assisted proposals. They are not sourced historical intent or teaching
attributed to the witnesses cited elsewhere.}

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{The angel and the princes distinguish mediation from mastery.\enspace\properrefs{Grad., Gosp., Off.}}
\emph{Precedent result: near analogue located.} The Gradual denies final
trust to men and princes, the Offertory receives an angelic protector, and the
Gospel makes mastery exclusive. Their mechanism is a distinction between an
instrument through which God helps and a lord to whom allegiance terminates.
\textbf{Fruit.} Created aid can be welcomed without becoming the final object
of trust. \textbf{What a separate reading misses.} The apparent opposition
between refusing princes and receiving an angel becomes a coherent ordering
only when Matthew's two masters stands between them. \textbf{Strongest
limit.} Augustine does not cite Matthew here; the relation neither forbids
ordinary authority and expertise nor identifies every created aid with a
rival master.

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{The protective camp opens toward the desired courts.\enspace\properrefs{Int., Gosp., Off.}}
\emph{Precedent result: near analogue located.} The Offertory's encircling
camp, the Introit's desired courts, and the Gospel's refusal of anxious mastery
can recast self-enclosure as safety received from God and open toward worship
and pilgrimage. \textbf{Fruit.} Security becomes belonging and direction
rather than possession of an invulnerable boundary. \textbf{What a separate
reading misses.} Camp and court name opposite spatial motions---enclosure and
approach---that the Gospel can order without collapsing. \textbf{Strongest
limit.} The psalms use different spatial genres, the Offertory names no
personal guardian angel, and the connection promises no physical immunity.

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{What is added does not become what directs or leads.\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Gosp., Comm., Postcomm.}}
\emph{Precedent result: near analogue located.} Material necessities are
\latin{adicientur}; divine helps direct mortality \latin{ad salutaria}; and
the sacraments \latin{ducant} toward salvation's perpetual effect. The
mechanism is grammatical agency: added goods accompany the journey, while
grace gives it direction and end. \textbf{Fruit.} Provision can be received
without being mistaken for the good that orders life. \textbf{What a separate
reading misses.} The Collect's beginning and the Postcommunion's ending frame
the Gospel and Communion with verbs of finality. \textbf{Strongest limit.}
The verbs belong to different genres, establish no hidden compositional plan,
and support no prosperity promise.

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Tasting can expose which master orders desire.\enspace\properrefs{Gosp., Off., Sec., Comm.}}
\emph{Precedent result: near analogue located.} The Gospel's mutually
exclusive service can be read through appetite: tasting the Lord, approaching
the saving victim, and receiving again the kingdom's priority place desire
away from mammon's mastery. \textbf{Fruit.} What one seeks, consumes, and
shares can disclose the allegiance under which desire is being trained.
\textbf{What a separate reading misses.} The Offertory's sensory verb becomes
a bridge between the Gospel's lordship and the sacramental action of Secret
and Communion. \textbf{Strongest limit.} Augustine's Eucharistic tasting does
not exhaust Psalm~33's literal sense, felt sweetness does not measure
sacramental fruit, and the relation proves no compiler's intent.

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{One fruit answers a Church liable to slide.\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Ep., Postcomm.}}
\emph{Precedent result: near analogue located.} The Collect's Church and
mortal humanity, the Epistle's singular fruit, and the Postcommunion's
corporate purification and strengthening can place personal transformation
inside an ecclesial reception of grace. \textbf{Fruit.} Virtue and sacrament
are freed from an isolated self-improvement model. \textbf{What a separate
reading misses.} Singular fruit and plural sacramental recipients disclose
one grace taking distinct moral forms within one body. \textbf{Strongest
limit.} Grammatical number proves no ecclesiological theory, personal
responsibility remains, and the Vulgate/common-Greek difference forbids
numerology.

\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{One desire-root turns toward opposite objects.\enspace\properrefs{Int., Ep.}}
\emph{Precedent result: near analogue located.} The Introit's
\latin{concupiscit anima mea in atria Domini} and the Epistle's
\latin{caro concupiscit adversus spiritum}, together with
\latin{concupiscentiis} in the immediate Pauline context, let one desire-root
stage not desire's extinction but its redirection. \textbf{Fruit.} Moral
struggle receives a positive object of longing in God's courts.
\textbf{What a separate reading misses.} The same verbal root can carry desire
toward worship or against the Spirit, so the issue becomes its object and
order rather than mere intensity. \textbf{Strongest limit.} The common root
may be ordinary coincidence; the Introit form occurs in the psalm verse, and
the positive Pauline \latin{caro} clause lies one clause beyond the appointed
cut. No compiler's intention follows.
