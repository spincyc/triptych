\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\label{triptych:brief-synthesis:start}
\begingroup\fontsize{9.5}{11.5}\selectfont
\setlength{\parskip}{0.32em}

God's continuing help enables the Church to seek his kingdom with an undivided
allegiance. The Spirit gives moral fruit, the Father knows bodily need, and
the saving offering and sacraments purify and strengthen the people who ask
to be led to perpetual salvation.

\subsection*{Divine help sets mortal life in motion\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Ep., Gosp., Comm., Postcomm.}}

The Collect begins with the Church and then names the weakness shared by her
members: \latin{sine te lábitur humána mortálitas}. Mortal humanity slips
without God. The prayer asks that the same humanity be withdrawn from harms
and directed toward saving things by his continual helps. Withdrawal has a
positive destination. The Church seeks the favor that makes obedience
possible and the direction in which obedience can advance.

Paul gives that advance its active form: walk by the Spirit. Being led does
not make the recipient motionless; the Spirit's fruit appears in charity,
peace, patience and the other virtues. The flanking verses make the communal
work explicit: serve one another through charity, cease biting and devouring,
restore the fallen gently, and bear one another's burdens
(Gal.~5:13--15, 25--6:2). The appointed warning against enmity, faction and
envy belongs to this life together. Seeking the kingdom concerns the way
Christ's members treat one another as well as their inward intentions.

Chrysostom locates the flesh's disorder in moral choice, not in bodily
substance. The body hears, preaches and writes in God's service; strife and
heresy are not properties of physical matter. Augustine distinguishes the
opposition a person suffers from the sinful consent a person gives. Conflict
can remain while grace makes resistance fruitful. Aquinas describes virtuous
acts as fruits produced by the Spirit, with a delight that begins beatitude
without becoming its final fulfillment (Chrysostom, \emph{Galatians} 5;
Augustine, \emph{Exp. Gal.} §§47--51; Aquinas, \emph{Super Gal.} V,
lects.~6--7).

The Gospel names the decisive allegiance: God and mammon cannot both be
masters. Food and clothing remain good, but they cannot dictate the end of
life. The kingdom that Christ commands his hearers to seek is the kingdom
Paul says the practitioners of the flesh's works will not obtain. The
Communion repeats \latin{Primum quǽrite regnum Dei}; the Postcommunion asks
that the sacraments lead the recipients to perpetual salvation. Command,
warning and petition concern one people whose salvation is still sought as
a gift, with real conduct required of them.

\subsection*{The Father sustains the body and gives helpers their power\enspace\properrefs{Int., Coll., Grad., Gosp., Off.}}

The Gospel reasons from life and body to food and clothing: the greater gifts
already received give ground for confidence about the lesser necessities.
Birds fed by the heavenly Father and grass clothed more splendidly than
Solomon display his care. The flesh Paul commands Christians to crucify with
its vices is therefore not the bodily life Christ declares precious. The
Father knows what his children need; their dependence is a reason for trust
within embodied life.

Chrysostom distinguishes possessing riches from serving them and presents Job
as their steward. His birds and lilies require freedom from anxious care,
not cessation of work. Augustine points to Christ's common purse, Pauline
collections, relief during famine and manual labor. He also names Paul's
hunger and nakedness: divine providence can govern a faithful life through
deprivation. A promise of necessary provision is not a promise that every
faithful person will enjoy visible security (Chrysostom, \emph{Matthew}
homilies~21 §§1--4 and 22 §§1--3; Augustine, \emph{De sermone} II.17
§§57--58).

The Gradual places confidence in God above man and princes. Augustine includes
good angels among these princes and explains that good helpers act through
the God who made them good. Hugh and Bellarmine likewise permit created help
whose power remains received from God. Theodoret stresses another side of
the comparison: humans die, change their minds, or lack power even when
willing, and princely authority is short-lived (Augustine, Ps.~117 §4;
Hugh, Ps.~117:8--9; Bellarmine, p.~370; Theodoret, PG~80:1811--1812).

The Offertory brings angelic protection beside this profession of confidence.
Basil speaks expressly of an angel attending each believer; Augustine reads
the angel as Christ, messenger of the great counsel. Their identifications
differ. In both, deliverance directs the worshipper toward the Lord's
goodness. The Introit's Protector and the Collect's continuing helps name the
God in whom confidence rests, including when help arrives through a minister
(Basil, \emph{Hom. Ps.}~33 §5; Augustine, Ps.~33, second exposition §10).

\subsection*{Holy desire becomes praise and saving reception\enspace\properrefs{Int., Ep., All., Off., Sec., Postcomm.}}

The Introit gives Christian desire a positive object before the lessons
expose its disorders. One day in God's courts is better than thousands; the
soul longs and faints for them. Augustine understands the plea to behold
Christ's face as asking that Christ become known to all. Theodoret hears the
saved people, Christ's body, in that face; Hugh sees the Son shielding sinners
through the wounds of his Passion; Bellarmine appeals to the Messiah's
merits before the Father. These readings make divine protection inseparable
from Christ and his people (Augustine, Ps.~83 §13; Theodoret,
PG~80:1543--1544; Hugh, Ps.~83:10--11; Bellarmine, p.~255).
Chrysostom quotes the soul's longing in Psalm~83:3 while explaining
Galatians~5:17: desire can be directed toward God even while contrary
desires trouble the person. The Introit's attraction to the courts and
the Epistle's conflict thus have an actual link in his commentary
(Chrysostom, \emph{In Gal.}, ch.~5).

The Alleluia answers longing with a shared invitation to rejoice. Augustine
explains approach to the everywhere-present God through restored likeness:
sin estranges, forgiveness and renewal bring near. Hugh applies the same
verse to the Invitatory: the singer who calls others to come must already
walk the commandments. Bellarmine joins interior joy to the lips that express
it. Praise is an act of the person, and the community's joyful voice asks for
a corresponding life (Augustine, Ps.~94 §§1--4; Hugh, Ps.~94:1;
Bellarmine, p.~298).

At the Offertory, the Lord's goodness is to be tasted. The complete Psalm~33
moves from deliverance to instruction in guarded speech, good action and
peace; its just person still knows many afflictions. Augustine explicitly
expounds the tasting by reception of Christ's Body and Blood. Basil names
Christ the true bread and distinguishes a present taste of grace from its
full future enjoyment. Aquinas explains inward experience that leads to
spiritual sight; his paragraph does not name the Eucharistic species
(Ps.~33:13--20; Augustine, second exposition §§10--13; Basil §§5--6;
Aquinas, Ps.~33 nn.~8--9).

The Secret asks that the saving offering become purification of sins and
propitiation before God; the Postcommunion asks purification, strengthening
and a saving end. Schuster expressly links the two prayers and describes
Communion as strengthening virtue toward eternal salvation. The moral demands
of the lessons thus stand within an action in which the Church asks God to
cleanse and sustain her. The saving offering is integral to her response,
not an ornament to a lesson in composure (Schuster, \emph{Sacramentary} III,
pp.~136--138).

\subsection*{Temporal provision serves a lasting saving end\enspace\properrefs{Int., Coll., Gosp., Comm., Postcomm.}}

Christ's \latin{primum} establishes an order of ends. Augustine says that
necessities are sought for the kingdom, while the kingdom is not sought for
necessities. Food and clothing retain their worth as aids; they become
misordered when service to God is made a means of obtaining them. The
Communion's shorter antiphon keeps this priority. Its \latin{ómnia} refers
back to the needs the Gospel has named, even though the chant omits the
Gospel's demonstrative and its phrase about justice (Matt.~6:31--33;
Augustine, \emph{De sermone} II.16--17 §§55--58).

The Father's giving and withholding remain directed toward good. Augustine
uses the physician's care to explain why deprivation can belong within that
direction toward perpetual rest. Anthony, expounding Matthew under his
Fifteenth Sunday with a different Epistle, similarly distinguishes the supreme
good from secondary goods: gifts summon thanksgiving, and loss can be a
trial. This teaching leaves the sufferer's material need real while refusing
to make abundance the measure of divine favor (Augustine, II.17 §58;
Anthony, Sunday~XV §15, trans.~Spilsbury).

The Introit's better day opens toward that final good in Augustine's reading
of everlasting life. The Collect asks perpetual favor amid mortal
instability, and the Postcommunion seeks perpetual salvation through
sacraments that purify and strengthen now. Aquinas compares present virtuous
acts to flowers in relation to future beatitude: their delight has a further
end. Christian hope can therefore value today's obedient act and today's
necessary bread without making either the fullness promised by God
(Augustine, Ps.~83 §14; Aquinas, \emph{Super Gal.} V, lect.~6).

The community's present fruit remains concrete. The Catechism joins Communion
to union with Christ, growth in charity, incorporation in the Church and
commitment to the poor. The last of these belongs explicitly to paragraph
1397. Paul's charity and peace have neighbors to serve; the Church praying
\latin{nos} has members to sustain. The kingdom's priority bears fruit in
shared life as the sacraments lead the worshippers onward
(CCC~1391--1397; Gal.~5:13--6:2).
