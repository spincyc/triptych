\subsection*{The opposition is divided mastery, not spirit against creation\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Ep., Gosp., Comm.}}

The Epistle and Gospel can be misjoined in a way that makes the formulary say
what neither text says: ``spirit'' could be treated as the inward and
immaterial realm, ``flesh'' as bodily necessity, and birds and lilies as a
command to escape ordinary work. The checked witnesses prevent that reading.
Chrysostom on Galatians refuses to blame bodily substance for the works of the
flesh; on Matthew he refuses to turn the birds into a ban on sowing and points
to Paul's labor. Augustine likewise treats the Pauline conflict as wounded
desire under healing grace and the Matthean solicitude as a disorder of
intention. Material food, clothing, work, and bodily life remain good. What
cannot remain is their rule as masters.

The Collect sharpens the distinction. Human mortality \latin{labitur}: the
problem is not that mortality is material but that it slips without God.
Divine helps withdraw it from harms and direct it toward saving things. Paul
commands the same directed agency with \latin{Spíritu ambuláte}; Christ names
its first object, the kingdom and justice of God. The Communion repeats the
priority after reception. No text allows grace to become an excuse for
inactivity; none permits activity to become self-salvation.

The relation is therefore one of order. Flesh in Paul's polemical sense is
human life turned against the Spirit; mammon is a created good turned into a
lord; solicitude is prudent concern turned into attempted mastery of a future
the creature cannot secure. Grace heals the order without annihilating the
creature. A Spirit-led person can plant, reap, plan, clothe, serve, govern,
and provide while refusing to make any result the final measure of fidelity.

The doctrinal limit belongs inside the synthesis. Providence is not a promise
that the faithful will escape hunger or deprivation, and Spirit-bearing life
is not recognizable by comfort. The Sermon on the Mount blesses persecuted
disciples; Paul names crucifixion of passions and the Cross. The kingdom sought
first can demand costly action, including action for a neighbor whose needs
are not dismissed by an appeal to the birds.

\subsection*{Mediation is received under God rather than rejected as rivalry\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Grad., Gosp., Off., Sec.}}

The Gradual and Offertory place an apparent tension beside the altar. Trust
the Lord rather than humans or princes; an angel of the Lord encamps around
those who fear him. If ``rather than princes'' meant that every created
mediation betrayed faith, the angelic promise would contradict it. Augustine
and Hugh resolve the tension by distinguishing source from instrument. Good
people and good angels help through goodness and power received from God. The
recipient may acknowledge the real help without allowing confidence to stop
there. Theodoret's direct Greek reading is sharper at the appointed verses:
mortal helpers change, fail, and die, and princely authority is brief, whereas
God both wills and can accomplish the good (PG~80:1811--1812). The difference
in emphasis is retained without converting it into a denial of all mediation.

The Gospel extends that distinction into material providence. The Father feeds
birds and clothes grass through the created order; the disciple can work and
receive the fruit of work without serving it. The Collect's plural
\latin{auxiliis} fits this economy. Sacraments, angels, neighbors, knowledge,
authority, and material means may mediate aid. Their created status does not
make them illusory; their dependence prevents them from becoming gods.

The Secret gives the distinction its christological center. The saving victim
is neither one created aid among others nor an offering that competes with
God's mercy. It is Christ's one self-offering made sacramentally present. The
Father gives, the Son offers, and the Church participates by grace. Every
other mediation---angelic, ministerial, social, or material---is ordered under
that one Mediator and sacrifice.

This cross-proper relation protects against contrary errors. Rejecting every
human or ecclesial help in the name of Psalm~117 turns final trust into
isolation. Treating an angel, leader, institution, technique, or sacrament as
an autonomous guarantee turns mediation into mastery. The chants instead
teach grateful reception, truthful discernment, and confidence that passes
through the gift to the Giver.

\subsection*{The desired courts open toward a kingdom received at the altar\enspace\properrefs{Int., All., Gosp., Off., Comm., Postcomm.}}

The Introit and Alleluia begin with movement: the soul desires the courts;
the assembly is told to come. Augustine reads the one better day as eternity
and the anointed face as Christ; his exposition of Psalm~94 says coming to God
means restoration to likeness rather than geographical motion alone. The
literal worshipping body nevertheless matters. It enters a court, hears the
Gospel, brings gifts, and receives a sacrament. Spiritual approach is enacted
through embodied liturgy.

Theodoret supplies complementary and partly different Greek reception. On
Psalm~83 he applies exile-longing to Christian churches and altars but reads
the anointed face corporately as Christ's saved body, not as the head
(PG~80:1537--1544). On Psalm~94 he transmits a Josianic repentance setting and
extends the opening hymn to apostles and martyrs (PG~80:1639--1640). These are
received interpretations, not recovered composition dates.

Christ identifies the destination as the kingdom and its justice. The
Offertory then joins protection to \latin{gustáte et vidéte}. Basil,
Augustine, and Aquinas receive Psalm~33 with spiritual, christological,
angelic, and Eucharistic depths. The sequence warrants a sacramental
application: the worshipper who has heard the kingdom sought first prepares
to taste the Lord's goodness under the saving victim. Yet the psalm's literal
invitation to experience divine goodness remains wider than one sacramental
application, and Eucharistic grace is never measured by bodily taste or felt
sweetness.

The Communion compresses the Gospel to its governing priority. It does not
teach that reception purchases ``all things.'' It places the receiving person
under the dominical word: the kingdom is first, necessities are added. The
Postcommunion then carries the movement beyond the altar. The sacraments lead
to perpetual salvation's effect. The courts are a real place of grace and a
sign of the final dwelling; the received kingdom is already active and not yet
unveiled in fullness.

This relation joins desire, command, sacrifice, reception, and hope without
claiming a recoverable compiler's intention. The evidence is the appointed
sequence and documented reception, not an assertion that a medieval redactor
planned a five-stage symbolic program. Its theological fruit is more modest:
Christian desire is not satisfied by entering a building or feeling a moment;
it is given Christ, reordered toward the kingdom, and led onward.

\subsection*{Spirit-bearing fruit and sacramental fruit refuse both moralism and mechanism\enspace\properrefs{Ep., Sec., Postcomm.}}

The Epistle names actions that exclude from the kingdom and a fruit produced
by the Spirit. Augustine and Aquinas emphasize the fruit's coherence in
charity and the grace-enabled character of virtuous action. The Secret and
Postcommunion name another field of agency: the saving victim purifies and
propitiates; the sacraments purify, strengthen, and lead. Read together, the
texts do not assign morality to the believer and grace to the ritual. Grace
precedes, accompanies, and perfects both.

Moralism would make the twelve Vulgate fruit terms a checklist of achievements
by which one secures the kingdom. Paul instead calls them the Spirit's fruit
and makes belonging to Christ prior to the crucifixion of disordered passions.
Sacramental mechanism would make external reception sufficient regardless of
repentance and charity. The orations instead ask for effects that remain gifts
and the Church's doctrine retains the conditions of fruitful reception. The
Eucharist strengthens charity and cleanses venial sin; it does not forgive
unrepented mortal sin apart from Reconciliation or make subjective sweetness
an infallible sign (CCC~1391--1396).

The singular fruit is especially helpful when placed beside the corporate
pronouns. The Collect asks for the Church and names mortal humanity; the
Secret says ``our sins''; the Postcommunion asks purification and strength for
``us.'' The Spirit does not form isolated moral specimens. The Eucharist joins
persons to Christ and therefore more deeply to his body. Ecclesial unity is a
sacramental fruit, while charity, patience, fidelity, and gentleness are forms
that unity must take in conduct.

The historical prayer transmission keeps this synthesis honest. The three
orations travel together in Gellone, Sant Ruf, and Vilabertran under a Sunday
number different from 1962. They establish a received set, not a patristic
commentary on Galatians or Matthew. The doctrinal relation is source-grounded
in the present formulary and the Church's account of grace, but it is not put
into an anonymous oration author's mouth.

\subsection*{Liturgy answers anxiety by forming a pattern of dependence\enspace\properrefs{Int., Grad., All., Gosp., Off., Comm.}}

Matthew's anxious person tries to carry food, clothing, stature, and tomorrow
in advance. Christ's answer is not a technique for feeling calm. It is a
different pattern of belonging: one Father knows, one kingdom is first, and
added goods are received as added. The chants surrounding the Gospel make
that pattern performative.

The Introit asks the protector to look and declares one day in his courts
better. The Gradual locates confidence in God. The Alleluia turns the body
toward praise and the ear toward today's command. The Offertory joins reverent
fear, angelic encampment, tasting, and hope. The Communion returns to the
kingdom's priority. Each act trains attention away from autonomous control
and toward promise, presence, praise, gift, and obedient seeking.

The complete psalms prevent this pattern from becoming escapism. Psalm~83
passes through the vale of tears. Psalm~117 remembers distress and encircling
enemies. Psalm~94 warns about hardened hearts and denied rest. Psalm~33 begins
in danger and teaches guarded speech, positive good, and pursued peace. Trust
is therefore practiced amid threat, work, and moral responsibility rather
than after their removal.

Nor is anxiety itself treated as a moral spectacle by which suffering persons
are blamed. The Collect begins with shared mortal instability and asks for
perpetual mercy. The Gospel grounds the command in the Father's knowledge,
not in the disciple's emotional success. The liturgy offers repeated acts of
dependence because the Church expects mortal humanity to need them repeatedly.
Its answer is patient formation: walk, praise, seek, offer, receive, and be led.
