\subsection*{One kingdom is excluded, sought, and received as first\enspace\properrefs{Int., Ep., Gosp., Comm., Postcomm.}}

The Epistle and Gospel meet at the exact phrase \latin{regnum Dei}. Paul
warns that those who practice the works of the flesh will not obtain the
kingdom; Christ commands that the kingdom and its justice be sought first.
The Communion brings the command to sacramental reception in an adapted form:
\latin{Primum quǽrite regnum Dei}. The Epistle supplies the negative boundary,
the Gospel the positive priority, and the Communion the enacted return.

This movement makes ``first'' more than an ordering of devotional interests.
Fornication, idolatry, enmity, drunkenness, and their like are acts of rival
service. Mammon names the same disorder at the level of mastery: a created
good becomes a lord when its possession, loss, or promise governs allegiance.
The necessities Christ says will be added can therefore never become the
kingdom's purpose or the price of seeking it.
Chrysostom tests that order against the practical objection that trust would
abolish work: the birds do not forbid sowing, and freedom from anxious
solicitude is not idleness (\emph{Homilies 21--22 on Matthew}).

Psalm~83 and the Postcommunion place this allegiance within a longer horizon.
The Introit prefers one day in God's courts to thousands elsewhere; Augustine
receives that day as the everlasting communion toward which the pilgrim
Church travels. The Postcommunion asks that the sacraments lead to salvation's
perpetual effect. Schuster likewise reads the Communion as the Gospel's
enacted return after oblation and sacrifice (\emph{The Sacramentary}, III,
pp.~136--138). The kingdom is sought in present obedience, received under the
Lord's word at the altar, and desired as the final dwelling with God
(Augustine, \emph{Exposition on Psalm 84} §§13--15).

\subsection*{Grace makes obedience possible without making it passive\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Ep., Gosp., Postcomm.}}

The Collect confesses that unaided mortal humanity slips and asks that the
Church be preserved, withdrawn from harms, and directed toward saving things.
The Epistle nevertheless commands walking by the Spirit and crucifying
disordered desires; the Gospel commands seeking; the Postcommunion asks to be
led. The prayers and lessons speak of one grace-enabled agency: God initiates,
heals, directs, and strengthens a person who truly walks and seeks.

Augustine describes this order by joining divine grace to the real command of
charity. Fallen nature cannot heal itself, yet grace does not render the will
idle; it restores freedom for obedience (\emph{On Nature and Grace} 67 [57]).
His Sermon-on-the-Mount exposition gives ``first'' an inward and practical
order: temporal goods serve the kingdom rather than turning the kingdom into
their servant (II.14--16 §§47--53). Dependence on grace is therefore not
passivity, and the Gospel's imperatives are not detached from divine
initiative.

Chrysostom joins human diligence to divine loving kindness in the Spirit's
fruit and preserves ordinary labor beneath the Father's providence
(\emph{Homily 5 on Galatians}; \emph{Homilies 21--22 on Matthew}). Aquinas
likewise distinguishes proportionate labor and due foresight from the
solicitude that displaces the final good (\emph{Summa Theologiae} II-II
q.~55 aa.~6--7; q.~83 a.~6). Augustine's healing grace, Chrysostom's
grace-assisted diligence, and Aquinas's ordered prudence together exclude
both autonomous achievement and passive quietism.

The same order governs ordinary need. Christ affirms the value of life and
the body and says that the Father knows food and clothing are needed. Anxiety
is refused as a master because it cannot create life or guarantee the future,
not because prudent work is alien to grace. The Collect's confession rescues
action from self-salvation; the command to seek rescues dependence from
quietism.

\subsection*{Final reliance leaves room for created mediation\enspace\properrefs{Grad., Gosp., Off.}}

The Gradual prefers trust in the Lord to confidence in men or princes. The
Offertory then promises that the Lord's angel encamps around those who fear
him. The chants distinguish final reliance from instrumental service: a
creature may truly help without becoming the terminus of hope or the master
named by the Gospel.

Augustine says that good human and angelic help acts through goodness and
power received from God (\emph{Exposition on Psalm 118} §4). Hugh of
Saint-Cher follows that instrumental trajectory at Psalm~117:8--9. Theodoret
stresses the instability of mortal and princely aid: created power changes,
fails, and passes, while God wills and can accomplish the good
(\emph{Interpretatio in Psalmos}, PG~80:1811--1812). His sharp contrast and
Augustine's account of dependent instruments remain distinct accents within
the same ordering of trust.

Psalm~33 supplies the positive image of mediation. Basil reads the angelic
camp as a surrounding rampart, and Augustine reads the Angel
christologically before carrying ``taste and see'' toward Eucharistic
reception. The verse neither condemns friendship, authority, expertise, or
angelic ministry nor makes any created minister infallible. The angel encamps;
the Lord delivers and is tasted. Mediation serves well when it passes the
heart toward God rather than fixing the heart upon itself.

\subsection*{Providence protects creation against an anti-bodily reading\enspace\properrefs{Ep., Grad., All., Gosp., Off., Comm.}}

Galatians' flesh--Spirit opposition cannot be identified with a division
between the evil body and a good immaterial soul. Chrysostom locates the
conflict in opposed moral choices and explicitly refuses to blame bodily
substance (\emph{Homily 5 on Galatians}). Matthew confirms the distinction:
bodily life is more than food, the body more than clothing, and both stand
within the Father's knowledge. Mammon is not material creation but created
wealth served as lord.

Chrysostom also refuses the inference that the birds prohibit sowing. Their
freedom from anxiety does not turn into idleness; it exposes the inability of
solicitude to secure life (\emph{Homilies 21--22 on Matthew}). Aquinas
distinguishes prudent provision from inordinate solicitude (\emph{Summa
Theologiae} II-II q.~55 aa.~6--7; q.~83 a.~6). Providence promises no
prosperity or exemption from deprivation. It frees labor and care from the
fantasy that their success can become lord over tomorrow.

The chants give that freedom embodied actions. The Gradual trusts, the
Alleluia praises and hears, the Offertory fears and tastes, and the Communion
renews the kingdom's priority. These are not escapes from created life. They
form the body within worship so that real needs can be received as gifts and
real duties performed without mammon or anxiety becoming master.

\subsection*{The Spirit's fruit and sacramental fruit belong to one ecclesial economy\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Ep., Off., Sec., Gosp., Comm., Postcomm.}}

The Epistle names one fruit of the Spirit through a plurality of virtues.
Aquinas identifies such fruit as grace-produced virtuous action bearing the
sweetness proper to its good end (\emph{Super Galatas} 5, lect.~6, [87779]).
The Offertory's tasting, the Secret's saving victim, and the Postcommunion's
purification, strengthening, and leading join this moral fruit to sacramental
gift. Augustine's Eucharistic reading of ``taste and see'' gives the movement
a direct witness in the Church's reception.

The grammar is ecclesial throughout. The Collect names the Church; Paul
addresses brethren who belong to Christ; the Gospel says ``your Father'';
the Secret speaks of our sins; the Postcommunion asks that the sacraments act
upon us. The Eucharistic sacrifice makes Christ's one offering sacramentally
present, and Communion deepens union with Christ and his body. Charity and the
other fruits give that union visible moral form (CCC §§1362--1368,
1391--1396, 1830--1832).

Sacramental fruit is neither automatic nor reducible to felt sweetness.
Paul's warning preserves personal responsibility, while the orations preserve
the priority of grace. Those who are Christ's crucify disordered desire; the
saving victim purifies; the sacraments strengthen and lead. The kingdom's
priority is thus received by persons within the Church and becomes a life of
Spirit-bearing charity ordered toward salvation.
