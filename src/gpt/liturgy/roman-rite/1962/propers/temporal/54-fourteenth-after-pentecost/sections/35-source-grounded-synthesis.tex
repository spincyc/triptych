\section*{Source-Grounded Synthesis Across the Propers}

\subsection*{Freedom takes the form of service\enspace\properrefs{Coll., Ep., Gosp., Comm.}}

The full argument of Galatians gives the Epistle's moral contrast an ecclesial
setting. Paul addresses churches called away from their former service of
non-gods and toward the promise, adoption, and freedom given in Christ
(Gal.~3--4). Immediately before the appointed passage, he commands service
through charity and warns against biting and devouring one another
(5:13--15). The works that follow include social antagonisms as well as bodily
excesses. Spirit-led freedom establishes a way of living with others.

The Gospel's two-master saying and the Communion's first priority address a
corresponding question of service. The disciple's necessities remain real,
but possessions cannot command the allegiance due to God. The Collect asks
that divine helps move unstable mortal life toward what saves. Chrysostom's
Galatians commentary excludes the body from blame as an evil substance;
his Matthew homilies distinguish wealth possessed from wealth served
(\emph{In Gal.}, ch.~5; \emph{Hom. in Matt.}~21--22). The common direction is
active fidelity under God, expressed in charity toward a neighbor. Paul's
kingdom warning and Christ's kingdom command have this practical force even
though Galatians and Matthew address different original audiences.

\subsection*{Trust remembers danger and accepts aid\enspace\properrefs{Int., Grad., Gosp., Off.}}

The complete psalms place the short chants amid real vulnerability. Psalm~83
passes through a vale of tears on the way to God's house; Psalm~117 remembers
distress and surrounding enemies; Psalm~33 recalls deliverance and teaches
fear of the Lord, guarded speech, and the pursuit of peace. Their praise
arises within lives exposed to trouble. The appointed Gradual's preference
for God over princes belongs to the communal thanksgiving of Psalm~117,
not to a command that persons cease helping one another.

Augustine makes this dependence explicit: good people and angels help through
God's goodness (Ps.~117.4). Theodoret emphasizes how little mortal and changing
princes can finally secure (PG~80:1811--1812). The Offertory's angelic camp
expresses protection under the same Lord, with distinct received readings:
Basil's guardian, Augustine's Christ as messenger, and Aquinas's minister,
Christ, or prelate (\emph{Hom. in Ps.}~33.5--6; Ps.~33, second exposition,
§10; \emph{In Ps.}~33.8). Matthew supplies the Father's knowledge of need.
Augustine's account of Paul's hunger and nakedness keeps that knowledge from
being equated with exemption from deprivation (\emph{De sermone Domini},
II.17.58). Confidence can therefore accept another person's aid, act for
someone in distress, and endure what present help cannot remove.

\subsection*{Praise opens the hearer toward obedience\enspace\properrefs{Int., All., Ep., Gosp.}}

The Alleluia appoints Psalm~94:1, the joyful invitation. The complete psalm
continues into adoration, hearing God's voice today, and the warning against
hardness of heart. Hebrews~3--4 explicitly receives that later warning and
develops its promise of rest. Those later verses are not sung in this
Alleluia; they disclose the canonical context in which its summons stands.
Joyful approach and obedient hearing belong to one psalm.

Augustine's restored divine likeness gives the summons a moral depth
(Ps.~94.2--3). Hugh's Matins interpretation adds the concrete discipline of
attentive common praise (\emph{Postilla}, Ps.~94:1). The Introit's desire for
the courts thus meets an invitation to come as a hearer whose conduct can
change. In Chrysostom's explanation of Galatians~5:17, the actual citation of
Psalm~83:3 shows that longing can serve God: desire's direction distinguishes
the soul's attraction to the courts from desires resisting the Spirit
(\emph{In Gal.}, ch.~5). Christ names the kingdom and its justice as the
first object of seeking. The Introit's longing and Alleluia's invitation
address the same worshipper whom Paul calls to walk by the Spirit and Christ
commands to seek the kingdom. Praise gives voice to a desire whose direction
appears in obedience.

\subsection*{Tasting reaches charity and peace\enspace\properrefs{Ep., Off., Sec., Postcomm.}}

The Offertory ends with tasting and seeing the Lord's goodness. Its chant
does not include the following beatitude or the psalm's later instructions,
but the complete Psalm~33 carries experience of deliverance into truthful
speech, positive good, and pursued peace. First Peter~2:1--3 receives the
tasting language beside the rejection of malice, deceit, hypocrisy, envy,
and detraction; 3:10--12 receives the psalm's instruction about speech and
peace. This apostolic use gives the taste of goodness a demanding communal
consequence. The same kinds of hostility appear in the Galatian catalogue.

Augustine's explicit application to Christ's Body and Blood supplies a direct
Eucharistic reception of the Offertory verse (Ps.~33, second exposition, §11).
Basil's exposition speaks of Christ as true bread and an experienced knowledge
of goodness; Aquinas explains inward taste before sight without explicitly
naming Eucharistic species at this locus (\emph{Hom. in Ps.}~33.6;
\emph{In Ps.}~33.9). The Secret's petition for purification and the
Postcommunion's petition for strength place moral need before God in prayer.
CCC~1391--1397 relates Eucharistic union and charity to ecclesial unity and
the poor. The result is a specific pattern of life: someone who receives
must resist faction, guard speech, pursue peace, and recognize a neighbor's
need. These are consequences of grace, whose effects the prayers ask God to
bring about.

\subsection*{Added necessities remain beneath the final end\enspace\properrefs{Int., Coll., Gosp., Comm., Postcomm.}}

Matthew's preceding temptation narrative already places bodily need under
God's word by quoting Deuteronomy~8:3 (Matt.~4:4). Deuteronomy's wider chapter
joins hunger, provision, obedience, and the danger of forgetting God in
prosperity. Matthew~6 likewise places food and clothing within the Father's
care while his kingdom and justice govern their use. In both passages,
provision sustains a life of obedience and does not make possessions the
source of security.

The Communion preserves that priority through a liturgical abbreviation:
it moves \latin{primum}, omits the Gospel's \latin{ergo}, \latin{et iustítiam
eius}, and \latin{hæc}, and adds \latin{dicit Dóminus}. Its promise of
addition remains attached to the Gospel's named necessities. Augustine
explains God's giving and withholding by the physician's direction toward
perpetual rest (\emph{De sermone Domini}, II.17.58); the Introit's better day
receives an eternal application in his Psalm~83.14. The Collect's saving
things and the Postcommunion's perpetual salvation name the same order of
ends in petitions. Schuster's direct treatment of the closing prayers joins
sacramental strength for virtue to eternal salvation (III, pp.~137--138).
The present goods are received with gratitude and used responsibly; their
loss does not erase the final destination for which the Church prays.
