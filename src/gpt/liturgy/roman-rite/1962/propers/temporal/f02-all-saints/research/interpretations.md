# All Saints: two proposed interpretations

Research owner: this 1962 F02 leaf, for 1 November 2026. These are editorial whole-Mass syntheses over the independently checked texts and reception in scope.md. None is attributed as a whole-Mass construction to a biblical commentator. Both retain the complete dated inventory, including three Sunday commemoration prayers and the Common Preface. They are complementary accounts of different questions, rather than rival doctrines.

## One people praises God across nations and death

Stable key: `communion-of-saints`. Carrying authors: Bede and Augustine. Bede explains the Lesson's numbered people and worship; Augustine explains the continued ecclesial membership of the departed. Schuster supplies additional reception of the actual ten feast texts, without controlling 1962 occurrence or rubrics.

### Question and checked inference

Apocalypse 7:4 gives an exact number and vv.5–8 repeat twelve thousand for each tribe; v.9 then gives a crowd nobody can count, from every nation. An interpretation which skips the list has evaded the appointed passage's difficulty. Bede, *Explanation of the Apocalypse* I, on7:4, Marshall1878 p.46, takes the definite number as a figure of the entire Church: membership in Abraham's seed includes imitation of his faith, and twelve squared and multiplied by thousands signifies completeness. His warrant is Galatians3:29 and the apostles' mission throughout the world, rather than a statistical ceiling on salvation. On7:5–8, pp.46–51, the ordered names depict virtues from confession to final inheritance. These are Bede's spiritual readings of names, not independent Hebrew etymology.

On7:9, p.52, Bede resumes the victory over persecution after a recapitulation; he also allows Israel's enumeration followed by the Gentiles' salvation. The latter is a genuine alternative in his own exposition. Do not erase it or equate the two identifications in every detail. His interpretation of robes as baptism and palms as Christ's victory makes admission a divine gift. His further robe sense also survives. On7:10–12, pp.52–53, praise confesses God's gifts. Angels may signify the worshipping multitude or actual spirits rejoicing over Gentile salvation. The Introit's actual angels are most readily compared with the latter alternative; Bede does not require that the Introit's angels mean human worshippers.

Augustine, *City of God* XX.9, Dods1871 volumeII, explains why remembrance of the departed at the altar belongs to the Church's present life: their souls remain members of Christ, although their bodies await resurrection. He distinguishes the present kingdom, which still contains wheat and tares, from the final kingdom without evil. His native lemma is Apocalypse20, not Apocalypse7. The editorial inference is that the feast's intercession and offering can be understood as the acts of one Church whose members' conditions differ. Neither all present churchgoers' final salvation nor bodily resurrection already accomplished follows from it.

Augustine's exposition of Ps87 §5 (NPNF PsalmLXXXVIII §4) gives a direct Wisdom3:1 connection: righteous souls await the body's resurrection yet remain under God's protection. The apparent absence of bodily rescue is therefore compatible with the Offertory's divine custody. Christ freely lays down and resumes his life; his members follow him with a different temporal order. This is substantive reasoning, not merely an analogy between two phrases about hands.

### Four senses

- **Literal:** John hears a numbered Israel and sees an innumerable multinational assembly worshipping God and the Lamb; the Mass celebrates all saints and asks mercy and protection for its living participants.
- **Allegorical:** Bede's Abrahamic seed and Augustine's membership in Christ explain the unity of a people gathered through Christ, including departed faithful, whose present and final conditions must still be distinguished.
- **Moral:** Upright praise requires submission to God, confession of fragility, mercy and fidelity. Belonging to the Church demands the Gospel's holiness rather than self-assured counting of the saved.
- **Anagogical:** The kingdom promised in the Beatitudes, the Offertory's peace and final common praise orient the pilgrim Church toward resurrection and completed communion; the living do not yet possess that completion.

## Christ gives rest by making his people humble, pure and peaceful

Stable key: `purification-and-rest`. Carrying authors: Augustine and John Chrysostom. Both develop the Beatitudes as connected formation; Chrysostom expressly connects Christ's yoke to their humble beginning. Aquinas develops the Gradual's difficult promise; Gregory the Great's Wisdom reuse supplies a further account of fruitful suffering. Their additions are attributed at their actual loci.

### Question and checked inference

The Alleluia promises refreshment to the burdened, but the Gospel blesses hunger, mourning and persecution. The Gradual promises no want to those who fear God, while the Offertory acknowledges apparent death. If refreshment meant exemption from costly obedience, the appointed texts would contradict one another.

Chrysostom, MatthewXXXVIII.3–5, reads11:28 within11:25–30. Christ's invitation removes the burden of sin; his yoke is carried through meekness and humility, the same foundation with which he began the Beatitudes. Chrysostom directly asks how a light burden accords with the narrow gate and the cross. His answer includes the present freedom of virtue, vice's greater anxiety and bondage, Christ's example, and promised glory. Do not reduce it to a denial that disciples actually suffer. Only11:28 is appointed; the yoke verses supply literary and authorial explanation.

Augustine, *Sermon on the Mount* I.1.3–I.4.12, takes poverty of spirit as humility and fear of God; it begins the ordered ascent through grief, hunger for righteousness, mercy, purity and peace. InI.2.8 the pure heart is single: God is seen by a purified inward faculty rather than bodily eyes. InI.2.9 peace orders desire under reason and reason under the Son, since one cannot govern lower things without submitting to the higher. External persecution then manifests rather than destroys the established inward good. I.3.10 explains the first and eighth kingdom promises as a return to the beginning which shows completeness; I.4.12 allows real present fulfillment while retaining a future transformation beyond words.

ChrysostomXV.6–7 adds what an exclusively inward exposition could miss: purity cannot be replaced by almsgiving alone, and peacemakers reconcile other people. He immediately reads persecution for righteousness as qualifying peace: agreement in evil is not blessed peace. Reviling is blessed only when false and for Christ's sake. The common moral inference is a renewed person whose peace acts outwardly and can endure opposition without granting every conflict a martyr's crown.

Augustine on the Gradual (native Ps33 enarratioII §§14–15; NPNF HebrewPsalmXXXIV §§13–14) explicitly faces the wealthy sinner's prosperous death and the devout worshipper's poverty. He distinguishes spiritual goods—justice, truth, charity, faith and endurance—from possessions and identifies the living bread with Christ, explicitly citing the hunger Beatitude. Aquinas, *Super Psalmo33* nn.10–11, retains several explanations: spiritual treasure, contentment, providential help and the physician's beneficial withholding of food. Do not turn those into a promise of immediate material abundance. The Gradual omits the first half of33:11, but that clause remains the psalmic context Augustine and Aquinas expound.

Gregory, *Moralia* XII.5.6, quotes Wisdom3:2 while describing preaching despised and the righteous person's body killed. The Spirit makes the example of suffering awaken virtue in others. XII.5.7 gives a distinct bodily-resurrection interpretation. His lemma is Job14:8–9; his actual Wisdom reuse may illuminate the chant without being misrepresented as a continuous Wisdom commentary or an exposition of All Saints.

### Four senses

- **Literal:** Jesus promises blessings to particular dispositions and fidelity, invites the burdened, and promises his kingdom under righteous persecution; the prayers ask pardon, mercy, fruitful offering and protection.
- **Allegorical:** The Son who is meek and reconciles the divided forms God's children; the church's eucharistic prayer asks salvation through him while praising God with the heavenly company.
- **Moral:** Humility, singleness of heart, just desire and practical reconciliation displace pride and sin's burdens. Fear preserves those already called saints; fidelity is tested by suffering rather than guaranteed material comfort.
- **Anagogical:** Present purity and peace anticipate the promised sight of God and final kingdom, while the dead's peace awaits bodily resurrection. Future transformation remains greater than present fulfillment.

## Concrete comparison and consequential differences

The first interpretation asks who belongs to the celebrating company and how its members remain united across death. Its decisive inference is from the apparently bounded number to the Church's completeness, then from continued membership to altar remembrance and intercession. The second asks how Christ's refreshment can coexist with hunger and persecution. Its decisive inference is from humility and reordered desire to a peace suffering cannot simply abolish. Their outcomes differ: common worship and ecclesial membership in the first; formation of desire and public peacemaking in the second. Neither can be reduced to the generic application of behaving well now in order to reach heaven later.

The authors also preserve a real difference at Matthew5's inherited earth. AugustineI.2.4 identifies the stable land of the living and the saints' rest; ChrysostomXV.5 defends a sensible present reward while also retaining the future. Do not silently merge their referents. Likewise Augustine emphasizes ordered peace within; Chrysostom requires reconciliation of others. These are complementary contributions, not evidence that Augustine rejects outward charity or that Chrysostom rejects inward order. Both make several rewards descriptions of the kingdom. Neither makes mourning and persecution enjoyable in themselves or calls all poverty voluntary humility.

The composed orations receive grammatical and liturgical exposition, with Schuster's actual feast and Sunday commentary where checked. No direct patristic commentary on those exact composed bodies was located in the documented bounded searches. Their contribution is argued from their own petitions; the editor must not fill that limit by assigning a prayer to a Father's vocabulary.
