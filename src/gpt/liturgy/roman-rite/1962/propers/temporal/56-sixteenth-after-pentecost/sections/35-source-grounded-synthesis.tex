\sectionguard\section*{Source-Grounded Synthesis Across the Propers}
\subsection*{Fruitfulness remains dependent on its giver}
The Collect asks grace to sustain good works, the Epistle seeks strength for indwelling charity, and the Secret asks purification for participation. Their common consequence is that the fruit of mercy does not outgrow dependence upon mercy. Augustine's exposition of the Communion psalm gives this its sharpest expression: God remains necessary after he has taught the way. The active life therefore has neither an unaided beginning nor a point at which its success makes prayer obsolete. \sourcecite{Collect; Eph.~3:16--20; Secret; Augustine, \textit{Enarrationes} 70, abridged NPNF ``Psalm LXXI,'' §§18--21.}

\subsection*{Humility permits the reception of good}
The Gospel's inviter can confer honor, while its guests must relinquish their self-assigned precedence. Paul's glory includes affliction for others and ends in praise of God. The Communion remembers divine justice. These relations distinguish receiving good from using it as a claim to superiority. Francis de Sales's rejection of performed humility and of a humility that hinders charity gives both sides practical force: the heart should neither manipulate praise nor refuse gifts it can use in love. \sourcecite{Luke~14:7--11; Eph.~3:13,21; Francis de Sales, III.5.}

\subsection*{Interior growth bears a common form}
Christ's dwelling in hearts is joined in Ephesians to all the saints and the Church. The Gradual brings nations and kings before Sion's divine builder, and the Alleluia turns wonders into public praise. The \textit{Liturgical Year} continuation's indwelling--Sion--praise relation thus develops a communal movement already present in the texts. Augustine's present Church-building and future judgement and Theodoret's restoration exceeding its first observers give that movement distinct theological articulations. Charity's growth is shared without making the heart, city and Church interchangeable objects. \sourcecite{\textit{Liturgical Year}, pp.~359,363; Augustine, \textit{Enarrationes} 101, abridged NPNF ``Psalm CII,'' §§16--18; Theodoret, PG~80, 1679B--1682B.}

\subsection*{Purification concerns embodied persons}
Inward strengthening, a bodily cure, cleansing through sacrifice and renewal with bodily help are different works of divine aid within one formulary. The sacramental prayers' ancient pairing and the continuation's interpretation of bodily renewal give this relation historical depth. The person who receives interior grace remains a person with a body, and the future good sought for that body exceeds its immediate relief. The prayer for bodily help after Communion carries no guarantee of immediate medical cure. \sourcecite{Eph.~3:16; Luke~14:4; Secret; Postcommunion; Wilson, \textit{Gelasian} III.12, p.~231; \textit{Liturgical Year}, pp.~370--371.}

\subsection*{Abundance sustains continuing need}
Paul's doxology is preceded by affliction; the Communion's remembrance ends in a plea; the Offertory repeats its call for rescue. Received help and continuing vulnerability coexist. Augustine's prayer of the members and Bellarmine's lifelong testimony make the recurrence intelligible: the history of mercy gives reasons to turn again toward God. The Church can sing wonders, ask that a threat be defeated and pray for help hereafter within the same act of worship. \sourcecite{Eph.~3:13,20--21; Augustine, \textit{Enarrationes} 39, abridged NPNF ``Psalm XL,'' §§21--25; Bellarmine, pp.~212--213; Postcommunion.}
