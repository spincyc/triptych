\subsection*{Introit: mercy hears the poor}
The chant places the plea of Psalm~85:3 first, follows it with the reason for confidence in verse~5, then sings the poverty of verse~1. The servant calls because God is merciful; his poverty describes the dependence of someone who expects to be heard. The whole psalm holds fidelity and need together. Its servant trusts God, praises him among the nations and still faces attackers seeking his life. The return to danger in verses~14--17 gives the opening plea urgency without canceling the confidence that carries it.

The abundant mercy of verse~5 belongs with the fuller divine-mercy formula in verse~15 and Exodus~34:6--9. In Exodus, God's proclamation accompanies forgiveness, renewed accompaniment and covenant; judgement remains within that proclamation. The psalm's mercy is God's faithful action toward his servant, extending into the promised worship of the nations. The Introit's Latin also has its own liturgical shape: \latin{mihi} replaces the Clementine \latin{mei}, the mildness clause is recast, and the psalm verse adds \latin{mihi} after the divine ear. Challoner's English remains the biblical wording beside these changes.

Augustine begins his exposition with Christ and the Church praying together. The cry of the whole day passes through the generations of Christ's members. Poverty before God concerns humility: a rich person can be poor in this sense, whereas reliance on possessions can prevent recognition of need. Augustine also locates divine graciousness in God's patience with the distracted petitioner; God's willingness to hear does not promise the fulfillment of every desired object. \sourcecite{\textit{Enarrationes} 85, abridged NPNF ``Psalm LXXXVI,'' §§1--3,5,7.}

Theodoret likewise sees humility compatible with abundance in righteousness. His attention to the Greek versions adds precision: Symmachus's daily expression and Aquila and Theodotion's placability describe repeated approach to a God willing to receive the petitioner. Bellarmine explicitly develops Augustine's distinction between material poverty and freedom from trust in wealth. At the entrance to Mass, this prayer therefore asks the mercy needed for the Collect's sustained good works and the Epistle's strengthening. \sourcecite{Theodoret, PG~80, 1553C--1556B; Bellarmine, pp.~259--260.}

\subsection*{Collect: grace before and after}
The Collect asks that grace always precede and follow, with constant attention to good works as its intended fruit. Its verbs describe a dependence that surrounds action rather than suspending it. Cummiskey's historical English uses ``prevent'' for the preceding gift and then asks zeal in practice. The older English must be read with that movement of the Latin, not as a request that grace hinder the person.

The prayer has more than one place in the Gregorian tradition. Wilson prints it among evening or morning prayers, as the fifth \latin{Alia} on p.~135, and again in the Sunday supplement on p.~174. The apparatus records a word-order variant in the former use. In supplement XXXV, Sunday XVII after Pentecost, it accompanies \latin{Munda nos} and \latin{Purifica}. The supplement's ordinal and the different post-Pentecost reckoning in Wilson's manuscripts belong to those witnesses. \sourcecite{Wilson, \textit{Gregorian Sacramentary}, pp.~133--135,174; introduction pp.~xviii,xli.}

Paul's prayer makes the Collect's divine initiative concrete: the Father gives strength through the Spirit so that Christ may dwell through faith. Aquinas reads Paul's knees as the bodily acknowledgment of smallness and weakness, and the perseverance requested as God's gift. Augustine's Communion-psalm exposition extends the same dependence through life: divine teaching initiates a way on which God remains necessary. These expositions illuminate the prayer's preceding and following grace, while its own request remains the practical constancy of good works. \sourcecite{Aquinas, \textit{Super Ephesios} 3.4; Augustine, \textit{Enarrationes} 70, abridged NPNF ``Psalm LXXI,'' §§18--21.}

\subsection*{Epistle: charity that cannot be contained}
The reading opens with apostolic affliction. Ephesians~3:1--12 has explained Paul's mission to the Gentiles; verse~13 asks its beneficiaries not to faint at the sufferings endured for them. The knees of verse~14 resume the prayer interrupted at the beginning of the chapter. Gentile co-inheritance, access through one Spirit to the Father and the common dwelling of chapter~2 give the prayer its communal horizon. The next chapter's humility, patience and unity show what this strengthened life demands. \sourcecite{Eph.~2:11--22; 3:1--21; 4:1--16.}

The prayer is explicitly Trinitarian. The Father grants, the Spirit strengthens and Christ dwells in the hearts of believers through faith. Roots and foundations are two images of charity's stability; comprehension happens with all the saints. The Latin \latin{omnis patérnitas} and Challoner's paternity retain one received wording. Aquinas distinguishes the order in which human beings learn the name of fatherhood from the priority of its reality in God. Chrysostom considers both derivative fatherhood and heavenly families or orders. The USCCB note on verses~14--15 explains the Father/family wordplay through alternatives concerning every family or God's whole family. \sourcecite{Aquinas, \textit{Super Ephesios} 3.4; Chrysostom VII; USCCB, Ephesians~3 notes.}

The four dimensions in verse~18 have no explicit object in that verse. Christ's charity in verse~19 gives the immediate context for interpreting them; the USCCB note also records the universe as an alternative. Chrysostom explains the dimensions as immensity: the point is to recognize love's transcendence, not to measure it. He also offers two accounts of fullness, through Trinitarian knowledge or the plenitude of virtue. The doxology then gives thanks for gifts exceeding what the recipients had requested or expected, while their faith and love remain necessary responses. \sourcecite{Chrysostom, \textit{Homilies on Ephesians} VII, on 3:13--21.}

Augustine and Aquinas develop an explicit reading through the Cross. Augustine describes charity's good works, perseverance, heavenly hope and hidden origin in grace; the unseen root of the Cross gives the fourth dimension its force. The gift's beginning is unearned and cannot be mastered by an explanation of God's hidden counsel. In the same letter, common life among the saints extends toward the one bread and body and toward love of enemies. \sourcecite{Augustine, Letter~140 to Honoratus, 25.62--26.64, Latin digital edition.}

Aquinas offers both the immeasurable perfections of God and the charity signified by the Cross. Comprehending God means attaining knowledge of his presence, not containing all that he is. Charity's breadth, perseverance, heavenly aim and divine origin permit genuine understanding while maintaining the excess of the gift over the knower. Benedict XVI similarly reads the passage as loving contemplation of Christ's inexhaustible mystery; his treatment concerns Colossians and Ephesians together. \sourcecite{Aquinas, \textit{Super Ephesios} 3.5; Benedict XVI, General Audience, 14 January 2009, mystery paragraph.}

Colossians~1--2 strengthens these relations through apostolic affliction for the Church, fruitful conduct, roots and building, and fullness in Christ. John~14:15--26 joins love, obedience, divine indwelling and the Spirit's teaching. In the Mass, the Epistle's abundance belongs with the Collect's fruitful grace and the Secret's purification for participation. Paul's opening affliction continues to qualify that abundance: divine power is already at work among those who still require courage.

\subsection*{Gradual: the city built before the nations}
Psalm~101 begins with an afflicted man's frailty: bodily wasting, wakefulness, reproach and the shortness of life. Against this stands God's enduring remembrance. Mercy for Sion and concern for its stones and dust lead into the appointed nations, kings, building and manifested glory. The next verses join the humble person's prayer to prisoners, future generations and peoples assembled for worship. The closing contrast between creation's change and God's permanence sustains the children of his servants. \sourcecite{Ps.~101:1--29.}

The psalm's restoration has a close counterpart in Isaias~60, where glory over Jerusalem draws nations and kings and accompanies rebuilding. Hebrews~1:10--12 applies Psalm~101:26--28 to the Son; those are the psalm's concluding verses, not the Gradual's selection. The chant itself changes the Clementine's opening connective and renders the final glory with \latin{in maiestáte sua}. Its liturgical words announce the divine builder's majesty.

Augustine reads the nations' gathering as the Church's present construction, completed before Christ's glorious judgement. Theodoret first explains the recovery of a city whose humiliation had seemed to imply divine weakness. Yet the response of neighboring peoples to that restoration did not fulfill universal conversion: some marveled and still attacked. He therefore directs the promise beyond the return toward the Incarnation and a fulfillment still unfolding. Bellarmine places Christ's present Church-building beside the future appearance in glory after his first coming in humility. \sourcecite{Augustine, \textit{Enarrationes} 101, abridged NPNF ``Psalm CII,'' §§16--18; Theodoret, PG~80, 1679B--1682B; Bellarmine, p.~317, the Gentiles and built-Sion lemmas.}

The Epistle has already asked for Christ's dwelling in hearts and comprehension with all saints. The Gradual gives this communal divine work a city and a gathering: the \textit{Liturgical Year} continuation joins the two in its account of inward strengthening, ecclesial unity and Sion's praise. Schuster also identifies the chant's use at the Third Sunday after Epiphany. \sourcecite{\textit{Liturgical Year}, pp.~359,363; Schuster III, pp.~143--144.}

\subsection*{Alleluia: the new life that sings}
The chant sings the opening of Psalm~97:1, with the explicit final \latin{Dóminus}; the saving right hand and holy arm belong to the verse's continuation. The whole psalm makes Israel's salvation visible before the nations, then calls singers, instruments, sea, rivers and mountains into praise before the Lord's equitable judgement. The wonders summon a common response. Isaias~52:7--10 likewise joins salvation seen by the ends of the earth with the holy arm and the nations; Simeon's canticle in Luke~2:28--32 gives salvation seen, Gentile revelation and Israel's glory their Christological expression.

Augustine interprets new song as new life in Christ and the peace of the Church. He then distinguishes bodily recovery from inward healing for God while expounding the rest of verse~1. His immediate Gospel allusion is the raising at Nain in Luke~7, not this Sunday's cure in Luke~14. The difference leaves his argument clear: wonderful bodily acts call for a life renewed in its relation to God. \sourcecite{\textit{Enarrationes} 97, abridged NPNF ``Psalm XCVIII,'' §1.}

Theodoret connects the song's newness with the Savior's new manner of worship and life, and the wonders with acts beyond human expectation. Bellarmine draws those wonders through the Incarnation, healings, Resurrection, Ascension and mission. His treatment of the unappointed right hand and arm emphasizes victory through humility, obedience and the Cross. In the Mass, the acclamation precedes a Gospel where Christ heals and corrects ambition: both mercy and humility belong to the life that praises his works. \sourcecite{Theodoret, PG~80, 1657C--1658D; Bellarmine, p.~306.}

\subsection*{Gospel: healing and the lower place}
Jesus enters the house of an unnamed leading Pharisee for a Sabbath meal. Luke~13:22 places the surrounding narrative on the journey toward Jerusalem; the town is not given. The observers watch Jesus; he questions them about healing, receives silence, heals the man and dismisses him. His rescue question follows the cure. The man has no recorded speech or petition, and Luke gives no cause of his illness. The subsequent wedding scene is a parable addressed to the guests, not a description of the actual meal. \sourcecite{Luke~13:22; 14:1--11.}

The Missal and Clementine text use ass and ox. Cyril's Payne Smith translation uses son and ox, so his appeal to parental affection rests upon a different textual witness. Cyril interprets Christ's power as revealing the law's merciful purpose and develops a spiritual Sabbath in which rest from sin accompanies holy action. Deuteronomy~22:4 commands aid to a fallen ass or ox; Exodus~23:4--5 includes an enemy's animal and verse~12 extends Sabbath rest to servants and animals. Luke~13:10--17 contrasts animal watering with the freeing of a daughter of Abraham. These comparisons give the rescue question its concrete moral weight. \sourcecite{Cyril, Sermon CI, pp.~471--475; USCCB, Luke~14:5 note.}

The meal's second scene concerns guests who choose the first places. Jesus allows the inviter to confer a higher place and honor before the company. Proverbs~25:6--7 gives a close wisdom parallel; Luke~18:9--14 repeats the final reversal in a parable directed against self-trust and contempt. Luke~22:24--30 places the disciples' competition for greatness beside Christ's table-service. These passages reveal a humility deeper than avoiding an embarrassing seating mistake.

Cyril grounds the low place in truthful self-knowledge. Wealth is transient, the body weak and mortal; the person who recognizes this can relinquish even honor he might claim without blame. Ambrose praises Christ's gentle correction, in which persuasion reforms ambition. His preceding interpretation of dropsy as fleshly excess belongs to his spiritual exposition, not to a diagnosis of the man's moral character. Ambrose then develops generosity to poor and disabled guests from verses~12--14, which lie beyond this Sunday's Gospel. \sourcecite{Cyril, Sermon CII, pp.~476--479; Ambrose, \textit{Expositio in Lucam} VII.195, PL~15, 1752A--B, Latin web text.}

Francis de Sales's last-place echo exposes an inward contradiction: performing lowliness in order to secure greater honor keeps ambition alive. He also refuses a false humility that declines divine gifts or interrupts charity. Benedict XVI reads the overlapping postconciliar Gospel through humanity's need for redemption and the humility of Christ's Cross, interpreting exaltation as nearness to the Lord. The latter address concerns Luke~14:1,7--14, not the present formulary's whole selection. \sourcecite{Francis de Sales, III.5; Benedict XVI, Angelus, 29 August 2010.}

The Gospel thus gives the Collect's good works both mercy's positive act and ambition's correction. Received honor remains possible, as does the afflicted apostle's glory in serving others. Neither the healed man's seat nor the observers' eventual inward conversion is narrated.

\subsection*{Offertory: the threatened members call for help}
The chant repeats its opening plea around a request that those seeking the singer's life be confounded and ashamed. It selects from Psalm~39:14--15, omitting the first petition of verse~14, the word \latin{simul} and the latter part of verse~15. The whole psalm has already remembered rescue, spoken of obedience and proclaimed justice in the assembly before returning to evils and iniquities surrounding the speaker. Prior mercy makes renewed need speak with hope. Psalm~69 repeats the closing petitions of Psalm~39; Hebrews~10:5--10 applies earlier verses~7--9 to Christ's obedient offering, not to the appointed chant. Hebrews' prepared body and the psalm's pierced ears also differ within the same English Bible.

Augustine hears the suffering members appeal to their physician in the setting of Christ's Passion. On the continuation about turning enemies backward, he develops conversion: those who tried to precede Christ can learn to follow him. That backward-turning clause is omitted by the chant. The chant still asks for the frustration and shame of a real destructive threat. \sourcecite{\textit{Enarrationes} 39, abridged NPNF ``Psalm XL,'' §§21--25.}

Theodoret gives the Church one voice that includes strong and weak members, and emphasizes the thwarting of persecutors' intended harm. Aquinas understands divine regard as help to escape evil and to perform good. His alternatives for the enemy's shame include both penitence and punishment under God's justice. These readings preserve the seriousness of harm while entrusting its defeat to God. Their distinct explanations also prevent the prayer from becoming either a general mood of peace or permission for personal vengeance. \sourcecite{Theodoret, PG~80, 1159A--1160D; Aquinas, \textit{Super Psalmo} 39 nn.~6--7.}

The Offertory's dependence belongs with the Introit and Communion, and its aid toward good with the Collect. Schuster calls its wording similar to the Offertory of Friday after the Second Sunday in Lent; his comparison is of similar text, not a demonstrated identity of every word. \sourcecite{Schuster III, pp.~143--144.}

\subsection*{Secret: cleansed for participation}
The Secret asks cleansing through the effect of the present sacrifice and merciful completion in the people so that they may participate. Sacrifice, mercy and fitness for participation belong together in the prayer. Cummiskey expresses its requested fruit as being made worthy through mercy. The communicant's self-appraisal is not the source of that effect.

The Secret and Postcommunion already occupy their respective places together in Wilson's Old Gelasian book III, section XII. Its two opening prayers differ from the current Collect. Wilson's Gregorian supplement XXXV then gives the present triad, but under Sunday XVII. These historical associations distinguish the sacramental pair from the longer history of its present arrangement. \sourcecite{Wilson, \textit{Gelasian}, p.~231; \textit{Gregorian}, p.~174.}

The \textit{Liturgical Year} continuation relates this Secret to the imminent consecration and ensuing Communion. Its interpretation develops the ritual position of a prayer whose Latin already attributes cleansing to the present sacrifice. The Epistle asks divine strength for Christ's indwelling; the Secret seeks divinely effected fitness for participation. Both ask for transformation in the recipient, and the Postcommunion continues to ask renewal after reception. \sourcecite{\textit{Liturgical Year}, p.~370; Eph.~3:16--20.}

\subsection*{Communion: the justice remembered through life}
The chant assembles three clauses: remembrance of God's justice alone, instruction from youth and the plea not to be forsaken in old age. Psalm~70's complete setting includes failing strength and enemies who claim that God has abandoned the petitioner. His answer is continued hope and praise. The chant stops before the purpose of proclaiming God's arm to the coming generation; that continuation gives the whole psalm's request a public fruit. Psalm~77:3--7 likewise joins remembered wonders, children and the transmission of hope.

Augustine interprets God's justice alone as the renunciation of self-grounded righteousness. Youth means conversion received without prior deserving; old age means continued need for the grace that began the life. God remains indispensable after teaching the way. Augustine extends the ages both to the person persevering to the last breath and the Church enduring to the world's end. \sourcecite{\textit{Enarrationes} 70, abridged NPNF ``Psalm LXXI,'' §§18--21.}

Theodoret gives a different corporate subject to the ages: the people schooled through Moses and the oldness of the law, followed by testimony to the Church from the nations. His relation to Hebrews~8:13 belongs to that exposition. The PG edition brackets some additions; the Christian-grace comparison in its brackets cannot be treated as undifferentiated text. Bellarmine instead develops David's personal remembrance of God's help and testimony through the Psalms, with divine power taking precedence over human counsel and strength. \sourcecite{Theodoret, PG~80, 1423B--1426C; Bellarmine, pp.~212--213.}

The psalm narrates no Eucharistic meal. Its Communion placement makes remembrance and dependence the voice of those receiving the sacrament, and the \textit{Liturgical Year} continuation reads its thanksgiving toward fruitfulness. Schuster identifies the same Communion verses on Thursday after the Fourth Sunday in Lent. The old-age prayer remains fitting after a divine gift: remembrance does not make the giver unnecessary. \sourcecite{\textit{Liturgical Year}, pp.~370--371; Schuster III, pp.~143--144.}

\subsection*{Postcommunion: renewal reaches embodied life}
The Postcommunion asks God to purify minds kindly and renew them through heavenly mysteries, so that bodily help may follow, both now and hereafter. The Latin expressly names bodies. Cummiskey's historical English speaks of purification of souls and help while in mortal bodies and hereafter; its wording carries that translation's own articulation of the prayer.

The Gelasian pairing with the Secret and the Gregorian three-prayer group keep purification before and after participation within the same sacramental sequence. The \textit{Liturgical Year} continuation develops the bodily good in this life and the next. The prayer's interior renewal therefore includes embodied need, while its future horizon exceeds the particular cure described in Luke. \sourcecite{Wilson, \textit{Gelasian} III.12, p.~231; \textit{Gregorian}, supplement XXXV, p.~174; \textit{Liturgical Year}, pp.~370--371.}

The Lord who helps the sick man and strengthens the saints is asked to continue his work in the worshippers. The Communion's aging voice and the Postcommunion's future help give received grace a continuing horizon. Neither the body's mortality nor the need to pray again refutes the abundance Paul celebrates; their endurance is entrusted to the giver whose power works within the Church. \sourcecite{Luke~14:4; Eph.~3:16--21; Ps.~70:16--18, selected.}
