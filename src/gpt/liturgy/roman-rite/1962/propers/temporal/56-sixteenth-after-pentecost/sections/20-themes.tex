\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\label{triptych:brief-synthesis:start}
God's mercy sustains a life rich in charity, fruitful in good works and open to others, while freeing it from the desire to possess its own greatness. The fullness for which Paul prays belongs with the low place Jesus teaches, the purification sought at the altar and the aging voice that still asks for help.

\subsection*{Grace gives strength for the work of charity}
The Introit begins with a cry that lasts all day. The singer appeals to God's sweetness and abundant mercy before naming his poverty in the psalm verse. Need therefore speaks with confidence: God is accessible to those who call upon him. Augustine hears Christ and his members praying together, and reads the continuing cry across the Church's successive generations; the materially rich, too, can be God's poor when they depend upon him. \sourcecite{Augustine, \textit{Enarrationes} 85, abridged NPNF ``Psalm LXXXVI,'' §§1--3,5,7.}

The Collect gives this dependence an active purpose. Grace is asked to precede and follow so that good works receive constant attention. Paul's prayer names the divine agency more fully: the Father grants strength through the Spirit, Christ dwells through faith, and charity roots and establishes the saints. He seeks this gift for people who might lose heart at his affliction on their behalf. The prayer's abundance does not require an untroubled life; it strengthens a people within the demands of their common vocation. \sourcecite{Collect; Eph.~3:13--21; Chrysostom, \textit{Homilies on Ephesians} VII.}

Mercy then receives concrete expression in the Gospel. Jesus takes hold of the sick man, heals him and sends him away before his observers have answered his question. He also corrects the guests' choice of precedence. Both bodily relief and the reform of ambition concern the life the Collect asks grace to sustain. Cyril teaches that spiritual Sabbath rest includes holy works and the cessation of sin: mercy belongs to the law's divine purpose. \sourcecite{Luke~14:1--11; Cyril, \textit{Commentary on Luke}, Sermon CI, pp.~471--475.}

The Secret brings the same dependence into the offering. The Church asks the present sacrifice to cleanse her and divine mercy to make her fit for participation. Her good works remain real, but they do not render the giver of grace dispensable. Augustine's exposition of the Communion psalm makes precisely this point: God does not merely show a road and then leave the traveler to complete it alone. \sourcecite{Secret; Augustine, \textit{Enarrationes} 70, abridged NPNF ``Psalm LXXI,'' §§18--21.}

\subsection*{Honor becomes a gift received without grasping}
Jesus' parable leaves room for honor: the host may invite a guest higher. Its rebuke falls on the guest who chooses the first place for himself. The difference concerns both action and desire. Francis de Sales exposes the false humility that takes a low place only to obtain a better one; his echo of the seating saying requires the heart to agree with its outward signs. The same chapter rejects a supposed humility that refuses God's gifts or obstructs charity. \sourcecite{Luke~14:7--11; Francis de Sales, \textit{Introduction à la vie dévote} III.5.}

Paul's use of glory deepens this correction. He calls his afflictions the recipients' glory, asks from the riches of God's glory and ends by giving glory to God in the Church and Christ. Honor before dinner companions and doxological glory have different objects. Together they place human good within relations of gift: a host confers a place, an apostle suffers for others, and the Church praises the giver. Cyril likewise directs the guest beyond wealth and social display toward truthful self-knowledge and honor from God. \sourcecite{Eph.~3:13,16,21; Cyril, Sermon CII, pp.~476--479.}

The Offertory keeps this humility close to a threatened life. Its repeated appeal asks help and the shame of those seeking the singer's destruction. Humility can seek protection. The Communion's remembrance of God's justice gives grateful dependence a clear object, without making the sufferer responsible for an enemy's harm. \sourcecite{Ps.~39:14--15 and 70:16--18, appointed selections; Aquinas, \textit{Super Psalmo} 39 n.~7.}

\subsection*{Christ's inward dwelling gathers a people of praise}
Paul asks that Christ dwell in hearts, but the prayer never closes the heart upon itself. Comprehension is with all the saints; the doxology is in the Church. The surrounding epistle names Gentile co-inheritance and the common dwelling of God in the Spirit, then turns toward humility, patience and unity. Rooted charity grows through a life shared with others. \sourcecite{Eph.~2:18--22; 3:6,17--21; 4:1--6.}

The Gradual's Sion is God's work of building, revealed before nations and kings. Augustine reads this as the Church gathered and built now before Christ's glorious judgement. Theodoret begins with the restoration of the humbled city, then argues that the universal conversion promised in the psalm exceeds that return: the neighboring peoples' wonder did not mean that all had believed. The restored city's glory and the Church's gathering thus remain distinct moments within his exposition. \sourcecite{Augustine, \textit{Enarrationes} 101, abridged NPNF ``Psalm CII,'' §§16--18; Theodoret, \textit{Interpretatio}, PG~80, 1679B--1682B.}

The Alleluia summons the new canticle because the Lord has done wonders. In the whole psalm, Israel's salvation becomes visible to the nations and creation welcomes the Lord's just judgement. Theodoret interprets the new song through the Savior's new manner of worship and life; Bellarmine unfolds the wonders through Christ's life and saving victory. Praise answers divine action and draws singers into its public acknowledgment. \sourcecite{Ps.~97:1--9; Theodoret, PG~80, 1657C--1658D; Bellarmine, \textit{Commentary}, p.~306.}

The \textit{Liturgical Year} continuation already joins the Epistle's inward strengthening to Sion's divine building and common praise. Its movement agrees with the prayer's own breadth: charity enlarges the saints' common life. Chrysostom interprets Paul's dimensions as the immensity of Christ's love. Augustine and Aquinas also unfold them through the Cross, where charity works, perseveres, hopes for heaven and receives its hidden beginning from God. Their spiritual readings teach a greatness whose source is gift. \sourcecite{\textit{Liturgical Year}, 1909 continuation, pp.~359,363; Chrysostom VII; Augustine, Letter~140, 25.62--26.64; Aquinas, \textit{Super Ephesios} 3.5.}

\subsection*{The whole person continues to receive God's help}
The man before Jesus needs bodily healing. Paul asks for strength in the inner person. The Secret asks cleansing through sacrifice; the Postcommunion asks the purification and renewal of minds and help for bodies, now and hereafter. These are distinct gifts to embodied persons. The inward language of grace does not make bodily need irrelevant, and the bodily cure does not exhaust the fullness of charity for which the Church prays. \sourcecite{Luke~14:2--4; Eph.~3:16--19; Secret; Postcommunion.}

The sacramental prayers have a long association: Wilson's Gelasian edition sets \latin{Munda nos} and \latin{Purifica} in their respective positions within the same Mass; the Gregorian supplement groups all three orations under Sunday XVII. The \textit{Liturgical Year} continuation interprets the Secret in relation to ensuing Communion and the Postcommunion's renewal as reaching bodily life and its future good. The healing in Luke remains a particular cure; the prayers after Communion promise no automatic medical recovery. \sourcecite{Wilson, \textit{Gelasian Sacramentary} III.12, p.~231; \textit{Gregorian Sacramentary}, supplement XXXV, p.~174; \textit{Liturgical Year}, pp.~370--371.}

The Communion gives this continuing help a personal voice. God has taught the singer from youth; old age still needs his presence. Augustine reads both a person's perseverance to the last breath and the Church's endurance to the world's end. Bellarmine hears David remembering divine power rather than trusting human strength. The remembered gift is a reason to ask again. The Introit's all-day appeal, the Offertory's repeated plea and the Postcommunion's future hope therefore coexist with Paul's confidence in abundant divine power: mercy sustains the life that praises it. \sourcecite{Augustine, \textit{Enarrationes} 70, abridged NPNF ``Psalm LXXI,'' §§18--21; Bellarmine, pp.~212--213; Eph.~3:20--21.}
