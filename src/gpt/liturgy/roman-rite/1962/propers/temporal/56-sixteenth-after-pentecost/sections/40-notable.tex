\sectionguard\section*{The Propers: Notable and Quotable}
\subsection*{An eternity spent waiting for a lover}
The Epistle ends its doxology with ``world without end'' (Eph.~3:21, Challoner). Shakespeare's Sonnet~57 turns the phrase into ``Nor dare I chide the world-without-end hour'' (line~5, Folger edition). The speaker presents himself as the beloved's slave, unable to complain of absence while jealousy makes a single hour oppressive. Doxological eternity becomes the subjective duration of waiting. The hyphenated expression is a biblical and liturgical idiom echoed; dependence specifically on this Pauline verse or this Mass is not documented. \sourcecite{Shakespeare, \textit{Sonnets} 57, lines~1--6,9--14; Folger edition, Mowat and Werstine, with Poston and Niles, FDT~0.9.0.1.}

\subsection*{Humility accused of ambition}
The Gospel promises that ``he that humbleth himself shall be exalted'' (Luke~14:11, Challoner). Nietzsche rewrites the saying: ``He that humbleth himself wisheth to be exalted.'' His heading is ``Luke 18:14 Improved.'' The explicit target is the parallel saying in Luke~18, not the appointed pericope. Alexander Harvey's English makes the change from promised vindication to suspected desire unmistakable: humility itself is accused of concealing ambition. This is a hostile psychological reversal of wording heard in the Gospel. \sourcecite{Nietzsche, \textit{Human, All Too Human}, trans. Harvey, 1908, ``History of the Moral Feelings,'' aphorism~87, p.~109; Gutenberg eBook~38145.}

\subsection*{Paul's dimensions in a human love poem}
Paul asks comprehension of ``the breadth and length and height and depth,'' then names Christ's charity (Eph.~3:18--19, Challoner). Elizabeth Barrett Browning writes: ``I love thee to the depth and breadth and height / My soul can reach'' (\textit{Sonnets from the Portuguese} XLIII, lines~2--3). She reorders the dimensions and omits length. The verbal and contextual echo transfers immeasurable love toward a human beloved, while the sonnet retains soul, grace, faith, saints and God. The poem does not simply remove religion from the language, and no authorial acknowledgment of dependence on Ephesians was located. \sourcecite{Caradoc Press printing, 1906; David Price transcription, Gutenberg eBook~2002.}

\subsection*{A dean who wants the higher place}
The Gospel's ``Friend, go up higher'' and final humiliation--exaltation saying become social satire in Percy Fitzgerald's \textit{Fatal Zero}. A fictional narrator remembers the dean preaching on ``Go up higher,'' then watches him seek a place beside a lord and interprets his humility as a hope for promotion. The scriptural language becomes an accusation against status-seeking, but the narrator also admits that his judgement may be unfair. These are a character's judgements, not events established in a real dean's biography. \sourcecite{\textit{All the Year Round}, new series I, 16 January 1869, p.~163, left column, paragraph beginning ``Of course he had not heard of my fall in the world.''}

The journal's conductor was Charles Dickens; the story was Fitzgerald's. Dickens himself identifies its author in his letter to Mrs James T.~Fields of 16 December 1868. The attribution matters to the joke's literary setting, rather than making the journal's running head its author's name. \sourcecite{Dickens, \textit{Letters}, ed. Mamie Dickens and Georgina Hogarth, III, 1882, that letter; Gutenberg eBook~25854.}

\subsection*{An ox the Senate should leave in the ditch}
Jesus asks who would not rescue ``an ox fall into a pit'' even on the Sabbath (Luke~14:5, Challoner). During a natural-gas pricing debate, Senator Riegle declared: ``Sometimes you have to leave the ox in the ditch, if that is where it belongs.'' He opposed treating a substantive dispute and the risk of higher consumer costs as merely an obstruction to Senate business. Rescue ceased to be the automatic good: the legislative predicament might properly remain unresolved. \sourcecite{\textit{Congressional Record---Senate}, 4 October 1977, p.~32287, right column.}

``Ditch'' belongs to the later idiom; Challoner prints ``pit.'' Doug Batchelor explicitly links the idiom to Luke~14:5 in \textit{How to Keep the Sabbath Holy}, p.~78 (2014). That modern witness establishes a consciously biblical use of the expression, not that Riegle had read Luke or intended an argument about Christ's cure. The political reversal concerns legislative action.
