\sectionguard\section*{The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities}
\emph{These six connections are exploratory editorial and AI-assisted proposals, not sourced historical intent or teaching attributed to the witnesses. Their precedent results refer to the bounded comparison recorded in the research audit.}

\begin{proposal}{Distorted fullness and receptive plenitude}
\pfield{Anchors} Gospel, Luke~14:2--4: the dropsical man and his cure; Epistle, Eph.~3:19--20: divine fullness and abundant power; Secret: cleansing for participation.
\pfield{Mechanism} Bodily swelling and being filled through a gift offer sharply different images of enlargement. Placed beside the Secret's cleansing, their contrast can become an examination of desire: accumulation may enlarge a claim to importance, while charity enlarges a capacity to receive and give. Ambrose's spiritual account of fleshly excess in Luke~14 supplies one received point of comparison; the three-element relation is the proposal. \sourcecite{Ambrose, \textit{Expositio in Lucam} VII.195.}
\pfield{Fruit} A worshipper may ask what kind of growth a desired good enables. Receiving charity gives a different account of plenitude from collecting reasons to occupy the first place.
\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses} The two images of fullness ordinarily remain in different passages; cleansing for participation makes their difference spiritually productive without turning them into synonyms.
\pfield{Precedent result} Near analogue located: the Eleventh-Sunday study's ``From unvoid grace to heavenly fullness,'' and Anthony's Sunday XVII, §§8--12. Anthony pairs this Gospel with Ephesians~4, not the present Epistle.
\pfield{Strongest limit} Bodily disease is no proof of personal sin. Divine plenitude is not a physiological quantity, and the analogy fails if it makes Luke's patient morally guilty or his cure an increase in the quantity of divinity.
\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{Grace across daily, lifelong and final horizons}
\pfield{Anchors} Collect: grace preceding, following and always present; Communion, Ps.~70:17--18: youth and old age; Postcommunion: present and future help; Introit, Ps.~85:3: the all-day cry.
\pfield{Mechanism} These spans can be heard together as simultaneous horizons of dependence. A day's petition belongs within a remembered life; grace needed for an action belongs with grace still needed afterward; bodily help now stands beside help hereafter. The spans illuminate each other without becoming stages on a single calendar.
\pfield{Fruit} Perseverance can include both today's task and the tasks a changed life will bring. Memory becomes a resource for asking help in circumstances not yet encountered.
\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses} Separate exposition leaves daily prayer, aging and final hope in their own units. Their coexistence allows the person to understand one received life across different scales of need.
\pfield{Precedent result} Near analogue located: the Fifteenth-Sunday study's ``The proper's several clocks'' and the Twelfth-Sunday study's ``Mercy's longer clock.'' Neither comparison coordinates these three principal prayers.
\pfield{Strongest limit} No timetable of grace, guarantee of longevity or uninterrupted health follows. The words supply no chronological assignment or ritual-age numerology.
\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{Interior hospitality and room for another}
\pfield{Anchors} Epistle: Christ dwelling in hearts, rooted and founded in charity; Gradual: God building Sion; Gospel: yielding a place and reclining at the contested table.
\pfield{Mechanism} The established relation between inward dwelling and common building can be extended into the use of shared space. Receiving a guest inwardly and being built into a people may change the will to control a seat outwardly. Hospitality then concerns a disposition as well as a room: a place ceases to be only a possession that fixes one's rank.
\pfield{Fruit} The invitation to receive Christ can test the habits by which others are granted room, attention and honor. A heart made hospitable has reason to loosen its grasp upon precedence.
\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses} Separate exposition does not develop the movement from the Pauline heart and the psalmic city into the Gospel's social allocation of places.
\pfield{Precedent result} Near analogue located for the fuller conjunction. The shorter indwelling--Sion--praise relation has precedent in the \textit{Liturgical Year} continuation, pp.~359,363; the Fourteenth-Sunday study's camp-and-courts proposal supplies a further dwelling analogue.
\pfield{Strongest limit} Heart, Sion and dining room have different referents. The parable does not explicitly identify the inviter as Christ. The healed man is dismissed and is never narrated taking a seat; no architectural code or compiler's programme follows.
\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{Lowliness compatible with enlargement}
\pfield{Anchors} Introit: the inclined divine ear and the needy petitioner; Epistle: bent knees, height and depth; Gospel: reclining in the low place and ascending at the inviter's word.
\pfield{Mechanism} Divine inclination, bodily kneeling, charity's dimensions and honor received from another put lowliness beside enlargement. A lowered posture and a higher place can each express reception, while an insistence on taking height for oneself can obstruct it.
\pfield{Fruit} Humility can be understood without contempt for one's worth or resistance to God's gifts. Kneeling and growth in charity need not compete, and accepting an honor can remain obedient to the giver rather than self-promoting.
\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses} The different spatial expressions cross chant, prayer and parable. Together they disclose how lowliness and enlargement can belong to the same disposition without becoming one literal movement.
\pfield{Precedent result} Near analogue located: the Tenth-Sunday study's ``Downcast eyes can accompany a lifted soul'' and the Fifteenth-Sunday study's inclined ear and raised appeal.
\pfield{Strongest limit} Posture alone proves no virtue. God's ear is figurative, whereas kneeling is bodily prayer. Paul's dimensions are not a map of ritual gestures, and the sequence licenses no numerological scheme.
\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{The petitioner also receives correction}
\pfield{Anchors} Offertory: shame requested against those seeking the singer's life; Gospel: the shame of a guest told to yield a place he assumed for himself.
\pfield{Mechanism} The prayer's requested judgement about another can stand beside the guest's possible experience of correction. A person who rightly seeks deliverance can also relinquish pride. The change of direction concerns willingness to be corrected, not the substitution of self-accusation for protection.
\pfield{Fruit} Prayer against harm can remain truthful without becoming a certificate of personal superiority. The worshipper can ask that a threat fail while also accepting correction of an unrelated fault.
\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses} Independent accounts leave the endangered singer and the ambitious guest apart. Their juxtaposition asks whether the praying self is prepared to receive the kind of moral correction he readily desires for another.
\pfield{Precedent result} Near analogue located: the Tenth-Sunday study's ``Divine leveling without competitive self-ranking'' compares protection from adversaries with correction of self-exaltation.
\pfield{Strongest limit} The threatened singer is not blamed for the threat; enemies are not identified with fellow guests or current opponents. A victim is never required to accept injury as deserved humiliation. Augustine's conversion, Theodoret's thwarted persecution and Aquinas's penitence-or-punishment readings retain their distinct meanings.
\end{proposal}

\begin{proposal}{A new song in an aging voice}
\pfield{Anchors} Alleluia, Ps.~97:1: the new canticle; Communion, Ps.~70:17--18: instruction from youth and prayer into old age; Postcommunion: renewal through heavenly mysteries.
\pfield{Mechanism} New praise and sacramental renewal can coexist with a long received history. Augustine's separate readings of new life and persevering dependence support the two psalmic anchors; their conjunction allows memory to become a condition for fresh thanksgiving rather than an obstacle to it. \sourcecite{\textit{Enarrationes} 97, abridged NPNF ``Psalm XCVIII,'' §1; 70, ``Psalm LXXI,'' §§18--21.}
\pfield{Fruit} Renewal need not imitate chronological youth. An aging Christian can discover new reasons for praise through the very history of help that makes the present plea possible.
\pfield{What the element-by-element reading misses} Separate exposition leaves new song, old-age prayer and sacramental renewal as parallel themes; the conjunction considers their coexistence in one present worshipper.
\pfield{Precedent result} Not located in the checked corpus: the 328-file English and Latin proper-study snapshot, Anthony's Sundays XVI and XVII and the \textit{Liturgical Year} continuation's complete Sixteenth-Sunday chapter. The nearest age and song passages did not develop this combined interpretation; unsearched psalm homilies, poetry, music and sermon collections remain outside that result.
\pfield{Strongest limit} The chants do not identify their historical speakers as one person. Spiritual renewal promises no reversal of biological aging, and the bounded precedent result supports no claim of universal novelty or priority.
\end{proposal}
