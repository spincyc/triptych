# Two defensible readings of the whole formulary

These are proposed arguments for the expansive study, its concise comparison
and a later homily, based on the checked loci in [scope.md](scope.md).
Stable keys: `mercy-received` and `protected-pilgrimage`. Both have substantive
contributions from multiple Fathers/saints. Every cross-element construction
below is editorial synthesis unless a named commentator's own received-Mass
argument is specified. No Father is credited with explaining all seventeen
texts or selecting their appointments. The base Sunday governs; mission and
St Luke prayers contribute only under their recorded dated conditions.

Both readings were independently reassessed on 13 October 2026 against the
source passages recorded in scope.md's replacement-run inspection. Their
distinct inferences, seventeen-element coverage, four senses and stated
disagreements remain supported; the earlier text is retained on that basis.

## 1. Mercy received must become mercy given (`mercy-received`)

**Controlling claim.** The assembly comes before God as a family already
dependent on gratuitous mercy. Its decisive spiritual battle is therefore
not mastery over a human debtor but fidelity to the gift it has received:
the forgiven must become merciful. The Mass asks God to make his gifts reach
the heart and conduct. Judgment tests that transformation; it does not turn
God's first gift into wages earned by our subsequent kindness.

**Principal authors and actual reasoning.** Chrysostom, Matthew Homily61
§§1–5, makes the disproportion of the debts intelligible through God's
antecedent gifts and makes the repeated plea of the second servant the
decisive exposure of ingratitude. The king's gift exceeds the request for
delay. The first servant's later refusal is free and culpable; his pardon
was real. This supplies the reading's causal order: mercy first, merciful
response owed, judgment on refusal. Chrysostom's Ephesians Homily22 adds
the common heavenly Master of6:9 and the redirection of wrath from fellow
human beings to the spiritual enemy. Homilies23–24 distinguish the spiritual
weapons from a mandate to attack people.

In the exposition of 6:9, Chrysostom explicitly invokes Matthew 18:32's
rebuke of the forgiven debtor when warning masters against oppression.
That verse precedes the appointed 6:10–17; the connection is his reception
of the Gospel in the surrounding Epistle context. Applying it across this
Sunday's formulary remains the editor's synthesis.

Augustine, Sermon83 §§1–8, supplies a different, substantial axis: each hearer
is both creditor and debtor, and remains a beggar before God. A person cannot
compartmentalize forgiving others from the daily request to be forgiven.
His baptismal warning preserves the reality of grace; his §8 explicitly
joins inward pardon to corrective care. His Psalm118 Aleph §§1–8 prevents
the “undefiled way” from becoming moral self-sufficiency. The link between
this received grace, these Gospel/Epistle arguments and the composed orations
is the editor's whole-formulary reading.

Aquinas, ST III,q88,aa1–4 (especially a1 and a3), is a later doctrinal clarifier: after real remission
the new mortal fault incurs a new debt of punishment, with ingratitude
aggravating the fault. He does not teach that pardon was a pretense or that
the entire old debt of punishment is mechanically revived in its former amount.
Schuster III,179–181 supplies actual liturgical reception of the Sunday
Collect, Secret and Postcommunion: divine protection leads to good works,
the offering seeks restoration, and sacramental reception must reach the
whole interior life. These later witnesses support, rather than replace,
Chrysostom's and Augustine's primary arguments.

**Consequential difficulty: “I forgave thee all the debt … until he should
pay all the debt … from your hearts” (Matt18:32–35).** How can genuine
remission end in judgment? Chrysostom reasons from the king's action: the
servant received more than he asked, so the renewed sentence exposes a new,
deliberate misuse of mercy. “Until” does not promise eventual repayment of
the impossible sum. Augustine locates the warning within the baptized
community, whose received pardon demands continuing pardon of others.
Aquinas later distinguishes renewed punishment from the old guilt's formal
return. The author must teach those steps, not erase the difficulty with
“God forgives everything anyway,” or imply that an injured person must
purchase God's first mercy by instant emotional reconciliation.

| --- | --- |

**Its four senses.** Literal: endangered Israel petitions the sovereign
creator; Paul equips a Christian community; Jesus' royal-account parable
shows actual remission followed by a merciless act and judgment; righteous
Job suffers. These situations are distinct before the liturgical comparison.
Allegorical: Augustine's Exodus and Chrysostom's baptismal gift permit an
ecclesial reading of the released household, fed and formed in Christ;
Schuster's Job-Christ typology supplies the innocent sufferer at the offering.
Moral: remember one's received pardon, forgive from the heart, practice
truthful correction and protection without hatred, and let sacramental gifts
form conduct. Anagogical: the king's final accounting and the Communion's
appeal point to divine judgment; immortal nourishment calls the family to
enduring fellowship, not merely restored earthly comfort.

**What this construction adds.** An element-by-element account can identify
mercy in the Gospel, peace in the armor and purity in the Postcommunion
without explaining their relation. This reading supplies the causal order:
gift received → battle against the misuse of freedom → gift made fruitful
in the heart → final accountability. It makes the human debtor the test of
the recipient's response, while Job prevents a universal theory of deserved
suffering. That argument distinguishes it from the following reading's
question about the meaning of protection during continuing affliction.

**Limits and pastoral use.** Forgiving inwardly does not abolish truth,
restitution, justice, discipline, boundaries or the protection of a victim.
Augustine's distinction warrants that limit; modern practical forms are the
editor's application. Do not equate trauma symptoms with the servant's
deliberate refusal. Avoid converting talents into modern money as if a
precise exchange rate carried the parable. This reading is especially suited
to a homily whose decisive turn is the second servant repeating the first
servant's own plea. The homily may develop one argument without reciting the
whole research matrix.

## 2. Protected on the pilgrimage, not exempt from trial (`protected-pilgrimage`)

**Controlling claim.** The divine protection requested at the entrance and
in the Collect is not contradicted by righteous Job's wounds or the
Communion's continuing persecution. God protects a pilgrim people by bounding
evil, supplying grace to stand, and feeding them toward immortality. Their
deliverance is real and still incomplete in its historical manifestation.
Mercy to others is part of their perseverance, not an alternative to the
honest lament and appeal for judgment that they continue to make.

**Principal authors and actual reasoning.** Gregory, Moralia II.10.16–11.19
and III.3.3–6.8, distinguishes evil intention from permitted power, and
affliction without antecedent guilt from purposelessness. The narrative
itself gives Job a righteous character and bounds Satan's attack. God is
not persuaded from a good will into an evil one; the language of movement
accommodates divine action to human understanding. This establishes why
protection cannot simply mean the absence of wounds. It also disallows a
second, sovereign evil power.

Augustine, Ps89 §§1–4, supplies the unchanging divine refuge; Ps113 §§1–6
reads the Exodus as baptismal deliverance with continuing danger; Ps118
Caph §§81–88 lets the entire Christ-body long for salvation and await final
judgment. The cry is desire that reaches toward salvation, not abandonment
of trust. His martyrs live through dying; *help me* is not reducible to
bodily escape. These are substantive claims distinct from Gregory's account
of permission. Chrysostom, Ephesians Homilies22–24, adds the pilgrim's
equipment: his sustained Exodus, Passover and sacramental-food argument in
Homily23 makes the start of the journey different from its end. Bellarmine's
Ps118:81–88 confirms the union of desire and hope and refuses date curiosity.
Schuster's complete Sunday unit supplies a received liturgical comparison
of God's help, Job, spiritual armor and sacramental nourishment.

**Consequential difficulty: “free from all adversities” beside “power … in
his flesh” and “when wilt thou judge?”** The prayer genuinely asks for
deliverance, and this reading must not paraphrase the petition into a request
for suffering. Gregory follows the Job narrative's distinction between
uprightness, trial and bounded permission. Augustine's Caph exposition follows
the apparent failure of help through martyrdom to the preservation of life
in its final sense. Chrysostom follows the already-delivered Exodus people
into the remaining wilderness. Together they allow prayer for concrete
rescue while denying the inference “the sufferer has wounds, therefore God
abandoned him or must be punishing a hidden crime.” God's specific purpose
in a particular sufferer's losses remains unknown.

**A contribution from every element.**

| Element | Contribution to this argument |
| --- | --- |
| Introit antiphon and Psalm verse | Mordecai's universal creator is invoked in mortal danger. The Psalm's walk supplies pilgrimage language; the prayer is confident dependence, not assurance that no hostile will acts. |
| Sunday Collect | The family asks real protection and freedom for good acts. Gregory's Job and the later Communion determine the meaning of its fulfillment without cancelling its petition for release from adversity. |
| Epistle | “Stand” is repeated because the evil day is real. Truth, righteousness, peace, faith, salvation and the word are gifts for continuing battle. Chrysostom's Exodus/Eucharist account joins equipment to a journey with a destination. |
| Gradual | Before mountains and through generations God remains refuge. Augustine's changelessness answers the temptation to infer a changing or defeated God from changing circumstances. |
| Alleluia | Israel truly left Egypt; departure was not yet the promised rest. Augustine and Chrysostom make deliverance plus perseverance a coherent sequence. The next Psalm verse helps identify peoplehood without being added to the chant. |
| Gospel | The pardoned servant has truly been released, yet can fall through cruelty. The story is a warning to delivered pilgrims: perseverance includes mercy, not merely endurance of outside attack. |
| Offertory | The innocent sufferer is central evidence that protection and bodily affliction can coexist. Gregory supplies the literal-theological distinction; Schuster's typological Job-Christ reading places offered suffering within redemption. |
| Sunday Secret | Restoration is asked through God's powerful loving-kindness in sacrificial gifts. The remedy is more than psychological resilience and is not the pilgrim's unaided self-preservation. |
| Trinity Preface | Eternal, equal divine majesty is confessed amid changing creaturely circumstances. Distinct Persons do not divide saving power. Thanksgiving already joins the earthly pilgrims to angelic worship. |
| Communion | The assembly still asks “when?” after confessing hope. Augustine's whole-Christ and final-judgment reading makes this a corporate eschatological petition; Schuster's present-judgment interpretation can appear as a contrasting emphasis, not its replacement. |
| Sunday Postcommunion | The food is named immortality's nourishment, and the mind must follow what the mouth received. The destination explains the kind of help now sought, while the moral petition excludes automatic sacramental success. |
| Mission Collect, when appointed | Pilgrims become workers: truth's word must run among all peoples. Its progress depends on God's sending and boldness, not secure circumstances or human control. |
| Mission Secret, when appointed | Christ's universal redemption and the clean offering from sunrise to sunset show the pilgrimage expanding into worship among the nations. The Church's travel is ordered to God's name. |
| Mission Postcommunion, when appointed | The gift of redemption sustains the continuing advance of true faith. The prayer explicitly asks for growth after reception, fitting the not-yet-completed journey. |
| Luke Collect, admitted branch | The cross is borne in a living body for God's name;2Cor4's outward affliction/inward renewal clarifies the apostolic form of pilgrimage. Do not diagnose Luke's manner of death. |
| Luke Secret, admitted branch | Heavenly gifts heal for free service and glory: the prayer holds present remedy and final destination together. |
| Luke Postcommunion, admitted branch | Sanctification and safety are requested through the received altar gift and saintly intercession. Read with Augustine's martyrs, safety can culminate in enduring communion with God through death. |

**Its four senses.** Literal: God is invoked in threatened Israel's crisis;
Paul commands steadfastness; righteous Job suffers within bounded permission;
the Psalmist remains persecuted; the forgiven servant must live differently.
Allegorical: Augustine's baptismal Exodus and Chrysostom's Passover/Spirit/
Eucharistic journey identify the Church's passage in Christ; Schuster's Job
as Christ is a complementary typology, not a claim that Job literally bore
personal guilt or that the friends were correct. Moral: stand in truth and
peace, seek rescue without despair, refuse retaliation and the false diagnosis
of another's suffering, and let received food shape the interior life.
Anagogical: the saints await judgment and unassailable rest; the Postcommunion's
immortality and the Preface's angelic worship orient the journey to communion
with the triune God beyond present affliction.

**What this construction adds.** Individually the Collect asks protection,
Job reports wounds, and Communion asks delayed judgment. This reading explains
why they are a coherent prayer without flattening their tension: beginning
deliverance → tested freedom and endurance → sustaining sacramental gifts →
final rest. The first reading centers the already-forgiven agent's obligation;
this one centers the already-delivered community's unfinished journey. The
Gospel keeps this second reading from becoming a consoling story in which
the protected people have no responsibility or risk of falling.

**Limits and pastoral use.** No one should tell the bereaved that their
loss was needed to improve them, or infer from Gregory a known divine reason
for a present abuse. Calling the enemy spiritual must not deny material
injustice or block lawful protection. The prayer can include immediate rescue
and the labor to obtain it. “Faith” is not a measurable shield whose failure
proves that a victim believed too little. This reading can sustain a homily
about the difference between being abandoned and still being on the way;
the preacher should keep one concrete image and the Communion's actual cry,
not turn the sermon into a theory of every evil.

## Comparison at the decisive questions

| Exegetical question and appointed words | Mercy received | Protected pilgrimage | What changes in the reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What does divine power mean: *universa sunt posita*? | The prior creator/redeemer gift makes the hearer a debtor before judging another. | The creator's sovereignty bounds evil without eliminating every affliction. | One inference establishes obligation from gift; the other establishes hope without immunity. Neither identifies Haman's or Satan's malice with God's moral approval. |
| Who is the enemy: *non…carnem et sanguinem*? | My fellow debtor must not be made the target of revenge. | The pilgrim's real spiritual battle remains after deliverance and needs God's armor. | Human mercy and sustained endurance are different consequences of the same exclusion. Chrysostom's disputed heavenly-prize grammar remains identified as his reading. |
| Was pardon real: *debitum dimisit*? | Yes; its gratuitous priority makes cruelty culpable. Chrysostom/Augustine support real pardon followed by judgment. | Yes; real release is the beginning, not a guarantee of final fidelity. Augustine's Exodus warning and Chrysostom's journey clarify that structure. | The first follows moral causation; the second follows the temporal course of salvation. Aquinas clarifies renewed punishment without retroactively nullifying forgiveness. |
| Can an upright person suffer: *simplex et rectus…gravi ulcere*? | Yes; Job forbids treating all injured people as guilty debtors and restrains coercive applications of forgiveness. | Yes; Job is the controlling counterexample to identifying protection with bodily immunity. | Gregory supplies the same factual-theological bound but has a more central organizing role in the second reading. |
| What judgment is sought: *quando facies…iudicium*? | A plea entrusted to God can coexist with heartfelt pardon; it differs from personal strangling of a debtor. | The whole Christ awaits consummation while already receiving help. | Augustine/Bellarmine's final horizon supports both. Schuster's present privation-of-truth judgment is a real different emphasis that must be shown beside, not substituted for, their answer. |
| What follows receiving: *ore percepimus…mente sectemur*? | Communion must bear the fruit of merciful inward conduct. | Immortal nourishment sustains the journey to the promised end and asks continuing growth. | Shared sacramental realism yields a different governing inference; neither reduces the Eucharist to a metaphor or assumes reception automatically guarantees perseverance. |

These are complementary readings, not a manufactured patristic controversy.
Actual disagreements survive at their proper scale: Chrysostom versus the
NPNF editor on *in caelestibus*; differing interpretations of the evil day;
Augustine's numerological account of Peter's question versus Chrysostom's
unlimited-frequency emphasis; and Schuster's present judgment beside the
Psalm commentators' final horizon. The concise study should interleave these
at the relevant questions. It must not replace each interpretation's four
senses with a single global table, or substitute differently titled summaries
for these distinct inferences.
