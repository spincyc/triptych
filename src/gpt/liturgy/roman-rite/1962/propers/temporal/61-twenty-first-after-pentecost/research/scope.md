## Biblical contexts actually read

The complete bounded contexts read in the registered DR text were Esther
3–4,13–14; Job 1–2 and 42; Matthew 17–19; Ephesians 4–6; Psalms 89,113,118;
1 Timothy 2; 2 Thessalonians 3; 2 Corinthians 4; John 17. These are not a
claim to have reread every chapter of each biblical book. Clementine verse
payloads were separately compared at Esther 13:9–11, Job 1:1,12;2:6–7 and
Psalm 118:1,81,84,86 to identify adaptation rather than harmonize it away.

Mordecai, not Esther, speaks the Introit's underlying prayer (13:8–18), in
the Vulgate arrangement of the Greek additions. Its modern Addition C
correspondence is C:2–4 for 13:9–11; some editions place it after 4:17.
Esther's own prayer follows in chapter 14. The surrounding decree threatens
Israel's destruction; sovereignty is pleaded for rescue, not deduced as
approval of Haman's action. The Introit's Psalm verse adds the walk of the
undefiled to a prayer of endangered people.

Ephesians' armor concludes a sustained summons to the new life: 4:26–32
opposes anger and revenge and commands forgiveness after God's forgiveness;
5:1–2 calls for imitation of divine love; 6:9 puts earthly masters under the
same heavenly Master. Verse 18's prayer for all saints is context, not part
of the appointed 6:10–17. The devil and hostile spiritual powers, rather
than flesh and blood, are named as the ultimate adversaries.

Matthew 18 holds together the little ones, the lost sheep, correction and
restoration, communal authority, Peter's question and the debt parable.
The lection starts at verse 23 and ends at 35: it does not include Peter's
numerical question. The debt owed by a fellow servant is real; the king's
remission precedes any merciful act by the first servant. The demand from
the heart and the final judgment are not dispensable endings. The narrative
frame is Galilee, with Capharnaum immediately preceding (17:23 in this DR
numbering), before departure in 19:1; the particular house/date of this
discourse is not supplied by the appointed verses themselves.

Job's narrator calls him upright before affliction. God's second permission
explicitly protects his life; the chant compresses the two permissions and
the losses without replacing the biblical account. Job 42 rebukes the friends'
account and makes Job their intercessor. It supplies no general license to
diagnose the moral cause of a contemporary sufferer's illness. Psalm 89's
opening names Moses, while the whole prayer moves through mortality, sin and
the petition that God establish our work. Psalm 113 remembers departure
from Egypt and continues with God's sanctuary and the sea; the Alleluia
appoints only its first verse. Psalm 118's Aleph and Caph passages locate
obedience within prayer for grace and longing under persecution. All Psalm
numbers here are Vulgate: 89=Hebrew90, 113:1=Hebrew114:1, 118=Hebrew119.

Mission orations weave scriptural language into composed prayers. Checked
contexts include the saving will and one mediator of 1 Timothy 2:1–8,
the word's progress and perseverance of 2 Thessalonians 3, and the knowledge,
mission and unity of John 17. Luke's *mortificationem…in suo corpore* is
illuminated by the complete 2 Corinthians 4 argument, especially 4:10–18.
These echoes are not additional Sunday lections or proof of a specific
author's compositional source. The mission Secret also evokes Psalm 83:10
and Malachi 1:11; these verbal leads need no invented patristic commentary
on the later prayer itself.

## Checked author arguments and disagreements

**Chrysostom on Matthew:** Homily61 does more than enjoin kindness. God's
prior gifts—creation, redemption, baptism, the Spirit—make the servant's
unpayable obligation intelligible. The threatening summons teaches him to
ask; he asks delay and receives total remission. The second servant repeats
his words, exposing the first's refusal as voluntary ingratitude. Forgiveness
was real, and the later wickedness matters. The final “until” is not a
promise of finite repayment: he cannot pay. Keep the force of the judgment.
The homily's examples of Job, Joseph, Moses, Paul and Stephen illuminate
nonretaliation; they are not proof that human violence causes no real harm.

**Augustine on Matthew:** Sermon83 §§1–2 places the hearer simultaneously
among debtors and creditors and connects giving/forgiving with the beggar's
daily need for God; §§3–5 interpret seventy-seven through transgression and
Luke's genealogy, rather than a numerical permission to stop forgiving;
§§6–7 use the truly pardoned debtor as warning after baptism; §8 explicitly
retains corrective discipline alongside inward pardon. His ancient examples
of corporal discipline are not a pastoral instruction for this assembly.
His number symbolism and Chrysostom's “never stop” emphasis are compatible
at the practical level but not identical exegesis. The counted question is
context, outside the appointed lection.

**Chrysostom on Ephesians:** Homily22 makes 6:9's common Master continuous
with the armor: human masters and servants are not ultimate enemies. He
takes *in the heavenly places* as the heavenly things contested for; the
NPNF note expressly prefers a locative reading and rejects his grammatical
explanation. Likewise his evil day as this present life is broader than
editorial alternatives of a particular temptation or final outbreak. Homily23
interprets girding through Exodus and develops Christ as Passover, the Spirit
as guidance, baptismal passage and Eucharistic food; departure is not arrival.
Homily24 treats righteousness as a whole virtuous life, gives two readings
of preparation for the Gospel of peace, and develops faith first as a gift
with power and then as confidence against assault. These nuances prevent
the “armor” from becoming seven unrelated spiritual objects or hostility
toward neighbors.

**Augustine on the Psalms:** Ps89 §2 expressly declines to infer that Moses
personally composed the Psalm from its heading. His first four sections make
God's refuge changeless though people turn to him. Ps113 §§1–6 relate Israel's
actual Exodus to the Church gathered from Jews and Gentiles, baptism and the
danger of falling after deliverance. Do not reproduce polemical generalizations
against Jews or make *barbaro* an ethnic judgment. Ps118 Aleph §§1–8 roots
walking in God's help; Caph §§81–88 reads desire for the saving Christ in his
whole body through history and final judgment, with martyrs living through
their dying. The NPNF Psalms text is an abridged historical translation with
visible ellipses; this research does not claim inspection of every Latin
sermon underlying it.

**Gregory on Job:** II.10.16–11.19 distinguishes the devil's malignant
intention from the power he receives by permission. III.3.3–6.8 explains
“without cause” as without a fault demanding punishment, not without any
providential end: trial manifests/increases virtue. The apparent movement
of God by Satan is accommodated language, not a mutable deity. God bounds
the permitted attack and protects life while affliction occurs. That account
supports patience and rejects both an independent evil deity and the friends'
automatic guilt diagnosis. It does not identify God's particular purpose in
each modern calamity or require a victim to remain exposed to an abuser.

**Bellarmine:** Psalm89:1–3 develops refuge through faith, hope and charity;
Ps113:1 explains “barbarous” by difference of language and the emergence of
Israel as a people; Ps118:81–88 reads the cry for judgment as longing for
everlasting security rather than curiosity about the world's remaining days.
His literal presentation of one righteous petitioner and Augustine's voice
of the whole Christ differ in scale without contradiction. Bellarmine's
David language is traditional attribution, not newly established authorship.

**Aquinas:** ST III,q88,a1 distinguishes the old sin's forgiven guilt from
the renewed debt of eternal punishment incurred through a new mortal sin;
a2 distinguishes kinds of ingratitude. Article3 rejects a mechanical
return of the entire earlier debt of punishment; article4 distinguishes
a special sin of ingratitude from an aggravating circumstance. This later
analysis helps explain Matthew's warning without saying that the king never
really forgave. It must not be retrojected as Chrysostom's or Augustine's
own technical vocabulary.

**Schuster:** VolumeIII,178–181 was read as a complete unit in the exact
1927 scan and OCR. It supplies real reception of the received Sunday, ten
base elements, with a sustained problem of suffering, God's help and judgment.
It does not cover this dated mission/Luke occurrence or the Trinity Preface.
Its Job-as-Christ reading is typological, not the literal narrator's guilt
verdict. Its Communion emphasis on judgment already realized as privation
of truth differs from Augustine/Bellarmine's final horizon; preserve both
without pretending that present judgment exhausts the chant's “when?”.
The printed Gospel reference “xxviii” is an actual typographical error for
Matthew18, not another appointment. Its Würzburg and extended-Offertory
history was not independently checked in the ancient witnesses and is not
used as proof of compositional intent. Its culturally ranked account of
“barbarians” and twentieth-century polemical applications are not necessary
to either proposed reading.
