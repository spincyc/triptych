\section{The words and actions of the Mass}

\subsection{Introit: justice acknowledged, mercy requested}
The antiphon combines two verses of Psalm 118 that occur in the reverse order in the complete psalm. First comes acknowledgment of God's righteousness and upright judgment, then the request that he deal with his servant according to mercy. The verse adds the blessedness of those who walk in the Lord's law. The singer does not bargain away judgment to obtain mercy. He appeals to the just God as a servant who needs to be taught and sustained in the life God's law describes.

The complete psalm holds promises and petitions together: devotion to God's commandments continually becomes a request for understanding, direction, and life. Augustine reads the petition at verse 124 accordingly. God's mercy makes the servant righteous; it does not reward a righteousness established without God. At verse 137, however, Augustine leaves the justice of divine judgment intact. The grief of the sinner acknowledges that judgment rather than accusing the judge. These are two aspects of conversion: the truth about wrong and the help by which a person turns from it.\footnote{Augustine, \textit{Expositions on the Psalms}, Ps.~118, English paragraphs 123 and 136 in NPNF1, vol.~8 (under the modern heading Psalm 119); compare paragraphs 34--36. These are this English edition's paragraph numbers.}

Bellarmine makes the requested teaching practical. At Psalm 118:124, God teaches by giving love as well as knowledge: a mind that can state a command is not thereby a heart that delights in fulfilling it. The Introit thus opens a question that reaches the Gospel. One may identify the greatest command and still need to be changed by the Lord who commands it.\footnote{Robert Bellarmine, \textit{Commentary on the Book of Psalms}, trans. John O'Sullivan (1866), Ps.~118, commentary on v.~124.}

\subsection{Collect: an undivided allegiance}
The Collect asks for two connected gifts: to avoid diabolical contagion and to follow God alone with a pure mind. Its plural subject is God's people. Purity here serves an allegiance shared by persons whose lives can be corrupted; the prayer does not say that belonging to the people has already made them immune. The prayer seeks freedom to follow, not merely distance from an external danger. Its first word, \textit{Da}, makes the whole movement a gift sought from the Lord.

Thomas's account of Ephesians 4 illuminates the social reach of this purity. Pride, anger, impatience, and disordered zeal work against peace. A mind divided by those impulses can defend religious positions while injuring the people it professes to serve. The Collect's request therefore has consequences for speech, correction, and endurance. Thomas is expounding the Epistle, not this prayer; his analysis gives concrete moral content to its plea for a purified following of God.\footnote{Thomas Aquinas, \textit{Super Epistolam ad Ephesios}, ch.~4, lect.~1; \textit{In omnes S. Pauli Apostoli epistolas commentaria}, vol.~2 (Leodii: Dessain, 1857), pp.~312--315.}

\subsection{Epistle: a calling made visible in conduct}
Ephesians 4:1--6 begins with the apostle as a prisoner in the Lord. His appeal is that the hearers walk worthily of their calling, and its first specifications are humility, meekness, patience, and bearing with one another in charity. Unity has to be kept with care. The passage then names the realities already shared: one body, one Spirit, one hope, one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father. The sequence binds conduct to gift. Christians do not manufacture the one Lord by agreeing with each other; their agreement in charity should express the calling they have received.

Chrysostom dwells on the image of a bond. Paul's chains lead to the bond of peace, which joins persons without cramping the freedom to serve. When the strong are joined to the weak, strength supports weakness rather than excluding it. Humility must also reach tone of voice and conduct toward enemies; it fails if it is offered to a favored few while others receive contempt. His interpretation gives Paul's large claims a bodily scale. A community's unity can be damaged by ordinary impatience well before it is challenged by a formal division.\footnote{John Chrysostom, \textit{Homilies on Ephesians}, hom.~9, exposition of Eph.~4:1--3, NPNF1, vol.~13.}

The chapter continues with different gifts and ministries, then truthfulness, work that allows sharing with the needy, speech that builds others up, and forgiveness. The appointed six verses are therefore not an appeal for everyone to have the same task or temperament. Their common foundation supports a differentiated body. Chrysostom's homily 11 explicitly separates gifts common to salvation from unequal gifts for service. What is given to one for the building of others is neither a private trophy nor a reason for another to despair.\footnote{Eph.~4:7--16,25--32; Chrysostom, \textit{Ephesians}, hom.~11, exposition of Eph.~4:7 and the ministries that follow.}

\subsection{Gradual: a people that receives its existence}
Psalm 32 praises the Lord whose word establishes the heavens, whose counsel stands, and whose mercy sustains those who fear him. Its chosen people is blessed because the Lord is its God and has chosen it as his inheritance. The chant places election before creation's Word and breath. Its two verses identify the people's blessedness and the power on which created reality depends. The larger psalm's warning against trust in armies and horses makes that dependence more pointed: visible strength does not secure salvation.

Bellarmine begins with the heavens as God's creation and explains Word and Spirit through inseparable Trinitarian action. Augustine gives the heavens a spiritual identification: the righteous are made firm by the Word, and their faith is strengthened by the Spirit. His reading rests the stability of holy persons on divine action rather than on their own strength. These explanations work at different levels. The created heavens remain the psalm's object of praise; Augustine's spiritual heavens show the same dependence in the life of God's people.\footnote{Ps.~32:1--22; Augustine, \textit{Psalms}, Ps.~32, first exposition, paragraphs 6 and 12; Bellarmine, \textit{Psalms}, Ps.~32, commentary on vv.~6 and 12.}

\subsection{Alleluia: the cry of affliction}
The Alleluia takes the opening petition of Psalm 101. The complete psalm moves from the loneliness and frailty of an afflicted person to the restoration of Sion and the gathering of peoples to serve the Lord. The short chant gives the assembly that voice of need before the Gospel. Praise and supplication coexist: the refrain acclaims God while the verse asks to be heard.

Augustine asks who can speak the psalm's poverty. Christ assumed poverty for us, and his body includes penitent members whose sorrow can be heard in the one voice of Christ and the Church. Personal sin belongs to the members, not to their sinless Head. With this distinction the afflicted Christian can pray within Christ's body, though not every sentence describes Jesus' personal experience in the same way. Bellarmine, attending to the opening petition, emphasizes that even effective prayer needs grace. The cry is already a work of the mercy it seeks.\footnote{Augustine, \textit{Psalms}, Ps.~101, paragraphs 1--3; Bellarmine, \textit{Psalms}, Ps.~101, commentary on opening v.~1, corresponding to the Missal's Ps.~101:2.}

\subsection{Gospel: the law of love and David's Lord}
Matthew 22 places this exchange after disputes about tribute and resurrection. A lawyer tests Jesus by asking which command is great in the law. Jesus answers with love of God in the whole heart, soul, and mind, and adds love of neighbor as oneself. The two commands sustain the law and the prophets. The second is like the first without becoming identical to it: God is loved as God, and the neighbor is loved as a fellow person whose good cannot be detached from that highest love.

Chrysostom reasons that the commands entail each other. Love of God takes form in obedience, and obedience includes care for the neighbor; hatred cannot coexist peacefully with a claim to fulfill the first command. Augustine develops the direction of love: the whole self is ordered toward God, and the neighbor is loved in relation to that same good. His account includes every human being, even an enemy, and recognizes Christ himself as neighbor through his mercy toward us. The neighbor is never a useful instrument of the believer's advancement.\footnote{Chrysostom, \textit{Homilies on Matthew}, hom.~71, opening exposition, NPNF1, vol.~10; Augustine, \textit{De doctrina christiana}, I.22.20--21 and I.30.31--33, NPNF1, vol.~2.}

The lawyer's motive receives more than one explanation. Chrysostom, comparing Mark, says the answer improved him. Augustine allows either such improvement or an initial testing undertaken with caution rather than malice. Neither Father diagnoses the inward state of every hearer. Matthew describes a test; Christ's response gives the testing person the truth he needs.\footnote{Augustine, \textit{De consensu evangelistarum}, II.73.141--142, NPNF1, vol.~6; Chrysostom, \textit{Matthew}, hom.~71.}

Jesus then becomes the questioner. He asks whose son the Christ is and receives the answer ``David's.'' Psalm 109:1 supplies the difficulty: David calls him Lord and speaks of his seat at God's right hand. Chrysostom insists that Jesus does not deny Davidic descent. He exposes the inadequacy of treating the Christ as merely human. David's Lord is also David's son. The Gospel ends without the opponents answering, and with further testing silenced. The commandment cannot be detached from this revelation of its teacher: the Lord claims a whole-hearted love whose meaning he himself discloses.

\subsection{Offertory: mercy for a named people}
The Offertory is a liturgical adaptation of Daniel 9:17--19, not a continuous extract. It introduces Daniel by name and joins the requests for hearing, the light of God's face upon the sanctuary, and gracious attention to the people bearing his name. In the full chapter Daniel confesses the community's failure, acknowledges God's justice, appeals to mercy rather than human righteousness, and seeks restoration. Its desolate sanctuary makes the petition concrete. Sin has consequences for a people's worship and common life; mercy is sought for that wounded whole.

Jerome explains the request concerning the sanctuary as an appeal that God's promised restoration take effect. He treats the language of God's ear and eyes as bodily language for divine attention. At verse 20 he offers two accounts of Daniel's confession: Daniel speaks as one of the people, or humbly joins himself to a sinful people without himself having committed the sin in question. These possibilities differ in personal culpability while agreeing in solidarity. Intercession neither claims that all are equally guilty nor allows the righteous to become indifferent to others' need.\footnote{Jerome, \textit{Commentaria in Danielem}, book II, on Dan.~9:17,18,20; Latin in Migne, PL 25, retained transcription, paragraph containing vv.~13--21.}

\subsection{Secret: past wounds and future danger}
The Secret addresses divine majesty in supplication and asks that the holy things being enacted free the people from offenses past and future. Cummiskey's historical English can sound as though later sins were forgiven in advance. Thomas's account of sacramental preservation draws the distinction. The Eucharist strengthens spiritual life by union with Christ and resists hostile powers through Christ's Passion. Its protection does not remove human freedom or the possibility of later sin. The petition concerns healing and preservation, not permission to act against the grace received.\footnote{Thomas, \textit{Summa theologiae}, III, q.~79, a.~6, corpus and ad 1; English Dominican translation in the Gutenberg Tertia electronic edition.}

The phrase describing what ``we enact'' joins prayer to the present holy action. The people ask that participation reach their history and their future conduct. Sin is not merely an obstacle left outside the church door. It is a wound brought to divine mercy, with the desire that the action now celebrated strengthen a different manner of life.

\subsection{Communion: gifts before the sovereign Lord}
Psalm 75 celebrates the judgment of God, before whom military power fails and the meek receive salvation. Its close commands vows and their payment, then gathers gift-bearers around the Lord who takes away the spirit of princes. The Communion retains both invitation and terror. The worshipper receives in the presence of one whose judgment cannot be managed by status or religious display.

Augustine distinguishes common fidelity---faith, hope, and godly living---from particular vowed commitments. He also insists that fulfillment depends on God's help. The command to pay does not make human strength self-sufficient. His next paragraph reads those around the Lord as persons who recognize truth as common and refuse to turn it into the property of a faction. Bellarmine's exposition stresses the concrete power of God over rulers' lives; Augustine applies the humbled spirit of princes to pride and turns rule inward, toward governance of one's own body. The sovereign judgment has public and personal force.\footnote{Augustine, \textit{Psalms}, Ps.~75, paragraphs 11--13; Bellarmine, \textit{Psalms}, Ps.~75, commentary on vv.~11--12 (Vulgate vv.~12--13).}

\subsection{Postcommunion: a cure with an eternal end}
The last prayer asks that sacred mysteries cure vices and procure an eternal remedy. Its realism is striking: reception is followed by a request for healing, not a declaration that moral struggle has disappeared. Thomas explains the Eucharist through spiritual nourishment---sustaining, increasing, restoring, and delighting the life of grace---and through the Passion of the Christ present in it. He then directs its fruit toward the perfect peace and unity of glory. The prayer thus names both the present wound and the final good. A change of conduct matters because love is being healed for communion that lasts.\footnote{Thomas, \textit{Summa}, III, q.~79, aa.~1--2, corpus.}
