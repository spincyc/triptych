\section{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\zlabel{triptych:concise:themes:start}

The Lord commands a whole-hearted love and gives the healing by which his people can live it. Confession, common calling, intercession and sacramental reception form a movement toward fidelity whose final good is an eternal remedy.

\subsection{Mercy makes the command a way to walk}
The Introit acknowledges God's justice before asking that he deal mercifully with his servant. Its verse names the blessed who walk the law. The desired end is therefore a life of obedience, while the means remains a mercy the servant must receive. Augustine explains the petition as a request for the righteousness God gives, rather than a claim upon righteousness achieved independently. Bellarmine brings the teaching of the same verse into desire: knowing a command is not yet loving its observance.\footnote{Augustine, \textit{Psalms}, Ps.~118, English paragraphs 123--124,136; Bellarmine, \textit{Psalms}, Ps.~118 ad 124.}

The Collect gives that dependence a direction. The people asks to escape corruption and follow God alone with a pure mind. The Gospel names the full reach of this allegiance: heart, soul and mind belong in love to God, while the neighbor is loved as oneself. Chrysostom joins the commands through obedience. A profession of love for God cannot make refusal of the neighbor harmless when God himself commands that neighbor's good. Augustine includes even the enemy within that obligation. The pure mind sought by the prayer must consequently become truthful mercy in the presence of another person.\footnote{Chrysostom, \textit{Matthew}, hom.~71; Augustine, \textit{De doctrina christiana}, I.22.20--21; I.30.31--33.}

The healing that the first reading follows already reaches beyond private improvement. The Collect prays for a people, and the Introit's servant stands among the blessed walkers. The heart is healed for a common life. A person asking to love more wholly must learn to carry a responsibility, tell a needed truth without contempt and continue in charity when feeling does not make obedience easy.

\subsection{One Lord gives a people its common life}
Paul's appeal begins with humility, meekness, patience and mutual bearing in charity. He then names what the called already share: body, Spirit, hope, Lord, faith, baptism and Father. Their effort to preserve peace answers a gift; it does not create the Lord whom agreement might otherwise treat as its own possession. Chrysostom describes the Spirit joining people separated by wealth, age and way of life. Thomas compares their common order to a city with one ruler, law, signs and end. Life and order illuminate the same calling from different directions.\footnote{Chrysostom, \textit{Ephesians}, homs.~9--10; Thomas, \textit{Super Ephesios}, ch.~4, lects.~1--2.}

The Gradual places the chosen people beside heavens established by Word and Spirit. Bellarmine begins with creation; Augustine also reads the righteous as spiritual heavens whose firmness comes from God. The chant thus sets divine gift before the people's achievement. The Alleluia then gives this people an afflicted person's cry. Augustine hears Christ with his members, preserving the distinction between their personal sin and the sinless Head. The community's praise therefore has room for the member who can only ask to be heard.\footnote{Augustine, \textit{Psalms}, Ps.~32, first exposition, paragraphs 6,12; Ps.~101, paragraphs 1--3; Bellarmine, \textit{Psalms}, Ps.~32 ad 6,12.}

The Gospel identifies the one Lord more fully. Davidic descent remains true, yet David calls the Christ his Lord. Chrysostom reads the question as disclosure of divinity, without denial of the human descent. The common people belongs to someone greater than its strongest personality or favored circle. Its unity must take the form of the love he commands.\footnote{Chrysostom, \textit{Matthew}, hom.~71, on Matt.~22:41--46.}

\subsection{Intercession brings the common wound to God}
Daniel's Offertory asks mercy for the sanctuary and the people bearing God's name. The full chapter admits communal failure and appeals to divine compassion. The chant does not turn the gathering into a collection of private claims to innocence. It teaches the worshipper to ask for the healing of a common life, including wounds that cannot be reduced to his own particular acts.

Jerome gives two accounts of Daniel's confession: he may speak as one of the people, or identify humbly with their sin without having committed it himself. The two accounts differ in personal culpability and agree in solidarity. Solidarity does not require an assertion that everyone bears equal guilt, and innocence of a particular wrong does not make another's need irrelevant. Jerome also reads the sanctuary petition through promised restoration. God's faithfulness supplies a reason to pray and to persevere, rather than a reason to remain silent.\footnote{Jerome, \textit{Daniel}, II, on Dan.~9:17--18,20--21.}

Here the three readings meet, each with its own emphasis. Healed charity asks that love become strong enough to carry another's burden. The gathered people recognizes the suffering member within its own voice. Faithful offering brings that need before God and accepts the work that mercy requires. Paul's patience and Thomas's fitting correction give intercession a practical consequence: one can seek another's good through both endurance and truthful action.\footnote{Thomas, \textit{Super Ephesios}, ch.~4, lect.~1.}

The Offertory's place in the formulary sharpens that consequence. Daniel's petition is not the historical scene of Paul's appeal or the Gospel controversy, and Daniel, not the afflicted psalmist, makes that confession. The Mass leaves those speakers distinct while ordering their words as one act of worship. The servant first asks mercy, the people hears its common calling, and intercession then accompanies an offering. Prayer for the common wound is thus more than an inward sentiment: it gives the worshipper a need to place before God and a good to pursue after the prayer. Healed charity supplies the converted desire; the gathered-people reading identifies whose good is carried; faithful offering makes that concern answerable in conduct.

\subsection{The mysteries give strength for fidelity and glory}
The Secret asks that the holy action free the people from past and future offenses. Thomas explains the Eucharist through spiritual nourishment, Christ present and his Passion. Grace sustains and strengthens charity. His account also preserves the freedom of a person who may later turn away. The petition is therefore no advance permission to sin; its future reach is the gift of a life to be lived faithfully. This teaching illuminates the prayer.\footnote{Thomas, \textit{Summa theologiae}, III, q.~79, aa.~1,6, corpus, and a.~6 ad 1.}

The Communion names fidelity as vows paid and gifts brought before the feared Lord. Augustine distinguishes obligations common to Christians from special undertakings, and insists that fulfillment needs God's help. The vow to be paid may be an existing promise or duty honored, rather than a new private vow. His reading of those standing around the Lord also opposes the private ownership of truth: the common center belongs to no faction. Bellarmine's emphasis on God's power over rulers and Augustine's rebuke of pride together make judgment public and personal.\footnote{Augustine, \textit{Psalms}, Ps.~75, paragraphs 11--13; Bellarmine, \textit{Psalms}, Ps.~75 ad 11--12.}

The Postcommunion asks for cured vices and an eternal remedy. Reception is followed by a plea for healing, so remaining struggle need not make the gift unreal. Yet present moral improvement is not the whole end. Thomas directs sacramental nourishment toward perfect peace and unity in glory. The heart's cure, the people's inheritance and worship's lasting fruit reach their completion together in God. The Mass gives hope enough to practice charity now, while teaching its recipients to keep asking for the remedy that brings charity to its end.\footnote{Thomas, \textit{Summa}, III, q.~79, a.~2, corpus.}
