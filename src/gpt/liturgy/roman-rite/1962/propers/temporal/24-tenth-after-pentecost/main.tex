\section*{The Propers: Themes and Movement}
\label{triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

\begin{massbox}{Governing thesis: received mercy turns gifted persons from comparison toward common worship}
The Eternal hears and sustains the burdened; divine omnipotence is chiefly shown in pardon and mercy; one Spirit distributes diverse manifestations for common benefit; Christ overturns self-justifying comparison; the offering and communicants await acceptance, renewal, and help from God.
\end{massbox}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0.1em]
\item \textbf{Cry and run:} burden becomes trust in merciful power.
\item \textbf{Receive and serve:} diverse gifts disclose one divine giver.
\item \textbf{Descend justified:} religious inventory yields to petition.
\item \textbf{Offer and be renewed:} acceptance remains God's gift.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\emph{Reading order.} These stages follow the appointed sequence. Their architecture is source-grounded editorial synthesis; no cited witness is made author of the complete formulary.

\subsection*{Burden becomes movement toward mercy\enspace\properrefs{Int., Coll.}}

Psalm 54 joins repeated prayer to a command to cast care upon the sustaining Lord. The cry does not deny fear or betrayal; trust acts within them. The Collect then locates divine power in pardon and mercy and asks that mercy be multiplied so that the petitioners may run toward the promises and share heavenly goods. Augustine hears the psalm's evening, morning, and noon as continual prayer; Aquinas receives the Collect's axiom as an account of pardoning authority. Neither makes distress unreal or mercy indifferent to repentance.

The old Gelasian witness places an early form of the Collect among other Masses, with \latin{gratiam} where the 1962 Missal has \latin{misericordiam}; the prayer later appears in the Gregorian supplement, not the core Hadrianum. This history establishes transmission, not authorship by Gelasius or Gregory and not the purpose of its placement here.

\subsection*{One giver makes difference useful rather than competitive\enspace\properrefs{Ep., Grad., All.}}

Paul moves from remembered subjection to mute idols to confession of the Lord Jesus in the Holy Spirit. Diverse gifts, ministries, and operations have the same Spirit, Lord, and God; each manifestation is given \latin{ad utilitatem}, for benefit. Chrysostom stresses that gifts are free rather than debts and that difference does not imply a different giver. Ambrosiaster similarly joins grace rather than merit to Trinitarian unity and ecclesial edification. The passage neither equates charism with office or talent nor guarantees every listed manifestation to every Christian.

The Gradual submits judgment to God's countenance and asks protection beneath God's wings; the Alleluia renders praise and vow in Zion. Augustine reads the pupil and wings Christologically and the Zion verse toward heavenly worship. Those are received spiritual readings, not exclusive historical senses. In the appointed sequence, guarded sight and praise restrain the temptation to convert spiritual manifestation into rank.

\newpage

\subsection*{Prayer ceases to be a comparative inventory\enspace\properrefs{Gosp., Off.}}

Luke identifies the audience before narrating the parable: Jesus speaks to some who trusted in themselves as just and despised others. The Pharisee's fasting and tithing become a self-comparison; the publican stands afar, lowers his eyes, strikes his breast, and petitions propitiating mercy. God, not posture alone, justifies. The target is self-grounded contempt, not the Jewish people, disciplined fasting, tithing, or good works as such.

Augustine's \emph{Sermon 115}---the same work numbered 65 in the older English New Testament series---contrasts self-praise and insult with confession and pardon. Cyril emphasizes the difference between boastful comparison and humble petition. Chrysostom uses the parable in two discrete ways: fasting without accompanying duties is fruitless (\emph{On the Statues} 3.8), and even a true accusation does not license evil-speaking (3.13). Anthony of Padua's collection gives this Gospel to its Eleventh Sunday after Pentecost with a different Introit and Epistle; its penitential reading is direct Gospel reception, not reception of this entire 1962 Mass.

The Offertory's lifted soul prevents humility from collapsing into despair. The publican does not lift his eyes, yet Psalm 24 lifts the soul toward God in trust. Augustine reads waiting without shame as persevering dependence. The cross-proper relation is demonstrable in the appointed words, though no checked witness establishes it as compiler intent.

\subsection*{The altar receives what boasting cannot secure\enspace\properrefs{Sec., Comm., Postcomm.}}

The Secret asks that dedicated sacrifices be rendered to God and become remedies for the offerers. The Communion quotes Psalm 50's final expectation of accepted sacrifice. In the complete psalm, contrite heart and restored Zion precede that verse; acceptance and contrition are partners, not rivals. Augustine refuses both an abolition of sacrifice and confidence in external performance detached from the one saving sacrifice.

The Postcommunion ends in continued dependence: God does not cease restoring recipients by divine mysteries, and they ask not to be deprived of gracious aids. The Trinity Preface directs the celebration to triune praise. Mercy therefore does not erase gifts, works, judgment, or offering; it relocates their source, measure, acceptance, and fruit in God.

\begin{threestable}{Movement}{Decisive gift}{Controlling limit}
\textbf{Cry and run} & God hears, sustains, pardons, and multiplies mercy. & Trust does not deny distress; mercy does not bless contempt.\\
\textbf{Receive and serve} & One divine giver distributes manifestations for common benefit. & Gifts are neither merit badges nor interchangeable offices.\\
\textbf{Descend justified} & God justifies the sinner who petitions mercy. & Humility is not self-hatred; good works are not condemned.\\
\textbf{Offer and renew} & God accepts sacrifice, makes it remedy, and continually restores. & Neither ritual inventory nor inward feeling secures acceptance.\\
\end{threestable}

\section*{The Propers: Detailed Commentary}

\subsection*{The heard cry and merciful omnipotence\enspace\properrefs{Int., Coll.}}

Psalm 54 is not serene abstraction. Betrayal, fear, desire for flight, hostile speech, and an appeal for judgment surround the selected verses. The Introit gathers the cry at appointed hours, the Eternal's judgment, and the command to cast care upon God. Augustine's direct exposition treats evening, morning, and noon as prayer embracing Christ's Passion and the Church's whole day; his ecclesial reading does not replace the lament's human crisis. Theodoret likewise follows the movement from affliction to confidence in divine care. The liturgical speaker can therefore entrust a burden without pretending that danger is imaginary.

The Collect's grammar moves from confession to petition and end: God's omnipotence is chiefly manifested by sparing and showing mercy; mercy is to be multiplied; the petitioners run toward promises; God makes them sharers in heavenly goods. Thomas cites the prayer's axiom when explaining that mercy manifests power because God pardons by supreme authority. \emph{Misericordiae vultus} 6 later calls the prayer ancient and uses its axiom to identify mercy, not domination, as divine power's disclosure. These are receptions of the prayer, not evidence of its original Sunday assignment.

The Old Gelasian sacramentary, Book III, ``Item alia missa,'' transmits the set with \latin{gratiam}; the later Gregorian supplement, SupG 1159--1161, assigns it under a historically offset Sunday heading. The 1962 form has \latin{misericordiam}. The supplementary status matters: \latin{SupG} is not the core Gregorian/Hadrianum and cannot establish Gregory the Great's authorship. Nor does the early set prove that all ten 1962 elements were composed together.

\sectionguard
\subsection*{Charisms ordered to common benefit\enspace\properrefs{Ep.}}

The literary context checks isolated uses of the gift list. Paul first recalls former movement toward mute idols, then supplies a criterion centered on Jesus' lordship in the Holy Spirit. The repeated \latin{divisiones} and \latin{idem} hold difference and unity together: gifts, ministries, and operations differ, while Spirit, Lord, and God remain the source. Verse 7 states the social end before individual examples appear: manifestation is given \latin{ad utilitatem}. Chapters 12--14 continue through one body, love, and intelligible order in assembly.

Chrysostom's \emph{Homily 29} follows the unit verse by verse. He calls the manifestations gifts rather than debts, distinguishes Spirit, Lord, and God without dividing their action, and directs each manifestation toward common profit. His historical account of tongues among the newly baptized and of some gifts becoming obscure belongs to his reception; it is not a universal magisterial settlement of cessation. Ambrosiaster similarly argues that grace excludes boasting and reads the triadic clauses as one divine working for ecclesial usefulness.

Aquinas's \emph{Super I ad Corinthios} XII distinguishes gifts, ministries, and operations while returning each to the common source and useful end. Basil cites verses 4--6 and 11 in \emph{On the Holy Spirit} 16.37 to argue inseparable divine operation with genuine agency of the Spirit. The Second Vatican Council receives verse 7 in \emph{Lumen gentium} 12 and \emph{Apostolicam actuositatem} 3 for diverse charisms ordered to building and discernment. That later reception cannot be retrojected into Paul's first-century terminology, but it confirms that gift and common service remain inseparable.

\begin{fourstable}{Textual step}{Gift}{Social force}{Limit}
\textbf{Former leading} & The Corinthians remember subjection to mute idols. & Christian discernment begins with confession of Jesus. & Past pagan identity does not make Gentile Christians lesser members.\\
\textbf{One source} & Spirit, Lord, and God act amid differences. & Difference need not become rivalry. & The three clauses are not three unrelated economies.\\
\textbf{Manifestation} & Each receives for benefit. & A gift is accountable to the body. & Not every ability or claim is thereby authenticated.\\
\textbf{Distribution} & The Spirit divides as he wills. & No recipient authors the gift's status. & Stewardship and discernment remain required.\\
\end{fourstable}

\subsection*{Guarded sight and praise rendered\enspace\properrefs{Grad., All.}}

Psalm 16 asks that judgment emerge from God's face and that divine eyes see equity before asking protection as the pupil of an eye under wings. Augustine reads the praying Christ together with his body, and the pupil Christologically as what receives light. Theodoret emphasizes exact divine discernment and shelter. Neither reading turns the petitioner into a self-certifying innocent; judgment remains God's.

Psalm 64 gathers praise, vows, forgiveness, the prayer of all flesh, creation, and harvest at Zion. Augustine treats praise as owed to God and the vow as the self offered in worship; his spiritual Zion reading is reception rather than the sole literal horizon. His adjacent anti-Jewish and supersessionist rhetoric is neither repeated nor endorsed. Between the Epistle and Gospel, the two chants move from protected, equitable vision to praise owed in Zion. That movement is an editorial reading of sequence, not recovered compiler intent.

\sectionguard
\subsection*{The publican's petition and the Pharisee's comparison\enspace\properrefs{Gosp.}}

The audience statement is decisive. Jesus addresses ``some'' who trusted in themselves as just and despised others. The parable does not authorize a caricature of Jews or Pharisees as a people. Nor does it commend the publican's occupation. It contrasts prayer turned into comparative inventory with a sinner's petition to God. The Greek \latin{pros heauton} admits punctuation nuance around the Pharisee's prayer; either way, Luke's framing and conclusion disclose self-trust and contempt. \latin{Hilastheti} asks merciful or propitiating action, and the disputed comparative force of \latin{par' ekeinon} does not alter that God declares the reversal.

Augustine, \emph{Sermon 115.2--3} (older English New Testament series 65), observes that the Pharisee praises himself and insults the petitioner, while the publican confesses and receives pardon from the Judge. His anti-Pelagian extension belongs to the sermon's setting and should not make a verbal self-accusation automatically efficacious. Cyril's \emph{Sermon 120 on Luke} likewise opposes self-praise to embodied confession and keeps justification God's action.

Chrysostom's \emph{Homilies on the Statues} makes two sharply bounded reuses. At 3.8, the Pharisee's fast does not save a life lacking accompanying virtue. At 3.13, the Pharisee speaks evil even when the accusation is factually true and thereby loses his advantage; truth does not license detraction. Anthony of Padua's official collection assigns Luke 18 to its Eleventh Sunday after Pentecost, paired with a different Introit and Epistle. His account of pride, contrition, and grace is medieval Gospel reception only, not evidence for this 1962 formulary's complete architecture.

Eastern Byzantine reception places the parable at the opening of the Triodion, where the Sunday of the Publican and Pharisee begins preparation for Lent. Its hymns and contemporary Orthodox explanations praise humility without treating good works as evils. This is a distinct liturgical afterlife of Luke 18, not a branch of the Roman Sunday or evidence for its historical compilation.

\sectionguard
\subsection*{Trust, remedy, accepted sacrifice, and continued help\enspace\properrefs{Off., Sec., Comm., Postcomm.}}

Psalm 24 lifts the soul, places trust in God, and refuses to let shame or enemies become final judges. Augustine reads the waiting speaker as dependent and still in need of guidance and forgiveness. Heard after the Gospel, lifted soul and lowered eyes are complementary rather than contradictory: humility is Godward trust, not spiritual collapse. No checked witness proves that this juxtaposition caused the Roman arrangement.

The Secret's verbs keep gift and giver distinct. Dedicated sacrifices are rendered to God because God assigned them to the honor of his name, and God is asked to make those same gifts remedies for the offerers. The prayer neither states that offering earns mercy nor reduces sacrifice to subjective devotion. No direct ancient commentary on this prayer was located in the checked corpus; the reading remains grammatical and doctrinal.

Psalm 50's final verse must retain its context. Confession, cleansing, a contrite and humbled heart, and restored Zion precede the expectation that sacrifices of justice, oblations, and holocausts will be accepted. Augustine distinguishes former figures and the one saving sacrifice while refusing to detach outward offering from contrition. The Communion therefore does not abolish sacrifice and does not make contrition a substitute for Eucharistic oblation. It voices divine acceptance after the Gospel has exposed self-authentication.

\section*{The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities}

\noindent\emph{These exploratory proposals arose in an AI-assisted editorial process. They are attributed to none of the cited authorities and claim no historical compositional intent. Each names its anchors and controlling limit.}

\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Divine leveling without competitive self-ranking.\enspace\properrefs{Int., Gosp.}}
\emph{Classification: not located in the checked corpus.} The Introit says the Eternal humbles threatening adversaries; the Gospel says self-exalters are humbled and self-humblers exalted. Together they distinguish protective divine judgment from using another person's fall as one's own proof of justice. The fruit is resistance to both oppression and moral scorekeeping. Enemy language may not be assigned to contemporary opponents, and no checked witness joins these texts.

\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Downcast eyes can accompany a lifted soul.\enspace\properrefs{Grad., Gosp., Off.}}
\emph{Classification: near analogue located.} The publican will not lift his eyes; the Offertory lifts the soul; the Gradual asks God to guard the eye. Bodily abasement need not mean spiritual collapse: Godward desire can coexist with lowered gaze and protected vision. Anthony says the publican does not look so that he may be looked upon, but no located witness joins the two psalms. Posture alone does not establish humility.

\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Gift received, boast relinquished, offering accepted.\enspace\properrefs{Ep., Gosp., Comm.}}
\emph{Classification: near analogue located.} The Spirit distributes as he wills; the Pharisee inventories achievements; Communion awaits God's acceptance of sacrifice. Keeping giver, recipient, and acceptance distinct can make charisms and offerings noncompetitive. Chrysostom controls unequal gifts and one source, but no checked witness supplies the three-element relation. Stewardship is not excused by denying ownership.

\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Mercy is omnipotence enacted without spectacle.\enspace\properrefs{Int., Coll., Ep., Gosp.}}
\emph{Classification: near analogue located.} The Collect's received axiom locates power chiefly in pardon; the Gospel's sinner goes home justified; the Introit names sustaining help even while the Epistle lists miracles. Divine power's decisive movement here may be restoration rather than display. Aquinas receives the axiom but does not establish this Sunday-wide relation. Mercy does not negate judgment, repentance, or genuine signs.

\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{The altar receives what boasting cannot secure.\enspace\properrefs{Gosp., Sec., Comm.}}
\emph{Classification: not located in the checked corpus.} Fasting and tithing cannot self-justify; the Secret asks God to make gifts remedy; Communion says God will accept sacrifice. The sequence opposes self-authentication, not works or sacrifice, to mercy. Psalm 50's contrite-heart context supports the distinction but is canonical context beyond the selected verse. No compositional intent is claimed.

\Needspace{7\baselineskip}
\noindent\textbf{Many voices remain one heard prayer.\enspace\properrefs{Int., Ep., All.}}
\emph{Classification: near analogue located.} Prophecy, tongues, and interpretation stand between one afflicted cry heard by God and praise owed in Zion. Charismatic plurality may be framed by prayer and praise rather than display. Chrysostom's common-benefit reading is the nearest analogue; no source ties chant to Pauline tongues, and liturgical song must not be identified mechanically with a charism.
