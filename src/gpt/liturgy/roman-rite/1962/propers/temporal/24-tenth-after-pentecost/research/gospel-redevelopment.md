# Gospel redevelopment record — Luke 18:9–14

## Corrections controlled by this record

- The Temple is a setting within a parable, not the location of an
  independently recoverable event. The former `c. AD 29–30` event row was
  therefore rejected.
- Ancient attribution to Luke and Achaian provenance remain traditions.
  Modern Catholic orientation commonly places final composition about AD
  80–90 for a substantially Gentile audience outside Palestine; the city is
  not known.
- The interpretation is controlled by Luke 18:9 and 18:14. Neither posture,
  occupation, ethnicity, nor observance alone explains the verdict.

## Canonical and historical controls

The complete unit was read in its Lukan sequence: the persistent widow
(18:1–8), the parable (18:9–14), children received as the kingdom's pattern
(18:15–17), the rich ruler (18:18–30), passion prediction (18:31–34), and the
blind beggar (18:35–43). This context supports prayer, dependence, status, and
reception of the kingdom. It does not turn the parable into a report about two
identifiable worshipers.

The commentary avoids an anti-Jewish binary. Fasting and tithing are Jewish
religious goods rather than the objects of Jesus' condemnation; self-trust and
contempt deform them in the story. Luke gives Pharisees varied narrative
roles. The tax collector is not declared occupationally righteous: Luke's
Zacchaeus narrative retains repentance and restitution within the same
Gospel. General historical orientation concerning imperial tax collection
controls the social asymmetry, but no precise administrative status is
assigned to this fictional publican.

## Lexical controls

The checked Greek form is Luke 18:11–14 in SBLGNT:

- `pros heauton` permits a syntactic question—standing “by himself” or praying
  “to himself”—that punctuation alone cannot decide doctrinally;
- `hilastheti moi tō hamartōlō` asks divine merciful/propitiating action for
  “me, the sinner,” with God as agent;
- `par’ ekeinon` has a disputed comparative force (“rather than” or “in
  comparison with” the other), but both readings remain bounded by Luke's
  explicit exaltation/humbling conclusion.

No English rendering was silently revised on lexical grounds; the appointed
English remains the registered Douay–Rheims/Challoner text.

## Reception audit

| Witness | Exact locus and role | Retained result | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tertullian, *Adversus Marcionem* IV.36 | direct ancient reception | The Creator judges pride and justifies humble petition. | Anti-Marcionite argument is not Luke's narrative setting. |
| Augustine, *Sermon* 115.2–3 (older English NT 65) | direct patristic exegesis | Self-praise and accusation are opposed to confession and pardon. | Anti-Pelagian application does not make wording or posture automatically efficacious. |
| Cyril of Alexandria, *Commentary on Luke*, Sermon 120 | direct patristic exegesis | Boastful comparison is opposed to embodied confession; justification is God's. | English survives through Payne Smith's Syriac-based translation; it is not a complete Greek control. |
| Chrysostom, *On the Statues* 3.8, 3.13 | direct moral reception | Fasting without virtue is fruitless; even true accusation does not license detraction. | The two loci make distinct applications and must not be merged. |
| *Rule of Benedict* 7 | later Latin spiritual reception | Lowered gaze and breast-beating enter a structured account of humility. | Posture is evidence within a formed life, not a mechanism of justification. |
| Anthony of Padua, historically numbered Eleventh Sunday after Pentecost, §§3–14 | medieval liturgical-homiletic reception | Pride, contrition, and grace receive sustained treatment. | Its Introit and Epistle differ; it cannot establish this formulary's architecture. |
| Byzantine Triodion, Sunday of the Publican and Pharisee | distinct Eastern liturgical reception | The parable begins Lenten preparation and commends humility without condemning good works. | It is not evidence for the Roman Sunday's compilation. |

The Jesus Prayer was searched as a possible afterlife. No checked witness
established Luke 18:13 as its simple historical origin, so the publication
retains only a bounded verbal affinity and rejects an origin claim.

## Publication boundary

The replacement detailed commentary distinguishes textual observation,
historical orientation, and documented reception. Cross-proper claims remain
outside this fragment. The source URLs already printed in the canonical
reference list were checked as finding and verification routes; adding exact
repository passage records for every reception witness remains a separate
source-library task and must not be simulated by overclaiming binding state.
