## Medieval reception and modern historical synthesis

Amalarius, Innocent III, and Durandus control claims about influential medieval spiritual reception, not the first historical cause of every ceremony. Exact textual controls include Martène's printed ordines and *Missa Illyrica* loci; Silva-Tarouca's *Capitulare ecclesiastici ordinis*; Bernold's *Micrologus* in PL 151; Warner's edition of the Stowe Missal; Lippe's 1474 Missal; Botte's critical apparatus for the Roman Canon; and the conciliar collections and manuscript editions named in the unit matrix. Josef Jungmann is used as a secondary synthesis beside those primary witnesses, with printed volume and page ranges attached to discrete claims rather than cited as an undifferentiated authority. Where dating remains disputed, the publication uses ranges or calibrated language rather than a single scholar's reconstruction as fact. Broader works consulted for orientation but not used to control a published claim are not padded into the publication bibliography.

## Legacy thirty-three-step architecture audit

John T. McMahon, *Pray the Mass*, Part I, 13th revised ed. (Pellegrini, 1959), is the documented catechetical antecedent. The title/imprimatur and the express thirty-three-step/year-of-Christ explanation were checked in the public scan at <https://www.ccwatershed.org/pdfs/pray-mass-booklet/download/>; publication context was checked at <https://www.ccwatershed.org/2014/09/13/pray-mass-booklet-and-cards/>.

The publication does **not** claim that McMahon's list is official, ancient, universal, or coextensive with the 1962 Missal. An earlier edition nevertheless preserved his number through a different editorial reconstruction. The 2026-07-15 interpretive audit found that the number still controlled scale, boundaries, transitions, and visible evidence fields despite disclaimers to the contrary. The revised publication therefore retires the count from its reader-facing architecture. `sequence-inventory.md` retains the old rows only as a stable coverage concordance, and `interpretive-review.md` records the quantitative and movement-by-movement findings.

## Movement-level text, biblical-context, and chronology audit

The claim-level record is *unit-source-matrix.md*. It distinguishes literal quotation, canonical synthesis, typology, allusion, and analogy; records a first checked witness or safe terminus where one has actually been established; and marks secure, probable, traditional, and unresolved claims. Its row numbers preserve audit continuity but no longer dictate published chapters or word budgets. The former visible dossiers overstated the matrix's reach: several broad rows supported one chronological claim while enclosing several distinct prayers or acts, and secondary synthesis sometimes stood where a primary locus was still needed. The revision therefore downgrades those claims, names unresolved primary controls, and uses patristic or Thomistic material only where it bears directly on the movement.

Project-authored close English renderings were removed on 2026-07-14; remaining English statements of ritual function are visibly third person and do not supply recitation wording. A generic reference to “1474/1570” is not accepted as proof: the 1474 pages were checked directly, and four absences materially control the account---no opening sign or complete received step complex at p. 198, no exact doubled *Confiteor* there, no *Per evangelica dicta* after the principal Gospel at p. 199, and no Last Gospel at pp. 210--212.

The expanded scriptural audit uses complete literary settings rather than isolated verbal parallels. Examples include Psalm 42 as an exile-and-vindication lament; Isaiah 6 as judgment, purification, and mission; Luke 2 as a Messianic announcement answered in praise; Nehemiah 8 as covenant renewal; Revelation 19 as judgment, reign, and nuptial Alleluia; Daniel 3 as exilic prayer without temple sacrifice; Matthew 6 as filial common prayer joined to forgiveness; 1 Corinthians 10–11 as communion judged by ecclesial unity; and the ritual reading John 1:1--14 within the larger literary Prologue, commonly bounded at John 1:1--18. These contexts prevent a later Roman gesture from being presented as though a biblical verse directly prescribed it.

Additional dated controls include Nicaea (325; DS 125) and Chalcedon (451; ACO II.1.2, p. 128) for the Creed, with the surviving 381 acts' textual limitation expressly preserved; Third Toledo canon 2 (589; Mansi IX, 993) for Western Eucharistic use before the Lord's Prayer; Berno's account of Roman adoption in 1014 (PL 142:1060--1061); the *Praecepta synodalia* under Odo of Sully, around 1200, canon 28 (Pontal I, p. 82), as an early catalyst for Host elevation rather than its universal invention; and the Sacred Congregation of Rites decree *Novis hisce temporibus* (13 November 1962; AAS 54 [1962], 873), effective 8 December 1962, for Saint Joseph in *Communicantes*. The publication distinguishes promulgation of the typical edition from the juridical amendment, records that the collated facsimile contains the amendment, and does not infer the date of that physical impression.

The renewed deep dive directly checked Aquinas, *ST* III, q. 83, a. 4, ad 7--9, for the fruit sought in *Quam oblationem*, the accepted offerers signified by Abel, Abraham, and Melchizedek, and the non-spatial/heavenly-altar readings of *Supplices*; q. 83, a. 5, ad 7--10, separately for fraction, commingling, and ablution. Cyril's *Mystagogical Catechesis* V was used as a movement-shaped ancient mystagogy rather than a template for the Roman Canon. Cyprian, *De dominica oratione* 8--9 and 31, controls common filial prayer and the moral force of lifted hearts. Irenaeus IV.18 controls created gift, conscience, reconciliation, and resurrection; Augustine's Sermons 227 and 272 control the relation of sacramental and ecclesial body.

Fourth-century Eastern witnesses and seventh-century Roman-Canon manuscripts control the combined Sanctus. The received name of Saint Michael in the offertory-incense blessing is described from the attested form. *Lauda Sion* is dated only about 1264.

For medieval private prayers, the audit names the earliest checked ancestor, distinguishes partial from exact form and different from received placement, and then gives a printed terminus where one has actually been checked. It does not assign an author where the manuscript record establishes only gradual Roman, Gallican, or Frankish reception. In particular, the priest's triple *Domine, non sum dignus* is not backdated to the 1474 Missal's single recitation, and the faithful's *Ecce Agnus* complex is kept distinct from the priest's medieval preparation. The traditional Celestine I Introit notice, Gregory I attribution for part of *Hanc igitur*, and Sergius I Agnus notice are preserved as retrospective evidence rather than treated as contemporary autograph decrees.

The appendix paraphrases the 1962-era form and quotes only short Latin incipits. It distinguishes the short 1886 Saint Michael prayer from the much longer exorcism formula directly checked in the 1890 publication and rejects the alleged post-Mass vision or dialogue attributed to Leo XIII as undocumented legend: no contemporary primary witness was found, popular dates conflict, and the story cannot carry a historical claim. The base law's phrase “Mass without chant” is retained instead of silently converting it into a claim about every possible Low Mass; the controlling 1960 exceptions are stated from AAS 52 (1960), 360.

## Known limits and review state

- This is not a diplomatic edition or exhaustive manuscript stemma.
- Selective Missal quotations preserve the collated words, capitalization, and punctuation, with omissions marked by ellipses. As an explicit house-style normalization, they expand printed ligatures to `ae`/`oe`, omit stress accents, and use `V.`/`R.` for the printed versicle and response signs.
- The publication contains no project-authored English prayer or ritual formula and offers no original translation of the Latin. Its English prose analyzes ritual function; any English prayer wording would require an exact, identified historical or approved human witness.
- Exact historical dating of some Canon clauses, preparatory prayers, and cross-regional influence remains probabilistic.
- The central Roman locus for the 1886 Leonine revision remains outstanding; the authoritative 1962-horizon exception corpus is controlled by the 1960 decree.
- Medieval mystical readings are labeled as reception rather than silently converted into compositional history.
- Present canonical authorization for celebrations using the 1962 Missal is outside scope.
