# Research scope: The Ordinary and Order of the Mass

## Question and intended reader

How do the stable texts, proper positions, gestures, voices, silences, and historical strata of the 1962 Roman Order of Mass form one act of Christ and the Church? The intended reader is a serious lay reader who can follow basic liturgical and theological vocabulary but should not need a ceremonial manual or specialist command of Latin.

The work is textual, historical, patristic, symbolic, and theological. It is not a critical edition, a complete history of every manuscript family, a performance manual, a polemic between Missal editions, or a judgment about the canonical authorization of a particular celebration.

The revised exposition follows natural textual and ritual movements rather than forcing the rite through thirty-three equal dossiers. Its numbered movement headings identify the received incipit or standard ritual name; interpretive titles remain visibly subordinate. Each movement asks a question demanded by its material and uses the fitting method: literary exegesis, rhetoric, analysis of speakers and sound, spatial or gestural interpretation, sacramental grammar, or historical stratigraphy. The research records still control received boundaries, biblical modes of use, historical witnesses, and the authority of patristic, Thomistic, doctrinal, and editorial readings. This deepens the argument without converting the work into a diplomatic edition, an original English translation, or a catalog of unsupported firsts.

## Source roles

1. **Controlling received text and rite:** the 1962 *Missale Romanum*, especially `Ritus servandus`, `Ordo Missae`, and `Canon Missae`.
2. **Doctrinal control:** Scripture; Trent, Session XXII; *Mediator Dei*; *Sacrosanctum Concilium* 47--58; *Lumen gentium* 10--11; *Presbyterorum ordinis* 2, 5; the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* 1322--1419; and Aquinas, *Summa theologiae* III, qq. 73--83.
3. **Early structural witnesses:** *Didache* 9--10, 14; Justin Martyr, *First Apology* 65--67; Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* IV.18; Cyprian, *Epistle* 63; the text conventionally called the *Apostolic Tradition*; Cyril of Jerusalem, *Mystagogical Catechesis* V; Ambrose, *De sacramentis* IV--V; Augustine, Sermons 227 and 272.
4. **Roman and medieval development:** early Roman sacramentaries; *Ordo Romanus I*; Gregory the Great where securely relevant; Amalarius, *Liber officialis*; Innocent III, *De sacro altaris mysterio*; Durandus, *Rationale divinorum officiorum* IV; and the late medieval curial/full-Missal tradition.
5. **Codification and modern edition history:** the 1474 printed Roman Missal; Pius V's 1570 Missal and *Quo primum*; Clement VIII's 1604 and Urban VIII's 1634 revisions; the twentieth-century rubrical reforms; John XXIII's *Rubricarum instructum* and the 1962 typical edition.
6. **Historical synthesis:** identified modern liturgical historians are used to distinguish textual strata and disputed dating; their reconstruction is not assigned magisterial authority.
7. **Legacy thirty-three-step antecedent:** John T. McMahon, *Pray the Mass*, Part I, 13th rev. ed. (1959), documents a modern catechetical scheme and its explicit years-of-Christ symbolism. It explains the superseded architecture and remains in the research concordance; it does not control the present chapters.
8. **Adjacent post-Mass law:** Sacred Congregation of Rites acts from 1884, 1887, and 1960; the Sacred Congregation of Indulgences and Sacred Relics acts of 1904 under Pius X; Pius XI's 1930 direction; and *Inter Oecumenici* no. 48(j) control the final Leonine-prayer appendix. The 1962 Missal itself controls the negative boundary: its *Ordo Missae* ends with the Last Gospel and does not print those prayers as part of Mass.

## Claim and quotation discipline

- Short Latin incipits are checked against the facsimile. The publication does not compose or translate English prayer or ritual formulas. Any English prayer wording would require an exact, identified historical or approved human witness; ordinary English prose remains third-person analysis rather than a recitation substitute.
- A Father's direct comment on a scriptural or eucharistic text is distinguished from later doctrinal illumination of a medieval Roman prayer.
- Patristic structure is not forced into identity with the 1962 sequence. Continuity concerns the Eucharistic action's basic lines; later units may be genuine organic developments without being textually apostolic.
- Medieval allegories are received as historical spiritual interpretations, not automatic explanations of original ritual intent.
- The Roman Canon's stability is described with calibrated dating; “unchanged since Gregory the Great” is not used as a literal claim that every word and rubric was fixed by one pope.
- Pius V standardized and promulgated an inherited Roman Missal after Trent; he did not compose the rite ex nihilo.
- “Secure,” “probable,” “traditional,” and “unresolved” are distinct audit states. A retrospective papal notice, a modern consensus range, and a dated decree are not narrated with the same certainty.
- Biblical use is identified as literal, canonical, typological, allusive, or analogical. A passage can illuminate a gesture without being its historical institution, and the surrounding argument or narrative controls the reuse.
- The Leonine appendix quotes only short stable incipits and otherwise paraphrases the received sequence. It distinguishes universal prescription, encouragement, local additions, intention, and eventual suppression rather than blending them into one timeless form.

## Material uncertainties and limits

- The authorship, date, and reconstructable text of the *Apostolic Tradition* are disputed. It is used as a redactionally complex early witness, not uncritically as “Hippolytus's Roman Canon.”
- The exact dating of individual Roman Canon clauses and the direction of influence among Roman, Gallican, and Frankish materials are often probabilistic.
- The `Ordo Missae` prints a priest-centered complete-Missal presentation while Solemn Mass preserves differentiated ministries. The exposition names that relationship without claiming that Low Mass is historically prior.
- The former thirty-three divisions were editorial, not official or historically intrinsic. They remain only as a coverage concordance; the publication now combines and divides material at natural ritual and textual joints.
- McMahon's thirty-three “steps” are acknowledged as the closest documented antecedent to the superseded architecture, not as an anatomy of the Missal or proof of compositional intent.
- The Leonine prayers were externally prescribed after Masses without chant and were still operative in 1962, but they are outside the Mass. The 1962-horizon exception corpus is controlled by the Sacred Congregation of Rites' 9 March 1960 decree (AAS 52 [1960], 360); the central Roman locus for the 1886 revision remains outstanding. The 17 June and 19 August 1904 acts have been directly checked for the threefold Sacred Heart invocation, indulgence, desired uniformity, and absence of a strict universal obligation.
- The alleged Leo XIII vision is excluded as undocumented legend. The short 1886 Saint Michael prayer is not confused with the much longer 1890 exorcism formula, and Pius XI's act is not attributed to Fatima without documentary evidence.
- Independent review by a liturgical historian, patristics specialist, and dogmatic/sacramental theologian remains outstanding.
