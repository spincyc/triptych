\section{How the calendar was constructed}

\subsection{Two interlocking cycles}

The calendar embodied in the 1962 Missal is not a list devised at one moment. Its oldest structural layer is the weekly and annual cycle centered on Sunday and Pascha. Around that center grew the seasons of preparation and celebration, the anniversaries of martyrs and other saints, dedications, and feasts of the Lord and the Blessed Virgin Mary. In the mature Roman books these appear as two interlocking series: the \emph{Temporale}, whose dates are generated chiefly from Christmas and Easter, and the \emph{Sanctorale}, whose observances normally occupy fixed month-and-day dates. The proper calendars of places and religious institutes add a third, particular layer.

The collision of those layers required rules of precedence, occurrence, transfer, commemoration, and concurrence. A calendar entry consequently does not, by itself, settle every celebration in a given year. It supplies one candidate and its class; the rubrics decide the result. Thomas J. Talley's historical synthesis is useful for the formation of the annual cycle, while the successive Roman liturgical books and decrees are the controlling witnesses for the calendar's juridical form.

\subsection{From curial use to a standardized printed book}

Medieval Roman and local calendars varied. The printed books issued after the Council of Trent did not create the liturgical year, but they standardized a Roman curial inheritance on a wide scale. Pius V's 1568 Breviary reform and 1570 Missal placed a revised general calendar within books intended for broad Latin-Church use, while preserving uses that met the conditions stated in the promulgating documents. Later popes added saints and feasts, revised ranks, and adjusted texts and rubrics. The general calendar therefore continued to accumulate material rather than remaining frozen in 1570.

By the beginning of the twentieth century, the frequency and rank of saints' days could displace much of the older ferial psalter and seasonal cycle. Pius X's reform, promulgated in \emph{Divino afflatu} (1911) and implemented through new rubrics, reordered the psalter and precedence so that ferial and seasonal offices could recur. It was a structural recalibration, not merely the insertion or deletion of isolated names.

\subsection{The immediate construction of the 1962 form}

Pius XII's mid-century reforms supplied the next decisive layer. The restored Holy Week order changed the center of the Paschal cycle; the general decree \emph{Cum hac nostra aetate} and the rubrics effective in 1956 suppressed many vigils and octaves, simplified ranks, and reduced commemorations. These measures left the calendar visibly leaner before the 1960 codification.

John XXIII then promulgated the \emph{Codex rubricarum} by \emph{Rubricarum instructum} on 25 July 1960. The new code replaced the older hierarchy of doubles and simples with four classes, supplied integrated rules for the calendar, and was to govern the Roman Breviary and Missal from 1 January 1961. The 1962 typical Missal incorporated that system and its revised general calendar. Thus ``the 1962 calendar'' is best understood as the printed calendar of the 1962 typical edition operating inside the 1960 rubrical settlement, rather than as an independent reform issued in isolation in 1962.

Particular calendars were also to be brought into conformity with the new code. The Sacred Congregation of Rites' 1961 instruction on revising particular calendars regulated which local celebrations could remain and how their rank was to be assigned. That process explains why a complete practical calendar cannot be reconstructed by copying the general calendar alone.
