# Historical Source Matrix — Sources, Persons, and Forums

| Period or witness | Exact locus | Institutional or intellectual problem | Contribution to orders, persons, or forums | Later reception and caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| c. 441 BC, Sophocles | *Antigone* 450–460 | Can mortal decree conflict with an unwritten divine demand? | Dramatizes the cost of acting under incompatible asserted commands | Tragedy and character speech, not a systematic natural-law doctrine |
| 4th c. BC, Plato | *Gorgias* 482e–484c; *Laws* I, X | Does nature justify domination, or does rational order measure law? | Exposes equivocal appeals to nature and develops a rationally ordered polity | Dialogue speakers and argumentative sequence control |
| 4th c. BC, Aristotle | *Nicomachean Ethics* V.7; *Rhetoric* I.13; *Politics* III.16 | How do natural and legal justice, general rules, and concrete cases relate? | Distinguishes natural/legal justice and explains reasoned correction where a general formulation fails an unforeseen case | Not the completed scholastic taxonomy |
| 3rd–1st c. BC, Stoicism and Cicero | Diogenes Laertius VII.87–89; Cleanthes; Digest 1.3.2; Cicero, *De legibus* I–II and *De re publica* III.33 | Can a common rational law measure civic enactment across peoples? | Joins reason, nature, divine governance, and human community | Fragmentary Stoic transmission; Cicero supplies no modern judicial-review mechanism |
| 2nd c. BC–3rd c. AD, Roman jurists | Gaius 1.1–7; Digest 1.1, 1.3, 1.5, 1.8, 44.7, 50.17 | How are status, source, jurisdiction, action, and remedy classified? | Develops civil, gentium, natural, praetorian, written, and unwritten law with concrete effects | Heterogeneous jurists; *ius gentium* is not modern international law |
| 1st c., Scripture | Rom 2; Matt 22; Rom 13; Acts 5 | How do creation, civil authority, revelation, and God's prior claim reach the same person? | Supplies natural moral knowledge, real civil obedience, and a limit where obedience conflicts with God | The texts are not a modern separation constitution |
| c. 49, Acts 15 | Acts 15; Pontifical Biblical Commission, *Bible and Morality* 151–153 | How does the Church resolve a disputed obligation among peoples with different inherited practices? | Shows assembly, testimony, scriptural discernment, competent decision, letter, messengers, reception, and differentiated obligations | Scriptural and ecclesial event, not a template making every later council identical |
| 1st–3rd c., early communities | *Didache* 1–5, 15; *Diognetus* 5; Tertullian, *Apology* 33–34 | How does the Church govern before imperial recognition? | Worship, ministry, discipline, reconciliation, charity, and civil loyalty operate through ecclesial and customary forms | Sources differ in genre and locality; no complete code or rights theory |
| 4th–5th c., Christian empire | Councils, imperial constitutions, Augustine | What follows when civil law protects, regulates, and sometimes coerces ecclesial life? | Civil and ecclesiastical consequences increasingly overlap; Augustine relates temporal peace to eternal justice and later supports coercive correction | Cooperation, privilege, jurisdictional conflict, and coercion must all remain visible |
| 494, Gelasius I | *Famulae pietatis*, Gratian D.96 c.10; compare c.6 | How are episcopal and royal offices distinguished amid an imperial ecclesial dispute? | Assigns differentiated and asymmetric responsibility in divine and temporal matters | Acacian-schism setting and Gratian's later reception both matter; not modern privatization |
| 533–535, Justinian | Digest and Institutes; Novel 6 | Can imperial compilation coordinate civil jurisprudence and ecclesiastical policy? | Preserves Roman juristic plurality and presents priesthood and empire as gifts requiring concord | Novel 6 is imperial law; juxtaposition in the compilation is not conceptual unanimity |
| 7th–10th c., Eastern development | Trullo; nomocanonical collections; later summary in *Sacri canones* | How do conciliar canons, patristic sources, imperial law, and local tradition interact? | Develops a distinct Greek and Eastern canonical trajectory and patterns of civil/ecclesiastical coordination | *Sacri canones* is an official reception account, not the sole neutral proof; Wagschal supplies historical control |
| 9th–11th c., Western collections and reform | Regional collections, pseudo-Isidorian material, reform-era collections and controversies | Which authority controls office, appointment, property, and discipline? | Accumulation and conflict of sources create demand for authenticated collections and reconciliation | Collection history and disputed authenticity prevent a linear institutional story |
| late 11th–12th c., schools and Roman recovery | Justinianic study at Bologna; papal adjudication; reform disputes | How can proliferating civil and ecclesiastical texts be taught and applied coherently? | Juristic methods, schools, litigation, and papal governance prepare systematic canon law | Winroth's chronology and institutional account control against a single-author origin myth |
| c. 1140, Gratian and decretists | *Decretum* D.1, D.4; Rufinus, *Summa* 4–10 | How can discordant scriptural, patristic, conciliar, papal, customary, and Roman-derived authorities be reconciled? | Develops distinctions, cases, dicta, and interpretive concord; decretists refine natural, divine, gentium, and positive categories | Compilation and school texts, not current law; recensions and attributions remain material |
| 1234 and after, *Liber Extra* | Gregory IX's compilation; decretalist commentary | How are later papal decretals ordered for courts, teaching, and governance? | Organizes decretals into a promulgated collection and supports a professional jurisprudence of jurisdiction and remedy | Case origins, later compilation, and glosses must be distinguished |
| 12th–15th c., *ius commune*, *iura propria*, and courts | Roman and canon law with their juristic literature; Romano-canonical procedure; local, corporate, feudal, royal, and customary norms | How does a person litigate amid overlapping sources and forums? | Courts apply the learned *ius commune* in interaction with particular sources; pleading, proof, representation, appeal, status, action, and remedy make the plurality operational | “Legal pluralism” is a modern analytical category; do not project fully separate modern systems backward |
| 12th–15th c., penitential and contentious fora | Goering and Müller on internal-forum historiography | How are sin, sacramental discipline, public status, and contentious judgment distinguished? | Development of multiple fora explains why related conduct can have different effects | Date and meaning of the two-fora vocabulary are disputed; current canonical categories cannot be read backward unchanged |
| 1265–1274, Aquinas | ST I–II qq. 90–108; II–II q. 120 | How do reason, authority, common good, revelation, and exceptional application cohere? | Coordinates eternal, natural, human, and revealed law; conclusion/determination; injustice; custom; dispensation; equity | Theological synthesis, not legislation |
| 16th c., Salamanca and Valladolid | Vitoria, *De Indis*, q. 1, pp. 239–251 and conclusion at 250 in Pagden–Lawrance; Valladolid sources | Do conquest, evangelization, and cultural difference defeat Indigenous status, property, and political authority? | Uses natural law and *ius gentium* to criticize titles and recognize rights while debating mission, war, and jurisdiction | Critical resources coexist with contested anthropology and imperial assumptions |
| 1545–1612, Trent and Suárez | Trent Sessions VII and XXIV; Suárez, *De legibus* | How do ecclesiastical competence, mutable discipline, lawgiver, promulgation, and custom relate? | Trent distinguishes received competence from disciplinary determination; Suárez provides an early-modern synthesis | Conciliar doctrine and discipline remain distinct; Suárez is not reducible to Aquinas or later command theory |
| 16th–17th c., Reformation and confessionalization | Religious Peace of Augsburg (1555); *Instrumentum Pacis Osnabrugensis* art. V (1648); territorial church orders; Schilling as scholarly control | How are jurisdiction and religious status allocated after Western ecclesial division? | Territorial rule, pacification, privilege, migration, minority protection, and later toleration reorganize civil/ecclesiastical relations | Augsburg did not create a general individual right; Westphalia did not invent modern separation or the nation-state |
| 18th–19th c., state consolidation and codification | National civil codes and administrative institutions; Stein, *Roman Law in European History*, chs. 6–7 | How can law be unified, public, accessible, and territorially administered? | Reduces reliance on parts of the learned plurality and makes legislation and state institutions central sources | Codification can be natural-law inspired and is not identical to positivism |
| 19th–20th c., legal positivisms | Austin, Kelsen, Hart | Can legal validity be identified from command, norm-validity, or social rules apart from moral merit? | Separates source-validity from justice in several nonidentical ways | No theory is reduced to approval of tyranny; Hart expressly resists that historical inference |
| 1864–1888, Pius IX and Leo XIII | *Quanta cura*/*Syllabus*; *Immortale Dei* 13–14; *Libertas* 6–10 | How should Church independence and moral limits on civil law be defended amid liberal state consolidation? | Rejects indifferentism and state moral autonomy; articulates distinct powers and concord | Source contexts and confessional horizon must be stated |
| 1917, first Latin code | *Providentissima Mater*; 1917 CIC c. 6, 6° | Can Latin discipline be systematized without claiming to author divine law? | Codifies mutable law while preserving unincorporated natural and divine-positive norms | Superseded law; historical evidence for the distinction |
| 1929, Lateran settlement | Lateran Treaty arts. 2–4 | How is Holy See independence secured after loss of the Papal States? | Recognizes Holy See sovereignty and creates Vatican City as a territorial instrument | Treaty and state law, not universal canon law |
| 1937–1946, totalitarian law | *Mit brennender Sorge* 29–31; *Summi Pontificatus*; Radbruch (1946) | What moral and juridical limits remain when race, nation, or state claims total authority? | Reasserts universal moral law, personal and family rights, and the problem of extreme statutory injustice | Positivism did not simply cause totalitarianism; papal teaching and Radbruch use different genres and theories |
| 1948–1963, postwar rights | UDHR; *Pacem in terris* 9–38, 46–79 | How are inherent dignity, reciprocal duties, positive recognition, and public remedies related? | Moves the person bearing rights and duties toward the center of international and Catholic public-law analysis | UDHR is a declaration, not a treaty; positive remedies remain jurisdiction-specific |
| 1953–1965, religious freedom | *Ci riesce*; *Dignitatis humanae* 1–7, 10–13; *Gaudium et spes* 36, 74–76 | How do objective truth, toleration, civil immunity, temporal autonomy, and cooperation relate? | Moves from prudential non-repression to a dignity-grounded civil immunity while preserving duties to truth and just public order | Development is genuine; DH does not require disestablishment or erase earlier coercive history |
| 1983 and 1990 | CIC and CCEO | How are distinct Latin and Eastern traditions codified after Vatican II? | Current codes state subjects, rights, sources, divine-law limits, civil reception, authority, procedure, and remedies | Must be read with later law; neither code exhausts the legal order |

## Interpretation controls

1. Historical sequence is causal and institutional, not a list of authorities who allegedly held one timeless vocabulary.
2. Persons, jurisdiction, proof, and remedy are traced alongside philosophical categories.
3. Eastern and Latin trajectories are related without treating the East as an appendix to Latin codification.
4. Premodern plurality is described without projecting modern federal or conflicts systems backward.
5. Codification, positivism, totalitarianism, and postwar rights are distinguished rather than turned into one causal chain.
6. Historical sources never establish current law without a current reception or promulgated source.
