\section{A Survey of the Relevant Fields}

A study of law's sources can become imprecise by asking one discipline to answer another's question. Philosophy can test whether an enactment is just without establishing whether a tribunal has jurisdiction. A code can establish legal effects without proving the moral adequacy of its rule. History can explain how a category emerged without making an obsolete norm binding today. The table surveys the fields that bear materially on the problem; later sections engage the central philosophical, theological, historical, canonical, civil, and conflict-of-orders perspectives in greater depth. Comparative law and legal sociology remain methodological controls rather than independent surveys.

\subsection{Three kinds of priority}

The word \emph{hierarchy} can conceal three different relations:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Moral priority.} Natural and revealed moral norms measure human action and the justice of every human enactment. This priority concerns conscience and the limits of legitimate authority.
\item \textbf{Normative rank within an order.} A constitution, code, universal law, particular law, administrative act, contract, and judgment can occupy different ranks under that order's own rules. Later, special, or higher norms can prevail only as the governing system provides.
\item \textbf{Allocation among orders.} Civil and ecclesiastical jurisdictions can govern different aspects of the same event. Neither is simply an appellate court for the other. Treaty, reception, recognition, or conflict rules may coordinate them; otherwise each forum determines the effects within its competence.
\end{enumerate}

These relations must not be collapsed. A norm can be morally defective yet remain civilly operative until a competent remedy succeeds. A canonically invalid alienation can be civilly effective. A civil divorce can alter civil status while leaving the canonical bond question unresolved. Conversely, civil law can impose duties that canon law does not reproduce, and Catholics can be morally obliged to obey them because just civil authority is real.

\subsection{The common analytical path}

The fields meet in a disciplined sequence: establish the facts; identify every claimed norm and forum; determine applicability and positive legal effect; assess moral obligation under the applicable natural and revealed norms; then identify the action and remedy appropriate to each order. The sequence is not mechanical. Facts can be contested, natural-law conclusions can require difficult reasoning, and authorities can disagree. Its value is to show where the disagreement lies and what evidence could resolve it.

The rest of the study follows that path. The historical chapters explain why the vocabulary is layered. The doctrinal chapters state the Catholic synthesis. The current-law chapters identify positive sources and authorities. The responsibility chapter and dossiers return the material to the person who must decide and act.
