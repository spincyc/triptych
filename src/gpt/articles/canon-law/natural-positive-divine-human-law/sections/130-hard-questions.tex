\section{Objections and Limiting Cases}

\subsection{Does legal validity imply moral authority?}

Legal validity and moral obligation answer different questions. A source rule can identify an enactment that officials must apply even when the enactment is unjust. That fact explains its external effects; it does not make official will the source of justice. Conversely, a correct moral objection does not automatically eliminate jurisdiction, liability, or the need for a legal remedy.

Positive law is therefore broader than legal positivism. Every developed legal order has enacted or otherwise posited norms. Legal positivism is a family of theories about law's social sources and its relation to morality. Catholic natural-law criticism is strongest when it addresses the particular theory and shows why social pedigree cannot by itself supply moral obligation.

\subsection{Can natural law develop or change?}

Basic moral goods and first principles do not change through enactment. Human knowledge of them, factual premises, derived conclusions, positive specifications, and institutional applications can develop. A rule historically described as natural may combine a sound principle with a false account of a class of persons or with a contingent custom. Revision then corrects the inference; it does not make human will the author of the underlying good.

This distinction permits historical accountability. Appeals to nature helped criticize conquest, slavery, and state absolutism, but contested anthropology also narrowed who counted as a bearer of the announced rights. The evidence must show both the principle and the path from that principle to the proposed rule.

\subsection{Can divine positive law change?}

Human authority cannot repeal or dispense a divine norm. Salvation history nevertheless contains distinct covenantal economies, and Christ fulfills the Old Law. Its ceremonial and judicial precepts cease in their former juridical form without a creature exercising legislative superiority over God; its moral precepts retain the natural and revealed content that the New Law fulfills. Analysis must specify the norm, addressee, covenantal setting, and mode of fulfillment.

\subsection{Can the Pope dispense divine law?}

Papal power can teach, authentically declare, protect, and apply divine law. It can dispense merely ecclesiastical law within the conditions established by law. It can also judge facts and juridical conditions on which a divine-law consequence depends. These are distinct acts. A declaration that a valid bond never arose does not dissolve that bond; a dispensation from a human form requirement does not dispense the nature of marriage.

The same precision applies beyond marriage. Supreme ecclesiastical power is supreme among human authorities in the Church; it remains a received ministry within the Church's divine constitution and end.

\subsection{May a person disobey an unjust command?}

Some commands must be refused because the chosen act would be intrinsically wrongful or otherwise gravely impermissible. Other defective rules can be obeyed temporarily to avoid disproportionate disorder while interpretation, exemption, litigation, recourse, or reform is pursued. The decision depends on the exact act, certainty and gravity of the moral judgment, the person's cooperation, available alternatives, the rights of others, and the positive consequences of refusal.

Conscience does not become an appellate tribunal. It determines the person's moral choice after responsible formation. The external forum may still impose effects, and the person may have duties of notice, care, restitution, or orderly transition. Where the positive order offers a remedy, using it can itself be an obligation of justice rather than a compromise of principle.

\subsection{Does religious freedom protect error?}

The civil right protects persons and communities from coercion within due limits. Its subject is the person, not the truth-value of a proposition. The right neither declares every belief true nor removes the moral duty to seek truth. Positive law gives the immunity enforceable form and can regulate its exercise for the rights of others and just public order.

Special civil recognition of one religious community is not categorically excluded by \emph{Dignitatis humanae}; effective freedom and equality for all must still be secured. A state may neither make theological truth depend on majority vote nor use asserted neutrality to exclude religious persons and communities from public freedom.

\subsection{Is everything in a code human and mutable?}

The CIC and CCEO are human legislative instruments, but their propositions do not all have the same source. A canon may declare a divine institution or norm, enact a mutable discipline, receive civil law, preserve an agreement, recognize custom, create a presumption, or allocate a forum. The proposition must be isolated before mutability, dispensability, or effect can be determined.

The reverse inference also fails. Theological importance does not make every implementing rule divine. A human rule can protect an unchangeable truth while remaining reformable in its procedures, terminology, presumptions, or jurisdictional arrangements.

\subsection{What error most often distorts the whole analysis?}

The central error is an unsupported move among source, competence, and effect. A natural good does not authorize every official to pursue it by every means. An ecclesiastical norm is not dispensable by every superior. A civil permission is not moral approval. A moral prohibition does not automatically create civil invalidity, canonical nullity, or a penalty.

The remedy is equally concise: identify the proposition; establish the facts and subjects; classify its source and rank; determine the competent authority and forum; separate moral, validative, disciplinary, penal, and remedial effects; then state the action required of the person.
