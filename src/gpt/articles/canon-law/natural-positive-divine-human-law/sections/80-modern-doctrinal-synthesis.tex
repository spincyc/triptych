\section{Contemporary Catholic Synthesis}

\subsection{An ordered plurality of law}

The \emph{Catechism of the Catholic Church} 1950--1986 coordinates eternal law, natural moral law, the revealed Old and New Law, and civil and ecclesiastical law (\sourceurl{https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_one/chapter_three/article_1/the_moral_law.html}{CCC 1950--1986}). The categories are related but perform different work. Eternal law names divine wisdom as the ultimate order; natural law names rational participation in that order; the Old and New Law name revealed economies; human laws give determinate juridical form to common life.

Natural law is universal and stable in its first principles, yet knowledge and application can be impaired by error, passion, social conditions, and sin (CCC 1954--1960). Revelation supplies light and grace; it does not make human nature unintelligible to reason. Civil and ecclesiastical legislation remains necessary because moral principles alone do not select every office, procedure, calendar, remedy, or institutional arrangement.

\subsection{One person bearing reciprocal rights and duties}

\emph{Pacem in terris} 28--34 treats natural rights and duties as correlative. A person's right entails duties in others to recognize and respect it; the right-holder also bears duties to exercise freedom responsibly and to contribute to the common good. Public authority must coordinate and protect these relations rather than treat the person only as the object of administration (\sourceurl{https://www.vatican.va/content/john-xxiii/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_j-xxiii_enc_11041963_pacem.html}{text}).

This reciprocity prevents an individual obligation account from becoming individualistic. Status, office, family, property, contract, and citizenship create relationships in which another person can hold a corresponding claim. A remedy protects a right; it can also specify who owes performance, what proof is required, and how competing claims are ordered.

\subsection{Freedom, embodiment, and the moral object}

John Paul II's \emph{Veritatis splendor} 35--53 rejects accounts that separate freedom from truth or reason from embodied human nature. Natural law is rational participation by the human person, not a biological regularity imposed without reason (\sourceurl{https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_jp-ii_enc_06081993_veritatis-splendor.html}{text}). Universality and concrete judgment therefore belong together.

The encyclical's treatment of the moral object, intention, and circumstances at nn.~71--83 also limits legal analysis. A good institutional aim cannot make an intrinsically wrongful chosen act good. At the same time, a general moral norm does not settle every question of attribution, cooperation, evidence, remedy, or positive validity. Those questions require their own facts and competent methods.

\subsection{Public moral reasoning}

The International Theological Commission's 2009 \emph{In Search of a Universal Ethic} surveys wisdom traditions, Greco-Roman thought, Scripture, Christian development, moral experience, first principles, historicity, political society, and Christological fulfillment (\sourceurl{https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/cti_documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_20090520_legge-naturale_en.html}{text}). It is an expert commission study rather than an act of the Magisterium. Its methodological contribution is the proposal that natural-law argument should articulate human goods and practical reasons accessible to common inquiry.

Such accessibility does not require citizens to conceal religious convictions. It requires an argument proportioned to the public authority claimed and the rights affected. Dignity, solidarity, subsidiarity, common good, and protection of the vulnerable guide judgment without functioning as a ready-made municipal code. Several positive specifications can be morally reasonable; empirical claims and distributive effects must therefore be tested rather than assumed.

\subsection{Teaching, legislation, administration, and judgment}

Ecclesiastical functions must be classified before their effects are asserted. The Magisterium teaches faith and morals. Legislators enact general norms. Executive authorities issue general or singular administrative acts within law. Tribunals decide controversies. One papal act can contain more than one function, but document genre, operative words, competence, approval, promulgation, subjects, and effective date remain decisive.

\begin{fourstable}{Act}{What it can establish}{What must still be shown}{Typical error}
Doctrinal teaching & An authoritative proposal or judgment of a proposition in faith or morals. & The level of authority, proposition taught, and required response. & Treating every sentence as a penal or procedural rule.\\
Legislation & A general juridical norm for defined subjects. & Competence, promulgation, scope, effective date, and amendments. & Inferring legal force from theological importance alone.\\
Administrative act & An implementation, decision, command, favor, or dispensation in the assigned sphere. & Legal basis, competence, facts, notification, reasons, and recourse. & Treating a singular act as universal legislation.\\
Judgment & A binding resolution of a case within jurisdiction. & Parties, issue decided, proof, procedural posture, and avenues of challenge. & Treating the result as binding every person and case.\\
\end{fourstable}

CIC c.~747 \S2 states the Church's competence to announce moral principles concerning the social order and to judge human affairs when fundamental rights or the salvation of souls require it; cc.~749--754 distinguish forms of teaching and response (\sourceurl{https://www.vatican.va/archive/cod-iuris-canonici/eng/documents/cic_lib3-cann747-755_en.html}{CIC 747--755}). These canons neither reduce moral teaching to legislation nor make every prudential statement a universal command.

\subsection{Natural ground and positive recognition}

A right can be natural in ground and positive in legal form. The state does not create human dignity, but constitutional and statutory recognition supplies jurisdiction, standing, remedies, procedures, and duties for institutions. Recognition is therefore neither the source of the underlying worth nor a redundant repetition of moral truth.

\emph{Dignitatis humanae} uses this structure. It grounds religious immunity in the nature and dignity of the person and calls for recognition of the right in constitutional law. Positive recognition can make the right legally cognizable and supply specified protections or remedies, as the domestic order provides. Its regulation must remain directed to the rights of others and just public order; majority discomfort or religious error alone does not satisfy that measure.

\subsection{Conscience, error, and external effect}

Conscience is practical judgment about the moral quality of a concrete act. It must be formed and followed when certain; culpable and inculpable error require separate moral analysis (CCC 1776--1802; \sourceurl{https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_one/chapter_one/article_6/moral_conscience.html}{text}). Conscience applies moral truth to action. It does not enact civil or canonical law, dispense a legal obligation, determine disputed facts for every forum, or guarantee immunity from external consequences.

A person facing conflict must therefore ask more than whether conscience is invoked. The inquiry identifies the act to be performed, its object and intended end, the person's causal and institutional role, alternatives, duress, effects on third parties, available exemption or review, and the consequences of action or refusal. A canonical internal-forum act is itself a juridically defined ecclesial act; it is not another name for every private judgment of conscience.

\subsection{The range of legitimate dispute}

Catholic thinkers continue to dispute the best philosophical account of natural law, the relation of basic goods to metaphysics, the status of the \emph{ius gentium}, the criteria of legal validity, and many prudential applications. Different approaches overlap without becoming interchangeable, and this survey does not adjudicate among them.

\begin{threestable}{Point}{Established for this study}{Question left open}
Objective moral measure & Human enactment does not create every reason for action or every basic right. & Which philosophical account best explains practical reason, basic goods, and metaphysical participation?\\
Positive specification & Competent authority can choose among morally permissible determinations and give them juridical effects. & Which specification best serves the common good under disputed empirical conditions?\\
Validity and morality & Source-validity and moral obligation require distinct conclusions. & Which conceptual relation between law and morality best explains radically unjust enactments?\\
Rights and community & Natural dignity, reciprocal duties, positive recognition, and remedies must be held together. & How should particular rights be specified and balanced in a concrete constitutional order?\\
\end{threestable}

The taxonomy identifies the question each field must answer and the evidence appropriate to it. Philosophy and moral theology assess reasons and acts; history reconstructs development; legislation supplies authoritative form; procedure establishes facts and remedies; comparative and social inquiry test assumptions about how institutions operate.
