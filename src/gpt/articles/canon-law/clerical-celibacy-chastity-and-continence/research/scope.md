## Governing thesis

Catholic discipline is coherent but not uniform. Chastity binds all according to state of life. The Latin Church ordinarily selects celibate presbyters, admits defined married-priest exceptions, and admits married men to the permanent diaconate. Eastern common law honors both celibate and married clergy and delegates admission to particular or Apostolic law. Bishops are selected from men not bound by marriage, and neither code ordinarily permits marriage after sacred orders. The history securely contains married ministers, explicit fourth-century Western continence law, a durable postordination-marriage ban, and divergent Eastern and Western settlements. A uniform apostolic oral law of perpetual clerical continence remains a serious but disputed historical thesis.

## Legal scope block

- Governing bodies of law: 1983 Codex Iuris Canonici for the Latin Church; 1990 Codex Canonum Ecclesiarum Orientalium for the twenty-three Eastern Catholic Churches sui iuris.
- Controlling language: official Latin. Holy See English CIC web text and the CLSA 2001 Latin–English CCEO edition assist readers.
- Persons: Catholic candidates for and recipients of sacred orders, affected spouses and families, and clerics who are celibate, married, separated, widowed, dismissed, or seeking a dispensation.
- Jurisdiction: universal/common law plus identified Apostolic, particular, proper, and local law. No Latin canon is automatically applied to an Eastern Catholic.
- Currentness: relevant common-law pages, current Latin Book VI, 2014 Eastern married-clergy decree, universal permanent-diaconate norms, and identified current Church-specific pages checked through the cutoff.
- Material amendments: Latin penal discussion uses Book VI as revised by Pascite gregem Dei, effective 8 December 2021. No later universal amendment to CIC cann. 266, 277, 288, 290–291, 1031, 1037, 1042, 1050, 1087 or CCEO cann. 180, 373–375, 758–760, 769, and 804 was located.
- Particular law: a universal capacity is not described as current implementation. Unknown public particular law remains unknown.

## Included scope

1. Exact distinction among chastity, continence, perfect/perpetual continence, celibacy, clerical obligation, and religious vow.
2. New Testament evidence and its interpretive limits.
3. Second- through fifth-century ascetical, conciliar, papal, and patristic witnesses, including evidence against easy uniformity.
4. Elvira, Siricius, the African corpus, Paphnutius, Apostolic church orders, and Trullo with transmission and authority qualifications.
5. Medieval Western consolidation, Reformation and Trent, the 1917 Code, Eastern reunion and diaspora restrictions, Vatican II, the restored permanent diaconate, and modern codes.
6. Present Latin admission, continence/celibacy, marriage impediment, loss-of-state, and penal rules.
7. Present Eastern common law, the 2014 decree, territorial ministry, and particular-law boundary.
8. Permanent deacons: age, wife’s consent, marriage and family, canon 277 dispute, separation, widowhood, and exceptional remarriage petitions.
9. Appendix covering all twenty-four Catholic Churches sui iuris and separating presbyterate, stable diaconate, and married-stable-deacon evidence.
10. Safeguarding boundary: sexual abuse and coercion are not reduced to “failure of celibacy.”

## Method and source hierarchy

1. Official Latin code and AAS texts establish universal/common law.
2. Ecumenical councils, papal acts, and competent dicasterial acts establish authoritative teaching and discipline according to genre.
3. Synodal and eparchial sources establish only their actual jurisdiction, date, program, or person.
4. Patristic and conciliar editions are read by date, genre, place, textual transmission, and legal reach.
5. Professional scholarship supplies textual criticism, historiography, and social context; schools of interpretation are named.
6. Editorial synthesis states confidence levels and preserves disagreement.

## Search and negative-result record

- The current Holy See CIC/CCEO pages and relevant Vatican document indices were checked for the controlling text and later acts through the cutoff.
- The 2014 AAS decree was treated as the principal comparative Eastern baseline.
- Public Church-specific searches found active/restored stable-diaconate evidence for several Churches but no sufficiently current authoritative Church-wide implementation text for every Church. Unknown cells were retained.
- Public evidence did not support treating country-level Catholic statistics as Church-specific in mixed Latin/Eastern countries.
- The 2014 Syro-Malabar exception could not be treated as an absolute current ban on married stable deacons because a 2016 official eparchial witness documents one.
- No promulgated authentic interpretation of CIC canon 277 settling the married-permanent-deacon question was located. Two 2011 PCLT presidential clarifications were located and classified below authentic interpretation.
