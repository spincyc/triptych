\section{At Reason's Shore}

The recognition of present dependence did not disappear when medieval vocabulary ceased to govern philosophy. In the Third Meditation, Descartes argues that past existence does not by itself account for present existence. Preservation and creation differ only conceptually in the respect under discussion: a cause is required not only for the self's production but for its conservation now. His method and metaphysics differ sharply from Aquinas's, but the present tense of dependence survives.

The Catholic doctrinal boundary was stated with particular force by Vatican I. \emph{Dei Filius} confesses a God really and essentially distinct from the world, freely creating not to acquire or increase divine beatitude but to manifest perfection through goods bestowed upon creatures (ch.~1). When the constitution turns to faith, it says that the human person wholly depends upon God as Creator and Lord (ch.~3). The council defines the Creator--creature distinction and the freedom of creation; it does not canonize every Thomistic explanation used here.

Within Aquinas's own cumulative argument, the First Mover is not left as an unnamed source of motion. Subsequent questions treat the first principle as free of potency and composition, subsistent being, supreme good, intelligent, and willing (ST I, qq.~3, 6, 14, 19). Because the divine goodness is already complete, creatures are not willed as though God needed them in order to become perfect. They proceed from will, not from a necessity imposed by deficiency (q.~19, aa.~3--4; q.~44, a.~4).

Aquinas can therefore speak of divine love without reducing it to a passion. To love is to will good to another. Human love is awakened by a goodness it encounters; God's will causes the being and goodness that it loves. God loves every existing thing, Aquinas argues, because every actuality and perfection exists through the divine will (q.~20, aa.~1--2). Natural theology can accordingly reach a true, analogical judgment: the uncreated source freely wills the good of creatures.

It cannot, by that judgment alone, penetrate the inner life of God. Gregory Nazianzen permits knowledge that God is from the order of effects while denying comprehension of what the divine nature is (\emph{Oration} 28.3--6). Pseudo-Dionysius likewise lets causal affirmation pass through negation and eminence: the source is not contained within the creaturely mode of any predicate (\emph{Divine Names} V; \emph{Mystical Theology} I). Aquinas expressly denies that the Trinity can be demonstrated by natural reason (ST I, q.~32, a.~1).

Here the distinction between discovery and revelation becomes decisive. Metaphysics can discover that the first source lacks nothing, gives being freely, and wills the good of what exists. Revelation alone discloses the Trinitarian depth of that giving. The exact confession occurs in the First Letter of John: ``God is love'' (1 John 4:8, 16). Its context defines the words by the sending of the Son for the life of sinners and by the charity to which the beloved are called (4:7--21). The phrase is not the inversion \emph{love is God}; God's self-disclosure governs what love means.

John also says that perfect love casts out fear (4:18). This does not abolish wonder, creaturely reverence, or Augustine's simultaneous trembling and desire. It casts out servility---the stance that relates to God chiefly under fear of punishment. Aquinas distinguishes that servility, which charity removes, from fear of punishment itself, which may remain but diminishes as charity grows; filial fear---the fear of offending or being separated from the beloved---grows with charity (ST II--II, q.~19, a.~10).

The \emph{Catechism} gathers the movement. The God who creates from wisdom and love acts freely, not from need (CCC 293--295); the God whose ``very being is love'' eternally communicates the life of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit and destines creatures to share in it (CCC 221, 257). Love does not cancel ontological asymmetry. It reveals why asymmetry need not be interpreted as rivalry.
