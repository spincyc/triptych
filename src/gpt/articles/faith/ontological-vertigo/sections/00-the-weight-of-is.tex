\section{When a Fact Changes Its Weight}

A fact can be known for years and then change its weight. You may have long known, in some loose sense, that you did not cause your own birth. Birth, however, can be held safely in the past. The harder question concerns this moment: how are you here to ask it at all? You think, choose, resist, and understand through powers that are genuinely yours; yet you did not first supply the existence in which those powers operate. You are unmistakably real and active. But are you the source of the act by which you exist?

You can explain a great deal about yourself. Parents and biology help to explain your body; teachers and experience help to explain what you know; reasons, habits, and circumstances help to explain what you choose. Beneath all of those answers, however, lies a question they presuppose: why is there anyone here with a body, powers, and choices to explain? It is easy to treat existence as already secured and then ask only what has happened within it. The deeper question asks how there is anyone here at all.

Why should that question matter? Because dependence can sound like subtraction. If your being is received, are you less real? If God causes your existence and action, is there less room for you to exist and act? Must the recognition that you are not self-grounded end in abasement or recoil? Or have we mistaken the condition of creaturely reality for a threat to it?

The word \emph{vertigo} is metaphorical. Nothing must spin, no unusual experience is promised, and no feeling proves a metaphysical conclusion. The term names a possible interruption: a judgment kept at a safe distance becomes personal. The familiar self remains solidly real, yet can no longer imagine that it has ever stood outside the dependence under consideration.

The questions are personal, but they cannot be answered by intensity of feeling. They require distinctions inherited from a long argument: participation, act and potency, creation from nothing, conservation, received existence, primary and secondary causality, humility, and contemplative unknowing. Each protects the inquiry from a different distortion. The first task is to ask whether derivative actuality can finally account for itself.
