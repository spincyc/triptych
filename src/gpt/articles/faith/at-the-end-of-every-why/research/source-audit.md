# Source Audit — At the End of Every Why

The core online witnesses were checked through 2026-07-20; the added Augustine, *Homilies on First John* IX and Aquinas ST I--II, q. 109, a. 6 and II--II, qq. 19 and 29 loci were checked on 2026-07-21. Public English translations are working study witnesses unless otherwise stated. Consequential claims are governed by the identified work and exact locus, not by a claim that an online translation or re-typeset edition is critical.

## Core claim-to-source matrix

| Love of God and neighbor is commanded as the end toward which the moral law is ordered. | Deut 6:4–5; Matt 22:37–40; Aquinas, ST II–II, q. 44, aa. 1–5. | Scriptural command with Thomistic analysis. The article distinguishes a command binding the will from coercion manufacturing love. |
| The obligation of charity does not oppose freedom because the act of love can be fulfilled only voluntarily. | Aquinas, ST II–II, q. 44, a. 1, especially ad 2; I–II, q. 10, a. 4. | Governs the command/freedom distinction and noncoercive divine motion. |
| The New Covenant includes law written within and the gift of the Spirit enabling obedience. | Jer 31:31–34; Ezek 36:26–27; Rom 5:5. | Scriptural grammar for interior grace; not treated as private inspiration detached from revelation and sacrament. |
| External law teaches and accuses but does not by itself heal the will; the Spirit pours charity into the heart. | Augustine, *On the Spirit and the Letter* 32–36; *Confessions* X.29.40. | Patristic account of command and assisting grace. The brief petition is used as theological compression, not a denial of responsibility. |
| The New Law is chiefly the grace of the Holy Spirit and secondarily written instruction. | Aquinas, ST I–II, q. 106, a. 1; CCC 1965–1974, especially 1972. | Thomistic articulation and authoritative synthesis. Written Gospel and ecclesial instruction remain necessary. |
| Supernatural charity is infused rather than acquired by natural power; grace heals and elevates the will. | Aquinas, ST I–II, q. 109, aa. 3–4; II–II, q. 24, a. 2; CCC 1996–2002. | Governs the distinction between natural love, grace, and the infused theological virtue. |
| The free turning toward grace remains genuinely voluntary, yet even its preparation requires God's gratuitous inward motion. | Aquinas, ST I–II, q. 10, a. 4; q. 109, a. 6. | Governs the dependent yet genuinely creaturely answer. Universal divine motion and supernatural grace are not collapsed into one argument. |
| No antecedent merit of the recipient earns initial justification or the first sanctifying grace; Christ's Passion merits justification, and merit within grace is not denied. | CCC 1992 and 2006–2011, especially 2010. | Controls the terse final statement about first grace. |
| Charity is friendship with God, founded on God's communication of beatitude; it loves God principally and the neighbor in relation to God, and remains a created participation. | John 15:9–17; Aquinas, ST II–II, q. 23, aa. 1–3 and 5; CCC 1812–1813, 1822–1829. | Thomistic friendship grammar under scriptural and catechetical boundaries. It implies neither equality nor absorption. |
| Christ fulfills and interiorizes the moral law; the liberty of children and friends produces a fuller obedience that benefits the creature rather than God. | Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* IV.13.1–4; IV.14.1. | Early patristic witness joining liberty, command, friendship, divine non-need, and creaturely benefit. |
| Fear of punishment recedes as charity grows and is cast out by perfect charity, while chaste fear remains watchful for communion with the beloved. | Augustine, *Homilies on First John* IX.4–6. | Lived patristic texture for transformed fear. “Departure” concerns communion, not cessation of ontological conservation. |
| Charity casts out servility; fear of punishment may remain but diminishes as charity grows; filial fear increases. | Charity gives the other virtues their order to the supernatural final end without erasing their proper objects. | Aquinas, ST II–II, q. 23, a. 8; CCC 1827, 2095. | Governs the claim that charity gives justice and religion their final form. |
| Justice causes peace indirectly by removing obstacles; charity causes it directly by unifying appetite and communion in one good. | Aquinas, ST II–II, q. 29, a. 3. | Governs formal peace without promising constant felt serenity; the wayfarer's peace remains imperfect. |
| Love fulfills rather than violates the law and is tested in the true good of the visible neighbor. | Rom 13:8–10; 1 John 4:20–21; Augustine, *Homilies on First John* VII.8–9. | Scriptural and patristic ethical boundary. Augustine's short maxim does not authorize impulse or evil means. |
| Divine love is manifested in the Father's initiative, the Son's mission, and the Son's free laying down of his life. | 1 John 4:7–14, 19; John 10:17–18; 15:9–17. | Governs the Paschal content of charity and excludes sentimentality or autonomous self-definition. |
| Love moves the creature beyond self-enclosure and returns toward the Good as source and end. | Pseudo-Dionysius, *Divine Names* IV.13–14. | Mystical-theological grammar used without necessary emanation, emotionalism, or annihilation. |
| Created love can answer wholly though not equally, continually receiving from its divine source. | Bernard of Clairvaux, *Sermons on the Song of Songs* 83.3–6. | Governs the fountain, return, and *etsi non ex aequo* formulations. |
| God not only teaches but enables the graced soul to love in the Holy Spirit; John can speak of equality or conformity in love while union remains participation rather than identity of essence. | John of the Cross, *Spiritual Canticle* B, stanza 38.2–6 and 39.4–6, second redaction; 2 Pet 1:4. | Carmelite mystical witness under an explicit participation boundary: equality concerns received love, not essence, source, or causal priority. |
| The temporal missions manifest divine initiative and communicate grace without need or change in God. | Rom 8:14–17; Gal 4:4–7; Aquinas, ST I, q. 43, aa. 1–3. | Trinitarian and soteriological boundary for Father, Son, Spirit, and adoption. |
| Love is an essential divine name and a personal name of the Holy Spirit as proceeding Love; created charity is neither divine essence nor Person. | Aquinas, ST I, q. 37, aa. 1–2; II–II, q. 23, a. 2. | Governs Trinitarian terminology and created participation. |
| The Johannine confession is Trinitarian, Paschal, and ethically tested. | 1 John 4:7–21, especially vv. 8–14, 16, 19–21; CCC 221, 257. | Revealed culmination, not a natural demonstration or inversion of divine and created love. |

## Scripture and authoritative Catholic witnesses

The rendered article uses Deut 6:4–5; Jer 31:31–34; Ezek 36:26–27; Matt 22:37–40; John 10:17–18 and 15:9–17; Rom 5:5, 8:14–17, and 13:8–10; Gal 4:4–7; 2 Pet 1:4; and 1 John 4:7–21.

The exact phrase “God is love” is located at 1 John 4:8 and 4:16, not in the Gospel of John. The controlling scriptural unit includes the Father's initiative, the Son's mission, the gift of the Spirit, prior divine love, the command to love, and the visible neighbor as ethical test.

Authoritative doctrinal loci:

- First Vatican Council, *Dei Filius*, ch. 1 and canon 1.5 (DS 3002, 3025): God creates freely to manifest perfection through goods bestowed, not to acquire or increase divine beatitude.
- *Catechism of the Catholic Church* 1812–1829: theological virtues, charity, friendship, commandment, form of the virtues, spiritual freedom, and love of neighbor.
- CCC 1965–1974, especially 1972: the New Law as law of love, grace, and freedom.
- CCC 1996–2005: grace as gratuitous participation in divine life, free response, and the limits of experiential assurance.
- CCC 2095–2097: charity informing religion, justice toward God, and adoration.

Official English witnesses: <https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_one/chapter_one/article_7/ii_the_theological_virtues.html>, <https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_one/chapter_three/article_1/iii_the_new_law_or_the_law_of_the_gospel.html>, and <https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_one/chapter_three/article_2/ii_grace.html>.

## Patristic witnesses

- St. Irenaeus of Lyons, *Against Heresies* IV.13.1–4 and IV.14.1: Christ fulfills and extends the natural precepts; liberty produces fuller obedience; servants become friends; friendship and service benefit the creature, not the God who needs nothing. Roberts–Rambaut ANF working English: <https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103413.htm> and <https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103414.htm>.
- St. Augustine, *On the Spirit and the Letter* 32–36: external precept, inward Spirit, and charity as fulfillment of the law; <https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1502.htm>.
- Augustine, *Confessions* X.29.40: the brief petition asking God to give what he commands. The article reproduces only the short traditional formula.
- Augustine, *Homilies on First John* VII.8–9: morally similar appearances distinguished by the root of charity; correction and mercy must each proceed from love; <https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/170207.htm>.
- Augustine, *Homilies on First John* IX.4–6: fear of punishment and chaste fear within love; <https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/170209.htm>. The article does not turn “departure” into loss of divine conservation.

## Thomistic source corpus

The governing Latin is the Leonine-text corpus exposed through Corpus Thomisticum: <https://www.corpusthomisticum.org/iopera.html>. The New Advent *Summa* index (<https://www.newadvent.org/summa/>) served as a working public English aid. Citations by work, question, article, and reply take precedence over web wording.

Particularly controlling distinctions:

- ST I–II, q. 10, a. 4: God moves the will according to its voluntary mode rather than by coercion.
- ST I–II, q. 106, a. 1: the New Law is chiefly grace of the Holy Spirit and secondarily written teaching.
- ST I–II, q. 109, aa. 3–4 and 6: healing grace, love of God under fallen nature, and gratuitous motion in the voluntary turning toward grace.
- ST II–II, q. 19, aa. 10–11: charity expels servility, fear of punishment diminishes, and filial fear increases; beatitude retains secure wonder and perfect subjection before infinite Creator–creature distance.
- ST II–II, q. 23, a. 1: charity as friendship founded on communication of beatitude.
- ST II–II, q. 23, a. 2: charity as something created in the soul, distinct from the Holy Spirit as Person.
- ST II–II, q. 23, a. 8: charity directs the other virtues to the final end and is their form in that sense.
- ST II–II, q. 29, a. 3: justice removes obstacles to peace indirectly, while charity directly unifies desire.
- ST II–II, q. 24, a. 2: charity is caused by infusion of the Holy Spirit rather than natural acquisition.
- ST II–II, q. 44, a. 1, especially ad 2: charity can be commanded as due without opposition to liberty.
- ST I, q. 37, aa. 1–2 and q. 43, aa. 1–3: Love as essential and personal name, and temporal missions in relation to eternal processions.

## Consequential negative results and rejected formulations

- The article does not say that love cannot be commanded. Scripture commands it, and Aquinas explains why. The narrower claim is that an external command cannot by itself infuse charity.
- Freedom is not defined as absence of obligation, indifference to the good, or autonomous self-creation.
- Grace is not represented as divine action replacing human willing. A graced act remains voluntary and attributable to the person.
- Ontological vertigo is not identified with servile fear, filial fear, or the Thomistic gift of fear. The comparison concerns transformation of the response, not identity of technical terms.
- The resolution does not make God less absolute or promise emotional calm. It removes alienation and the demand for independent ground while infinite asymmetry remains.
- “Interior law” is not a private revelation, feeling, or exemption from Scripture, sacrament, Church, and objective moral truth.
- Charity is not reduced to emotion, attraction, permissiveness, philanthropy, or benevolence without supernatural friendship with God.
- Friendship does not make God and creature equal, satisfy a need in God, or abolish lordship and worship.
- Charity as form does not erase justice or the proper objects of other virtues. No loving intention makes an intrinsically evil object good.
- Augustine's “Love, and do what you will” is not used as permission to follow impulse; its controlling context is the root of charity and the beloved's true good.
- Mystical return does not imply necessary emanation, absorption, identity of essence, or a reproducible extraordinary experience.
- The exact Johannine confession is not found in the Gospel, is not naturally demonstrated, and is not reversed into “love is God.”
