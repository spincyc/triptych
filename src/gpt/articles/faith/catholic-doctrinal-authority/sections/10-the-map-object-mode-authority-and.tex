\section{The map: object, mode, authority, and response}

No single ladder answers every question. Four axes must be kept together:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Object}: Is the proposition formally revealed, necessarily
connected with Revelation, authoritatively taught without definitive
proposal, disciplinary, prudential, historical, or theological?
\item \textbf{Mode}: Is it defined by pope or ecumenical council, taught
infallibly by the ordinary and universal Magisterium, proposed authentically
but non-definitively, legislated, judged administratively, or argued by a
theologian?
\item \textbf{Competence and intention}: Who speaks, about what, to whom, and
with what manifested intention? Office, genre, wording, repetition, and
context matter.
\item \textbf{Response}: Is divine and Catholic faith required, definitive
holding, religious submission of intellect and will, juridical obedience, a
prudent reception of counsel, or free but responsible theological judgment?
\end{enumerate}

The 1989 \emph{Professio fidei} and the Congregation for the Doctrine of the
Faith's 1998 doctrinal commentary give the clearest modern three-paragraph map:
truths to be believed as revealed; truths to be held definitively; and
teachings to which religious submission is owed. Canonical expression appears
in the Latin and Eastern codes. \emph{Ad tuendam fidem} inserted the definitive-
holding norm into CIC canon 750 \S2 and CCEO canon 598 \S2, with parallel
sanctioning provisions; the rest of either code must be read in its own
jurisdiction. The map describes different objects of assent. It is not a list
produced by counting how solemn a document sounds.
