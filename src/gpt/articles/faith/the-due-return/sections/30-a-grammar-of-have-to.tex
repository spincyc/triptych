\section{A Grammar of ``Have To''}

One phrase can conceal several kinds of necessity. ``I have to'' may report an unchosen fact, a moral judgment, a juridical command, or external compulsion. A clear account must separate them before reconnecting them.

\begin{studybox}{Five terms}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.45em,style=nextline,itemsep=0.25em,topsep=0.2em]
\item[Dependence] What is true whether or not I consent. A creature receives being; refusal cannot make it uncreated.
\item[Duty] What ought to be chosen. Moral obligation addresses a rational and voluntary power; it binds without physically compelling it.
\item[Precept] A duty, or a determinate mode of fulfilling one, commanded by competent authority for identified subjects and circumstances.
\item[Culpability] Personal responsibility for a concrete failure, judged with knowledge, freedom, intention, matter, and relevant circumstances distinguished.
\item[Counsel] A good proposed beyond universal precept as a freer means to perfection; it never licenses neglect of what justice already requires.
\end{description}
\end{studybox}

The taxonomy is this article's synthesis. Its components are traditional, but the sequence protects several boundaries at once.

Dependence is ontological. It is neither a statute nor a moral achievement. Duty is moral: the order of reason identifies an act as fitting and binds the will without physically determining it. A precept adds the act of a lawgiver. It may state a duty already knowable in substance, or determine how a general duty is to be observed in common. Culpability concerns the responsible agent in a concrete act; it cannot be read directly from the bare statement of a rule. A counsel proposes a genuine good without converting every omission into injustice (ST I--II, q.~108, a.~4; CCC 1973--1974).

The distinctions explain why several common inferences fail. ``I depend upon God'' does not by itself yield the current Latin canon specifying when Sunday Mass participation satisfies an ecclesiastical precept. ``The Church commands this act'' does not mean every person who fails externally is equally culpable. ``This act is counseled'' does not mean it is morally trivial. ``This act is owed'' does not mean it is coerced or incapable of love.

\subsection{Relief is not evasion}

Positive law also recognizes that a general command meets particular facts. Three further terms are needed.

An \emph{excuse} arises when relevant circumstances---for example, impossibility or a sufficiently serious reason under the applicable norm---mean that a person is not required to perform the act in the ordinary manner. A \emph{dispensation} is an act of competent authority relaxing a merely ecclesiastical law in a particular case. A \emph{commutation} replaces an obligation with another work where the law and competent authority permit it. These are not three names for private preference.

In the Sunday example, CIC c.~1248 \S2 addresses impossibility because of the absence of a sacred minister or another grave cause. The \emph{Catechism} names illness and care of infants as examples of serious reason (CCC 2181). CIC c.~1245 permits a pastor, for a just cause and under the diocesan bishop's prescriptions, to dispense in individual cases from observance of a feast day or commute it into another pious work; diocesan bishops retain their power under c.~87. The cause, authority, subject, and effect must not be blurred.

This grammar changes the sound of ``what you have to do now.'' The phrase no longer means a flat inventory of demands. It asks a sequence of questions: What relation is already true? What good is morally due? Has revelation or competent authority specified its form? To whom does the norm apply? What facts affect obligation? With what knowledge and freedom did the person act? Is the act commanded, counseled, excused, dispensed, or commuted?

The questions do not weaken obligation. They identify it.
