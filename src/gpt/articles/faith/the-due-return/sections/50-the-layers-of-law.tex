\section{The Layers of Law}

\subsection{Not four equal boxes}

Law is an ordinance of reason for the common good, made and promulgated by one who has care of the community (ST I--II, q.~90). Within Aquinas's architecture, eternal law, natural law, divine law, and human law do not name four independent legislatures occupying one plane.

\emph{Eternal law} is divine wisdom ordering acts and ends. \emph{Natural law} is the rational creature's participation in that order through practical reason. Its first precept is that good is to be done and pursued and evil avoided; further precepts follow the goods and relations of human nature (ST I--II, q.~94, a.~2). \emph{Divine positive law} is used here for precepts known because God has revealed and promulgated them within the economy of salvation. \emph{Human positive law}, civil or ecclesiastical, makes determinations for a community through competent authority.

The labels therefore answer different questions. Is the norm knowable through practical reason? Has God revealed or commanded it? Has human authority enacted a common determination? A single practice can stand under more than one description without the sources becoming interchangeable. Theological reasons do not by themselves create the juridical effect of a canon; a juridical command does not exhaust the moral or sacramental good it protects.

\subsection{From a general due to a common form}

Aquinas gives a terse account of the passage from natural due to positive determination. Natural reason dictates that the human person do something in reverence for God. That \emph{this} rather than \emph{that} determinate act be required can be established by divine or human law (ST II--II, q.~81, a.~2, ad 3). Sacrifice likewise belongs generically to natural law, while its concrete signs are determined by God or human authority (q.~85, a.~1, ad 1).

The same structure appears in the Lord's Day. The natural moral command requires outward, visible, public, and regular worship as acknowledgment of God's universal beneficence (CCC 2176). Revelation identifies Christ's Resurrection and the Eucharistic form of Christian worship. Apostolic tradition gives Sunday its Paschal identity. Ecclesiastical law states which faithful are bound, what satisfies the precept, and how impossibility and dispensation are treated.

Sunday is thus not a natural-law theorem. Neither is it an arbitrary weekly tax upon private time. It is the Paschal, apostolic, and ecclesially specified fulfillment of a natural moral due.

Positive determination is necessary because persons worship in a body, a household, and a Church. A purely private intention cannot by itself establish a common feast, assemble a parish, protect shared rest, or secure regular access to the Eucharistic celebration. Law gives stable public form to goods that isolated choice cannot coordinate. The precepts of the Church describe a necessary minimum in prayer, sacramental life, moral effort, and growth in love of God and neighbor (CCC 2041). A minimum is not the measure of holiness; it is a common floor beneath which ecclesial life is gravely injured.

\subsection{Authority, persons, and facts}

A precept is concrete only when its legal identity is known. One must identify the legislator, promulgated text, jurisdiction, persons bound, effective date, material facts, and any exception, dispensation, particular law, or later amendment. General theological statements cannot supply these data.

The Code itself sometimes marks the layers. CIC c.~1249 says that divine law binds all the Christian faithful to penance, then explains that common penitential days are prescribed so that the faithful may be united in shared observance. Canons 1250--1253 determine days, ages, and the competence of episcopal conferences. The underlying duty and its ecclesiastical specification are related but not identical.

The same discipline governs Sunday. The divine and moral reasons for worship do not eliminate the need to read CIC cc.~1246--1248. Conversely, those canons should not be read as if a three-line juridical text created the Resurrection, the Eucharist, or the virtue of religion. Law states an obligation within a reality it did not originate.

This article therefore turns to one bounded legal example. The controlling body of law is the 1983 Code of Canon Law as amended, for the Latin Church, under universal law as of 20 July 2026. The official Latin controls; the Holy See's English web rendering is a working reference. Particular territorial determinations and individual cases remain outside the conclusion.
