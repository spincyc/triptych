\section{The Grace of the Answer}

\subsection{Justice opens into hope}

The due exposes another asymmetry. No creature can repay creation, and no sinner can reach the supernatural end unaided. The first incapacity is permanent: no creature becomes equal to the Creator. The second is healed by grace. Justice therefore ends not by withdrawing the command but by hoping for the help that enables a filial act without making it unreal.

\subsection{The office of hope}

Here hope enters without becoming another name for justice. The \emph{Catechism} says that when God reveals himself and calls, a person cannot fully respond to divine love by his own powers. He must hope that God will give the capacity to love in return and to act according to the commandments of charity (CCC 2090). Aquinas describes theological hope as tending toward future beatitude, arduous but possible through divine assistance (ST II--II, q.~17, aa.~1--2).

Justice names the answer owed. Hope refuses the fiction that the answer can be made alone.

Hope also excludes two evasions. Presumption appeals to mercy while refusing conversion, or trusts one's own powers as sufficient. Despair treats forgiveness, assistance, or beatitude as unavailable (CCC 2091--2092). Presumption says grace makes obedience unnecessary. Despair says obedience is futile because grace will not come. Neither receives gift as gift.

Devotion joins this hope to the will. Aquinas says meditation gives rise to devotion when one considers divine goodness and one's own insufficiency. The first awakens love; the second excludes presumption and makes the creature lean upon divine help. The resulting joy is joined to sorrow over present defect and to hope of assistance (ST II--II, q.~82, aa.~3--4). Proper abasement is therefore not paralysis. It is the truthful loss of self-sufficiency in a will made ready to act.

\subsection{The living sacrifice}

Psalm 116 asks the question in the language of thanksgiving: ``How can I repay the Lord for all the good done for me?'' Its answer is not a possession capable of equal exchange. The psalmist receives again: he raises the cup of salvation, calls upon the Lord's name, fulfills his vows among the people, and offers thanksgiving (Ps 116:12--19).

Paul's ``therefore'' can now bear its full weight: because no one first gives to God, and because all is mercy, offer your bodies as a living sacrifice (Rom 11:35--12:1). The body offered is already created; mercy already precedes the offering; the many who are members of Christ and of one another make the offering ecclesial (Rom 12:4--5). The return is real and entirely encompassed by gift.

Augustine calls every work ordered to holy fellowship with God a true sacrifice. The person becomes an offering; works of mercy are referred to the supreme good; the redeemed city is offered through the High Priest who first offered himself (\emph{City of God} X.6). Christian sacrifice is therefore neither destruction for its own sake nor a tribute God consumes. In Christ it is the creature's whole life restored to right relation: reason renewed, body made available to good, neighbor received as fellow member, praise made common.

\emph{Gift received. Due recognized. Self offered.}

The creature gives God nothing he lacks. It gives no equivalent for being, redemption, or glory. It gives the answer that justice can truly call due: body, reason, time, labor, praise, and service, offered through powers and mercies already received.

But justice has not yet spoken the last word. It has named what ought to be rendered; hope, by whose help. One question remains: can the will desire the good disclosed by duty---not merely as an order imposed by the highest Lord, but as communion with the beloved?

What is owed can be freely loved. What is freely loved does not cease to be owed. That movement belongs to charity.
