\section{What Can Be Given Back?}

``How can I repay the Lord?'' (Ps 116:12). The question becomes harder the longer one stays with it. What could be given to the One from whom every power of giving has already come? The breath of thanks, the body that kneels, the hour kept for worship, the bread and wine carried to the altar---all have first been received.

The psalmist's answer is surprising. He will receive the cup, call upon the name of the Lord, fulfill his vows, and give thanks in the presence of the people (Ps 116:13--19). He does not settle an account. He receives more deeply and lets the gift take hold of his words, his promises, and his common life.

This is not a puzzle for the seminar room alone. It waits inside every ``I have to go to Mass,'' every command that sounds like another will pressing against our own, and every attempt to keep worship separate from work, possessions, and the claims of the neighbor. If gift is truly gift, can anything be owed? If God needs nothing, why does an answer matter? If worship is due, can it still be free?

The first movement of this series reached two judgments: \emph{I am, and I am not from myself.} A creature depends upon its source before it understands, welcomes, or resists that dependence. Now the truth presses into action: what are we to do with lives we did not give ourselves?

St.~Paul places that question within an apparent contradiction. ``Who has given [God] anything that he may be repaid?'' The answer is no one. ``From him and through him and for him are all things'' (Rom 11:35--36). No creature reaches behind the first gift, supplies a divine lack, or makes God its debtor. Paul then draws a practical conclusion: ``therefore,'' by the mercies of God, offer your bodies as a living sacrifice and spiritual worship (Rom 12:1). The denial of repayment does not end the return. It purifies it.

Dependence and obligation are not the same thing. Dependence is true whether or not the creature consents; obligation concerns the fitting act of a rational will. Sin can reject the order of the due, but it cannot repeal creaturehood. Nor does metaphysics by itself specify every commandment, sacrament, holy day, or ecclesiastical precept.

The title \emph{due return} names neither equivalent repayment nor an exchange of benefits. God receives no new actuality from the response. If the answer is to be real, it must become a way of living within the truth that we are creatures.
