\section{Sunday: The Due Made Common}

\subsection{A Paschal obligation}

Sunday is first the Lord's Day. Christ rose on the first day of the week; the Christian assembly receives that day as the first feast and the sign of the new creation (CCC 2174). The Church's weekly gathering is attested in the apostolic age and in early Christian witness. St.~Justin Martyr describes Christians assembling on the day called Sunday for readings, common prayer, Eucharistic thanksgiving, Communion, and aid to those in need (\emph{First Apology} 67). Worship and mercy already belong to one act of the gathered body.

The Second Vatican Council calls Sunday the original feast day and directs that it be taught and observed as a day of joy and freedom from work (\emph{Sacrosanctum Concilium} 106). The current canon does not invent this history. It gives the tradition universal juridical form in the Latin Church.

\subsection{The controlling Latin law}

CIC c.~1 limits the Code to the Latin Church. Under c.~11, merely ecclesiastical laws bind those baptized in the Catholic Church or received into it who possess sufficient use of reason and, unless the law provides otherwise, have completed seven years of age. These general norms control the apparently unqualified word ``faithful'' in the feast-day canons.

Canon 1246 \S1 identifies Sunday, on which the Paschal mystery is celebrated by apostolic tradition, as the primordial holy day of obligation in the universal Church. It also lists other holy days. Under \S2 an episcopal conference, with the prior approval of the Apostolic See, may suppress some of those other holy days or transfer them to Sunday. Consequently, a universal article cannot give one territorially complete calendar of holy days beyond Sunday.

Canon 1247 states two obligations. On Sundays and other holy days of obligation, the faithful are obliged to participate in Mass. They are also to abstain from works and affairs that hinder the worship owed to God, the joy proper to the Lord's Day, or suitable relaxation of mind and body. The second duty is not a generic ban on employment or exertion. Its objects---worship, festal joy, and humane rest---govern its meaning.

Canon 1248 \S1 states what satisfies the Mass precept: assistance at a Mass celebrated anywhere in a Catholic rite, either on the feast itself or in the evening of the preceding day. The law requires participation in Mass; it does not by that precept require reception of Holy Communion on every Sunday. Reception is governed separately by the sacramental canons and the person's condition (CIC cc.~912--923).

Section 2 addresses a different case. If the absence of a sacred minister or another grave cause makes participation in the Eucharistic celebration impossible, the law strongly recommends participation in a liturgy of the Word celebrated according to the diocesan bishop's prescriptions, or prayer for a suitable time personally, in a family, or in groups of families. The recommended alternative is spiritually serious, but the text does not rename it a Mass or declare it performance of the impossible act.

\subsection{Excuse, dispensation, and rest}

The \emph{Catechism} reads these canons within moral theology. The faithful are obliged to participate unless excused for a serious reason---illness and care of infants are given as examples---or dispensed by their pastor (CCC 2181). Canon 1245 permits a pastor, for a just cause and according to the diocesan bishop's prescriptions, to dispense in an individual case from observance of a feast day or to commute the obligation into another pious work. The diocesan bishop's broader power under c.~87 and the general law of dispensations in cc.~85--93 remain controlling.

The duty of rest also has limits. Family needs and important social service can legitimately excuse from Sunday rest (CCC 2185). Works of mercy belong positively to the day; Christians should also avoid imposing unnecessary work upon others and should attend to those whose poverty denies them ordinary leisure (CCC 2186--2187). The day is not sanctified by making necessary care invisible.

The distinctions prevent both laxity and rigorism. Private inconvenience is not automatically grave cause. A serious reason is not contempt for the precept. Dispensation is not self-dispensation. Necessary work does not make worship unimportant; the rest precept does not condemn mercy, family care, or essential public service. General examples do not adjudicate every case.

\begin{studybox}{What the canon does and does not say}
It binds identified faithful to a common Eucharistic and festal practice. It specifies time, form, and certain relief. It does not establish a territorial list without particular law, govern Eastern Catholics through the Latin Code, decide every disputed fact, or pronounce the full culpability of a person from the external omission alone.
\end{studybox}

The Sunday precept is therefore an instance of responsive justice. The Church does not command God to become present or the Eucharist to become a private possession. She commands the faithful to enter the common act in which Christ gathers his body and gives what that body cannot supply for itself. Time received from God is returned as praise, rest, communion, and mercy---and received again as grace.
