\section{The Enemy We Cannot Outsource}

Someone else can stand beside a deathbed, close the eyes, carry the coffin, and speak the eulogy. No one can die another's death. At that frontier every borrowed identity fails. Possessions remain where they were; offices pass to successors; reputation survives only in other minds. The person goes where none of these can accompany him.

Christian faith does not weaken this fact with the language of a merely natural transition. Scripture calls death an enemy, indeed ``the last enemy'' (1~Cor 15:26). It is the tearing apart of what belongs together: the human soul and body. The body is not a used instrument discarded by the real person. A human being is a bodily creature, and death wounds that unity. Christian burial therefore honors the body, while Christian hope awaits its resurrection.

Nor does faith treat death as ultimate. The enemy has been entered by the Son of God. Christ did not evade mortality by appearing to die. He truly died, descended to the dead, rose bodily, and made death---still grievous---a possible passage ``with Christ'' (Phil 1:23). The Christian claim is not that death has ceased to hurt. It is that death no longer possesses the last word.

\section{What Death Reveals}

Death first reveals contingency. We did not call ourselves from nothing, and we cannot guarantee ourselves another hour. ``Teach us to number our days'' is not arithmetic but wisdom (Ps 90:12): to receive time as finite gift rather than private estate. The remembrance of death is sane when it exposes false ultimacies. It becomes deformed when it breeds fascination, despair, or contempt for earthly duties.

Death also reveals moral seriousness. Bodily life is the arena in which freedom becomes biography. The Church teaches that death ends the period in which a person may accept or reject the grace manifested in Christ (CCC 1021). This does not reduce salvation to a last-minute scorecard. It means that love must become actual in time. Intentions that never consent to truth, repentance, mercy, or costly fidelity do not become virtuous merely because they remained inward possibilities.

Finally, death reveals communion. The dying person is radically unable to save himself, yet he need not be abandoned. The Church surrounds the dying with reconciliation, anointing, Viaticum, Scripture, presence, and prayer. These are not magical techniques for controlling the hour. They are acts of Christ's body, entrusting one member to the Lord who died and rose.

\section{Sin, Nature, and the Death of Christ}

Genesis presents death inside the catastrophe of sin: humanity grasps autonomy and returns to dust (Gen 2:17; 3:19). Paul names death the wages of sin and contrasts Adam's trespass with Christ's obedience (Rom 5:12--21; 6:23). Catholic theology therefore speaks of death both as natural in a bodily organism considered in itself and as contrary to the original divine gift by which humanity was preserved from death. Those propositions concern different orders. The body is materially corruptible; the loss of original justice leaves humanity subject to the corruption from which grace had preserved it (CCC 1008).

This boundary matters pastorally. It is cruel to infer a person's unusual guilt from illness, accident, or early death. Christ rejects the easy conversion of calamity into a verdict on the victim (Lk 13:1--5; Jn 9:1--3). The universal reign of sin and death is not a license for accusing mourners or explaining a particular tragedy as a deciphered divine punishment.

On Calvary, Christ makes the human extremity his own. He suffers death freely, not because death acquires goodness, but because obedient love passes through it. ``By death'' he destroys the one who has the power of death and frees those held by lifelong fear (Heb 2:14--15). The Cross does not romanticize dying. It disarms death by filling the place of abandonment with the Son's self-offering to the Father.

\subsection{The fathers refuse a cheap immortality}

St.~Ignatius of Antioch can desire martyrdom because he desires Christ, not because bodily life is worthless (\emph{Romans} 4--7). St.~Irenaeus argues against salvation imagined as escape from flesh: the hands that serve, the eyes that see, and the body fed by the Eucharist belong to the person God raises (\emph{Against Heresies} V.6--14). St.~Athanasius places the scandal inside the Incarnation: the immortal Word accepts a mortal body so that corruption may be undone from within (\emph{On the Incarnation} 8--10, 20--29). Their common pressure is precise. Christian courage before death rests neither on contempt for creation nor on an indestructible self; it rests on Christ's fidelity to the creature he assumed.

\section{The Soul Without the Body}

The Church teaches that the spiritual soul survives bodily death and is judged, while awaiting reunion with the risen body (CCC 997, 1022). This intermediate state is real but incomplete. The blessed are truly with Christ; the lost are truly separated from him; those assured of salvation may undergo purification. Yet the Creed does not end with ``the immortality of the soul.'' It professes ``the resurrection of the body.''

This guards two truths. Personal identity is not extinguished at death, and bodily existence is not an embarrassing prelude to a purely spiritual destiny. The same human persons will rise, transformed by divine power. Paul uses seed and harvest to express continuity through transformation: what is sown corruptible is raised incorruptible (1~Cor 15:35--49). The image does not explain a mechanism. It denies both annihilation and mere resuscitation.

\section{How to Prepare Without Trying to Control}

Preparation for death is preparation to live truthfully. The traditional \emph{memento mori} is not an invitation to rehearse terror. It asks whether present choices could bear eternal light. Reconciliation should not be deferred; debts of justice should be repaired; dependents should be cared for; wills and medical wishes should be made intelligible; the sacraments should be sought before consciousness fails. Ordinary fidelity is the principal school of a good death.

No technique guarantees a peaceful final hour. Some saints died serenely; Christ died with a cry. Medication that proportionately relieves grave pain may be used even when an unintended risk of shortening life is foreseen, but death must not be intended as means or end (CCC 2276--2279). Refusing disproportionate treatment can accept mortality; it is not the same act as causing death. Concrete decisions require competent medical and pastoral counsel.

For survivors, grief is not unbelief. Jesus wept at Lazarus's tomb before calling him forth (Jn 11:33--44). The Christian mourns a real rupture and hopes in a real resurrection. Prayer for the dead refuses both despair and presumption: it commends the person to mercy and confesses that love does not become useless at the grave.

The Roman funeral liturgy acts out this refusal of despair and denial. It honors the body, intercedes for the dead, and brings the body to committal under the sign of resurrection. Its sober grammar matters: the assembly neither canonizes the deceased nor speaks as though death were a hygienic disappearance. It gives thanks, laments, pleads, and hopes.

\section{The Last Enemy Is Not the Last Thing}

Death strips away the fantasy of self-possession, but Christ strips death of sovereignty. The Christian can neither worship life at any cost nor despise it as disposable. Life is received for love; death is suffered as an enemy; resurrection is awaited as gift.

The right response is therefore neither denial nor morbidity. It is readiness: to inhabit today without pretending it is endless, to repent while repentance is possible, to accompany the dying without false promises, and to entrust the dead to the Judge who first entered the tomb. ``Whether we live or whether we die, we are the Lord's'' (Rom 14:8). That belonging, not biological endurance, is the ground of Christian courage.

\section{Scope, Method, and Qualifications}
This independent article addresses serious general Catholic readers. It treats human death in relation to sin, Christ's Paschal mystery, the intermediate state, bodily resurrection, preparation, and grief. It does not offer medical directives for a case, a complete theology of martyrdom, a manual of funeral rites, or a judgment about any deceased person. Scripture and definitive or authoritative Catholic teaching govern doctrinal claims; patristic, Thomistic, and liturgical witnesses illuminate their reception; pastoral applications are project synthesis. Scriptural wording is kept brief and cited by locus. The theological account of bodily corruptibility and the preternatural gift follows a common Thomistic synthesis and is not presented as a biological theory. No independent theological, medical, pastoral, or ecclesiastical review is recorded.
