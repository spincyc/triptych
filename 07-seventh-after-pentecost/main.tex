\begin{sensestable}
Literal & Romans 6 contrasts slavery to sin, shame, and death with service to God, sanctification, and eternal life; Matthew 7 commands vigilance toward false prophets and identifies obedience to the Father's will as decisive fruit.\\\\
Allegorical & Christ is the true teacher and obedient Son; the Church bears holy fruit only by his gift. The Gradual's teacher and the Secret's one perfect sacrifice disclose positively what false prophecy and corrupt sacrifice counterfeit.\\\\
Moral & Test a life by works rather than costume or slogans, submit one's own conduct to the same judgment, and remember that moral fruit does not by itself establish the truth of doctrine.\\\\
Anagogical & The tree's fire and Paul's opposed ends, death and eternal life, reveal final judgment; medicinal grace is offered now so that the sinner may be changed and bear fruit for life.\\\\

\section*{Voice, Hands, Fruit, and the Healed Will}

This Sunday is organized by \emph{congruence}: confession must agree with action, teaching with life, visible sacrifice with the offered heart, and the human will with the Father's will. Counterfeit religion divides these. The liturgy does not merely expose the division in others; it asks God to heal it in the worshipper.

\begin{threestable}{Faculty or sign}{Where the Mass places it}{What the sequence establishes}
Voice and hands & Introit and Alleluia: all nations acclaim the King with exultant voice and clapping hands. & Praise is already embodied before Christ contrasts saying ``Lord, Lord'' with doing the Father's will.\\
Ear and face & Gradual: children hear the true teacher, approach the Lord, receive light, and lose their shame. & Discernment begins as discipleship. The hearer does not manufacture light or judge from an imagined position above the Church.\\
Members, fruit, and end & Epistle and Gospel: bodily members serve a master; trees bear according to their condition; both paths disclose a fruit and an end. & A mask can delay recognition but cannot alter the causal relation among allegiance, habit, action, and final destiny.\\
Gift, heart, and will & Offertory through Postcommunion: a humbled people offers, receives, asks rescue, and is medicinally freed and redirected. & The altar turns the examiner into an offerer and patient. Grace must produce the integrity which discernment seeks.\\
\end{threestable}

\begin{sourcecard}{St. Augustine --- \emph{Exposition on Psalm 46}, 2--3}
Commenting directly on the Introit and Alleluia verse, Augustine reads the hands as good works and the voice as confession: ``Agree together must the hands and tongue. Let this confess, these work.'' He then places every nation beneath Christ, the King who bought the world by his blood. Applying that exegesis to the verse's two positions in this Mass is an editorial liturgical synthesis.
\end{sourcecard}

\noindent\emph{Reading order:} the liturgy moves left to right. Recognition is enclosed by prior teaching and subsequent healing; the Gospel's test is not an autonomous technique.

\begin{massbox}{The governing reversal}
The question ``Which teacher is a wolf?'' becomes ``Has the universal King joined my voice, members, offering, and will?'' Christian discernment remains real, but no one applies it truthfully without also undergoing it.
\end{massbox}

\section*{The Gospel: Appearance, Fruit, and Time}

Matthew 7:15--21 follows the narrow gate and leads into the contrast between verbal profession and obedience. The images move inward and forward: clothing is immediately visible, a root is hidden, fruit appears through action, and fire names God's final judgment. The Fathers agree on the need for vigilance but do not reduce the warning to one identical referent.

\begin{fourstable}{Witness}{Primary emphasis}{Evidence named}{Interpretive boundary}
St. John Chrysostom & \emph{Homily 23 on Matthew}, 8--9, reads the false prophets here chiefly as people of corrupt life masked by virtue; \emph{Homily 24} extends the test to ``Lord, Lord'' and mighty works. & The demanding way eventually exposes one who wants virtue's appearance without its labor; correct confession and gifts benefiting others do not prove the worker holy. & ``Cannot'' is not fatalism: while remaining evil one cannot bear good fruit, but the person may be changed and become good.\\
St. Augustine & \emph{On the Sermon on the Mount} II.24--25 includes deceptive claimants to Christian wisdom and heretics after the narrow-gate saying. & Fasting, prayer, and almsgiving may be genuine sheep's fleece or simulated costume. Trials involving temporal gain or loss disclose what ordinary appearances hide. & The hearer cannot see another's heart directly. Manifest words, deeds, and what trials disclose are the proper field of judgment.\\
St. Hilary of Poitiers & \emph{Commentary on Matthew} 6.4--5 holds smooth speech, simulated meekness, action, and obedience together. & Actions must test self-presentation; invocation, prophecy, and signs do not by themselves prove the worker's merit. & Divine gifts may benefit another through an unworthy instrument, so striking effects are not a certificate of sanctity.\\
St. Jerome & \emph{Commentary on Matthew}, book I, at 7:15--21, applies the contrast broadly to dress and speech contradicted by deeds and particularly to poisoned teaching beneath ascetic appearance. & Teaching and life reciprocally test the teacher: an attractive life cannot establish corrupt doctrine, while evil works contradict true words in practice. & Jerome, like Augustine, rejects the claim that good and bad trees are fixed opposing human natures; conversion remains possible.\\
\end{fourstable}

\begin{studybox}{Five distinctions carried by the images}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.45em,itemsep=0.2em]
\item \textbf{Clothing is not automatically fraudulent.} Prayer, fasting, almsgiving, meek speech, and simplicity properly belong to sheep; their imitation by wolves does not discredit the virtues themselves.
\item \textbf{Fruit is more than an isolated result.} Augustine identifies it with the works of the flesh or fruit of the Spirit and ultimately with doing the Father's will.
\item \textbf{Useful effects do not canonize the instrument.} Augustine compares true teaching from a bad teacher to grapes gathered from a vine entangled in thorns: the truth belongs to the vine, not to the thorn.
\item \textbf{A bad tree is a present moral condition, not an unchangeable essence.} Chrysostom and Augustine preserve both Christ's strong ``cannot'' and the real possibility of conversion.
\item \textbf{Fire belongs to divine judgment.} The warning authorizes vigilance and refusal of deception, not private vengeance or certainty about hidden final guilt.
\end{enumerate}
\end{studybox}

\begin{sourcecard}{St. Augustine --- what counts as saying ``Lord''}
In II.25.83 Augustine distinguishes a merely vocal utterance from speech that truly represents understanding and will. The point is not to oppose confession to obedience, but to expose a confession whose sound is detached from the speaker's intention and life. The Mass's voice must become the body's act.
\end{sourcecard}

\begin{sourcecard}{The \emph{Didache} --- an early reception of the test}
Chapter 11 tests both content and conduct: destructive novelty is rejected; the prophet is known ``from [his] ways''; one who teaches truth but does not practice it is false; demands for money expose appetite. Chapter 14 then joins confessed sin, reconciliation, and a pure Lord's-Day sacrifice. This is early Christian reception, not a commentary on this Roman formulary.
\end{sourcecard}

\section*{Two Axes, One Ecclesial Act of Discernment}

The Fathers prevent a common category error. Personal holiness affects a teacher's credibility, but revealed doctrine is not generated by the observer's estimate of personality or outcomes. Conversely, saying something true does not make a corrupt life true.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{>{\bfseries\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.18\linewidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X}
\toprule
& \textbf{Holy moral fruit} & \textbf{Corrupt moral fruit}\\\\
\midrule
Apostolic doctrine & A credible witness whose teaching and life cohere. Holiness adorns the truth but remains grace; it does not create revelation. & Hypocrisy or scandal contradicts the teaching in practice. Augustine's ``vine among thorns'' explains how a hearer may still receive the true word from its apostolic source.\\\\
False doctrine & Admirable discipline, benevolence, or apparent success cannot make novelty revealed. Jerome's ascetic-looking heretic and Augustine's sheep's fleece govern this cell. & Both content and way fail their proper tests. Rejection needs no claim to read the hidden heart or predict the final judgment.\\\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\begin{sourcecard}{St. Irenaeus --- the public rule of apostolic truth}
\emph{Against Heresies} III.3 grounds doctrine in the public preaching handed down from the apostles through the Churches and their succession, not in a claimant's private prestige. Irenaeus is not commenting on Matthew 7 here; he supplies the ecclesial norm which the Gospel's moral test cannot replace. The formulary's prescribed Preface of the Most Holy Trinity then performs a positive act of received doctrine after the warning against false prophecy; it is not another proper or proof of the formulary's compositional history.
\end{sourcecard}

\begin{massbox}{A five-question protocol}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.45em,itemsep=0.2em]
\item \textbf{What is being taught?} Test content within Scripture read in the apostolic faith of the Church; do not infer revelation from charm or results.
\item \textbf{What way of life persists?} Look for stable obedience, charity, truthfulness, humility, and freedom from exploitation rather than a curated moment.
\item \textbf{What does trial disclose?} Augustine names hoped-for gain and feared loss as pressures that reveal an otherwise concealed governing love.
\item \textbf{What is still unknown?} Interior intention and final guilt belong to God. Prudential distance from harm is not a claim to omniscience.
\item \textbf{Who is also being tested?} The Collect, Offertory, Communion, and Postcommunion place the observer under providence, sacrifice, rescue, and medicine.
\end{enumerate}
\end{massbox}

\sectionguard
\section*{The Collect: Providence Before Private Judgment}

The Collect addresses the God whose providence \latin{in sui dispositione non fallitur}---does not fail in its ordering---and asks him to remove every harmful thing and grant everything profitable. The prayer does not transfer divine infallibility to the worshipper. It makes discernment a petition for purification and right ordering.

\begin{studybox}{Related Thomistic control, not commentary on the Collect}
In \emph{Summa theologiae} I, q.22, a.4, St. Thomas distinguishes the certainty of providence from necessity in every created effect: God infallibly orders necessary and contingent things according to their proper modes. Human judgment and willing remain genuinely creaturely; dependence upon providence is not fatalism.
\end{studybox}

\sectionguard
\section*{Paul: Freedom Is a Transfer of Service}

Romans 6 does not oppose bondage to morally empty independence. It contrasts two masters, two formations, two fruits, and two ends. The will remains an agent, but its habits incline it; grace does not merely change the label on the servant, it heals what the servant can love and do.

\begin{threestable}{Paul's term}{Logical force in Romans 6}{Patristic and scholastic precision}
\latin{Exhibuistis / exhibete} & The same members once presented to uncleanness are now deliberately presented to justice. Past agency becomes the measure of present dedication, though the better Master deserves more. & Chrysostom and Aquinas read the bodily presentation as enacted works. The Introit's hands and the later offering therefore belong to Paul's moral grammar without being direct Pauline references to those chants.\\
\latin{Servi / liberati} & Liberation from sin immediately becomes service to God. There is no morally neutral masterless state. & Augustine says the Son frees the slave of lust to become a servant of love. Aquinas calls willing service under justice true freedom because it inclines the person toward the good proper to rational nature.\\
\latin{Fructum in sanctificationem} & Sanctification is already a present fruit, not only a future verdict or public credential. & Aquinas reads it as holiness enacted and increased in good works with a new spiritual delight. Chrysostom distinguishes this possessed fruit from eternal life still promised as end.\\
\latin{Finis} & Each service has an intrinsic trajectory: death or eternal life. The sinner need not consciously choose death for sin to tend there. & Aquinas distinguishes the agent's intended aim from the act's objective end. This prevents pleasant short-term outcomes from becoming the final measure of fruit.\\
\latin{Stipendia / gratia} & Death is sin's wage; eternal life is God's gift in Christ. Paul deliberately refuses a symmetrical ``wage of righteousness.'' & Chrysostom and Augustine preserve real holy action while denying autonomous desert. Sin pays what accords with its service; God crowns gifts which grace has already produced.\\
\end{threestable}

\noindent\begin{minipage}[t]{0.49\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\begin{sourcecard}{St. John Chrysostom --- \emph{Romans} 12}
On Romans 6:19--23, Chrysostom notes Paul's restrained comparison, the present possession of liberation and holiness, and the asymmetry between wages and gift: ``Of these the fruit is holiness, and where holiness is, there is all confidence.''
\end{sourcecard}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.49\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\begin{sourcecard}{St. Thomas Aquinas --- \emph{Romans} 6, lect. 4}
Numbers 508--515 distinguish coercion from inclination, apparent freedom from rational freedom, sanctification as fruitful delight in good works, and eternal life as end. Service to God is ``true freedom and the best form of slavery.''
\end{sourcecard}
\end{minipage}

\begin{studybox}{St. Augustine: reward remains grace}
\emph{On Grace and Free Will} 19--21 asks how eternal life can be both reward according to works and the gratuitous gift of Romans 6:23. Augustine answers that faith, charity, and the good works crowned are themselves God's gifts: eternal life is ``grace for grace.'' Chapters 32--33 preserve transformed agency---God makes the good willing possible, and the person truly wills and acts.
\end{studybox}

\begin{massbox}{No Pelagian fruit; no inert grace}
Holy fruit is genuinely the Christian's act, formed through repeated willing and obedience. Its root, growth, delight, perseverance, and supernatural end remain grace. The Gospel's demand is therefore neither self-salvation nor passive exemption from the Father's will.
\end{massbox}

\sectionguard
\section*{From Command to Cure}

The Postcommunion asks that God's \latin{medicinalis operatio} free the faithful from their perversities and lead them to what is right. St. Augustine's \emph{On Man's Perfection in Righteousness} 3 uses the image of lameness: command names the right walk, but ``the medicine has assisted his will.'' Grace changes the tree by healing the agent, not by declaring diseased fruit healthy.

\begin{threestable}{Opening petition}{Sacramental completion}{Relation to fruit}
Remove every harmful thing (\latin{noxia cuncta submoveas}) & Free us from our perversities (\latin{a nostris perversitatibus expediat}) & What obstructs right action must be removed and the entangled will released.\\
Grant everything profitable (\latin{omnia nobis profutura concedas}) & Lead us to the things that are right (\latin{ad ea quae sunt recta perducat}) & Gift becomes direction, enacted righteousness, holy fruit, and finally the end of life.\\
\end{threestable}

\section*{From the Furnace to the One Sacrifice}

The Offertory extracts Daniel 3:40 from Azariah's prayer in the furnace. Its context names a people without prince, prophet, leader, holocaust, sacrifice, oblation, incense, or place of offering. They ask to be received with a contrite soul and humble spirit, to become as the abundance of legal victims, to follow God wholeheartedly, and not to be confounded because they trust him. The Secret then moves from absent figures to Christ's one perfect sacrifice.

\noindent\emph{Editorial sacrificial genealogy.} The Secret itself supplies the movement from diverse legal victims to one sacrifice and from each offerer to all; Abel and Azariah retain their distinct biblical settings.

\begin{threestable}{Latin hinge in the Secret}{Movement}{What it prevents}
\latin{legalium differentiam hostiarum} / \latin{unius sacrificii perfectione} & The diversity of legal victims is consecrated or brought to completion by the perfection of one sacrifice. & The Old Testament figures are neither treated as unrelated relics nor confused with repeated rival sacrifices alongside Christ.\\
\latin{accipe sacrificium a devotis tibi famulis} & God is asked to receive a sacrifice from devoted servants; offering is ecclesial action under grace, not self-validating performance. & Correct external order is not made sufficient apart from devotion, obedience, and God's acceptance.\\
\latin{pari benedictione, sicut munera Abel, sanctifica} & The prayer invokes Abel's accepted gifts while asking God himself to sanctify what is offered now. & Abel is not reduced to a generic symbol of sincerity; divine blessing and a rightly ordered offerer remain joined.\\
\latin{quod singuli obtulerunt ... cunctis proficiat ad salutem} & What individuals offered to God's majesty is asked to benefit everyone unto salvation. & The personal offering is not private spiritual property. Its intended fruit is the common good of Christ's body.\\
\end{threestable}

\begin{fourstable}{Witness}{Direct teaching}{Contribution here}{Source boundary}
St. John Chrysostom & \emph{Homily 20 on Romans} joins Daniel's contrite furnace prayer to Romans 12's living sacrifice and enumerates eyes, tongue, hands, feet, hearing, and the other members as offerings. & It extends this Sunday's Romans 6 presentation of members into a whole-body oblation and supplies a patristic bridge to the Daniel Offertory. & Chrysostom comments on Romans 12 and Daniel's prayer, not this Secret or the historical composition of the formulary.\\
St. Irenaeus & \emph{Against Heresies} IV.18 reads Abel's acceptance through single-minded righteousness and warns that outwardly correct sacrifice cannot hide refusal of fellowship or secret sin. & Abel brings the Gospel's outside/inside test to the altar; sheep's clothing may include impeccable ritual appearance. & Irenaeus does not deny God's sanctifying action in the sacrament; he denies that outward offering mechanically makes a corrupt offerer pleasing.\\
St. Augustine & \emph{City of God} X.5--6, 20 calls visible sacrifice the sign of invisible self-offering, cites members yielded to righteousness, and says the whole redeemed city is offered through the High Priest. & It explains how the Secret can move from what each offers to the salvation of all: the many are one body in Christ and the Church learns to offer herself through him. & This is doctrinal illumination of the Secret, not evidence that Augustine authored or directly expounded it.\\
\end{fourstable}

\begin{massbox}{Why the discerner must become an offerer}
Azariah cannot offer the expected victims, yet offers contrition and faithful surrender. The Secret refuses both empty externalism and solitary inwardness: gifts, servants, Christ's one sacrifice, and the salvation of all are held together. St. Thomas later clarifies in \emph{Summa theologiae} III, q.79, a.7 that the Eucharist as sacrifice benefits others for whom it is offered, and in III, q.83, a.1 that its celebration sacramentally represents Christ's Passion and is therefore one sacrifice, not another victim alongside the Cross. This is doctrinal precision, not source history. The Church's answer to counterfeit religion is incorporation into Christ's obedient self-offering.
\end{massbox}

\begin{threestable}{Augustine's locus}{Direct reading of Psalm 33}{Liturgical resonance}
Sections 9 and 15 & Christ himself calls the children to hear; approaching and being enlightened is read Eucharistically as approaching the Crucified to receive his Body and Blood. Augustine says the feet that run are faith's longing and the twofold charity of God and neighbor. & The Gradual is not merely preliminary instruction. It already points toward sacramental approach and defines the movement toward Christ by charity rather than fascination with a human teacher.\\
Section 19 & God may seem not to hear because, like a physician who continues cutting away corruption, he heals for everlasting life rather than merely stopping present pain. & The Communion's \latin{accelera} is urgent without dictating the form or timing of deliverance; the Postcommunion names the deeper medicinal action.\\
Section 21 & Augustine explicitly names the Three Children delivered from the furnace, then contrasts martyrs not physically spared. Both are delivered, but not in the same temporal manner. & The same psalm's reception links the Gradual to Azariah and prevents the Offertory or Communion from promising immunity from suffering as proof of righteousness.\\
\end{threestable}

\sectionguard
\section*{Communion: Christ and His Body Ask to Be Heard}

In his \emph{Exposition on Psalm 30}, 1--4, Augustine reads the Psalm first in the voice of Christ the Mediator and then in the redeemed people. On the appointed verse, ``Bend down your ear unto me; make haste to deliver me,'' he hears the humbled Christ petitioning the Father; the following verse names God strength, refuge, guide, and nourisher. At Communion the Church does not borrow a stranger's panic: Christ's body enters the Son's filial prayer.

\begin{studybox}{A reciprocal pattern of hearing}
The Gradual commands the children to hear Christ; the Communion asks the Father to incline his ear. Between them, the Gospel tests whether hearing becomes the Father's will, and the altar unites the hearer to Christ's sacrifice. Human listening and divine hearing meet in obedient communion.
\end{studybox}

\section*{The Postcommunion's Three-Verb Treatment}

\begin{threestable}{Latin term}{Force}{Place in the Sunday's argument}
\latin{operatio} & An operation or effect being worked, not only an idea recalled. & The sacrament answers the Gospel at the level of causality: grace acts upon the patient who must bear new fruit.\\
\latin{expediat} & Free, extricate, or disentangle from \latin{perversitates}, disordered turnings. & Liberation in Romans 6 becomes medicinal release from the inclinations that made sin's service appear free.\\
\latin{perducat} & Lead through to \latin{ea quae sunt recta}, the things that are right or straight. & Cure has direction and end: the Father's will, sanctifying fruit, and finally eternal life.\\
\end{threestable}

St. Thomas supplies later doctrinal precision in \emph{Summa theologiae} III, q.62, a.1, where the New-Law sacraments instrumentally cause grace, and III, q.79, a.6, where the Eucharist strengthens the heart in good. Neither locus is a direct commentary on this Postcommunion.

Ignatius calls the one bread ``the medicine of immortality'' and joins it to ecclesial unity, one faith, and life forever in Christ. The phrase illuminates the Postcommunion's medicine and Paul's final gift without replacing the oration's own active verbs.
\end{sourcecard}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.49\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\begin{sourcecard}{St. Augustine --- \emph{On Perfection} 3}
Augustine's lame person remains responsible to walk rightly but cannot simply will away the defect: remedy must cure the foot and assist the will. This gives the Postcommunion's passage from perversity to rectitude its full anti-fatalist and anti-Pelagian force.
\end{sourcecard}
\end{minipage}

\begin{massbox}{A patristic listening sequence for the Mass}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.45em,itemsep=0.2em]
\item At the Introit, can the voice's royal confession and the hands' works be heard as one act rather than two parallel religious identities?
\item In the Collect, which judgments are surrendered to providence before any teacher is examined?
\item In Romans, note each causal term: master, members, fruit, sanctification, end, wages, gift. Which belong to present formation and which to final destiny?
\item At the Gradual, approach as a child who must hear Christ, not as an evaluator who already possesses the light.
\item In the Gospel, separate clothing, fruit, hidden root, and final fire. What evidence is available now, and what judgment remains God's?
\item At the Offertory, hear Azariah's lack, contrition, trust, and offered self; at the Secret, hear many figures gathered into one sacrifice and each offering ordered to all.
\item At Communion, enter Christ's petition for rescue rather than treating reception as proof that no healing remains necessary.
\item In the Postcommunion, listen for the full treatment: medicine operates, disentangles the perverse will, and leads it into rectitude and life.
\end{enumerate}
\end{massbox}

\begin{studybox}{Source boundaries to retain}
\begin{itemize}
\item Augustine's comments on the exact chant verses are \textbf{direct exegesis}; his comments on later verses of the same psalm are \textbf{documented same-psalm resonances}.
\item Irenaeus, Aquinas, and Ignatius offer \textbf{doctrinal illumination}; they are not witnesses to how this particular Roman formulary was composed.
\item The cross-proper maps---voice and hands, the Psalm 33 bridge, and the sacrificial genealogy---are \textbf{editorial liturgical syntheses} controlled by the cited texts.
\item Hilary and Jerome are summarized from their commentaries; no English sentence here is presented as a checked quotation from the Latin editions.
\end{itemize}
\end{studybox}
