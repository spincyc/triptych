## Depart from Me

At the end of the year the Church keeps the feast of Christ the King. In Year A of the Postconciliar Missal its Gospel is the scene that closes our Lord’s last discourse in Matthew: the Son of man come in his majesty with all the angels, the nations gathered before him, the sheep set on his right hand and the goats on his left.[^1] To those on his right he says, “Come, ye blessed of my Father, possess you the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world.” Then he turns to the others:

> Then he shall say to them also that shall be on his left hand: Depart from me, you cursed, into everlasting fire, which was prepared for the devil and his angels.[^2]

The sentence is spoken by the one who said, “Come to me all you that labor and are burdened, and I will refresh you,”[^3] and both sayings are his. The Lord himself chose the words of the sentence, the Church has kept them as he gave them, and no preacher may soften them.

Saint John Chrysostom, preaching to his people at Antioch, noticed how the sentence is framed. The blessed are sent to a kingdom “prepared for you”; the cursed are sent to a fire prepared for someone else.

> Even here He shows His love, and indicates the yearning which He hath toward thee: not saying, “Depart into the fire prepared for you,” but “prepared for the devil.”[^4]

Hell, then, was not made for man. It was prepared for the angels who would not serve, and a man comes into it only by joining them.

### What the Church confesses

The Church has confessed this sentence in her creeds, in her councils, and in the professions and definitions of her popes. The ancient creed that bears the name of Athanasius ends with the judgment: “At whose coming all men shall rise again with their bodies, and shall give account for their own works. And they that have done good shall go into life everlasting, and they that have done evil into everlasting fire.”[^5] The Fourth Lateran Council professed that all, the reprobate and the elect alike, will rise with their own bodies which they now bear, to receive according to their works: *illi cum diabolo poenam perpetuam, et isti cum Christo gloriam sempiternam*, the former perpetual punishment with the devil, the latter everlasting glory with Christ.[^6] The Second Council of Lyons professed that the souls of those who die in mortal sin go down at once into hell, *poenis tamen disparibus puniendas*, to be punished, but with unequal punishments;[^7] and Pope Benedict XII defined that the souls of those who die in actual mortal sin go down into hell immediately after death, *ubi poenis infernalibus cruciantur*, where they are tormented with the pains of hell.[^8] In 1968, at the close of a Year of Faith, Paul VI pronounced the Church’s creed anew: Christ will come to judge the living and the dead, “those who have responded to the love and piety of God going to eternal life, those who have refused them to the end going to the fire that is not extinguished.”[^9]

The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* gathers the doctrine in a few sentences. “To die in mortal sin without repenting and accepting God’s merciful love means remaining separated from him for ever by our own free choice. This state of definitive self-exclusion from communion with God and the blessed is called ‘hell.’ ” “Immediately after death the souls of those who die in a state of mortal sin descend into hell, where they suffer the punishments of hell, ‘eternal fire.’ The chief punishment of hell is eternal separation from God, in whom alone man can possess the life and happiness for which he was created and for which he longs.”[^10]

Together these texts say that hell is real and that those who die in mortal sin enter it at death; that it is eternal; that its chief pain is the loss of God, and that with that loss there is a pain of sense, which Christ and the creeds call fire; that the punishments are unequal, measured to each one’s sins; and that at the resurrection the body rises and shares the punishment of the soul. The Church holds each of these with the weight she has given it, and none of them is hers to change.

The *Catechism* gives the reason the Church sets these truths before her children. “The affirmations of Sacred Scripture and the teachings of the Church on the subject of hell,” it says, “are a call to the responsibility incumbent upon man to make use of his freedom in view of his eternal destiny. They are at the same time an urgent call to conversion.”[^11] The saints received them in that way. They looked at hell in order not to go there, and they looked long, because a short look is soon forgotten. Their way of looking, which the Church has made her own, was to take each part of the sentence in turn, to let it become real to the mind and heavy to the heart, and then to turn toward the mercy that still stands open.

## The Loss of God

Before the sentence names the fire, it says *Depart*. Saint Alphonsus Liguori, whose *Preparation for Death* sets the last things before the Christian one consideration at a time, found the whole of hell in that first word: “The entire, then, of the Hell of the damned consists in that first word of the sentence of their condemnation,—‘Depart from me, you cursed.’ ”[^12] The saints did not look away from the fire, but the Church teaches that the chief punishment of hell is the loss of God, and the Fathers and Doctors teach with her that this loss is the deeper wound.

### Separation from the light

Saint Irenaeus of Lyons, writing in the second century against the Gnostics, begins his account of hell with communion with God, the good of which the lost are deprived:

> And to as many as continue in their love towards God, does He grant communion with Him. But communion with God is life and light, and the enjoyment of all the benefits which He has in store. But on as many as, according to their own choice, depart from God, He inflicts that separation from Himself which they have chosen of their own accord. But separation from God is death, and separation from light is darkness; and separation from God consists in the loss of all the benefits which He has in store. Those, therefore, who cast away by apostasy these forementioned things, being in fact destitute of all good, do experience every kind of punishment. God, however, does not punish them immediately of Himself, but that punishment falls upon them because they are destitute of all that is good. Now, good things are eternal and without end with God, and therefore the loss of these is also eternal and never-ending. It is in this matter just as occurs in the case of a flood of light: those who have blinded themselves, or have been blinded by others, are for ever deprived of the enjoyment of light. It is not, \[however\], that the light has inflicted upon them the penalty of blindness, but it is that the blindness itself has brought calamity upon them.[^13]

Irenaeus says three things. The separation is chosen: God gives the soul what it has preferred. The suffering comes from the privation itself, as the blind man’s darkness comes from his blindness and not from the light, which still floods the world. And the loss is everlasting because the goods of God, which the soul has refused, do not end.

### Ten thousand hells

Saint John Chrysostom, preaching on Christ’s warning that the tree which brings forth no good fruit is cast into the fire, drew from it the distinction the schools would later name the pain of loss and the pain of sense, and he told his people which of the two was the greater:

> And though it appear indeed to be some single judgment, the being burnt up, yet if one examine carefully, these are two punishments. For he that is burnt is also cast of course out of God’s kingdom; and this latter punishment is more grievous than the other. Now I know indeed that many tremble only at hell, but I affirm the loss of that glory to be a far greater punishment than hell.

Then, having tried to make his hearers feel the loss by the image of a father cast out from the presence of a beloved child, he went on:

> No doubt hell, and that punishment, is a thing not to be borne. Yet though one suppose ten thousand hells, he will utter nothing like what it will be to fail of that blessed glory, to be hated of Christ, to hear “I know you not,” to be accused for not feeding Him when we saw Him an hungered. Yea, better surely to endure a thousand thunderbolts, than to see that face of mildness turning away from us, and that eye of peace not enduring to look upon us.[^14]

The face Chrysostom speaks of is Christ’s, and its turning away is what the word *Depart* means. It is the face that sought the sinner through all his life, that was spat upon for him and looked on him from the cross, and that now, because he would not have it, will not look on him again.

Saint Augustine, who defended the eternity of punishment at length in the *City of God*, says the same in his handbook of the faith. Even if the anger of God were the least that could be conceived, he writes, the loss itself would outweigh every pain:

> to be lost out of the kingdom of God, to be an exile from the city of God, to be alienated from the life of God, to have no share in that great goodness which God has laid up for them that fear Him, and has wrought out for them that trust in Him, would be a punishment so great, that, supposing it to be eternal, no torments that we know of, continued through as many ages as man’s imagination can conceive, could be compared with it.[^15]

### An infinite loss

Saint Thomas Aquinas gives the reason in the language of the schools. Every mortal sin, he teaches, has two sides: a turning away from God, the unchangeable good, and a disordered turning toward some changeable good. The punishment answers to each. “Accordingly, in so far as sin consists in turning away from something, its corresponding punishment is the *pain of loss,* which also is infinite, because it is the loss of the infinite good, i.e. God. But in so far as sin turns inordinately to something, its corresponding punishment is the *pain of sense,* which is also finite.”[^16] The pain of sense is measured, and so it can be greater for one sinner and less for another; were the punishment infinite in every respect, Aquinas observes, “the punishments of all mortal sins would be equal; because one infinite is not greater than another,” and the councils teach that the punishments are unequal. The *Catechism* likewise calls eternal separation from God “the chief punishment of hell.”

Alphonsus puts the doctrine in plain words. “But all these torments are nothing, compared with the pain of loss. Hell does not consist in the darkness, stench, shrieks and fire; the pain which constitutes Hell, is the pain of having lost God.” A man who loses a jewel grieves according to its worth; the damned soul “has lost God, who is an infinite good.”[^17] And he imagines what we should hear if we could ask one of the lost why he weeps: “I weep because I have lost God, and shall never see him more.”[^18] Saint Catherine of Siena heard the Eternal Father say the same in her *Dialogue*: of the four principal torments of the damned, the first “is, that they see themselves deprived of the vision of Me, which is such pain to them, that, were it possible, they would rather choose the fire, and the tortures and torments, and to see Me, than to be without the torments and not to see Me.”[^19]

### Why we do not feel it now

It is hard for us to fear this loss as the saints feared it, and Alphonsus says why: “because they live in the midst of darkness, sinners who are content to live for months and years without God, do not understand this pain. However, they shall know at death the great good which they lose.”[^20] In this life the soul is crowded with lesser goods, and a man can lose God for a season and hardly notice, because something else is always at hand to fill the place. At death those goods are taken from him. The soul, made for God, is left alone with its desire for him, and that desire becomes the measure of its anguish.

The pain of loss is felt in proportion to love, and so the saints, who loved God most, dreaded it most. Alphonsus records the saying of Ignatius of Loyola: “Lord, I am willing to bear every pain, but not the pain of being deprived of you.”[^21] Saint Francis de Sales sets the meditation on hell before the soul he guides toward devotion, and in the affections that close it he puts this loss to that soul as a question: “whether you can bear to lose your God for ever?”[^22] Chrysostom answered his own warning with a prayer, and his prayer may be ours: “But may this never be our case, O thou only-begotten Son of God, neither may we ever have any experience of this irremediable punishment.”[^23]

## The Fire That Is Not Extinguished

The loss is the chief pain, but it is not the only one. In the ninth chapter of Mark the Lord spoke of the hand, the foot, and the eye that cause a man to fall, and three times he closed the warning with the same words:

> And if thy hand scandalize thee, cut it off: it is better for thee to enter into life, maimed, than having two hands to go into hell, into unquenchable fire: Where their worm dieth not, and the fire is not extinguished. And if thy foot scandalize thee, cut it off: it is better for thee to enter lame into life everlasting than having two feet to be cast into the hell of unquenchable fire: Where their worm dieth not, and the fire is not extinguished. And if thy eye scandalize thee, pluck it out: it is better for thee with one eye to enter into the kingdom of God than having two eyes to be cast into the hell of fire: Where their worm dieth not, and the fire is not extinguished.[^24]

Augustine remarks on the repetition: “He did not shrink from using the same words three times over in one passage. And who is not terrified by this repetition, and by the threat of that punishment uttered so vehemently by the lips of the Lord Himself?”[^25] The Church has kept the word in her own teaching. The Creed names “everlasting fire”; the *Catechism*, quoting the councils, names “eternal fire”; and Paul VI ended his profession of faith with “the fire that is not extinguished.”

### A real fire

Augustine allowed a choice about the worm in Christ’s refrain, but not about the fire. Some, he reports, refer both the fire and the worm to the spirit; others take the fire as bodily and the worm as the gnawing grief of the soul. He himself found it “easier to understand both as referring to the body than to suppose that neither does,” and he let his reader choose concerning the worm: “Let each man, then, choose which he will, whether he thinks that the worm is real and pertains to the body, or that spiritual things are meant by bodily representations, and that it belongs to the soul.” But of the fire and the bodies of the damned he left no choice at all: “only, this we believe about those future bodies, that they shall be such as shall certainly be pained by the fire.”[^26] In the next chapter he goes further. The fire prepared for the devil and his angels is one fire, and it will torment both men and demons, the bodies of the one and the spirits of the other: “But that hell, which also is called a lake of fire and brimstone, will be material fire, and will torment the bodies of the damned, whether men or devils… One fire certainly shall be the lot of both, for thus the truth has declared.”[^27]

Saint Gregory the Great taught the same in the fourth book of his *Dialogues*, which he gave to the life of the soul after death. When his deacon Peter wonders how a bodily fire can hold a soul that has no body, Gregory answers that a spirit is held in the living body which it quickens, and so may be held in the fire in which it continually dies; and he adds that the soul suffers by seeing the fire and also by feeling it, “for the soul by seeing of the fire is afflicted, and burned it is, in that it seeth itself to be burned.” He appeals to the rich man of the parable, who begged for a drop of water “because I am tormented in this flame.”[^28] And when Peter asks whether there is one fire or many, Gregory gives the answer that later teachers repeat:

> The fire of hell is but one: yet doth it not in one manner torment all sinners. For every one there, according to the quantity of his sin, hath the measure of his pain. For as, in this world, many live under one and the same sun, and yet do not alike feel the heat thereof: for some be burnt more, and some less: so in that one fire, divers manners of burning be found, for that which in this world diversity of bodies doth, that in the next doth diversity of sins: so that although the fire be there all alike, yet doth it not in one manner and alike burn and torment them that be damned.[^29]

Gregory is describing the unequal punishments that the Second Council of Lyons would later profess.

The schoolmen kept this teaching. The Supplement to the *Summa*, reviewing the opinions about the fire, concludes that “whatever we may say of the fire that torments the separated souls, we must admit that the fire which will torment the bodies of the damned after the resurrection is corporeal, since one cannot fittingly apply a punishment to a body unless that punishment itself be bodily.” It holds, moreover, “that the fire of hell is of the same species as the fire we have, so far as the nature of fire is concerned,” while having “certain properties differing from our fire, for instance that it needs no kindling, nor is kept alive by fuel.”[^30] Saint John Damascene, as the Supplement cites him, had written that the ungodly will be cast into everlasting fire, “not material fire, such as that which we have, but such as God knoweth”; the Supplement answers that he “does not absolutely deny that this fire is material, but that it is material as our fire, since it differs from ours in some of its properties.”[^31] Long before, the Roman apologist Minucius Felix had described that difference: “Nor is there either measure or termination to these torments. There the intelligent fire burns the limbs and restores them, feeds on them and nourishes them.”[^32]

The Fathers and Doctors commonly hold, then, that it is a true fire, acting on the soul by the power of God and on the risen body as fire acts on a body, and unlike ours in that it burns without consuming and needs no fuel.

### The meditation of the fifth exercise

The saints also set the fire before the eyes of the soul in prayer, deliberately and in order. The best-known form is the fifth exercise of the first week of the *Spiritual Exercises* of Saint Ignatius, which its author calls simply “a meditation on hell.” It is short enough to give whole:

> <span class="smallcaps">First Prelude.</span> The first Prelude is the composition, which is here to see with the sight of the imagination the length, breadth and depth of Hell.
>
> <span class="smallcaps">Second Prelude.</span> The second, to ask for what I want: it will be here to ask for interior sense of the pain which the damned suffer, in order that, if, through my faults, I should forget the love of the Eternal Lord, at least the fear of the pains may help me not to come into sin.
>
> <span class="smallcaps">First Point.</span> The first Point will be to see with the sight of the imagination the great fires, and the souls as in bodies of fire.
>
> <span class="smallcaps">Second Point.</span> The second, to hear with the ears wailings, howlings, cries, blasphemies against Christ our Lord and against all His Saints.
>
> <span class="smallcaps">Third Point.</span> The third, to smell with the smell smoke, sulphur, dregs and putrid things.
>
> <span class="smallcaps">Fourth Point.</span> The fourth, to taste with the taste bitter things, like tears, sadness and the worm of conscience.
>
> <span class="smallcaps">Fifth Point.</span> The fifth, to touch with the touch; that is to say, how the fires touch and burn the souls.[^33]

Each point serves the grace asked in the second prelude, that if love should fail, fear at least may keep the soul from sin. The meditation ends in a colloquy with Christ, in which the soul recalls those who are lost, “some because they did not believe the Coming, others because, believing, they did not act according to His Commandments,” and then turns to give thanks: “with this I will give Him thanks that He has not let me fall into any of these divisions, ending my life. Likewise, I will consider how up to now He has always had so great pity and mercy on me. I will end with an <span class="smallcaps">Our Father</span>.”[^34]

Francis de Sales gives the same meditation to beginners in the devout life, and he composes the place in a single image: “Picture to yourself a dark city, reeking with the flames of sulphur and brimstone, inhabited by citizens who cannot get forth.” The lost, he says, suffer “in every sense and every member; and that because having used their members and senses for sin, it is just that through them they should suffer now.”[^35] Alphonsus walks through the same senses and comes at last to the touch, the sense that the fire torments most: “They shall have an abyss of fire below, an abyss of fire above, and an abyss of fire on every side.”[^36]

Such meditations are best made slowly and ended, as Ignatius ends them, with thanks. Whoever has looked at the fire honestly knows that his own strength did not keep him from it. The pity and mercy of Christ have kept him “up to now,” and he must go on asking for them.

## The Worm and the Darkness

Christ’s refrain names two things that do not end, the fire and the worm, and it begins with the worm: “Where their worm dieth not.” The words come from the last verse of Isaias, where the prophet sees those who have transgressed against the Lord, “their worm shall not die, and their fire shall not be quenched,”[^37] and the Lord repeats them. Where the fire comes upon the lost from without, the worm is a pain born within them.

### The remorse that does not repent

The common teaching of the Doctors reads the worm as the remorse of conscience. The Supplement to the *Summa* puts it this way: the worm ascribed to the damned “must be understood to be not of a corporeal but of a spiritual nature: and this is the remorse of conscience, which is called a worm because it originates from the corruption of sin, and torments the soul, as a corporeal worm born of corruption torments by gnawing.”[^38] Augustine let each reader choose whether the worm be bodily or spiritual; but either way the soul is gnawed, for “it is necessarily understood that in a body thus tormented the soul also is tortured with a fruitless repentance.”[^39]

The lost see what they have done, and they grieve, but their grief can no longer bring them back. The fifth chapter of Wisdom gives their words, where the wicked behold the just whom they despised:

> Therefore we have erred from the way of truth, and the light of justice hath not shined unto us, and the sun of understanding hath not risen upon us. We wearied ourselves in the way of iniquity and destruction, and have walked through hard ways, but the way of the Lord we have not known. What hath pride profited us? or what advantage hath the boasting of riches brought us? All those things are passed away like a shadow… Such things as these the sinners said in hell.[^40]

Saint Cyprian, the martyr-bishop of Carthage, set this very passage before Demetrianus, a pagan who blamed the Christians for the plagues of the age, and drew this conclusion from it: “The pain of punishment will then be without the fruit of penitence; weeping will be useless, and prayer ineffectual. Too late they will believe in eternal punishment who would not believe in eternal life.”[^41]

Saint Gregory Nazianzen names the shame of conscience together with the loss of God. At the judgment, he says, some will be welcomed into the vision of the Trinity; “the others among other torments, but above and before them all must endure the being outcast from God, and the shame of conscience which has no limit.”[^42] Saint Catherine’s *Dialogue* also puts the loss first and the worm second: the first pain, the loss of the vision of God, “revives in them, then, the second, the worm of Conscience, which gnaws unceasingly, seeing that the soul is deprived of Me.”[^43] The soul that sees itself deprived of God knows also that its own act shut it out.

Saint Bernard, writing to Pope Eugenius on the things a man should consider, found the worm in memory.

> This is ‘the worm that dieth not’—the memory of the past. Once it gets within, or rather is born within through sin, there it stays, and never by any means can be plucked out. It never ceases to gnaw the conscience; feeding on it as on food that never can be consumed it prolongs the life of misery. I shudder as I contemplate this biting worm, this never-dying death. I shudder at the thought of being the victim of this living death, this dying life.
>
> This is the second death, which never kills, but is always killing.[^44]

And he gave the reason why memory cannot release the soul: “These former things have passed away, and have not passed away. They are out of hand, but not out of mind. What has been done cannot be undone; the doing was in time, but the effect of the doing will be eternal.”[^45]

Alphonsus, who gave a whole consideration to the remorse of the damned, names the three thoughts that will gnaw most: the trifles for which they were lost, the little that would have saved them, and the great good they have thrown away. The second is the sharpest because it is so ordinary, and he gives it in the lost soul’s own words:

> If I mortified myself by not looking at such an object; if I overcame human respect at such a time; if I avoided such an occasion of sin, such a companion, such a conversation, I should not now be damned. If I had gone to confession every week; if I had frequented the congregation; if I had read every day a spiritual book; if I had recommended myself to Jesus Christ and to Mary, I should not have relapsed into sin. I have so often purposed to do these things, but have either neglected my resolutions, or, after having begun to practise these exercises, I gave them up, and therefore I am lost.[^46]

### The exterior darkness

Three times in Matthew the Lord says that the lost are cast “into the exterior darkness,” where there is weeping and gnashing of teeth: the children of the kingdom who did not believe, the guest without a wedding garment, the servant who buried his talent.[^47] Job, whose lament the Church reads in the Office of the Dead, speaks of hell as a house of darkness: “If I wait hell is my house, and I have made my bed in darkness.”[^48] And on All Souls’ Day the Church prays at the offertory of the Requiem that the souls of the faithful departed may be delivered “from the lion’s mouth, that hell engulf them not, nor they fall into darkness.”[^49]

Gregory the Great, commenting on Job’s “land of misery and darkness, where the shadow of death, and no order, but everlasting horror dwelleth,” saw a fire that burns without giving comfort. “Here the flame that burns gives light; There, as we have shewn by the words of the Psalmist, the fire that torments veils the light.” And he found in that place a death that cannot finish dying:

> Therefore there is done upon the wretches death without death, an end without ending, failing without failing; in that both death lives, and the end is ever beginning, and the failing is unable to fail.[^50]

He adds that the fire, which gives no light for comfort, gives just enough light for torment, that the lost may see those whom they loved against God suffering beside them. The Supplement, which cites him, concludes that in hell “the place must be so disposed for seeing as regards light and darkness, that nothing be seen clearly, and that only such things be dimly seen as are able to bring anguish to the heart. Wherefore, simply speaking, the place is dark.”[^51]

### The worm now

Remorse gnaws in this life too, and here it is a mercy, because on this side of death it can be the beginning of contrition. The saints prayed to keep it. Alphonsus, having made the lost soul speak, turns at once to his own: “My God, I feel a great remorse for having offended you; but this pain consoles me… because it encourages me to hope for pardon from you, who have promised to pardon all who repent. Yes, my Lord, I repent of having outraged you. I embrace this sweet pain of remorse.”[^52]

The two kinds of remorse differ in direction. Contrition looks back at sin and turns toward Christ, as Peter did when he wept bitterly and was restored. The worm that does not die is the remorse of a soul that has refused, to the end, to let its grief become a prayer. The examination of conscience each night, the confession that names sins plainly, and the resolution made again after each failure turn remorse into contrition while there is still time.

## For Ever and Ever

Every pain we know has an end. The worst illness breaks or kills; the longest grief is softened by years; even a life sentence is measured by a life. The last word of Christ’s sentence removes every such measure. The judgment scene closes: “And these shall go into everlasting punishment: but the just, into life everlasting.”[^53] On the last Sunday of the year the 1962 Missal reads the Lord’s saying about his own words: “Heaven and earth shall pass: but my words shall not pass.”[^54] The sentence of the Judge is among those words.

Augustine argues from the verse itself that the punishment lasts as long as the life:

> Then what a fond fancy is it to suppose that eternal punishment means long-continued punishment, while eternal life means life without end, since Christ in the very same passage spoke of both in similar terms in one and the same sentence, “These shall go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into life eternal!” If both destinies are “eternal,” then we must either understand both as long-continued but at last terminating, or both as endless. For they are correlative,—on the one hand, punishment eternal, on the other hand, life eternal. And to say in one and the same sense, life eternal shall be endless, punishment eternal shall come to an end, is the height of absurdity. Wherefore, as the eternal life of the saints shall be endless, so too the eternal punishment of those who are doomed to it shall have no end.[^55]

Other texts of the New Testament also speak of a punishment without end. The Lord says of the sin against the Holy Ghost that the one who commits it “shall never have forgiveness, but shall be guilty of an everlasting sin.”[^56] He says that it is better to enter life maimed “than having two hands or two feet, to be cast into everlasting fire.”[^57] He says of his betrayer, “It were better for him, if that man had not been born”;[^58] and of no one whose loss would end at last in glory could it be better never to have been born. Saint Paul says that those who know not God and obey not the gospel “shall suffer eternal punishment in destruction, from the face of the Lord and from the glory of his power.”[^59] And the Apocalypse, which closes the Scriptures, sees the devil “cast into the pool of fire and brimstone, where both the beast and the false prophet shall be tormented day and night for ever and ever,” and the smoke of the torments of those who worshipped the beast ascending “for ever and ever: neither have they rest day nor night.”[^60]

Gregory the Great answered those who said the Lord threatened eternal pain only to frighten men from sin. A threat that God did not mean would make a liar of him, and his promises would be no surer than his threats:

> Certain it is, and without all doubt most true, that as the good shall have no end of their joys, so the wicked never any release of their torments… Seeing, then, true it is, that which he hath promised to his friends: out of all question false it cannot be, that which he hath threatened to his enemies… For if he threatened that which he meant not to put into execution: whiles we are desirous to make him merciful, enforced we are likewise (which is horrible to speak) to affirm him to be deceitful.[^61]

When the opinion of a final restoration of the demons and the wicked, associated with the name of Origen and found also in a chapter of Gregory of Nyssa’s *Great Catechism*,[^62] was condemned at Constantinople in 543, the ninth anathema struck at anyone who “says or thinks that the punishment of demons and of impious men is only temporary, and will one day have an end.”[^63] The Fourth Lateran Council professed the punishment *perpetuam*, and the *Catechism* speaks of separation from God “for ever.”

### Why it cannot end

The Fathers and Doctors give two reasons why a sin committed in a moment is punished without end.

The first looks at the will of the sinner. Gregory says that the wicked made an end of sinning only because they made an end of living: “for willingly they would, had it been in their power, have lived without end, that they might in like manner have sinned without end.”[^64] The soul that dies in mortal sin has fixed its last end in something other than God. It has chosen, and death seals the choice. Aquinas takes up Gregory’s words and explains them: “A man is said to have sinned in his own eternity, not only as regards continual sinning throughout his whole life, but also because, from the very fact that he fixes his end in sin, he has the will to sin, everlastingly.”[^65]

The second looks at the wound itself. In the same article Aquinas compares mortal sin to the destruction of an eye:

> Now disturbance of an order is sometimes reparable, sometimes irreparable: because a defect which destroys the principle is irreparable, whereas if the principle be saved, defects can be repaired by virtue of that principle. For instance, if the principle of sight be destroyed, sight cannot be restored except by Divine power; whereas, if the principle of sight be preserved, while there arise certain impediments to the use of sight, these can be remedied by nature or by art… Consequently if a sin destroys the principle of the order whereby man’s will is subject to God, the disorder will be such as to be considered in itself, irreparable, although it is possible to repair it by the power of God. Now the principle of this order is the last end, to which man adheres by charity. Therefore whatever sins turn man away from God, so as to destroy charity, considered in themselves, incur a debt of eternal punishment.[^66]

Mortal sin kills charity, the principle by which the soul would return, and so the wound can be healed only by the power of God. In this life God offers that power in the grace of conversion and the sacrament of penance, and death closes the offer. The ancient homily known as the Second Letter of Clement teaches with the image of the potter that repentance belongs to this life:

> For as the potter, if he make a vessel, and it be distorted or broken in his hands, fashions it over again; but if he have before this cast it into the furnace of fire, can no longer find any help for it: so let us also, while we are in this world, repent with our whole heart of the evil deeds we have done in the flesh, that we may be saved by the Lord, while we have yet an opportunity of repentance. For after we have gone out of the world, no further power of confessing or repenting will there belong to us.[^67]

Gregory Nazianzen describes the judgment that follows, “after which there is no appeal, no higher court, no defence on the ground of subsequent conduct, no oil obtained from the wise virgins, or from them that sell, for the lamps going out, no repentance of the rich man wasting away in the flame.”[^68]

### The weight of eternity

Alphonsus gave his twenty-seventh consideration to this alone, and he began it bluntly: “WERE Hell not eternal, it should not be Hell.” He insists that it is certain: “This eternity is of faith: it is not an opinion, but a truth attested by God in so many places in the holy Scriptures.”[^69] Then he set the mind to work on it. “In Hell there is a gate for admission, but none for egress.” The damned feel not only each moment’s pain, but in each moment the whole of what is to come: “Hence the damned not only suffer the torments of each moment, but in each moment they endure the pain of eternity, saying: What I now suffer, I must suffer for ever.”[^70]

The *Imitation of Christ* measures it against our penances: “An hour’s pain there shall be more grievous than a hundred years here of the bitterest penitence. No quiet shall be there, no comfort for the lost, though here sometimes there is respite from pain, and enjoyment of the solace of friends.”[^71] And the Church, keeping vigil for her dead in the Office of the Dead, gives the soul words for its fear. After the seventh lesson of Matins she sings *Peccantem me quotidie, et non me poenitentem, timor mortis conturbat me: Quia in inferno nulla est redemptio, miserere mei, Deus, et salva me*.[^72] It is the voice of one who sins every day and does not repent, whom the fear of death troubles because in hell there is no redemption, and who therefore cries to God for mercy and salvation while the cry can still be heard.

The saints used the thought of eternity to spend their time well. Alphonsus taught his readers to pray, in words he gives as Augustine’s, “Here burn, here cut, here spare not, that you may spare for eternity.”[^73] And Augustine himself, in the midst of his argument for the endlessness of punishment, stopped to draw the conclusion for the living: “they who desire to be rid of eternal punishment ought to abstain from arguing against God, and rather, while yet there is opportunity, obey the divine commands.”[^74] Saint Paul names the time: “Behold, now is the acceptable time: behold, now is the day of salvation.”[^75]

## With Their Own Bodies

Hell is not only the state of a separated soul. The Athanasian Creed says that at Christ’s coming “all men shall rise again with their bodies,”[^76] and the Fourth Lateran Council professed that all will rise with their own bodies which they now bear, *cum suis propriis resurgent corporibus, quae nunc gestant*, to receive according to their works.[^77] The resurrection is a gift of Christ’s victory, and it is given to all; but for those who have refused him it becomes the occasion of a fuller loss, because the body that sinned with the soul rises to share its punishment.

The Fathers taught this from the beginning. Saint Justin Martyr told the Roman emperor that Christ will judge the wicked “in the same bodies united again to their spirits which are now to undergo everlasting punishment; and not only, as Plato said, for a period of a thousand years.”[^78] Cyprian wrote: “Souls with their bodies will be reserved in infinite tortures for suffering.”[^79] And Saint Cyril of Jerusalem, preparing catechumens for baptism, set the two resurrections side by side and gave the reason for the difference:

> We shall be raised therefore, all with our bodies eternal, but not all with bodies alike: for if a man is righteous, he will receive a heavenly body, that he may be able worthily to hold converse with Angels; but if a man is a sinner, he shall receive an eternal body, fitted to endure the penalties of sins, that he may burn eternally in fire, nor ever be consumed. And righteously will God assign this portion to either company; for we do nothing without the body. We blaspheme with the mouth, and with the mouth we pray. With the body we commit fornication, and with the body we keep chastity. With the hand we rob, and by the hand we bestow alms; and the rest in like manner. Since then the body has been our minister in all things, it shall also share with us in the future the fruits of the past.[^80]

This is why Augustine and the Supplement, whatever they allowed about the worm, held the fire that torments the risen flesh to be bodily.

The doctrine is a consolation as well as a warning, because it means that the body matters. The hand, the eye, the tongue and the flesh are parts of a man that will stand with him before God, and the mouth that prays and the hands that give alms will rise with him. Cyril draws the lesson at once: “Therefore, brethren, let us be careful of our bodies, nor misuse them as though not our own.”[^81]

It also explains the strange severity of Christ’s counsel to cut off the hand and pluck out the eye. Augustine reads the members as “those persons whom a man loves as the most useful members of his body”;[^82] the Lord commands a man to treat whatever leads him into sin as he would treat a limb that threatened his life. The occasion that seems as dear as an eye—the friendship, the habit, the pleasure, the place—is to be cut away, because the whole body is at stake, and the whole soul with it. “And fear ye not them that kill the body, and are not able to kill the soul: but rather fear him that can destroy both soul and body in hell.”[^83] Fasting, watchfulness and the avoidance of occasions keep the body from sin now, and so keep it for the resurrection of life.

## The Fire Which Burneth for an Hour

Among the first Christians to write of hell after the apostles were a bishop on the road to the beasts and an old man before the proconsul’s fire. They spoke of the eternal fire because it set every other fear in its place.

When Saint Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, stood before the proconsul, he would not curse Christ: “Eighty and six years have I served Him, and He never did me any injury: how then can I blaspheme my King and my Saviour?”[^84] He was threatened first with wild beasts and then with burning, and the Church of Smyrna recorded his answer:

> Thou threatenest me with fire which burneth for an hour, and after a little is extinguished, but art ignorant of the fire of the coming judgment and of eternal punishment, reserved for the ungodly. But why tarriest thou? Bring forth what thou wilt.[^85]

The same letter says of the martyrs who had gone before him that they “despised all the torments of this world, redeeming themselves from eternal punishment by \[the suffering of\] a single hour. For this reason the fire of their savage executioners appeared cool to them. For they kept before their view escape from that fire which is eternal and never shall be quenched.”[^86]

Saint Ignatius of Antioch, writing to the Ephesians on his way to die at Rome, turned the same fear against the corruption of the faith. “Do not err, my brethren. Those that corrupt families shall not inherit the kingdom of God. If, then, those who do this as respects the flesh have suffered death, how much more shall this be the case with any one who corrupts by wicked doctrine the faith of God, for which Jesus Christ was crucified! Such an one becoming defiled \[in this way\], shall go away into everlasting fire, and so shall every one that hearkens unto him.”[^87] The Second Letter of Clement applied Isaias’s verse to those who do not keep the seal of their baptism: “For of those who do not preserve the seal \[unbroken\], \[the Scripture\] says, ‘Their worm shall not die, and their fire shall not be quenched, and they shall be a spectacle to all flesh.’ ”[^88]

Already in the second century the pagans mocked the teaching as a scare devised to keep men in line, and Saint Justin answered them. “And that no one may say what is said by those who are deemed philosophers, that our assertions that the wicked are punished in eternal fire are big words and bugbears, and that we wish men to live virtuously through fear, and not because such a life is good and pleasant; I will briefly reply to this, that if this be not so, God does not exist; or, if He exists, He cares not for men, and neither virtue nor vice is anything.”[^89]

Tertullian made the thought of hell a spur to confession. Writing on repentance, he spoke to the Christian who shrinks from the humiliation of confessing his sins:

> If you shrink back from exomologesis, consider in your heart the hell, which exomologesis will extinguish for you; and imagine first the magnitude of the penalty, that you may not hesitate about the adoption of the remedy.[^90]

He goes on: “Why do you desert your own salvation? Why are you tardy to approach what you know heals you?”[^91] Tertullian asks the penitent to weigh the shame of confession against hell, as Polycarp weighed the proconsul’s fire against the eternal one.

The martyrs keep the order of fears that Christ himself gave, when he told his disciples not to fear those who kill the body, but him who can destroy both soul and body in hell. Most of what Christians fear is the fire that burns for an hour: loss of money, of reputation, of comfort, of friends, the mockery of the world, the discomfort of a hard duty. The martyrs feared hell more than all these, and so no threat of the world could move them.

## The Narrow Gate

Our Lord was asked once how many would be saved, and his answer is recorded by Saint Luke. “And a certain man said to him: Lord, are they few that are saved? But he said to them: Strive to enter by the narrow gate: for many, I say to you, shall seek to enter and shall not be able. But when the master of the house shall be gone in and shall shut the door, you shall begin to stand without; and knock at the door, saying: Lord, open to us. And he answering, shall say to you: I know you not, whence you are.”[^92] In the Sermon on the Mount he had said the same without being asked:

> Enter ye in at the narrow gate: for wide is the gate, and broad is the way that leadeth to destruction, and many there are who go in thereat. How narrow is the gate, and strait is the way that leadeth to life: and few there are that find it![^93]

And at the end of the parable of the wedding feast, after the guest without a garment has been cast into the exterior darkness, he closed with this sentence: “For many are called, but few are chosen.”[^94]

### How the Fathers and Doctors read it

Chrysostom, Augustine, Gregory, Thomas and Alphonsus take these words as they stand: the way to life is narrow, and those who walk in it are fewer than those who do not. Chrysostom, preaching on the narrow gate, told his people that in saying, “Few there be that find it,” the Lord exposed the carelessness of most men and “instructed His hearers not to regard the felicities of the many, but the labors of the few. For the more part, saith He, so far from walking this way, do not so much as make it their choice.”[^95] Later, preaching on the Acts of the Apostles, he warned his people not to trust in their numbers: “Many more in number are they who go down into hell: but greater than it is the Kingdom, however few it contain.” Then he asked how many in his city were likely to be saved, and answered his own question: “It is disagreeable, what I am going to say, but I will say it nevertheless. Among all these myriads, there are not to be found one hundred likely to be saved: nay, even as to these, I question it.”[^96]

Augustine, reflecting on the mass of mankind wounded by the first sin and on the mercy that draws some out of it, wrote that “many more are left under punishment than are delivered from it, in order that it may thus be shown what was due to all.”[^97] Gregory the Great, preaching on the labourers in the vineyard, stopped at the words *Multi enim sunt vocati, pauci vero electi* and turned them on his own congregation: many come to faith, he said, and few are brought to the heavenly kingdom; and looking at the walls of the church filled for the feast, he asked who could know how few of all that crowd were numbered in the flock of God’s elect, *quis sciat quam pauci sunt qui in illo electorum Dei grege numerentur?*[^98] In another homily, on the wedding garment, he drew a lesson for every Christian, *Quia vocati sumus, novimus; si sumus electi, nescimus*: each of us knows that he has been called, and none knows whether he is chosen.[^99]

Saint Thomas, in the First Part of the *Summa*, gives his reason. What exceeds the common state of nature is found in the few, as profound learning is found in the few; and eternal life with God exceeds all nature, the more so in a nature wounded by sin. Therefore “those who are saved are in the minority. In this especially, however, appears the mercy of God, that He has chosen some for that salvation, from which very many in accordance with the common course and tendency of nature fall short.”[^100] And Alphonsus, warning those who sin and count on God’s patience, states it as the common opinion of his own day: “Were God to bear for ever with sinners, no one should be damned: but the most common opinion is, that the greater part of adults, even among Christians, are lost.”[^101]

### The number known to God

The saints who spoke these hard words spoke them as a warning to each of their hearers. Chrysostom spoke so that his hearers would “not regard the many, nor be troubled thereat, but emulate the few.”[^102] Gregory spoke so that each might fear for himself, not knowing whether he was chosen. And Aquinas, in the very article in which he says the saved are the minority, will not name a number. He records the guesses of others—that as many men will be saved as angels fell, or as many as remained—and sets them aside: “It is, however, better to say that, ‘to God alone is known the number for whom is reserved eternal happiness.’ ”[^103] The words he quotes are the Church’s own, from the Roman Missal’s prayer for the living and the dead: *Deus, cui soli cognitus est numerus electorum in superna felicitate locandus*, “God, to Whom alone is known the number of the elect who shall attain the happiness of heaven.”[^104]

The Church has defined no number. She prays that her children may pass through the narrow gate, and she directs the fear the Fathers taught toward the possibility of one’s own loss rather than toward the multitude of the lost.

### The son of perdition

One man the Lord singled out. At the Last Supper he said of his betrayer, “woe to that man by whom the Son of man shall be betrayed. It were better for him, if that man had not been born.”[^105] In his prayer to the Father that same night he said, “Those whom thou gavest me have I kept: and none of them is lost, but the son of perdition: that the scripture may be fulfilled.”[^106] And the Church, at the Mass of Holy Thursday and again on Good Friday, prays in words that have stood in her Missal for centuries:

> *Deus, a quo et Judas reatus sui poenam, et confessionis suae latro praemium sumpsit, concede nobis tuae propitiationis effectum: ut, sicut in passione sua Jesus Christus, Dominus noster, diversa utrisque intulit stipendia meritorum; ita nobis, ablato vetustatis errore, resurrectionis suae gratiam largiatur.*
>
> O God, from Whom Judas received the punishment of his crime and the thief the reward of his confession, grant us the effect of Thy clemency, that, as Jesus Christ, our Lord, in His passion dealt according to their deserts with the one and the other, so, putting away from us the error of the past, He may bestow upon us the grace of His resurrection.[^107]

The collect names two men. Both were sinners, and both stood near Christ in his passion. One received the reward of his confession, “Lord, remember me when thou shalt come into thy kingdom,” and heard, “This day thou shalt be with me in paradise.”[^108] The other received the punishment of his crime. Augustine saw where the difference lay. Judas too repented after his fashion: “I have sinned in betraying innocent blood.”[^109] But “by despairing of God’s mercy in his sorrow that wrought death, he left to himself no place for a healing penitence.”[^110] Peter denied his Lord three times on the same night; then he remembered the word of Jesus, “And going forth, he wept bitterly,”[^111] and he was forgiven.

### Judgment belongs to God

The Church raises her saints to the altars, declaring that they reign with Christ, but beyond what God has revealed she pronounces no sentence of damnation on any soul. “Although we can judge that an act is in itself a grave offense,” the *Catechism* teaches, “we must entrust judgment of persons to the justice and mercy of God.”[^112] John Paul II said the same of hell itself: “Damnation remains a real possibility, but it is not granted to us, without special divine revelation, to know which human beings are effectively involved in it.”[^113] The warning of the narrow gate is given so that each of us may strive to enter, and pray for all who are still on the way.

## The Saints Who Saw

The Church has received, among the testimonies of her saints, two accounts of hell that have shaped the prayer of many Christians: the vision of Saint Teresa of Jesus, which she recorded in the thirty-second chapter of her *Life*, and the vision shown to three children at Fatima on 13 July 1917. Both are private revelations, which the Church does not propose for divine faith but permits the faithful to receive with prudence, as a help to the faith she teaches.[^114] Teresa’s account is the testimony of a canonized saint and Doctor of the Church; the visions of Fatima were declared worthy of belief by the Bishop of Leiria in 1930, and the Holy See published Sister Lúcia’s account of them in 2000.[^115] Each tells what it was to feel the pains the Fathers describe, and what came of it.

### Teresa

Teresa was already advanced in prayer when it happened. “I was one day in prayer when I found myself in a moment, without knowing how, plunged apparently into hell. I understood that it was our Lord’s will I should see the place which the devils kept in readiness for me, and which I had deserved by my sins. It was but a moment, but it seems to me impossible I should ever forget it even if I were to live many years.”[^116] She describes a narrow, dark, foul passage, and a hole in the wall in which she found herself confined. Then she turns from what she saw to what she felt:

> These sufferings were nothing in comparison with the anguish of my soul, a sense of oppression, of stifling, and of pain so keen, accompanied by so hopeless and cruel an infliction, that I know not how to speak of it. If I said that the soul is continually being torn from the body, it would be nothing, for that implies the destruction of life by the hands of another; but here it is the soul itself that is tearing itself in pieces. I cannot describe that inward fire or that despair, surpassing all torments and all pain… There was no light, but all was thick darkness. I do not understand how it is; though there was no light, yet everything that can give pain by being seen was visible.[^117]

The darkness in which whatever gives pain is still visible is the darkness Gregory and the Supplement describe. Teresa, who had read of hell in books and meditated on its pains, found her reading surpassed: “the one is a reality, the other a picture; and all burning here in this life is as nothing in comparison with the fire that is there.”[^118]

She understood the vision as a mercy: “I understood distinctly that it was a great mercy that our Lord would have me see with mine own eyes the very place from which His compassion saved me.”[^119] It freed her from every lesser fear, so that “everything seems endurable in comparison with one instant of suffering such as those I had then to bear in hell.”[^120] And it turned her whole heart outward, toward others in danger:

> It was that vision that filled me with the very great distress which I feel at the sight of so many lost souls… and also gave me the most vehement desires for the salvation of souls; for certainly I believe that, to save even one from those overwhelming torments, I would most willingly endure many deaths.[^121]

She drew the practical conclusion for every Christian: “Let us, then, for the love of God, avoid all occasions of sin, and our Lord will help us, as He has helped me.”[^122] In the same chapter she passes straight from the vision to the longing for penance that it left in her, and from that longing to the foundation of the monastery of Saint Joseph at Avila, the first house of her reform.[^123]

### Fatima

Sister Lúcia, the surviving witness, wrote down the first part of the secret of 13 July 1917 in her third memoir. “Our Lady showed us a great sea of fire which seemed to be under the earth,” she wrote, and she told how the vision filled them with fear. Then the Mother of God spoke to them, “so kindly and so sadly”:

> You have seen hell where the souls of poor sinners go. To save them, God wishes to establish in the world devotion to my Immaculate Heart. If what I say to you is done, many souls will be saved and there will be peace.[^124]

The vision lasted, Lúcia says, but an instant. Our Lady showed the children where sinners go so that they might help them not to go there. In the third part of the same secret the angel cries out three times, “Penance, Penance, Penance!”[^125]

### The fruit of seeing

Both visions, centuries apart, turned those who saw them to intercession for sinners. Gregory the Great had taught that the saints pray for sinners “at such time as their hearts may be turned to fruitful penance, and so be saved,”[^126] and that time is now, while those for whom we pray are still on the way. Teresa would have died many deaths to save one soul. For those around us who have given up the faith or live in mortal sin, the least we can do is pray, offer a sacrifice, and speak a word when charity and prudence allow.

## The Fear That Saves

“The fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom.”[^127] The saints who looked at hell did so because they believed that fear, rightly received, is a gift of God; and the Church has confirmed them in it. When it was taught in the sixteenth century that sorrow for sin born of the fear of hell only makes a man a hypocrite, the Council of Trent answered in its decree on penance. It taught that the imperfect contrition called attrition, conceived “either from the consideration of the turpitude of sin, or from the fear of hell and of punishment,” if it exclude the will to sin and hope for pardon,

> not only does not make a man a hypocrite, and a greater sinner, but that it is even a gift of God, and an impulse of the Holy Ghost—who does not indeed as yet dwell in the penitent, but only moves him—whereby the penitent being assisted prepares a way for himself unto justice.[^128]

It added an example from Scripture: “For, smitten profitably with this fear, the Ninevites, at the preaching of Jonas, did fearful penance and obtained mercy from the Lord.” And among its canons it condemned anyone who should say that the sorrow by which a man thinks over his life, “pondering on the grievousness, the multitude, the filthiness of his sins, the loss of eternal blessedness, and the eternal damnation which he has incurred, having therewith the purpose of a better life,” is not a true and profitable sorrow and does not prepare for grace.[^129]

Such fear is the work of the Holy Spirit in a soul not yet able to love as it should, moving it toward the sacrament in which love is restored. Thomas à Kempis writes: “Nevertheless it is a good thing, if love as yet cannot restrain thee from evil, that at least the fear of hell should hold thee back. But he who putteth aside the fear of God cannot long continue in good, but shall quickly fall into the snares of the devil.”[^130] It is the grace Ignatius teaches the retreatant to ask before the meditation on hell. Aquinas explains how the punishments of hell serve the living. The eternal punishments of the lost, he says, do not heal the lost; but they “are medicinal punishments for those who refrain from sin through the thought of those punishments.”[^131]

### The preacher’s duty

For that reason the Church’s pastors have never been free to keep silence about hell. Chrysostom, preaching on the fire in which every man’s work will be tried, told his people why he would not spare them. He knew, he said, that a chill came over them when they heard of these things; but he could not do otherwise:

> For this is God’s own command, continually to sound these things in your ears… and ordained as we have been unto the ministry of the word, we must give pain to our hearers, not willingly but on compulsion.

A little later he admits his own fear:

> The argument is irksome and pains the hearer: were it only by my own feelings, I know this. For indeed my heart is troubled and throbs; and the more I see the account of hell confirmed, the more do I tremble and shrink through fear. But it is necessary to say these things lest we fall into hell.[^132]

### Neither anxiety nor despair

John Paul II, who spoke of hell plainly as “a real possibility,” added at once how the thought of it should live in the heart: “The thought of hell—and even less the improper use of biblical images—must not create anxiety or despair, but is a necessary and healthy reminder of freedom within the proclamation that the risen Jesus has conquered Satan, giving us the Spirit of God who makes us cry ‘Abba, Father!’ ”[^133]

Gregory the Great’s deacon, having heard his master’s teaching on hell, asked: “What man is he, though never so holy, that, cometh to leave this mortal life, hath not just cause to fear the unspeakable sentence of damnation?”[^134] Paul himself commands such fear: “with fear and trembling work out your salvation,” and “he that thinketh himself to stand, let him take heed lest he fall.”[^135] Alphonsus gives the rule for its use in the hour of temptation, so that it drives the soul toward God:

> When the devil tempts you again to sin, remember Hell, have recourse to God and to the most holy Virgin. The thought of Hell shall preserve you from Hell, because it shall make you have recourse to God.[^136]

The fear that sends the soul to God is meant to end in love: “Fear is not in charity: but perfect charity casteth out fear,”[^137] and the *Imitation* adds that “he who loveth God with all his heart feareth not death, nor punishment, nor judgment, nor hell, because perfect love giveth sure access to God.”[^138] Until love is strong, the fear of hell keeps the soul from sin, and it gives way little by little as love grows.

## From Everlasting Death, Deliver Us

Hell is the work of a refusal, not of a decree that predestines. “God predestines no one to go to hell,” the *Catechism* teaches; “for this, a willful turning away from God (a mortal sin) is necessary, and persistence in it until the end.”[^139] The Second Council of Orange said it with unusual vehemence: that some have been predestined to evil by divine power, *non solum non credimus, sed etiam, si sunt qui tantum mali credere velint, cum omni detestatione illis anathema dicimus*—we not only do not believe it, but if there are any who wish to believe so great an evil, we say anathema to them with all detestation.[^140] The God who threatens hell is the God who swore by his own life that he does not desire it for anyone: “As I live, saith the Lord God, I desire not the death of the wicked, but that the wicked turn from his way, and live. Turn ye, turn ye from your evil ways: and why will you die, O house of Israel?”[^141] “The Lord… dealeth patiently for your sake, not willing that any should perish, but that all should return to penance.”[^142]

### The door that stands open

The saints spoke about hell to the living, for whom the door of mercy is still open. Cyprian, who had just set the eternal fire before the persecutor Demetrianus, turned in the next breath to offer him the remedy:

> Provide, therefore, while you may, for your safety and your life… Nor let any one be restrained either by his sins or by his years from coming to obtain salvation. To him who still remains in this world no repentance is too late. The approach to God’s mercy is open, and the access is easy to those who seek and apprehend the truth.[^143]

Christ himself promises: “All that the Father giveth to me shall come to me: and him that cometh to me, I will not cast out.”[^144] The thief on the cross came in the last hour and was received. Judas, as the Church’s prayer says, received the punishment of his crime; not because his sin was too great to be forgiven, but because he would not ask. No sin committed in this life is beyond the reach of the blood of Christ and the absolution of his Church. The only sin that cannot be forgiven is the one that refuses forgiveness to the end.[^145]

The saints name the way of escape plainly. It is confession: the humble telling of sins that Tertullian set against the fire, and that Alphonsus’s lost soul wished he had made every week. It is perseverance: the daily return to God, the avoidance of occasions, the prayer for the grace to persevere that Alphonsus places on the lips of his reader again and again, “obtain for me holy perseverance.”[^146] And it is prayer, the Church’s prayer above all, in which she has always asked for exactly this deliverance.

### The Church’s prayer

In the heart of the Roman Canon the priest extends his hands over the offering and prays *diesque nostros in tua pace disponas, atque ab æterna damnatione nos eripi, et in Electorum tuorum jubeas grege numerari*, in the words the *Catechism* gives: “Grant us your peace in this life, save us from final damnation, and count us among those you have chosen.”[^147] Bede records the tradition that it was Gregory the Great who added these words to the Canon: “he added three phrases full of great goodness and perfection: ‘And dispose our days in thy peace, and preserve us from eternal damnation, and rank us in the number of thy elect, through Christ our Lord.’ ”[^148] The pope whom Bede credits with this petition is the same who preached that many are called and few chosen, and who asked his people who could know how few of them were numbered in the flock of God’s elect.

On All Souls’ Day, when the Church offers the Holy Sacrifice for all her dead, she prays at the offertory, “O Lord Jesus Christ, King of glory, deliver the souls of all the faithful departed from the pains of hell and from the deep pit; deliver them from the lion’s mouth, that hell engulf them not, nor they fall into darkness, but let Michael, the holy standard-bearer, bring them into the holy light which Thou once didst promise to Abraham and his seed.”[^149] At the absolution of the dead she sings, “Deliver me, O Lord, from eternal death on that dreadful day when the heavens and the earth shall be moved, and Thou shalt come to judge the world by fire.”[^150] In the sequence of the Mass for the Dead the soul pleads before the Judge in its own name:

> *Confutatis maledictis,<br>
> Flammis acribus addictis,<br>
> Voca me cum benedictis.*
>
> While the wicked are confounded,<br>
> Doom’d to flames of woe unbounded,<br>
> Call me with Thy Saints surrounded.[^151]

And in the Litany of the Saints, which the Church sings on Holy Saturday, the invocations of the saints give way to the cry of the whole people: “From all evil,” and the answer, “O Lord, deliver us”; “From all sin”; “From everlasting death.”[^152]

The Church prays for this deliverance because the danger is real, and she prays with confidence because she prays to the Saviour of whom it is written, “For God sent not his Son into the world, to judge the world: but that the world may be saved by him.”[^153]

### Resolution

Francis de Sales ends his meditation on hell with a few simple acts, and the reader may end this consideration with them. First, confess before God that you have deserved it. Then, “Resolve henceforth to act differently, and to rescue yourself from this abyss. Resolve on distinct definite acts by which you may avoid sin, and thereby eternal death.” And last: “Give thanks, offer yourself, pray.”[^154]

The year is ending, and the Church will soon begin it again. On the first Sunday of Advent the 1962 Missal reads once more of the signs in the sun and moon and stars and of the Son of man coming with great power and majesty, and the Church hears him say, “when these things begin to come to pass, look up and lift up your heads, because your redemption is at hand.”[^155] The Judge who will say *Depart* to those on his left will say *Come* to those on his right, and the Church sets hell before her children so that they may hear the second word.

## Scope, Method, and Qualifications

##### Reader and purpose.

This is spiritual reading for Catholics at the close of the liturgical year, the third of four volumes on the last things. It sets out the Church’s faith concerning hell—its reality, its pains, its eternity, and the risen body’s share in it—through Scripture, the councils, the liturgy, and the Fathers, Doctors and saints, and turns that consideration toward contrition, confession, perseverance and prayer. Death, judgment and heaven, purgatory and prayer for the dead, and the particular and general judgment are treated in the companion volumes and appear here only where the subject requires.

##### Grades.

Each teaching is stated in the body at its degree in a single clause; the appendix *Teaching and Its Grades* gives the grade of each, using six terms: defined; Church teaching; common teaching of the Fathers and Doctors; theological opinion; pious tradition; approved private revelation. Where the Church leaves a question open, the appendix names the positions held.

##### Sources and translations.

Scripture is quoted from the Douay–Rheims (Challoner revision) in its Vulgate numbering, so that the refrain of Mark 9 stands at verses 43, 45 and 47. The Fathers are quoted from the nineteenth-century English translations of the *Ante-Nicene* and *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, the Dods translation of the *City of God*, the Library of the Fathers translation of Gregory’s *Morals*, and the 1608 English of Gregory’s *Dialogues* as re-edited in 1911, whose chapter numbers are used. The *Summa* and its Supplement are quoted in the translation of the Fathers of the English Dominican Province. Ignatius is quoted in Mullan’s translation of 1914, which prints no paragraph numbers, and is cited by week and exercise. Teresa is quoted in David Lewis’s translation; Alphonsus in the Dublin translation of 1843; Francis de Sales in the Rivingtons translation of 1876; Bernard in George Lewis’s translation of 1908; Catherine of Siena in Algar Thorold’s abridged edition of 1907; the *Imitation* in William Benham’s translation. Conciliar and papal Latin is quoted from the Denzinger numbering; Trent in English from Waterworth. Liturgical Latin is quoted from the *Rituale Romanum* of 1872, the 1962 Missal, and hand missals that print the Missal’s Latin; liturgical English from F. X. Lasance’s *New Roman Missal* (1945), the *Catechism*’s citation of the Roman Canon, and W. J. Irons’s *Dies irae*. Where no public-domain English was available (Gregory’s *Homilies on the Gospels*, the Office of the Dead), the Latin is given and its sense is paraphrased in the surrounding prose, not offered as a translation. The closing prayer is reproduced from its identified witness without alteration.

##### Rights.

Texts of the Holy See (the *Catechism*, the 1999 audience of John Paul II, Paul VI’s *Credo*, and the 2000 publication of the Fatima secret, including Sister Lúcia’s memoir in its official English) remain the property of their publishers and are quoted briefly with attribution. Scripture, liturgical texts, received prayers, and third-party translations are not relicensed by this publication.

##### Limits.

The doctrinal history of the eternity of punishment, the debates of the schools, and modern controversies lie outside its scope, and no judgment is made of the state of any person beyond what Scripture, the liturgy and the Fathers say of Judas. It is a work of study and devotion; it is not a catechism, does not replace the teaching of the Church’s pastors, and carries no ecclesiastical approval.
