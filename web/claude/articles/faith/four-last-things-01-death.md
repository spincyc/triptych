## Remember Thy Last End

November begins among the dead. On its first day the Church keeps the feast of all the saints, and on its second she remembers all the faithful departed; the Office of the Dead is said, and at its first lesson the Church takes the voice of Job and prays, *Parce mihi, Domine: nihil enim sunt dies mei*, “spare me, for my days are nothing.”[^1] The year is running out. On the Last Sunday after Pentecost, in the 1962 Missal, the Gospel of the Mass turns to the end of all things, to the abomination of desolation and the sign of the Son of man in heaven, and closes on the promise, “Heaven and earth shall pass: but my words shall not pass.”[^2] It is the season in which the Church sets before her children the four last things: death, judgment, hell, and heaven. The first of them is death, because it is the door to the other three, and because it is the one that every man will certainly pass.

The counsel that gave these things their name stands near the end of a chapter of practical wisdom in the book of Ecclesiasticus, and it is worth reading in the company the sage gave it. “A gift hath grace in the sight of all the living, and restrain not grace from the dead. Be not wanting in comforting them that weep, and walk with them that mourn. Be not slow to visit the sick: for by these things thou shalt be confirmed in love. In all thy works remember thy last end, and thou shalt never sin.”[^3] The three verses before it are works of mercy: kindness to the dead, comfort to the mourners, the visit to the sick. The fourth names what such works teach. The Latin says *memorare novissima tua*, “remember thy last things,” and from that plural the Church’s teachers drew the name *novissima*, the last things, for the realities that await every soul at the end of its course.[^4] So the remembrance of death is commended here at a bedside and a graveside. It is learned by keeping company with the dying and the bereaved, and it bears its fruit in a life that does not sin.

The Church writes the same counsel on the forehead. On Ash Wednesday, at the threshold of Lent, the priest puts blessed ashes on the heads of the clergy and people and says to each one, *Memento, homo, quia pulvis es, et in pulverem reverteris*: “Remember, O man, that thou art dust and unto dust thou shalt return.”[^5] The words are God’s own, spoken to Adam as he went out of paradise: “In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread till thou return to the earth out of which thou wast taken: for dust thou art, and into dust thou shalt return.”[^6] What the liturgy says once a year in Lent, the saints learned to say every day. Alphonsus Liguori opens his *Preparation for Death* on this verse, and his first consideration begins with it plainly: “Consider that you are dust, and that you shall return to dust. A day will come when you shall die, and rot in a grave.”[^7]

The promise attached to this remembrance is a large one: *thou shalt never sin*. The Fathers and the masters of the spiritual life did not take it for a figure of speech. They held that a man who truly keeps his death before him will order his life by it, and over the centuries they made the remembrance a daily discipline, a meditation for retreats, and at last a monthly exercise. The masters of the early modern centuries, Francis de Sales and Alphonsus among them, wrote for people in the world as well as for monks, and they taught the meditation in a fixed order: first the truth of death, as faith knows it; then its consideration, until the truth is felt; then the resolution it calls for, and the prayer in which the resolution is kept. The Church follows that order in her own year. She remembers the dead in November, prays the Requiem, watches through the last Sundays for the coming of her Lord, and opens Advent with the command to watch.

Death, then, is not proposed to the Christian as a riddle for the intellect or as a terror to be endured in silence. It is proposed as something to be remembered, prepared for, and in the end received from the hand of God. The Church has a great deal to say about it, because she has kept watch at deathbeds since the days of the Apostles, and her words are words of faith, of fear, and of a hope that does not deceive.

## The Debt of Nature

The Church says what death is in a single sentence: “In that ‘departure’ which is death the soul is separated from the body.”[^8] The sentence is short because the faith behind it is settled. The Council of Vienne defined that the rational soul is of itself and essentially the form of the human body, and the Fifth Lateran Council that this soul is immortal and is multiplied with the bodies into which it is infused.[^9] A man is therefore not a spirit lodged in a body, to be let out at the end like a bird from a cage. He is one living thing, soul and body together, and death undoes that unity. What dies is the man. What survives is his spiritual soul, still knowing and loving, which waits in its separation for the body God will give back to it on the last day. That the souls of the just, once purified, see God even before they take up their bodies again is the teaching of Benedict XII, expounded in *What Death Does Not Finish*.[^10]

Because body and soul belong together, their parting is violent, and the Fathers did not pretend otherwise. Augustine will not let the faithful call it gentle. “As regards bodily death, that is, the separation of the soul from the body, it is good unto none while it is being endured by those whom we say are in the article of death. For the very violence with which body and soul are wrenched asunder, which in the living had been conjoined and closely intertwined, brings with it a harsh experience, jarring horridly on nature so long as it continues…”[^11] Sometimes, he adds, the anguish is forestalled by a sudden stroke. However it comes, “when it is piously and faithfully borne, it increases the merit of patience, but does not make the name of punishment inapplicable.”[^12] Here is the first thing the Christian must know about death. Whatever grace may do with it, it is in itself a wrench, a tearing apart of what God joined, and to feel it so is no failure of faith.

### Natural, and the wages of sin

Why should there be such a tearing at all? The Church gives two answers and keeps both. “In a sense bodily death is natural, but for faith it is in fact ‘the wages of sin.’ ”[^13] It is natural because man is made of the dust, a body composed of elements that, left to themselves, come apart. It is the wages of sin because God did not leave man to himself. Scripture says it twice over: “For God made not death, neither hath he pleasure in the destruction of the living,” and “the wages of sin is death.”[^14] The Council of Trent defines that Adam, when he transgressed the commandment in paradise, lost the holiness and justice in which he had been set and incurred the death with which God had threatened him.[^15]

Thomas Aquinas joins the two answers with an image from Aristotle. Sin does not cause death in the way a blow causes a wound. It causes death as the man who pulls away a prop causes what was resting on it to fall: “thus it is stated in *Phys.* viii, text. 32, that ‘by displacing a pillar a man moves accidentally the stone resting thereon.’ In this way the sin of our first parent is the cause of death and all such like defects in human nature, in so far as by the sin of our first parent original justice was taken away.”[^16] The stone was always heavy. God’s gift held it up. When the gift was lost, the stone fell, and it falls still on every child of Adam. The doctrine is not a theory of biology. It is a statement about a gift: man was made for a life that the dust of which he is formed could not of itself secure, and he lost that life by turning from the Giver.

Ambrose, returning on the seventh day to the grave of his brother Satyrus, looks at the same sentence of Genesis from the side of mercy. He notices that God did not say to Adam, “because thou hast hearkened to the voice of the woman thou shalt return to the earth.” The curse fell on the ground and its thorns. The return to the earth was named as the term of man’s labour: “In sweat shalt thou eat thy bread until thou return to the earth.” “Death is given for a remedy,” Ambrose concludes, “because it is the end of evils. … You see that death is rather the goal of our penalties, by which an end is put to the course of this life.”[^17] Augustine stresses the penalty and Ambrose the remedy, and the Church keeps both. Death is the punishment of a race that sinned; it is also the mercy that will not let a fallen life go on for ever in its misery.

### The debt paid

The tradition has a name for death that holds all of this together. It calls it a debt. The anonymous *Craft of Dying* of the fifteenth century counts among the blessings of a good death the “paying of \[the\] debt of natural duty.”[^18] The Church’s own prayer at the deathbed says the same, as the priest commends the dying man to God: “I commend thee to Almighty God, dearly beloved brother, and commit thee to Him whose creature thou art; that, when thou shalt have paid the debt of humanity by passing through death, thou mayest return to thy Maker, who formed thee from the dust of the earth.”[^19] The debt is owed by nature, since every man is dust; it is owed by justice, since every man is a son of Adam; and it is paid only once. The word gives death its gravity without despair. A debt is real and cannot be evaded. But a debt paid is a debt discharged, and the one who pays it goes free.

So the Christian looks at death with both eyes open. He does not call it a friend, because Scripture calls it an enemy, and the last to be destroyed.[^20] He does not call it nothing, because it is the wrench that Augustine felt. But neither does he meet it as the pagan meets it, as the end of everything. For the debt that Adam contracted has been met by another, and the enemy that holds the world has itself been made to serve.

## The Fear of Death Undone

The Son of God did not abolish death by forbidding it to touch him. He entered it. “Therefore because the children are partakers of flesh and blood, he also himself in like manner hath been partaker of the same: that, through death, he might destroy him who had the empire of death, that is to say, the devil: and might deliver them, who through the fear of death were all their lifetime subject to servitude.”[^21] The Apostle names the bondage exactly. It is not death that enslaves, since death comes only once; it is the fear of death, which lasts a whole lifetime and bends every choice toward the one thing it cannot avoid. Christ breaks that servitude from within. He took the death that belongs to the human condition and accepted it in free submission to his Father’s will, and the Church confesses the result in one sentence: “The obedience of Jesus has transformed the curse of death into a blessing.”[^22]

### Die, that thou sin not

Augustine asks why, if death is the punishment of sin, the baptized still die, since their guilt has been washed away. His answer goes to the root of the matter. God left the parting of soul and body in place “for this reason, that if the immortality of the body followed immediately upon the sacrament of regeneration, faith itself would be thereby enervated. For faith is then only faith when it waits in hope for what is not yet seen in substance.”[^23] If baptism ended death, men would run to the font to escape the grave and not to find Christ, and there would be no martyrs, since there would be nothing to suffer. Instead, “by the greater and more admirable grace of the Saviour, the punishment of sin is turned to the service of righteousness. For then it was proclaimed to man, ‘If thou sinnest, thou shalt die;’ now it is said to the martyr, ‘Die, that thou sin not.’ ”[^24]

The threat that once guarded the commandment now keeps it. Augustine presses the inversion until it is complete: “For then death was incurred by sinning, now righteousness is fulfilled by dying.” Yet he will not let anyone conclude that death has become good. “Not that death, which was before an evil, has become something good, but only that God has granted to faith this grace, that death, which is the admitted opposite to life, should become the instrument by which life is reached.”[^25] Death has not been pardoned. It has been conscripted. The enemy remains an enemy, and serves.

That is why the Christian need not choose between dread and indifference. He fears death rightly, as a punishment and a wrench, and he hopes through it rightly, because Christ has passed through it before him and made it the road home. Augustine says in the next chapter that the good “die well, though death is an evil.”[^26]

### The martyrs’ longing

The first Christians who faced death for Christ show what this looks like in a human face. Ignatius of Antioch, carried under guard to Rome to be thrown to the beasts, wrote ahead to the Christians of that city and begged them not to obtain his release. “All the pleasures of the world, and all the kingdoms of this earth, shall profit me nothing. It is better for me to die in behalf of Jesus Christ, than to reign over all the ends of the earth. … Him I seek, who died for us: Him I desire, who rose again for our sake.” And he turns their kindness inside out: “do not hinder me from living, do not wish to keep me in a state of death.”[^27] For the martyr, to go on living in this world apart from Christ is the real death, and the arena is the door to life.

Polycarp of Smyrna was an old man when the proconsul urged him to curse Christ and go free. “Eighty and six years have I served Him, and He never did me any injury: how then can I blaspheme my King and my Saviour?”[^28] Bound at the stake, he looked up to heaven and gave thanks: “I give Thee thanks that Thou hast counted me worthy of this day and this hour, that I should have a part in the number of Thy martyrs, in the cup of thy Christ, to the resurrection of eternal life, both of soul and body.”[^29] His church gathered his bones afterward, “as being more precious than the most exquisite jewels,” and resolved to keep each year the anniversary of his martyrdom, which their Greek calls his birthday.[^30] The Church still keeps the feasts of her saints, for the most part, on the day of their death. In her calendar a Christian is born when he dies.

Few are asked to die as Ignatius and Polycarp died. But every Christian is asked to die in Christ, and the Apostle gives the pattern for all of them: “For to me, to live is Christ: and to die is gain. And if to live in the flesh: this is to me the fruit of labour. And what I shall choose I know not. But I am straitened between two: having a desire to be dissolved and to be with Christ, a thing by far the better.”[^31] Paul does not love death. He loves Christ, and he finds that death, which was the wages of sin, has become the way to him.

## At What Hour You Know Not

Of the hour of death only one thing is certain, that it is hidden. Christ says it in the same discourse from which the 1962 Missal reads on the Last Sunday after Pentecost: “Watch ye therefore, because you know not what hour your Lord will come. But this know ye, that, if the goodman of the house knew at what hour the thief would come, he would certainly watch and would not suffer his house to be broken open. Wherefore be you also ready, because at what hour you know not the Son of man will come.”[^32] He ends the parable of the ten virgins with the same command: “Watch ye therefore, because you know not the day nor the hour.”[^33] The words speak first of his coming at the end of the world, and the Church has always heard in them also the coming of the Lord to each soul at its death. The thief does not announce himself. A man who will not watch until he hears him at the door will not watch at all.

The Church turns this warning into prayer. Among the devotions for the sick in the *Manual of Prayers* that the Third Plenary Council of Baltimore ordered for the laity, the faithful ask:

> O God, who hast doomed all men to die, but hast concealed from all the hour of their death: grant that I may pass my days in the practice of holiness and justice, and that I may be made worthy to quit this world in the peace of a good conscience and in the embrace of Thy love. Through Christ our Lord. *Amen.*[^34]

The prayer asks for nothing about the hour. It asks to be ready at whatever hour God has kept to himself.

### The race toward death

Augustine saw that the road to the hidden hour is already being travelled. “For no sooner do we begin to live in this dying body, than we begin to move ceaselessly towards death.” Every day taken from life is taken from the time that remains, “so that our whole life is nothing but a race towards death, in which no one is allowed to stand still for a little space, or to go somewhat more slowly, but all are driven forwards with an impartial movement, and with equal rapidity. For he whose life is short spends a day no more swiftly than he whose life is longer.”[^35] The young man and the old run at the same pace. The only difference is the length of the course, and no runner can see the line.

Benedict wrote the consequence into his Rule. Among the instruments of good works he sets four in a row: *Diem iudicii timere. Gehennam expavescere. Vitam æternam omni concupiscentia spiritali desiderare. Mortem cotidie ante oculos suspectam habere*. To fear the day of judgment, to dread hell, to desire eternal life with all spiritual longing, and to keep death before one’s eyes each day as a thing that may come at any time: these are the four last things, in Benedict’s own order.[^36] The monk is not asked to imagine death once in a while as a spiritual exercise. He is asked to live as one whose death is already looking at him.

### The school of the *Imitation*

The *Imitation of Christ* teaches this lesson with unsparing plainness, and its chapter on the meditation of death deserves to be heard at length. “Very quickly will there be an end of thee here; take heed therefore how it will be with thee in another world. To-day man is, and to-morrow he will be seen no more. And being removed out of sight, quickly also he is out of mind. … Thou oughtest in every deed and thought so to order thyself, as if thou wert to die this day. If thou hadst a good conscience thou wouldst not greatly fear death. It were better for thee to watch against sin, than to fly from death. If to-day thou art not ready, how shalt thou be ready to-morrow?”[^37]

The author does not soften what he has seen. “If it is a fearful thing to die, it may be perchance a yet more fearful thing to live long. Happy is the man who hath the hour of his death always before his eyes, and daily prepareth himself to die. If thou hast ever seen one die, consider that thou also shalt pass away by the same road.”[^38] And then the counsel that makes the meditation fruitful rather than morbid: “Happy and wise is he who now striveth to be such in life as he would fain be found in death!” The things that give “great confidence of a happy death” are not feelings at the last hour but habits formed beforehand: contempt of the world, desire of virtue, love of discipline, repentance, obedience, denial of self, and patience under adversity “for love of Christ.”[^39] The chapter ends where all such meditation ought to end, in prayer: “To Him direct thy daily prayers with crying and tears, that thy spirit may be found worthy to pass happily after death unto its Lord.”[^40]

### The meditation of death

The masters of the following centuries made this remembrance a method. Francis de Sales, writing his *Introduction to the Devout Life* for men and women in the world, gives as its fifth meditation the meditation of death. The soul is to place itself in God’s presence, ask his grace, and “suppose yourself to be on your deathbed, in the last extremity, without the smallest hope of recovery.” Then it considers. “One day your soul will quit this body—will it be in summer or winter? in town or country? by day or by night? will it be suddenly or with warning? … Alas, of all these things we know absolutely nothing: all that we do know is that die we shall, and for the most part sooner than we expect.”[^41] It considers the farewell the soul will take of riches, pleasures, friends, “husband, wife and child, in short to all creation,” and last of all of its own body. And it considers the one question that matters: “when it quits the body the soul must go at once to the right hand or the left. To which will your soul go? what side will it take? none other, be sure, than that to which it had voluntarily drawn while yet in this world.”[^42] The meditation closes with a resolution and a prayer, and de Sales gives the soul a last instruction for the rest of the day: “Gather a bouquet of myrrh.”[^43]

Alphonsus asks for more. In his first consideration he sets the reader beside a body newly dead. “Imagine that you behold a person who has just expired. Look at that body still laid on the bed, the head fallen on the chest, the hair in disorder and still bathed in the sweat of death, the eyes sunk, the cheeks hollow, the face the colour of ashes.” Then he follows the mourners home and watches the dead man forgotten, his goods divided, his room given back to the living: “in the very room in which you shall have breathed forth your soul, and in which you shall be judged by Jesus Christ, others shall dance and eat and play and laugh as before. And where shall your soul then be?”[^44] The consideration does not end in the grave. It ends at the feet of Christ, in an act of sorrow and a plea for time: “Lord, I thank you: I accept my death in satisfaction for my sins, and I accept it in the manner in which you shall be pleased to send it. But since you have borne with me till now, wait for me a little longer.”[^45]

### He who lives well

The whole school can be summed up in Robert Bellarmine’s rule. In *The Art of Dying Well*, written in his old age in the quiet of retreat, he set one principle before all the others: “the general rule, ‘that he who lives well, will die well,’ must be mentioned before all others: for since death is nothing more than the end of life, it is certain that all who live well to the end, die well; nor can he die ill, who hath never lived ill.”[^46] Someone will name the good thief, who lived ill and died well. Bellarmine answers that the thief’s last hours were a life lived well, short as it was: he defended Christ, rebuked his blaspheming companion, and called upon the Lord before he died. And so “True, therefore, is the sentence, ‘He who lives well, dies well;’ and, ‘He who lives ill, dies ill.’ ”[^47] The hour is hidden so that every hour may be lived as if it were the last. That is the mercy in the concealment. God does not tell us when, in order that we may be ready always. And the readiness cannot be put off past the hour itself, for “Death is the end of man’s earthly pilgrimage, of the time of grace and mercy which God offers him so as to work out his earthly life in keeping with the divine plan, and to decide his ultimate destiny.”[^48]

## Not Lost, but Sent Before

Most of the deaths a Christian sees before his own are the deaths of others, and the Church teaches him how to grieve. The Apostle does not forbid sorrow. He forbids a certain kind of it: “And we will not have you ignorant brethren, concerning them that are asleep, that you be not sorrowful, even as others who have no hope. For if we believe that Jesus died and rose again: even so them who have slept through Jesus, will God bring with him.”[^49] The Christian mourns, and he mourns as one who hopes.

Ambrose had to learn this at his brother’s bier, before his own people, and he told them so. He had wept, and he did not repent of it. “Not all weeping proceeds from unbelief or weakness. Natural grief is one thing, distrustful sadness is another, and there is a very great difference between longing for what you have lost and lamenting that you have lost it. Not only grief has tears, joy also has tears of its own. … I confess, then, that I too wept, but the Lord also wept. He wept for one not related to Him, I for my brother. He wept for all in weeping for one, I will weep for thee in all, my brother.”[^50] The tears at the grave of Lazarus sanctify the tears at every Christian grave. What faith forbids is the sorrow that treats the dead as though they were no more.

### Cyprian in the plague

Cyprian of Carthage taught it with all the urgency of a pastor in a time of plague. He wrote *On the Mortality* while a pestilence was carrying off Christians and pagans alike, and he would not let his people bury their faith with their dead. He tells them that God had often charged him to declare it “that our brethren who are freed from this world by the Lord’s summons are not to be lamented, since we know that they are not lost, but sent before; that, departing from us, they precede us as travellers, as navigators are accustomed to do; that they should be desired, but not bewailed; that the black garments should not be taken upon us here, when they have already taken upon them white raiment there.”[^51] To mourn the departed as lost is to deny with the heart the faith that the lips profess.

For Cyprian death is a crossing. “That in the meantime we die, we are passing over to immortality by death; nor can eternal life follow, unless it should befall us to depart from this life. That is not an ending, but a transit, and, this journey of time being traversed, a passage to eternity. Who would not hasten to better things?”[^52] And he turns his flock from the house of mourning toward the house they are going to: “We should consider, dearly beloved brethren—we should ever and anon reflect that we have renounced the world, and are in the meantime living here as guests and strangers. Let us greet the day which assigns each of us to his own home, which snatches us hence, and sets us free from the snares of the world, and restores us to paradise and the kingdom.”[^53]

The home is not empty. “There a great number of our dear ones is awaiting us, and a dense crowd of parents, brothers, children, is longing for us, already assured of their own safety, and still solicitous for our salvation.”[^54] The martyr-bishop speaks here with the voice of the whole Church. The Council of Trent bids the pastors teach that the saints, reigning together with Christ, offer their prayers to God for men, and that it is good and useful to call upon them.[^55] The Catechism sets beside the teaching of the Second Vatican Council two sayings of saints who knew they were dying. Saint Dominic told his brethren: “Do not weep, for I shall be more useful to you after my death and I shall help you then more effectively than during my life.” Saint Thérèse said: “I want to spend my heaven in doing good on earth.”[^56] The communion of saints is not interrupted at the grave. The blessed in heaven are, in the words Paul VI made the Church’s profession, “interceding for us and helping our weakness by their fraternal concern.”[^57] The meeting with them, and the joy of it, belong to *Then Face to Face*.

Christian grief, then, has a shape. It weeps, as Christ wept. It does not wear black in the heart, because the dead in Christ are clothed in white. It prays for those who may still need its prayers, and it asks the prayers of those who have reached home. And it learns from every death it mourns the lesson of the sage at the bedside: remember thy last end. The one who has gone before has shown the road by which the mourner also will go.

## At the Lord’s Altar

The Latin Church has long treasured a death told by a son. In the ninth book of the *Confessions* Augustine recounts how, in the year of his baptism, he and his mother Monica waited at Ostia, at the mouth of the Tiber, for a ship to carry them home to Africa. “As the day now approached on which she was to depart this life (which day Thou knewest, we did not), it fell out—Thou, as I believe, by Thy secret ways arranging it—that she and I stood alone, leaning in a certain window, from which the garden of the house we occupied at Ostia could be seen.”[^58] The day was known to God and hidden from them. Neither of them was thinking of death. They were speaking of heaven.

“We were seeking between ourselves in the presence of the Truth, which Thou art, of what nature the eternal life of the saints would be, which eye hath not seen, nor ear heard, neither hath entered into the heart of man.”[^59] Their talk rose through every created thing, past the earth and the heavens and their own minds, toward the Wisdom by whom all things were made, “and while we were thus speaking, and straining after her, we slightly touched her with the whole effort of our heart.”[^60] For a moment they knew what it is for the soul to be silent before God alone, and they asked whether that was not the meaning of the Lord’s word, “Enter thou into the joy of Thy Lord.” Then they came back “to the noise of our own mouth, where the word uttered has both beginning and end.”[^61]

Monica spoke first. “Son, for myself, I have no longer any pleasure in aught in this life. What I want here further, and why I am here, I know not, now that my hopes in this world are satisfied. There was indeed one thing for which I wished to tarry a little in this life, and that was that I might see thee a Catholic Christian before I died. My God has exceeded this abundantly, so that I see thee despising all earthly felicity, made His servant,—what do I here?”[^62] Here is the whole of a good death in a few lines, spoken by a woman in good health. She had finished the work God gave her. Nothing held her in the world, and she was not afraid to leave it.

Within five days or not much more she fell ill of a fever. She swooned, came to herself, and looked at her two sons standing beside her. “Here,” she said, “shall you bury your mother.” Augustine’s brother wished aloud that she might die in her own country. She silenced him with a look and spoke to them both: “Lay this body anywhere, let not the care for it trouble you at all. This only I ask, that you will remember me at the Lord’s altar, wherever you be.”[^63] She had once been anxious to lie beside her husband in Africa. Now the place of her grave did not matter to her, and when friends asked whether she did not dread leaving her body so far from her own city, she answered, “Nothing is far to God; nor need I fear lest He should be ignorant at the end of the world of the place whence He is to raise me up.”[^64] On the ninth day of her sickness “was that religious and devout soul set free from the body.”[^65]

Everything the Church teaches about Christian death is in that room. There is the soul’s longing for God, already tasted at the window. There is the indifference to everything but God, which the masters of the spiritual life spend years trying to teach. There is the body entrusted without anxiety to the God who will raise it, and the one request a dying Christian makes of those who love her, which is to be remembered at the altar. The prayer of the Church for her dead, offered above all in the Sacrifice of the Mass, is expounded in *What Death Does Not Finish*. Monica, at the end, wanted nothing else.

Augustine records his own grief as honestly as her death. He closed her eyes and held back his tears through the burial, because “she neither died unhappy, nor did she altogether die.”[^66] Later, lying alone upon his bed, he let the memory of her return, “and it was pleasant to me to weep in Thy sight, for her and for me, concerning her and concerning myself.” He asks the reader not to laugh at him for weeping “for my mother during so small a part of an hour,—that mother who was for a while dead to mine eyes, who had for many years wept for me, that I might live in Thine eyes.”[^67] Ambrose’s lesson holds. The son who believed she had not altogether died still wept for her, and his tears were a prayer.

## The Craft of Dying

In the fifteenth century the Church’s long experience at the deathbed was gathered into a short Latin treatise on the art of dying. Its author is unknown; it cites “the Chancellor of Paris,” Jean Gerson, among its authorities. Many printed versions of it exist, and the picture-books of the same art, its editor records, are found “in almost every European language.”[^68] In English it was called *The Book of the Craft of Dying*, and it was written for everyone: “Forasmuch as the passage of death, of the wretchedness of the exile of this world, for uncunning of dying—not only to lewd men but also to religious and devout persons—seemeth wonderfully hard and perilous, and also right fearful and horrible,” the book offers “a short manner of exhortation, for teaching and comforting of them that be in point of death.”[^69] The “lewd men” are laymen, as the editor explains, and “uncunning” is ignorance. It is profitable, the book says, “to all true Christian men, to learn and have craft and knowledge to die well.”

The book begins with the commendation of death, and it begins with a distinction. Bodily death is dreadful, “yet spiritual death of the soul is as much more horrible and detestable, as the soul is more worthy and precious than the body.” The death of the good man, on the other hand, “is ever precious in the sight of God, what manner of bodily death that ever they die.”[^70] For such a man, it says, death is “a going \[out\] of prison, and an ending of exile; a discharging of an heavy burden, that is the body; finishing of all infirmities; a scaping of all perils; destroying of all evil things; breaking of all bonds; paying of \[the\] debt of natural duty; turning again into his country; and entering into bliss and joy.”[^71] The language is the Church’s own. The Psalmist prays, “Bring my soul out of prison, that I may praise thy name”; the Wise Man says that “the corruptible body is a load upon the soul”; and the Apostle that “if our earthly house of this habitation be dissolved,” we have “a house not made with hands, eternal in heaven.”[^72] The good man goes home. Then the book gives the whole art of dying in a sentence it borrows from a wise man: “To die well is to die gladly and wilfully,” that is, to give up to God with a willing heart the life that must be given up in any case.[^73]

### The five temptations

The second chapter warns that the dying “have greatest and most grievous temptations, and such as they never had before in all their life,” and it names five.[^74] The devil attacks the faith, “forasmuch as faith is fundament of all men’s soul’s-heal.” He urges despair, setting a man’s sins before him. He stirs impatience in the sufferer. He tempts the devout most subtly of all, with complacence, “that is spiritual pride, with the which the devil tempteth and beguileth most religious, and devout and perfect men,” whispering, “O how stable art thou in the faith! how strong in hope! how sad in patience! O how many good deeds hast thou done!” And he holds worldly men by “their wives, their children, their carnal friends, and their worldly riches, and other things that they have loved inordinately before.”[^75] Against all five the book sets one consolation. The devil “may compel no man, nor in no manner of wise prevail against him for to consent to him—as long as a man hath the use of reason with him—but if he will wilfully consent unto him.”[^76] God, as the book renders the Apostle, “is true, and will not suffer you to be tempted more than ye may bear.”

### The questions at the bedside

Then the book gives those who sit with the dying a set of questions to ask, which it attributes to Saint Anselm. They are short, because the dying have little breath:

> Brother, art thou glad that thou shalt die in the faith of Christ? The sick man answereth: Yea.
>
> Knowest thou well that thou hast not done as thou shouldst have done? He answereth: Yea.
>
> Repentest thee thereof? He answereth: Yea.
>
> Hast thou full will to amend thee, if thou mightest have full space of life? He answereth: Yea.
>
> Believest thou fully that Our Lord Jesu Christ, God’s Son, died for thee? He sayeth: Yea.
>
> Thankest thou Him thereof with all thine heart? He answereth: Yea.
>
> Believest thou verily that thou mayest not be saved but by Christ’s \[death and His\] passion? He answereth: Yea.[^77]

Faith, contrition, purpose of amendment, trust in the Passion, thanksgiving: in seven questions a whole life is gathered into the act of a dying man. And then the book puts into his mouth, against the terror of the judgment, a prayer of great simplicity: “Lord, I put the death of Our Lord Jesu Christ between me and mine evil deeds, between me and the judgment; otherwise will I not strive with Thee.” If God should say to him that he has deserved damnation, he is to answer, “The death of our Lord Jesu Christ I put between me and mine evil merits.” Last of all he is to say three times, *In manus tuas, Domine*, and if he can no longer speak, those who stand about him are to say it for him: *In manus tuas, Domine, commendamus spiritum eius*.[^78]

The fifth chapter turns to the living and rebukes them. “It is greatly to be noted, and to be taken heed of, that right seldom (that) any man—yea among religious and devout men—dispose themselves to death betimes as they ought. For every man weeneth himself to live long, and troweth not that he shall die in short time; and doubtless that cometh of the devil’s subtle temptation.”[^79] Men seek medicine for the body sooner than for the soul. The sick are flattered with hopes of recovery, and “as the great Clerk, the Chancellor of Paris saith: Often times by such a \[vain and a\] false cheering and comforting, and feigned behoting of bodily heal, and trusting thereupon, men run and fall into certain damnation everlastingly.”[^80] The “behoting” is the promising. The kindest thing to say to the dying is the truth, in time for them to use it.

### From a sudden and unforeseen death

The Church puts the same wisdom into her prayer. “The Church encourages us to prepare ourselves for the hour of our death. In the litany of the saints, for instance, she has us pray: ‘From a sudden and unforeseen death, deliver us, O Lord’; to ask the Mother of God to intercede for us ‘at the hour of our death’ in the Hail Mary; and to entrust ourselves to St. Joseph, the patron of a happy death.”[^81] In the Latin of the Litany it runs *A subitanea et improvisa morte*, “From sudden and unlooked-for death,” as the Baltimore *Manual of Prayers* renders it.[^82] The world counts a quick and unknowing death a mercy. The Church asks instead for time: time to confess, to receive the last sacraments, to forgive and be forgiven, to make the acts of faith and hope and love, and to say *In manus tuas*. She does not despise those who die suddenly. Scripture promises, “But the just man, if he be prevented with death, shall be in rest.”[^83] But she asks for every one of her children the grace to see death coming and to meet it.

Every Hail Mary asks it too. Each time they say it, the faithful pray, *Sancta Maria, Mater Dei, ora pro nobis peccatoribus, nunc et in hora mortis nostræ*: “Holy Mary, Mother of God, pray for us sinners, now and at the hour of our death.”[^84] Of all the hours of a life, the Church names only two in this prayer, the present one and the last, and she asks the Mother of God to stand at both. Beside Mary she places Joseph, whom she calls the patron of a happy death. The Baltimore *Manual* gives the sick three short invocations of the Holy Family to say while they can:

> Jesus, Mary, and Joseph, I give you my heart and my soul.
>
> Jesus, Mary, and Joseph, assist me in my last agony.
>
> Jesus, Mary, and Joseph, may I breathe forth my soul in peace with you.[^85]

### Precious in the sight of the Lord

The fear of a bad death is meant to lead to the hope of a good one, and the saints did not leave that hope abstract. Alphonsus gives a whole consideration to the death of the just, under the verse “Precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of his saints.” “Considered according to the senses, death excites fear and terror: but viewed with the eyes of faith, it is consoling and desirable. To sinners it appears full of terror; but to the saints it is amiable and precious.” He borrows Bernard’s words: it is precious “as the end of labours, the consummation of victory, the gate of life.”[^86] The just man is not torn from his goods, because his heart was never fastened to them. He does not fear to leave his family, because he loved them in God, and “at death he recommends them to his heavenly Father, who loves them more than he does.” He accepts the pains of death with peace, “and feels consoled in uniting the sacrifice of his death with the sacrifice which Jesus Christ offered for him on the cross to his eternal Father.”[^87]

Thérèse of Lisieux died in the Carmel of Lisieux on 30 September 1897, after long suffering. Some weeks before the end she wrote to one of the missionaries she called her brothers: “I am about to go before God, and I understand now more than ever that one thing only is needful—to work for Him alone, and do nothing for self or creatures. … but oh! what joy when comes the happy hour of going Home! I shall not die—I do but enter into Life.”[^88] On the last evening, when she was told that this was her agony, she answered, “Ah, very well then… very well… I do not wish to suffer less!” Then, looking at her crucifix, she said, “Oh! … I love Him! … My God, I… love… Thee!” Those were her last words.[^89]

That is what the craft of dying comes to. It is the art of making the last act of life an act of love.

## The Sacraments of the Departing

The craft of dying is not left to the devotion of the dying man and his friends. Christ gave his Church sacraments for this passage, and she gives them as long as there is breath to receive them. “Penance, the Anointing of the Sick and the Eucharist as viaticum constitute at the end of Christian life ‘the sacraments that prepare for our heavenly homeland’ or the sacraments that complete the earthly pilgrimage.”[^90]

The first is confession. The *Craft of Dying* told the sick man to make his peace with God “before all other things,” and to seek his soul’s health “by very contrition and confession.”[^91] At the end the Church’s power to bind and loose is offered once more, and the soul that has strayed far may return in an hour. Those who have confessed each month “as if it were the last of your life,” as Leonard of Port Maurice counsels, come to this confession as to one long rehearsed.

The second is the anointing that the Apostle James commands: “Is any man sick among you? Let him bring in the priests of the church and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord. And the prayer of faith shall save the sick man. And the Lord shall raise him up: and if he be in sins, they shall be forgiven him.”[^92] The Council of Trent defines that this anointing is truly and properly a sacrament, instituted by Christ and promulgated by the Apostle.[^93] In the Roman Ritual the priest anointed the senses through which the man had sinned, and at each he invoked the holy unction and the most tender mercy of God; at the lips he prayed, “may the Lord pardon thee whatsoever sins thou hast committed by taste and speech.”[^94] The present law of the Latin Church allows the sacrament to be given to any of the faithful “who, having reached the use of reason, begins to be in danger due to sickness or old age,” and to be repeated if the danger returns or grows.[^95] The Church does not wait for the last breath to strengthen the sick against the last struggle.

The third is the Body of the Lord given as food for the journey. When the priest brings Holy Communion to one who is dying, he says over him words the Church has spoken at countless bedsides: *Accipe, frater (vel soror) Viaticum Corporis Domini nostri Jesu Christi, qui te custodiat ab hoste maligno, et perducat in vitam æternam. Amen.* “Receive, brother, the Viaticum of the Body of our Lord Jesus Christ; may He preserve thee from the wicked enemy, and bring thee unto life everlasting. Amen.”[^96] The Catechism calls this the last sacrament of all: “As the sacrament of Christ’s Passover the Eucharist should always be the last sacrament of the earthly journey, the ‘viaticum’ for ‘passing over’ to eternal life.”[^97] Christ himself promised it: “He who eats my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life, and I will raise him up at the last day.”[^98]

Because the Viaticum matters so much, the Church’s law is unusually urgent about it. “Holy Viaticum for the sick is not to be delayed too long; those who have the care of souls are to be zealous and vigilant that the sick are nourished by Viaticum while fully conscious.”[^99] The canon asks something of the dying and of those who love them. It asks the sick man not to put off the priest for fear of frightening himself, and it asks his family not to put him off for fear of frightening the sick man. Love at a deathbed sends for the priest in time.

A Christian who has received these three sacraments at the end has been given everything the Church has to give. He has been forgiven, strengthened, and fed. He goes out of the world as he entered the Church, carried by grace, and the Church’s last service is to accompany him with her prayer to the door.

## The Church’s Last Words

When the sacraments have been given and the agony begins, the Church does not fall silent. The Roman Ritual provides an order for the commendation of a departing soul, and the Baltimore *Manual* gives its rubric in English: “Then while the soul is in the agony of its departure, the Priest recites the following prayers.”[^100] Some of the prayers are addressed to God for the dying man, and some to the dying man himself. The first of them begins *Proficiscere, anima christiana*, “Go forth, O Christian soul,” and it is the Church’s farewell to her child.

A prayer for pardon follows, *Deus misericors*, and after it the commendation proper, which opens with the words on the debt of humanity already heard and then surrounds the departing soul with the whole court of heaven: “When, therefore, thy soul goeth forth from thy body, may the glorious company of Angels meet thee; may the council of the Apostles who shall judge the world greet thee; may the triumphant army of white-robed Martyrs come out to welcome thee; may the band of shining Confessors, crowned with lilies, encircle thee; may the choir of joyous Virgins receive thee; and unto the bosom of blessed rest may the embrace of the Patriarchs clasp thee; gentle and joyful may the Face of Jesus Christ appear to thee, and may He award thee a place among those who stand before Him for ever.”[^101]

The prayer knows the danger it is asking God to avert. It names “the terror of darkness, the gnashing of teeth in the flames, the agonies of torment,” and prays that the soul may never know them. It bids Satan “give way” and “flee away into the vast chaos of eternal night.” And it closes with the sight that is the end of every Christian life: “Mayest thou behold thy Redeemer face to face; and, standing ever before Him, gaze with blessed eyes on the Truth made manifest.”[^102] Here, at the door, the Church holds both of the last things that lie beyond it together, the fire and the Face, and prays for the one with all her strength. At the deathbed they are not subjects of study. They are the two ends of a road a man is walking that night.

As the soul is about to depart, the Baltimore *Manual* directs those present to pray more earnestly than ever, and if the dying can no longer speak, to say again and again in his ear the name of Jesus and such words as these: “Into Thy hands, O Lord, I commend my spirit.—O Lord Jesus Christ, receive my spirit.—Holy Mary, pray for me.—O Mary, mother of grace, mother of mercy, do thou protect me from the enemy, and receive me at the hour of death.”[^103] The first of them are the words in which Christ himself died. The Christian dies in the words of his Lord.

### Come to his assistance

The moment of death passes unmarked by any sign that the bystanders can see. The Church marks it by a responsory, said as soon as the soul has gone:

> Come to his assistance, ye Saints of God, come forth to meet him, ye Angels of the Lord: Receiving his soul: Offering it in the sight of the Most High.
>
> *V.* May Christ receive thee, who hath called thee, and may the Angels bear thee into Abraham’s bosom.
>
> *R.* Receiving, etc.
>
> *V.* Eternal rest grant unto him, O Lord, and let perpetual light shine upon him.
>
> *R.* Offering, etc.[^104]

The living cannot follow the soul, but they can send it on with the saints and the angels. In the *Subvenite* the Church speaks to heaven on behalf of one who has just left her sight, and asks heaven to come out to meet him.

### Life is changed, not taken away

Then the Church carries her dead into her churches and offers for them the Sacrifice of the Mass. In the Roman Missal of 1920, every Mass for the dead, and so the Masses of All Souls’ Day, has a Preface that answers in a single sentence everything the Fathers said about death:

> It is truly meet and just, right and profitable for us, at all times, and in all places, to give thanks to Thee, O holy Lord, Father almighty, eternal God: through Christ, our Lord, in Whom the hope of a happy resurrection has shone on us, so that those whom the certain fate of dying renders sad, may be consoled by the promise of future immortality. For with regard to Thy faithful, O Lord, life is changed, not taken away; and the house of their earthly dwelling being destroyed, an eternal dwelling in heaven is obtained.[^105]

*Vita mutatur, non tollitur*. The Preface does not deny the sadness. It names “the certain fate of dying” and admits that it “renders sad.” Nor does it deny that the earthly house is destroyed; it says so in the words of the Apostle.[^106] But it sets beside the sadness the promise, and beside the ruined house the eternal one. This is the whole Christian teaching on death, prayed rather than argued: the wrench is real, the debt is paid, and the life that was given in baptism is changed in its condition and is not taken away.

The Office of the Dead says the same in the voice of Job. Its first lesson begs, “spare me, for my days are nothing.” Its eighth lesson answers: “For I know that my Redeemer liveth, and in the last day I shall rise out of the earth. And I shall be clothed again with my skin, and in my flesh I shall see my God.”[^107] The body laid in the grave will see God. That is why the Church buries her dead with honour, and why she does not cease to pray.

## A Day Each Month

Everything the saints teach about death comes down at last to a practice, because a truth that is only admired will not be ready at the hour it is needed. The *Craft of Dying* complained that men will not “dispose themselves to death betimes.” The remedy the saints proposed is to dispose oneself regularly, while in health, so that the last disposition is only the last of many.

The simplest form of the practice is a choice made in the light of death. Ignatius of Loyola gives it as a rule for the election, the choice of a state or way of life: “to consider, as if I were at the point of death, the form and measure which I would then want to have kept” in the matter now to be chosen, and to decide accordingly.[^108] Francis de Sales ends his meditation on death with a resolution of the same kind: “I will prepare for the hour of death and take every precaution for its peaceful arrival; I will thoroughly examine into the state of my conscience, and put in order whatever is wanting.”[^109] Neither asks for gloom. Both ask that the end be allowed to judge the means.

### The monthly retreat

The fullest form is the monthly day of preparation for death, commended in the eighteenth century by Leonard of Port Maurice and printed with the English *Preparation for Death* of Alphonsus. “The great act of Christian prudence, in my opinion, consists in preparing to make well that last step, for which, if once made badly, there is no remedy.” To be ready for it, Leonard says, two things are needed. The first is to pray constantly for the grace of final perseverance, “which is the grace of graces,” and to repeat often the words “My Jesus, mercy, mercy,” so that a habit formed in life may speak at death. “The second is, to select in each month a day, on which you shall be most free from other employments, and to consecrate the entire of it to the great affair of learning to die well.”[^110] He wrote for religious, and his directions assume a cell; but the substance of the day can be kept by anyone who will set aside its hours.

The day is ordered toward the deathbed. “The confession ought to be made as if it were the last of your life,” with sorrow “imagining that you must soon appear before the tribunal of God.” The Communion is to be received “as if you were going to receive the viaticum.”[^111] The morning meditation sets the soul in the room where it will die: “you shall represent to yourself in the most vivid manner, the state in which you shall find yourself at the hour of death,—given over by the physicians, separated for ever from your companions, and admonished by your confessor to breathe your soul into the hands of God.” It asks what one would then wish to have done toward God, toward oneself, and toward one’s neighbour, and it ends in contrition and a purpose to live “with as great fervour as if you expected death every day.”[^112]

The evening meditation turns from fear to acceptance. Leonard gives five motives for accepting death willingly: its necessity, which the Son of God himself did not refuse; justice, since a sinner has deserved it; humility; love, because death opens the country “where my sole occupation shall be to love you”; and resignation to the will of God, “for its execution in the year, in the month, on the day, in the moment, in the form and manner which may be most pleasing to him.”[^113] The day closes with an act of acceptance of death, and Leonard promises two fruits, the first on the word of the private revelations of Saint Gertrude. If death comes too suddenly for these acts to be made at the end, “the Lord shall, according to St. Gertrude, accept them as if you actually performed them at the moment of your going to eternity”; and if there is time, the soul “will have acquired by habit a facility of performing these acts.” “The man who has learned well in private the piece which he is to recite in public, delivers it on the stage with great ease.”[^114]

### The protestation of death

Alphonsus wrote such an act for his readers. He calls it a protestation of death, and it begins: “My God, prostrate in your presence, I adore you; and I intend to make the following protestation, as if I were on the point of passing from this life to eternity.”[^115] It professes the faith in which the Christian wishes to die. It hopes for pardon “not through my merits, but through the merits of Jesus Christ,” and it forestalls the last temptation: “should the devil, at death, tempt me to despair at the sight of my sins, I protest that I wish always to hope in you, my Lord, and that I wish to die in the loving arms of your goodness.” It forgives every enemy. It accepts death and its pains, “and all the pains which shall accompany my death,” in union with the death of Christ. It asks the Blessed Virgin, Saint Joseph, Saint Michael, and the guardian angel to stand by in “that last battle.” And it gives heart and soul to Jesus and Mary in advance, for the moment of the last breath: “Jesus and Mary, I give you my heart and my soul.”[^116]

With the protestation the same pages give a short prayer to be said every day for a good death:

> O Lord Jesus Christ, through that agony which your most noble soul suffered when it went forth from your blessed body, have mercy on my sinful soul when it shall depart from my body. Amen.[^117]

### Watch ye, therefore

The year turns, and the Church begins again where she ended. On the first Sunday of Advent, in the 1962 Missal, she reads the signs in the sun and the moon and the stars, and the Lord’s promise that “this generation shall not pass away till all things be fulfilled.”[^118] The verses that follow in Saint Luke are a counsel for every season: “And take heed to yourselves, lest perhaps your hearts be overcharged with surfeiting and drunkenness and the cares of this life: and that day come upon you suddenly. … Watch ye, therefore, praying at all times, that you may be accounted worthy to escape all these things that are to come and to stand before the Son of man.”[^119]

To watch is the whole of the practice. A Christian who keeps a day each month for his death, who chooses by the light of his last hour, who confesses as though for the last time and receives Communion as though it were his Viaticum, who says each day the prayer for a good death and each Hail Mary with attention to its last words, has done what the sage at the bedside asked: in all his works he has remembered his last end. He will not have conquered the fear of death by forgetting it. He will have conquered it as the martyrs did, by loving more than he fears. When the hour comes, whether with warning or without, the Church will be there with her sacraments and her prayers, and he will know her words, because he has said them all his life.

## Scope, Method, and Qualifications

##### Reader and purpose.

This is spiritual reading for Catholics at the close of the liturgical year and for the annual preparation for death. It treats death as the separation of soul and body, its natural and penal character, its transformation by Christ, the hidden hour, Christian grief, the art of dying, the last sacraments, the Church’s prayers for the dying and the dead, and the practice of preparation. The particular and general judgment, purgatory and prayer for the dead, hell, and heaven are treated in the three companion volumes and are named here only where the subject requires it. Questions of medical ethics, the determination of death, and funeral law are outside its scope.

##### Sources.

Scripture is quoted from the Douay–Rheims Bible (Challoner revision) in Vulgate numbering, with Latin from the Clementine Vulgate. The Fathers are quoted in the English of the Ante-Nicene and Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers series and, for the *City of God*, in Marcus Dods’s translation; Aquinas in the translation of the English Dominican Fathers. Later spiritual writers are quoted in public-domain English: the *Imitation* in William Benham’s translation, Francis de Sales in the Rivingtons translation of 1876, Ignatius in Elder Mullan’s, Alphonsus and Leonard of Port Maurice in the Dublin translation of 1843, Bellarmine in John Dalton’s, Thérèse in the edition of T. N. Taylor, and the *Craft of Dying* in Frances Comper’s modernized edition of 1917. The rites are quoted in Latin from the *Rituale Romanum* of 1872 and the Roman Missal, with English from the *Manual of Prayers* prepared by order of the Third Plenary Council of Baltimore (1889) and from Lasance’s *New Roman Missal*. Conciliar acts are cited by Denzinger–Schönmetzer number. The Catechism of the Catholic Church, the Code of Canon Law, and other official texts of the Holy See are quoted briefly in their official English; that text remains the property of the Libreria Editrice Vaticana.

##### Prayers.

Every prayer printed for use is reproduced in an identified received translation. Where only Latin is given, any English beside it is marked as a gloss and is not offered for recitation.

##### Grades.

The grades in the preceding table follow the usual distinctions of Catholic theology. A grade states the authority of the teaching as the cited witnesses present it; it does not replace the judgment of the Church’s magisterium.

##### Law and currency.

References to the Code of Canon Law are to the 1983 Code of the Latin Church, as of 16 October 2026. Liturgical references name the book from which they are taken. Nothing here is pastoral or canonical advice for a particular case; the dying and those who care for them should call a priest.
