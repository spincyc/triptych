## The Son of Man in His Majesty {#sec:majesty}

As the liturgical year draws to its close the Church lifts her eyes to its end. On the Last Sunday after Pentecost the 1962 Missal reads the discourse that Christ gave his disciples “sitting on mount Olivet,” when they asked him what should be the sign of his coming and of the consummation of the world (Matt 24:3). He speaks of the abomination of desolation standing in the holy place, of a tribulation such as has not been from the beginning of the world, of false christs and false prophets, of the sun darkened and the stars falling from heaven; and then of himself:

> And then shall appear the sign of the Son of man in heaven. And then shall all tribes of the earth mourn: and they shall see the Son of man coming in the clouds of heaven with much power and majesty. And he shall send his angels with a trumpet and a great voice: and they shall gather together his elect from the four winds, from the farthest parts of the heavens to the utmost bounds of them. (Matt 24:30–31)

The Gospel of that Sunday ends on a sentence that gathers the whole year into its hand: “Heaven and earth shall pass: but my words shall not pass” (Matt 24:35). A week later the year begins again, and the first Sunday of Advent reads in the same Missal the same prophecy as Luke records it, with the sun and moon and stars, the distress of nations, the roaring of the sea, and men “withering away for fear and expectation of what shall come upon the whole world.” Then, into the midst of the terror, a command: “But when these things begin to come to pass, look up and lift up your heads, because your redemption is at hand” (Luke 21:25–28). The Church ends one year and begins the next with the same coming before her eyes, read once in fear and once in hope.

In the Postconciliar Missal the year closes on the Solemnity of Christ the King, and in the year of Matthew (Year A) its Gospel is the scene with which the discourse on Olivet ends, the last thing Christ taught before he said to his disciples that after two days the Son of man would be delivered up to be crucified (Matt 26:1–2):

> And when the Son of man shall come in his majesty, and all the angels with him, then shall he sit upon the seat of his majesty. And all nations shall be gathered together before him: and he shall separate them one from another, as the shepherd separateth the sheep from the goats: and he shall set the sheep on his right hand, but the goats on his left. Then shall the king say to them that shall be on his right hand: Come, ye blessed of my Father, possess you the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world. (Matt 25:31–34)

Then follow the six works: the hungry fed, the thirsty given drink, the stranger taken in, the naked clothed, the sick visited, the prisoner sought out; and the word that turns every one of them toward Christ himself: “Amen I say to you, as long as you did it to one of these my least brethren, you did it to me” (Matt 25:40). To those on the left he says: “Depart from me, you cursed, into everlasting fire, which was prepared for the devil and his angels” (25:41). And the scene closes on a single sentence with two halves of equal weight: “And these shall go into everlasting punishment: but the just, into life everlasting” (25:46).

The Church has never treated this as one teaching among others. She confesses it every Sunday in the Creed, which says of the Son that “he will come again in glory to judge the living and the dead”;[^1] and Benedict XVI observes that from the earliest times this truth of the Creed “has influenced Christians in their daily living as a criterion by which to order their present life, as a summons to their conscience, and at the same time as hope in God’s justice.”[^2] Order, summons, and hope: those three words will serve as well as any to describe what the doctrine of judgment is for.

### The Church’s own meditation

The Church has also given her children the words in which to meditate on that day. On the second of November, when she commemorates all the faithful departed and every priest may offer the Holy Sacrifice three times for the dead, the Mass of Requiem in the 1962 Missal sets between the Epistle and the Gospel the sequence in which the Church herself meditates on the last judgment. It does not begin with an argument. It begins with a day:

> *Dies irae, dies illa,<br>
> Solvet saeclum in favilla:<br>
> Teste David cum Sibylla.<br>
> Quantus tremor est futurus,<br>
> Quando judex est venturus,<br>
> Cuncta stricte discussurus!*[^3]
>
> Day of Wrath! O day of mourning!<br>
> See fulfill’d the prophets’ warning!<br>
> Heav’n and earth in ashes burning!<br>
> Oh, what fear man’s bosom rendeth<br>
> When from Heav’n the Judge descendeth,<br>
> On Whose sentence all dependeth![^4]

The sequence moves as the soul moves when it truly considers the judgment. It begins with the cosmos—the world dissolving into ash, the trumpet sounding through the sepulchres of every land, death and nature struck dumb as the creature rises to answer its Judge. It narrows to a book in which everything is written, and to a Judge before whom whatever is hidden shall appear. Then, without warning, it falls from the third person into the first: *Quid sum miser tunc dicturus?* What shall I, wretched one, say then? From that line to the end it is no longer a description of the judgment but a prayer made in view of it, addressed to the Judge himself, and the name by which it addresses him is *Jesu pie*.

Alphonsus Liguori, writing his considerations on the particular judgment, records that “hearing the *Dies Iræ* sung, and reflecting on the terror of the soul when she shall be presented before the tribunal of Jesus Christ, the venerable P. Juvenal Ancina took, and afterwards executed, the resolution of forsaking the world.”[^5] That is what the Church intends the sequence to do. It is not sung over the dead in order to frighten the living for their own sake. It is sung so that the living, praying for the dead, may learn to pray for themselves while there is time.

### Twice before the Judge

The tradition the sequence sings presents the Christian standing before Christ the Judge twice. The first time is at the moment of death, alone, when the soul leaves the body and its whole life is set at once in the light of Christ: this the Church calls the particular judgment. The second is at his coming in glory, when all the dead shall rise in their own bodies and the whole human race shall stand together before the seat of his majesty: this is the general or last judgment. Between them lies, for many of the saved, the purification the Church calls purgatory, and over them both lies the charity by which the living help the dead with prayer, alms, and above all the Sacrifice of the altar.

These are not two stories about the same event, nor a provisional hearing followed by the real one. The sentence passed at death is final; the judgment at the end of the world is no less real. The Church holds both with the same firmness and joins them, in the acts that define them, with a single word: *nihilominus*, “nonetheless.” To meditate on judgment in the last weeks of the year is to learn why she holds both, and what each asks of a Christian who must one day stand before the Son of Man and does not know the day or the hour.

## At the Evening of Life {#sec:particular}

“It is appointed unto men once to die, and after this the judgment” (Heb 9:27). The Apostle does not say how long after, and the Church does not leave the question to conjecture. She teaches that the judgment of each soul follows death at once: “Each man receives his eternal retribution in his immortal soul at the very moment of his death, in a particular judgment that refers his life to Christ: either entrance into the blessedness of heaven—through a purification or immediately,—or immediate and everlasting damnation.”[^6] The Catechism sets beside that sentence a single line of St John of the Cross, which has become the briefest summary of the whole matter: “At the evening of life, we shall be judged on our love.”[^7]

The Scriptures speak of this judgment first by its effects. The beggar Lazarus dies and is “carried by the angels into Abraham’s bosom,” while the rich man is buried and lifts up his eyes in torment, and between them “there is fixed a great chaos” that no one can cross (Luke 16:22–26). To the thief who asks to be remembered Christ answers: “Amen I say to thee: This day thou shalt be with me in paradise” (Luke 23:43). Paul, weighing life against death, is “straitened between two: having a desire to be dissolved and to be with Christ, a thing by far the better” (Phil 1:23); and he writes to the Corinthians that “we are confident and have a good will to be absent rather from the body and to be present with the Lord” (2 Cor 5:8). Aquinas rests the whole doctrine on that last verse. Against those who held that the souls of the saints would not be rewarded, nor the souls of the lost punished, until the judgment day, he answers that to be present with the Lord is no longer to walk by faith but by sight, and that this is “to see God in His Essence, wherein consists ‘eternal life’ ”; and he concludes: “Hence it is manifest that the souls separated from bodies are in eternal life.”[^8]

### *Benedictus Deus*

What the schools commonly taught, the Church defined. In the 1330s a dispute had arisen in which the Pope himself had taken part: whether the purified souls of the just, separated from their bodies, see the divine essence with the vision the Apostle calls face to face before they take up their bodies again. John XXII closed his own share in it with a declaration of 3 December 1334, confessing that the purified souls separated from their bodies are in heaven with Christ in the company of the angels, and see God and the divine essence face to face, clearly, so far as the condition of a separated soul allows: *vident Deum de communi lege ac divinam essentiam facie ad faciem clare, in quantum status et condicio compatitur animae separatae*.[^9] On 29 January 1336 his successor Benedict XII settled the matter for the whole Church in the constitution *Benedictus Deus*, and he did so in the most solemn form a pope can use: *Hac in perpetuum valitura Constitutione auctoritate Apostolica diffinimus*: by a constitution to stand for ever, and by apostolic authority, he defines.[^10]

The definition is worth reading phrase by phrase, because every phrase carries weight.

It speaks first of whom it defines: the souls of the saints who died before Christ’s Passion; of the apostles, martyrs, confessors, virgins, and all the faithful who died after receiving his baptism; and of baptized children who die before the use of free will. It then distinguishes, among the adult faithful, two conditions. There are those *in quibus nihil purgabile fuit, quando decesserunt*—in whom there was nothing to be purged when they died; and there are those in whom there was something to be purged, *cum post mortem suam fuerint purgatae*, when they have been purged after their death. Purgatory enters the definition here, as a clause within the definition of the blessed. It is not set over against heaven as a third destiny; it is the way into heaven for those who need it.

Then comes the word on which the whole constitution turns: *mox*. Straightway after their death, and after that purification in those who needed it, *etiam ante resumptionem suorum corporum et iudicium generale*—even before the resumption of their bodies and the general judgment—these souls “have been, are and will be in heaven, in the heavenly Kingdom and celestial paradise with Christ, joined to the company of the holy angels.” And there they have seen and do see the divine essence *visione intuitiva et etiam faciali*, “with an intuitive vision, and even face to face, without the mediation of any creature,”[^11] the divine essence showing itself to them *immediate … nude, clare et aperte*, immediately, nakedly, clearly and openly. Seeing, they enjoy; enjoying, they are truly blessed and have eternal life and rest. The vision once begun is never interrupted, but continues *usque ad finale iudicium et ex tunc usque in sempiternum*, until the final judgment and from then on for ever.[^12]

The constitution then turns, in a single sentence, to the other side: *animae decedentium in actuali peccato mortali mox post mortem suam ad inferna descendunt*—the souls of those who die in actual mortal sin go down to hell straightway after their death, to be punished there.[^13] The same *mox* stands on both sides. There is no waiting-room between the deathbed and the soul’s destiny, no sleep of the soul, no interval in which a life already ended is still under deliberation. The particular judgment is not a preliminary hearing. It is the sentence itself, passed and carried out at the instant of death.

### What the moment holds

The saints who taught the faithful to prepare for death meditated on that instant with great concreteness. Alphonsus, opening his consideration on the particular judgment with Paul’s “We must all be manifested before the judgment-seat of Christ,” asks the reader to consider “the appearance, the accusation, the examination, and the sentence.” Of the first he writes: “it is the common opinion of theologians, that the particular judgment takes place at the very moment of death; and that on the very spot where the soul is separated from the body, she is judged by Jesus Christ, who shall not send, but will come himself to judge her according to her works.”[^14] He does not spare the reader the fear of it, and he cites Augustine to say that Christ will come “in love to the good, in terror to the wicked.” But his first response is not to describe the terror further. It is to pray:

> O my Jesus, I will always call you Jesus. Your name consoles and encourages me, because it reminds me that you are my Saviour, who have died for my salvation. … “Recordare, Jesu pie, quod sum causa tuæ viæ.” Pardon me, then, immediately, O my Jesus, before you come to judge me. I shall not then be able to ask pardon; I can now ask it from you, and I hope for it. Your wounds shall then fill me with terror, but now they give me confidence.[^15]

The line he borrows is from the *Dies irae*, which Irons renders: “Think, good Jesu, my salvation / Caused Thy wondrous Incarnation.” The saint understood that the particular judgment is not a stranger’s verdict. The Judge who will meet the soul at death is the same Jesus who came down for it, sought it, and died for it, and who now waits for it to ask what at death it will be too late to ask. The whole spiritual meaning of the particular judgment lies in that word *now*. Benedict XVI says it plainly: “With death, our life-choice becomes definitive—our life stands before the judge.”[^16] Whatever a man has made of himself by the time he dies, that he is before Christ, and nothing more can be added to it or taken from it. “In such state as a man departeth out of this life,” Gregory the Great teaches, “in the same he is presented in judgment before God.”[^17]

From this the masters of the spiritual life drew one practical conclusion above all others: to be judged now, by one’s own choice, rather than then. Alphonsus makes his own the saying he attributes to Bernard, *Volo judicatus praesentari, non judicandus*—I wish to be presented already judged, not to be judged—and turns it into a prayer: “O my Judge, I wish to be judged and punished during life, which is a time of mercy and pardon; for, after death shall be the time of justice.”[^18] Every confession made in sorrow is such a judgment. The sinner stands before Christ in the person of the priest, accuses himself, and receives a sentence of mercy; and the soul that has learned to come to that tribunal often will not come to the other as a stranger.

## Nonetheless, on the Day of Judgment {#sec:general}

If the soul’s case is closed at death and its sentence carried out at once, the Christian may well ask what remains for the last day. The Church has never answered by weakening the first judgment. She answers by setting the second beside it with a single adverb.

The profession of faith of the emperor Michael Palaeologus, received at the Second Council of Lyons in 1274, first teaches that the souls of the just, purified if they need it, are received into heaven *mox*, straightway, and that the souls of those who die in mortal sin go down *mox* into hell. Then it continues: *Eadem sacrosancta Ecclesia Romana firmiter credit et firmiter asseverat, quod nihilominus in die iudicii omnes homines ante tribunal Christi cum suis corporibus comparebunt, reddituri de propriis factis rationem*. The same holy Roman Church firmly believes and firmly asserts that *nonetheless* on the day of judgment all men will appear with their bodies before the tribunal of Christ, to render an account of their own deeds.[^19] Some sixty years later *Benedictus Deus*, having defined the immediate vision of the blessed and the immediate descent of the damned, uses the same word again: *et quod nihilominus in die iudicii omnes homines “ante tribunal Christi” cum suis corporibus comparebunt, reddituri de factis propriis rationem, “ut referat unusquisque propria corporis, prout gessit, sive bonum sive malum”*.[^20] The words it takes from Paul are those of the second letter to the Corinthians: “For we must all be manifested before the judgment seat of Christ, that every one may receive the proper things of the body, according as he hath done, whether it be good or evil” (2 Cor 5:10).

*Nihilominus*: nonetheless, none the less for that. The souls are already where they will be for ever, and none the less all will stand before Christ. The Church does not explain the word away. She lets both truths stand at their full height and binds them together.

### The body that must return

The general judgment has a condition that the particular judgment lacks: the resurrection. The Fourth Lateran Council confesses that Christ will come at the end of the age to judge the living and the dead and to render to each according to his works, to the reprobate and to the elect alike, *qui omnes cum suis propriis resurgent corporibus, quae nunc gestant*—who shall all rise with their own bodies, the bodies they now bear.[^21] The Catechism draws the order out plainly: “The resurrection of all the dead, ‘of both the just and the unjust,’ will precede the Last Judgment.”[^22] On All Souls’ Day the first Mass of the dead in the 1962 Missal reads the Gospel in which Christ announces it: “the hour cometh wherein all that are in the graves shall hear the voice of the Son of God. And they that have done good things shall come forth unto the resurrection of life: but they that have done evil, unto the resurrection of judgment” (John 5:28–29).

At death, then, what is judged is the soul, separated from the body it animated. At the last day it is the whole man who stands before Christ, soul and body together, the same man who lived and acted and sinned and repented in that flesh. Aquinas finds here one of the deepest reasons for the second judgment. The soul, he says, once separated from the body, “enters into an unchangeable condition, and receives its judgment. But the body remains subject to change down to the close of time: and therefore it must receive its reward or punishment then, in the last Judgment.”[^23] The body that was buried with honour or left without burial, that was laid in consecrated ground or scattered, that has fallen to dust—that body too belongs to the man, and it too will receive its due. The Church, which has the Creed of the resurrection on her lips, has never held that a man is only his soul.

The 1979 letter of the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith states the order of these mysteries in the language of the Council: the Church “looks for ‘the glorious manifestation of our Lord, Jesus Christ’ … believing it to be distinct and deferred with respect to the situation of people immediately after death.”[^24] The coming of Christ is not the soul’s private meeting with him at death writ large. It is a distinct event, still awaited, and it is the event at which the general judgment takes place.

### What a life leaves behind

Why must there be a judgment of all things together, when each soul has already received its due? Aquinas answers from the nature of judgment itself. “Judgment cannot be passed perfectly upon any changeable subject before its consummation: just as judgment cannot be given perfectly regarding the quality of any action before its completion in itself and in its results: because many actions appear to be profitable, which in their effects prove to be hurtful.” A man cannot be judged before his life is over, for he may still change; that is why “It is appointed unto men once to die, and after this the Judgment.” But a life does not wholly end when the man dies. “Although man’s temporal life in itself ends with death,” Aquinas continues, “still it continues dependent in a measure on what comes after it in the future.” And he names five ways:

> In one way, as it still lives on in men’s memories, in which sometimes, contrary to the truth, good or evil reputations linger on. In another way in a man’s children, who are so to speak something of their parent … And yet many good men have wicked sons, and conversely. Thirdly, as to the result of his actions: just as from the deceit of Arius and other false leaders unbelief continues to flourish down to the close of the world; and even until then faith will continue to derive its progress from the preaching of the apostles. In a fourth way, as to the body, which is sometimes buried with honor and sometimes left unburied, and finally falls to dust utterly. In a fifth way, as to the things upon which a man’s heart is set, such as temporal concerns, for example, some of which quickly lapse, while others endure longer.[^25]

“Now all these things are submitted to the verdict of the Divine Judgment; and consequently, a perfect and public Judgment cannot be made of all these things during the course of this present time. Wherefore, there must be a final Judgment at the last day, in which everything concerning every man in every respect shall be perfectly and publicly judged.”

The list rewards slow reading. A man’s name outlives him, and is sometimes slandered and sometimes undeservedly praised. His children carry something of him into the world, and are sometimes unlike him. His body lies where it was laid. What his heart was set on survives him for a while, or crumbles at once. And above all, his deeds go on working. Arius did not cease to deceive when he died; the deception he set going went on doing its work in souls he never met. The apostles did not cease to preach when they were martyred; the faith they planted goes on bearing fruit in every generation, and will until the end. Aquinas is careful to add that by such things “a man neither merits nor demerits”—his merit was fixed at death—“still in a measure they accompany his reward or punishment.”[^26] The good a man did, the evil he began, the scandal he gave, the grace he passed on: none of it is finished while the world lasts, and none of it can be fully seen until the world is over.

So the second judgment is no second trial of the same case. To the objection drawn from Nahum in the Septuagint, “God shall not judge twice the same thing,” Aquinas answers with a gloss on the words themselves, “i.e. in the same respect; but it is not unseemly for God to judge twice according to different respects.”[^27] The Supplement to the *Summa* states the principle in a single sentence: “Each man is both an individual person and a part of the whole human race: wherefore a twofold judgment is due to him.”[^28] The same article adds that the reward of the just is completed at the last day, for they “will receive an increase of reward after the judgment, both from the added glory of the body and from the completion of the number of the saints.” The blessed who already see God will see him then in the flesh, and they will see him in the company of all who are to be saved, the number of the elect at last made full.

### The hidden justice made plain

Augustine, beginning his book on the last judgment in *The City of God*, reminds his reader that God is not idle until that day. He has judged from the beginning, and he judges now. Yet the day is rightly called the day of judgment, “because in it there shall be no room left for the ignorant questioning why this wicked person is happy and that righteous man unhappy.”[^29] In this life, he writes, “we do not know by what judgment of God this good man is poor and that bad man rich,” why the innocent is condemned at the bar and the guilty goes free, why the useful man dies young and the wicked lives long. “But when we shall have come to that judgment … we shall then recognise the justice of all God’s judgments, not only of such as shall then be pronounced, but of all which take effect from the beginning,” even of those that now “defy the scrutiny of human sense or insight.” Until then, he adds, “it is not concealed from pious minds that what is concealed is just.”[^30]

The Supplement gives the same reason in the language of the schools: at the general judgment “the Divine justice will be made manifest in all things, whereas now it remains hidden.”[^31] And the Catechism gathers both into one sentence of great solemnity: “Then through his Son Jesus Christ he will pronounce the final word on all history. We shall know the ultimate meaning of the whole work of creation and of the entire economy of salvation and understand the marvellous ways by which his Providence led everything towards its final end.”[^32]

Here is the consolation hidden in the terror of the last day. Benedict XVI, reflecting on the long cry of the poor and the innocent through history, writes that “faith in the Last Judgement is first and foremost hope.” “There is a resurrection of the flesh. There is justice. There is an ‘undoing’ of past suffering, a reparation that sets things aright.”[^33] Every martyr unavenged, every slandered saint, every child wronged in secret, every good work done unseen and forgotten, waits for that day. Paul forbids the Christian to anticipate it: “Therefore, judge not before the time: until the Lord come, who both will bring to light the hidden things of darkness and will make manifest the counsels of the hearts.” And he ends the sentence with a promise: “And then shall every man have praise from God” (1 Cor 4:5).

The particular judgment, then, is a verdict; the general judgment is its vindication. At death the truth about a soul is pronounced, and it is final. At the last day that truth is shown, to all, with everything that flowed from the life in question, in the risen body that lived it. Nothing is reconsidered, and nothing is left hidden.

## The Judge Who Was Judged {#sec:judge}

“For neither does the Father judge any man: but hath given all judgment to the Son” (John 5:22). And a few verses later: “he hath given him power to do judgment, because he is the Son of man” (5:27). The Judge before whom every soul will stand at death, and before whom the whole race will be gathered at the end, is not an abstraction of justice, nor the Father hidden in unapproachable light, but the Word made flesh, who has a human face and bears in his hands and side the marks of his Passion. The Church sings it in the *Te Deum*: *Judex crederis esse venturus*: thou art believed to be the Judge who is to come.[^34] And at once she adds a petition, which Alphonsus takes into his own prayer: *Te ergo quaesumus tuis famulis subveni, quos pretioso sanguine redemisti*—“We therefore beseech thee, assist thy servants, whom thou hast redeemed with thy precious blood.”[^35]

### Why the Son of Man judges

Aquinas asks why judgment belongs to Christ not only as God but also as man. The ground he gives first is headship: Christ even in his human nature is head of the whole Church, and the power to judge belongs to the head. He then gives three reasons why it is fitting. The second is that at the last day the bodies of the dead will be raised through the Son of man; the third, that those who are to be judged should see their Judge. The first is one a frightened sinner can hold on to. Judiciary power belongs to Christ in his human nature “because of His likeness and kinship with men; for, as God works through intermediary causes, as being closer to the effects, so He judges men through the Man Christ, that His judgment may be sweeter to men.”[^36] And he cites at once the Epistle to the Hebrews: “For we have not a high priest who cannot have compassion on our infirmities: but one tempted in all things like as we are, without sin. Let us go therefore with confidence to the throne of grace” (Heb 4:15–16).

In the next article Aquinas adds that Christ received this power not only by right of his divinity and his headship but also by merit, “so that, in accordance with the Divine justice, He should be judge who fought for God’s justice, and conquered, and was unjustly condemned.”[^37] The Catechism says the same in fewer words: “Full right to pass definitive judgement on the works and hearts of men belongs to him as redeemer of the world. He ‘acquired’ this right by his cross.”[^38] The Judge of the living and the dead is the one who stood before Pilate and was condemned. He knows from within what it is to be judged unjustly, and he will judge no one unjustly.

This is why the *Dies irae*, having trembled before the *Rex tremendae majestatis*, does not flee from him but appeals to him. The appeal rests entirely on what he has already done:

> *Quaerens me, sedisti lassus:<br>
> Redemisti, crucem passus:<br>
> Tantus labor non sit cassus.*

The singer reminds Christ that he sat down weary in seeking him and redeemed him by suffering the cross, and begs that so great a labour may not be in vain. The words recall Jesus “wearied with his journey,” sitting on Jacob’s well where the Samaritan woman came to draw water (John 4:6–7), and they see in that weariness the whole of his search for the soul. The Judge is the Shepherd who went after the lost sheep. To fear him rightly is to fear wasting that labour; and the only answer to such fear is to let him find us.

### The least of the brethren

When Christ describes his judgment in Matthew 25, the matter of it is astonishingly plain. He does not ask the nations what they believed about him or how much they understood; he asks whether they fed him when he was hungry and visited him when he was sick. John Chrysostom, preaching on the passage, marvels at how little is asked:

> And mark how easy are His injunctions. He said not, “I was in prison, and ye set me free; I was sick, and ye raised me up again;” but, “ye visited me,” and, “ye came unto me.” And neither in hunger is the thing commanded grievous. For no costly table did He seek, but what is needful only, and His necessary food, and He sought in a suppliant’s garb …[^39]

Who are the least of his brethren? Chrysostom answers: “Why, for this reason they are brethren, because they are lowly, because they are poor, because they are outcast. For such doth He most invite to brotherhood, the unknown, the contemptible.” He refuses to restrict the word to monks or to the holy: “every believer; though he be a secular person, yet if he be hungry, and famishing, and naked, and a stranger, His will is he should have the benefit of all this care. For baptism renders a man a brother, and the partaking of the divine mysteries.”[^40] The Catechism, expounding the last judgment, quotes Augustine putting the same thought into Christ’s mouth: “I placed my poor little ones on earth for you. I as their head was seated in heaven at the right hand of my Father—but on earth my members were suffering, my members on earth were in need. If you gave anything to my members, what you gave would reach their Head.”[^41]

Two things in the scene are easy to pass over and should not be. The first is that both the blessed and the cursed are surprised. “Lord, when did we see thee hungry and fed thee: thirsty and gave thee drink?” ask the just; “Lord, when did we see thee hungry … and did not minister to thee?” ask the others (Matt 25:37, 44). Neither group knew that it was serving or refusing Christ. The just do not recognize their own record, because charity does not keep accounts of itself; a man who could produce the ledger of his good works at the judgment would be showing something other than charity.

The second is the difference between the two sentences. To the blessed the King says, “Come, ye blessed of my Father, possess you the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world.” To the others, “Depart from me, you cursed, into everlasting fire, which was prepared for the devil and his angels.” Chrysostom weighs every word. The cursed are “no longer of the Father; for not He laid the curse upon them, but their own works”; and the fire was not prepared for them:

> For concerning the kingdom indeed, when He had said, “Come, inherit the kingdom,” He added, “prepared for you before the foundation of the world;” but concerning the fire, no longer so, but, “prepared for the devil.” I, saith He, prepared the kingdom for you, but the fire no more for you, but “for the devil and his angels;” but since ye cast yourselves therein, impute it to yourselves.[^42]

The kingdom is prepared for men from the foundation of the world; hell is not. God’s first intention for every soul is the kingdom, and the Judge who pronounces the sentence is the same who prepared it. That is the light in which the Christian looks toward the judgment seat. Benedict XVI ends his reflection on the last judgment with the counsel that grace “allows us all to hope, and to go trustfully to meet the Judge whom we know as our ‘advocate’, or *parakletos*.”[^43] So the Apostle John had written: “if any man sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the just” (1 John 2:1). The Advocate and the Judge are one.

## The Books Were Opened {#sec:books}

The Apocalypse gives the last judgment its most enduring image:

> And I saw a great white throne and one sitting upon it, from whose face the earth and heaven fled away: and there was no place found for them. And I saw the dead, great and small, standing in the presence of the throne. And the books were opened: and another book was opened, which is the book of life. And the dead were judged by those things which were written in the books, according to their works. (Apoc 20:11–12)

The *Dies irae* takes up the image in two strophes:

> *Liber scriptus proferetur,<br>
> In quo totum continetur,<br>
> Unde mundus judicetur.<br>
> Judex ergo cum sedebit,<br>
> Quidquid latet, apparebit:<br>
> Nil inultum remanebit.*
>
> Lo! the Book exactly worded,<br>
> Wherein all hath been recorded;<br>
> Thence shall judgment be awarded.<br>
> When the Judge His seat attaineth,<br>
> And each hidden deed arraigneth,<br>
> Nothing unavenged remaineth.[^44]

What is this book in which everything is written? Augustine, reading the chapter closely in the twentieth book of *The City of God*, understood the plural books to be the Scriptures of the Old and New Testaments, “that out of them it might be shown what commandments God had enjoined,” and the single book, the book of each man’s life, to show “what commandments each man has done or omitted to do.” Then he asks how such a book could be read:

> If this book be materially considered, who can reckon its size or length, or the time it would take to read a book in which the whole life of every man is recorded? Shall there be present as many angels as men, and shall each man hear his life recited by the angel assigned to him? … We must therefore understand it of a certain divine power, by which it shall be brought about that every one shall recall to memory all his own works, whether good or evil, and shall mentally survey them with a marvellous rapidity, so that this knowledge will either accuse or excuse conscience, and thus all and each shall be simultaneously judged. And this divine power is called a book, because in it we shall as it were read all that it causes us to remember.[^45]

The book, then, is memory, opened by God. Nothing will be read to the soul from outside that it does not already carry within it. Every word, every act, every omission, every secret consent, everything that the passing of years has buried and the habit of excuse has softened, will be recalled at once and seen as it is. Paul had said as much of the Gentiles, “their conscience bearing witness to them: and their thoughts between themselves accusing or also defending one another, in the day when God shall judge the secrets of men by Jesus Christ” (Rom 2:15–16). Christ himself extends the account to the smallest coin of human life: “every idle word that men shall speak, they shall render an account for it in the day of judgment. For by thy words thou shalt be justified, and by thy words thou shalt be condemned” (Matt 12:36–37). And he promises that the account will be public: “For there is nothing covered that shall not be revealed: nor hidden that shall not be known. For whatsoever things you have spoken in darkness shall be published in the light” (Luke 12:2–3).

Alphonsus, following the ancient reading, speaks of two books at the particular judgment: “There shall be two books, the Gospel and conscience. In the Gospel shall be read what the accused should have done, and in his conscience, what he has done.”[^46] The two together make the whole judgment: the law of Christ, and the life measured against it.

### Reading the book now

The saints drew from this doctrine a practice that the Church has never ceased to commend: the examination of conscience. If the book of one’s life will be opened at death, it can be opened beforehand, by the soul itself, in the presence of God, while there is still time to write in it a different ending. Francis de Sales, in the meditation on judgment that he set in the first part of the *Introduction to the Devout Life*, leads the reader through the separation of the good from the wicked and then says:

> This separation made, the books of conscience will be opened, and all men will behold the malice of the wicked, and how they have contemned God; as also the penitence of the good, and the results of the grace they received. Nothing will be hid. O my God, what confusion to the one, what rejoicing to the other![^47]

Notice what the good have to show: not their innocence, but “the penitence of the good, and the results of the grace they received.” The book of the saved is not a blank page. It is a page on which sins are written and also their repentance, and over both the grace of God that brought the one out of the other. De Sales draws the conclusion in the affections that follow:

> Abhor your sins, which alone can cause you to be lost when that fearful day comes. Surely I will judge myself now, that I be not judged;—I will examine my conscience, accuse, condemn, punish myself, that the Judge may not condemn me then. I will confess my faults, and follow the counsels given me.[^48]

His instruction is to end the meditation by thanking God “for having given you means of safety in that terrible Day, and time for repentance.”[^49] The time for repentance is the present. The book is still open, and the hand that writes in it is still one’s own.

Augustine’s image is also a comfort. A book written by an accuser might contain lies; the book God opens at the judgment contains only the truth, and the truth includes every act of contrition, every confession, every sin that the blood of Christ has washed. That is why de Sales can say that the good will be seen in their penitence and in the fruits of grace: what has been confessed and forgiven stands in the book as forgiven. The soul that has learned to read its own book honestly before the Lord, day by day, will find at the judgment that nothing in it is a surprise, and that the last line on every page is mercy.

## A Mercy Shown to the Saved {#sec:purgatory}

Between the particular judgment and the vision of God there stands, for many of the saved, a purification. The Church has taught it at two councils in nearly the same words. At Lyons in 1274 and again at Florence in 1439 she confessed that those who die truly penitent in charity, before they have made satisfaction by worthy fruits of penance for what they did and left undone, are purified after death by purgatorial pains; Florence’s words are *eorum animas poenis purgatoriis post mortem purgari*, where the Lyons profession had added after *purgatoriis* the Greek-derived synonym *seu catharteriis* and a clause naming the friar who had explained it.[^50] The Council of Trent, in its last session, recalled that the Church, “instructed by the Holy Ghost, has, from the sacred writings, and the ancient tradition of the Fathers, taught, in sacred Councils, and very recently in this oecumenical Synod, that there is a Purgatory, and that the souls there detained are helped by the suffrages of the faithful, but principally by the acceptable sacrifice of the altar.”[^51]

Where purgatory stands matters more than anything else about it. In *Benedictus Deus* it appears inside the definition of the blessed: the souls who had something to be purged are in heaven *cum post mortem suam fuerint purgatae*, when they have been purged after their death. The Catechism puts it without qualification: “All who die in God’s grace and friendship, but still imperfectly purified, are indeed assured of their eternal salvation; but after death they undergo purification, so as to achieve the holiness necessary to enter the joy of heaven. The Church gives the name Purgatory to this final purification of the elect, which is entirely different from the punishment of the damned.”[^52]

Purgatory, then, is not a second chance, a probation, or an appeal. No one enters it whose case is still open, and no one is ever lost from it. It lies wholly on the far side of the particular judgment, as the working out of a sentence already passed in the soul’s favour. The tradition kept the mercy of purgatory by making it a mercy shown to the saved. A soul there has heard the word “Come, ye blessed of my Father”; it is on its way.

### The cleansing fire

The Church’s tradition, the Catechism says, “by reference to certain texts of Scripture, speaks of a cleansing fire.”[^53] The first of those texts is Paul’s word on the foundation and what is built on it:

> Every man’s work shall be manifest. For the day of the Lord shall declare it, because it shall be revealed in fire. And the fire shall try every man’s work, of what sort it is. If any man’s work abide, which he hath built thereupon, he shall receive a reward. If any man’s work burn, he shall suffer loss: but he himself shall be saved, yet so as by fire. (1 Cor 3:13–15)

Gregory the Great allows it to be read of the fire to come. “But yet we must believe that before the day of judgment there is a Purgatory fire for certain small sins,” he writes in the *Dialogues*, and he reasons from Christ’s saying that the sin against the Holy Ghost will not be forgiven “neither in this world, nor in the world to come”: “Out of which sentence we learn, that some sins are forgiven in this world, and some other may be pardoned in the next.”[^54] He names the kind of faults he means: “daily idle talk, immoderate laughter, negligence in the care of our family … ignorant errors in matters of no great weight.” And he adds a warning that turns the doctrine back upon the living: “none can be there purged, no, not for the least sins that be, unless in his lifetime he deserved by virtuous works to find such favour in that place.”[^55] The Catechism takes its own words on the purifying fire from this chapter.[^56]

Augustine had taught the same truth plainly before him. Against those who would make every punishment after death purgatorial, he writes in *The City of God*: “temporary punishments are suffered by some in this life only, by others after death, by others both now and then; but all of them before that last and strictest judgment.”[^57] His reserve concerned only the reading of Paul’s verse. He referred the fire of 1 Corinthians 3 chiefly to the tribulations of this life, which try the loves a Christian has built on Christ and burn away whatever was earthly in them; and when he came to the opinion that the spirits of the departed pass through such a fire in the interval between death and the resurrection, he answered with a reverent reserve that the Latin text preserves exactly: *non redarguo, quia forsitan uerum est*—“this I do not contradict, because possibly it is true.”[^58] The Church has taught in council what Augustine already held: there is a purification after death, and the prayers of the living help those who undergo it.

The Church leaves open what this fire is, and her teachers have spoken of it in more than one way. Gregory speaks of it simply as fire, and the Latin schools after him held it to be a real fire: the appendix to the Supplement of the *Summa* names the pain of sense in purgatory “punishment by corporeal fire.”[^59] The Council of Florence, defining the doctrine for Latins and Greeks together, speaks only of purgatorial pains and does not name the fire. Benedict XVI records the view of “some recent theologians” that “the fire which both burns and saves is Christ himself, the Judge and Saviour,” and he develops it with great beauty: “Before his gaze all falsehood melts away. This encounter with him, as it burns us, transforms and frees us, allowing us to become truly ourselves. … But it is a blessed pain, in which the holy power of his love sears through us like a flame, enabling us to become totally ourselves and thus totally of God.”[^60]

### Joy in the fire

No saint has spoken of purgatory from within more movingly than Catherine of Genoa. Her *Treatise on Purgatory* rests on what she says God made known to her “by special grace”; it is the private revelation of a canonized saint, which the Church honours but has not made her own doctrine. It describes the souls there as wholly given over to the will of God, unable any longer to sin or to wish anything but what God wills. Her teaching holds together two things that seem incompatible. The souls suffer, and they rejoice:

> I do not believe it would be possible to find any joy comparable to that of a soul in purgatory, except the joy of the blessed in paradise,—a joy which goes on increasing day by day, as God more and more flows in upon the soul, which He does abundantly in proportion as every hindrance to His entrance is consumed away. The hindrance is the rust of sin; the fire consumes the rust, and thus the soul goes on laying itself open to the Divine inflowing.[^61]

And yet, she continues, “they suffer a torment so extreme, that no tongue could describe it, no intellect could form the least idea of it, if God had not made it known by special grace.”[^62] The pain is the pain of love delayed: the soul sees God as its whole good and is held back for a time from him by what remains in it of sin. The joy is the joy of love that knows it is going home. Both grow from the same root, and neither cancels the other.

Here is the deepest reason why purgatory is a mercy. No one would wish to enter the presence of God carrying what he would be ashamed to bring there. The *Imitation of Christ* draws the practical conclusion for the living: “Better is it now to purify the soul from sin, than to cling to sins from which we must be purged hereafter.”[^63] The same purification can be begun here, by penance patiently borne, by injuries forgiven, by daily faults confessed and amended. Every suffering accepted in union with Christ is purgatory begun on this side of death.

## Remember Me at the Altar of the Lord {#sec:suffrage}

The Church has never prayed for the dead because a theology persuaded her to. She prayed for them from the beginning, and her theology of purgatory is in great part the account she gives of her own prayer. The Catechism says so directly: the teaching on purification “is also based on the practice of prayer for the dead, already mentioned in Sacred Scripture,” and it points to Judas Machabeus, who after the battle sent an offering to Jerusalem “for sacrifice to be offered for the sins of the dead” (2 Mach 12:43).[^64] The sacred writer comments: “It is therefore a holy and wholesome thought to pray for the dead, that they may be loosed from sins” (12:46).

### The witness of the Fathers

The earliest Fathers speak of the practice as something every Christian already knows. Tertullian lists it among the customs that the Church keeps by tradition, beside the renunciations of baptism and the sign of the cross: “As often as the anniversary comes round, we make offerings for the dead as birthday honours.”[^65] Year by year, on the anniversary of a Christian’s death, the Church offered for him.

Cyril of Jerusalem, explaining the Eucharistic liturgy to the newly baptized, describes the commemoration of the departed that follows, “after the spiritual sacrifice, the bloodless service, is completed”:

> Then we commemorate also those who have fallen asleep before us, first Patriarchs, Prophets, Apostles, Martyrs, that at their prayers and intercessions God would receive our petition. Then on behalf also of the Holy Fathers and Bishops who have fallen asleep before us, and in a word of all who in past years have fallen asleep among us, believing that it will be a very great benefit to the souls, for whom the supplication is put up, while that holy and most awful sacrifice is set forth.[^66]

To those who asked what such a commemoration could profit a soul that had died in sin, Cyril answered with a parable. If a king had banished certain men who had offended him, and their kinsmen wove a crown and offered it to him on their behalf, would he not grant a remission of their penalties? “In the same way we, when we offer to Him our supplications for those who have fallen asleep, though they be sinners, weave no crown, but offer up Christ sacrificed for our sins, propitiating our merciful God for them as well as for ourselves.”[^67]

John Chrysostom appeals to the same rite as an apostolic ordinance: “Not in vain did the Apostles order that remembrance should be made of the dead in the dreadful Mysteries. They know that great gain resulteth to them, great benefit.”[^68] And to mourners who asked what they could still do for their dead, he gave an answer that the Catechism has made its own:

> Let us then give them aid and perform commemoration for them. For if the children of Job were purged by the sacrifice of their father, why dost thou doubt that when we too offer for the departed, some consolation arises to them? … Let us not then be weary in giving aid to the departed, both by offering on their behalf and obtaining prayers for them.[^69]

“Why therefore dost thou grieve?” he asks. “Why mourn, when it is in thy power to gather so much pardon for the departed?”

Augustine gives the doctrine its exact form. “Nor can it be denied that the souls of the dead are benefited by the piety of their living friends, who offer the sacrifice of the Mediator, or give alms in the church on their behalf.” But these services help only those who in life have made themselves capable of being helped: “there is a manner of life which is neither so good as not to require these services after death, nor so bad that such services are of no avail after death.” And he draws the sober conclusion that every Christian must draw for himself: “Therefore, it is in this life that all the merit or demerit is acquired, which can either relieve or aggravate a man’s sufferings after this life.”[^70] At his own mother’s deathbed he had heard it asked of him. Monica, dying at Ostia far from her home, told her sons: “Lay this body anywhere, let not the care for it trouble you at all. This only I ask, that you will remember me at the Lord’s altar, wherever you be.”[^71]

### Thirty days of Masses

Gregory the Great tells, in the fourth book of his *Dialogues*, what happened in his own monastery. A monk named Justus, a physician who had cared for Gregory in his illnesses, was found on his deathbed to have hidden three crowns of gold, against the rule that the monks should own nothing of their own. Gregory, grieved, forbade the brethren to comfort him, so that sorrow might purge his heart before death; and Justus, learning why he had been abandoned, “straightways sighed for his sin, and in that sorrow gave up the ghost.” Thirty days after his death Gregory took pity on him:

> “It is now a good while since that our brother which is departed remaineth in the torments of fire, and therefore we must shew him some charity, and labour what we may to procure his delivery: wherefore go your way, and see that for thirty days following sacrifice be offered for him, so that no one day pass in which, for his absolution and discharge, the healthful sacrifice be not offered.”[^72]

On the thirtieth day, Gregory reports, Justus appeared to his brother and said: “Hitherto have I been in bad case, but now I am well; for this day have I received the communion.” Gregory concludes that “the dead monk was by the holy sacrifice delivered from his pains.”[^73] The apparition is Gregory’s report and belongs to pious tradition, not to the Church’s teaching; but he tells the story because it teaches what the Church believes: that the Mass offered for the dead reaches them, and that charity does not end at the grave.

### All Souls

The Church gathers all of this into one day. On the second of November she prays for all the faithful departed at the altar. The Council of Trent, which taught that the souls in purgatory are helped above all by the Sacrifice of the altar, charged the bishops to see that “the suffrages of the faithful who are living, to wit, the sacrifices of masses, prayers, alms, and other works of piety, which have been wont to be performed by the faithful for the other faithful departed, be piously and devoutly performed in accordance with the institutes of the Church.”[^74]

The Offertory of the Requiem, the same in the 1962 Missal as in the older books, prays for the departed in words of great boldness: *Domine Jesu Christe, Rex gloriae, libera animas omnium fidelium defunctorum de poenis inferni, et de profundo lacu: libera eas de ore leonis, ne absorbeat eas tartarus, ne cadant in obscurum: sed signifer sanctus Michael repraesentet eas in lucem sanctam: Quam olim Abrahae promisisti, et semini ejus*.[^75] The Church begs the King of glory to deliver the souls of all the faithful departed from the pains of hell, from the deep pit and the mouth of the lion, that they may not fall into darkness, and that the standard-bearer Saint Michael may bring them into the holy light which God promised of old to Abraham and his seed. It is the prayer of a Church that takes the judgment seriously and her own power to help seriously as well.

Benedict XVI writes of this charity: “The belief that love can reach into the afterlife, that reciprocal giving and receiving is possible, in which our affection for one another continues beyond the limits of death—this has been a fundamental conviction of Christianity throughout the ages and it remains a source of comfort today.” And he adds: “It is never too late to touch the heart of another, nor is it ever in vain.”[^76] The practical resolution is simple, and it belongs especially to November: to have Masses offered for one’s dead, to remember them by name at the altar, to give alms on their behalf, and to pray for those whom no one remembers. The souls who are helped will not forget it; and the one who helps them is learning, against the day of his own death, to trust the charity of the Church.

## The Fear of That Day, and Hope {#sec:fear}

Among the *instrumenta bonorum operum*, the tools of good works that St Benedict sets before his monks in the fourth chapter of his Rule stand three short sentences in a row: *Diem iudicii timere. Gehennam expavescere. Vitam aeternam omni concupiscentia spiritali desiderare.*[^77] To fear the day of judgment; to dread hell; to desire eternal life with all the longing of the spirit. The three belong together, and their order is instructive. Fear comes first, and it is fear of a day and of a place; but the sentence that completes them is desire. The fear the Church teaches is a fear that turns the soul toward what it loves.

The Catechism names this fear and joins it to hope in the same breath. The message of the last judgment “calls men to conversion while God is still giving them ‘the acceptable time, … the day of salvation.’ It inspires a holy fear of God and commits them to the justice of the Kingdom of God.”[^78] And it proclaims, the Catechism continues, the “blessed hope” of the Lord’s return (Tit 2:13). Holy fear and blessed hope are not two moods between which the Christian must choose. They are two movements of one heart before one Judge, and the Scriptures command both: “with fear and trembling work out your salvation” (Phil 2:12); “look up and lift up your heads, because your redemption is at hand” (Luke 21:28).

### The day and the hour

“But of that day and hour no one knoweth: no, not the angels of heaven, but the Father alone” (Matt 24:36). The Lord who will judge has kept from his Church the date of his coming, and the Catechism repeats the reservation without softening it: “Only the Father knows the day and the hour; only he determines the moment of its coming.”[^79] Nor does anyone know the hour of his own death, which is the hour of his particular judgment. The two ignorances are the same ignorance in two forms, and Christ gives one command for both: “Watch ye therefore, because you know not what hour your Lord will come. … Wherefore be you also ready, because at what hour you know not the Son of man will come” (Matt 24:42, 44).

The *Imitation of Christ* turns this watchfulness into a habit of the mind:

> In all that thou doest, remember the end, and how thou wilt stand before a strict judge, from whom nothing is hid, who is not bribed with gifts, nor accepteth excuses, but will judge righteous judgment. O most miserable and foolish sinner, who art sometimes in fear of the countenance of an angry man, what wilt thou answer to God, who knoweth all thy misdeeds? Why dost thou not provide for thyself against the day of judgment, when no man shall be able to be excused or defended by means of another, but each one shall bear his burden himself alone? Now doth thy labour bring forth fruit, now is thy weeping acceptable, thy groaning heard, thy sorrow well pleasing to God, and cleansing to thy soul.[^80]

The passage turns twice on the word *now*. The fear of the judgment is not meant to paralyse but to make the present precious. “Be thou anxious now and sorrowful for thy sins,” the same chapter continues, “that in the day of judgment thou mayest have boldness with the blessed.”[^81]

### Choosing as at the judgment

Ignatius of Loyola made the thought of the judgment an instrument of choice. Among the rules he gives in the *Spiritual Exercises* for making a good and sound election, when a soul must decide how to order its life, the fourth is this:

> The fourth, looking and considering how I shall find myself on the Day of Judgment, to think how I would then want to have deliberated about the present matter, and to take now the rule which I would then wish to have kept, in order that I may then find myself in entire pleasure and joy.[^82]

The rule asks nothing exotic. It asks the soul to borrow, for the present decision, the clarity it will have at the judgment, when every choice will be seen at last for what it was. Ignatius does not offer it to frighten but to free: the soul that chooses as it will wish to have chosen will meet the judgment “in entire pleasure and joy.” A Christian can make such a rule a daily practice. Before a decision, before a word spoken in anger, before a pleasure that the conscience questions, he can ask how it will look when the book is opened, and choose accordingly.

### Perfect love casteth out fear

Fear is not the last word, and the saints never meant it to be. “In this is the charity of God perfected with us, that we may have confidence in the day of judgment. … Fear is not in charity: but perfect charity casteth out fear, because fear hath pain” (1 John 4:17–18). Benedict XVI, asking whether the image of the last judgment is not a frightening one, answers: “I would say: it is an image that evokes responsibility, an image, therefore, of that fear of which Saint Hilary spoke when he said that all our fear has its place in love.” And he sets the whole doctrine on its true foundation: “The image of the Last Judgement is not primarily an image of terror, but an image of hope; for us it may even be the decisive image of hope.”[^83] Justice and grace meet in the Judge. “If it were merely grace, making all earthly things cease to matter, God would still owe us an answer to the question about justice … If it were merely justice, in the end it could bring only fear to us all.”[^84] Because he is both just and merciful, the Christian can fear him and run to him at once.

One judgment the Christian is forbidden to make: the judgment of another’s soul. “Although we can judge that an act is in itself a grave offense,” the Catechism teaches, “we must entrust judgment of persons to the justice and mercy of God.”[^85] The only soul whose judgment a Christian is commanded to anticipate is his own.

### Verdict and vindication

The year ends and the year begins with the same coming before the Church’s eyes. On the last Sunday she hears that heaven and earth shall pass; on the first Sunday of Advent she hears that her redemption is at hand. Between those two Gospels lies the whole Christian life, lived under the eyes of a Judge who has already come once to save and will come again to judge, and whose last word in the Scriptures is a promise: “Surely, I come quickly”. The Church’s answer is the last prayer of the Bible: “Amen. Come, Lord Jesus” (Apoc 22:20).

A man’s own case closes when he dies; what he did goes on working in the world he has left until history itself is over. God has so ordered the two judgments, and the Church confesses both: that a man is answerable at the instant of his death, without extension or appeal, and that he is more than the instant of his answering. The last day is not a rehearing. It is the moment at which the sentence already passed on a soul becomes the truth made visible about a life—the whole of it, including the part that outlived him—before the Judge who died for him, in the sight of all the saints, to the glory of God.

## Scope, Method, and Qualifications {#app:scope}

##### Reader and purpose.

Spiritual reading for Catholics at the close of the liturgical year and in the annual preparation for death: the particular judgment, the general judgment, purgatory, prayer for the dead, and the holy fear and hope with which a Christian awaits Christ the Judge. It is the second of four essays under the title *The Four Last Things*; death itself belongs to *The Debt of Nature*, the pains and eternity of hell to *The Fire That Is Not Extinguished*, and the vision of God to *Then Face to Face*. It is a study aid, not a catechism or an act of the Magisterium.

##### Teaching and grades.

Each teaching is stated in the body at its degree in a single clause and graded in Appendix [A](#app:grades), using six grades: defined; Church teaching; common teaching of the Fathers and Doctors; theological opinion; pious tradition; approved private revelation. Two witnesses rest on private revelation and are labelled where they are introduced: Catherine of Genoa’s *Treatise on Purgatory*, which she says God made known to her by special grace (the private revelation of a canonized saint, honoured but not made the Church’s doctrine), and the appearance of the monk Justus after thirty Masses, which Gregory reports (pious tradition). No approved private revelation is used. Nothing here judges the eternal state of any person.

##### Witnesses and translations.

Scripture is quoted from the Douay–Rheims in Bishop Challoner’s revision, with Vulgate numbering. Conciliar and papal Latin is quoted from the *Enchiridion symbolorum*; where English is given for these acts outside quotation marks it is a paraphrase, and the Catechism’s official English is used where it quotes them. The same holds for the liturgical and monastic Latin: English given outside quotation marks for a line of the *Dies irae*, the *Te Deum*, the Requiem Offertory or the Rule of St Benedict is a paraphrase, not a translation for prayer. Trent is quoted in Waterworth’s English (1848). The liturgical Latin is that of the *Missale Romanum* printed at Tournai in 1889; the 1962 and Postconciliar Missals are named for the readings and chants they appoint. The English of the *Dies irae* is W. J. Irons’s, as printed in *Hymns Ancient and Modern*; the verse of the *Te Deum* on the Judge is taken from the Latin printed in J. H. Blunt’s *Annotated Book of Common Prayer* (1866), and the Rule of St Benedict from the Latin text of Wikisource. The Fathers are quoted in the nineteenth-century translations of the *Ante-Nicene* and *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* and of Marcus Dods (Augustine, *The City of God*); Gregory’s *Dialogues* in the English of 1608 as re-edited in 1911; Aquinas in the translation of the English Dominican Fathers. The Supplement to the *Summa*, quoted from question 88, article 1, was compiled after Aquinas’s death from his commentary on the *Sentences*. Alphonsus is quoted in the Dublin translation of 1843, de Sales in the Rivingtons translation of 1876, Ignatius in Elder Mullan’s of 1914, the *Imitation* in William Benham’s, and Catherine of Genoa in the London translation of 1858.
