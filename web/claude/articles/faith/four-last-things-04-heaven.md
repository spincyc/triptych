## They Shall See His Face

The Church ends her year with her eyes lifted. On the last Sunday after Pentecost the 1962 Missal reads the discourse of the Lord on the end of the age, with its promise that the nations “shall see the Son of man coming in the clouds of heaven with much power and majesty”; and on the First Sunday of Advent, beginning the year again, it reads the same promise in Luke with a command attached: “look up and lift up your heads, because your redemption is at hand.”[^1] The last weeks of the year are full of judgment. The season, though, opened with All Saints, when both Missals read the Beatitudes, among them the promise of the vision: “Blessed are the clean of heart: for they shall see God.” The 1962 Missal sings that verse again at the Communion of the day, while the faithful receive Christ; the Postconciliar Missal sets beside the Gospel a reading from the first epistle of John.[^2]

In it John says what Christians are now, and why they cannot yet know what they will be:

> Behold what manner of charity the Father hath bestowed upon us, that we should be called and should be the sons of God. … Dearly beloved, we are now the sons of God: and it hath not yet appeared what we shall be. We know that when he shall appear we shall be like to him: because we shall see him as he is.[^3]

John first admits what is not known: *It hath not yet appeared what we shall be.* Then he gives a reason, and the reason is a promise: we shall be like him *because* we shall see him as he is. This life is lived by faith and does not give that sight, so no one can yet describe the likeness it will bring. The apostle draws the practical consequence in the next verse: “every one that hath this hope in him sanctifieth himself, as he also is holy.”[^4]

### The city described by what it lacks

The last two chapters of Scripture describe the city of God at length, and nearly every line of the description takes something away. In the new heaven and the new earth “the sea is now no more.” “God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes: and death shall be no more. Nor mourning, nor crying, nor sorrow shall be any more, for the former things are passed away.” “I saw no temple therein. For the Lord God Almighty is the temple thereof, and the Lamb.” “The city hath no need of the sun, nor of the moon, to shine in it.” “The gates thereof shall not be shut by day: for there shall be no night there.” “There shall be no curse any more.”[^5] The Apocalypse speaks of heaven mainly by subtraction, as one must speak of something the hearer has no positive notion of: it removes, one by one, the conditions of this life that wound.

Among the negations stands one clause that takes nothing away:

> And they shall see his face: and his name shall be on their foreheads.[^6]

Several of the subtractions are explained by that presence. There is no temple because the whole city stands before God, and there is no sun because “the glory of God hath enlightened it: and the Lamb is the lamp thereof.”[^7] The gates stand open because nothing is left to fear, and the name written on the foreheads marks the blessed as belonging to the one whose face they see.

Speaking by subtraction also keeps the reader from furnishing heaven with what he already knows. Alphonsus, setting the joys of paradise before his readers, gives them a homely warning about the measure of their imagination:

> Were a horse capable of reasoning, he would, if he expected a rich feast from his master, imagine it to consist in excellent hay and oats; for these are the only species of food of which he has any idea. It is thus we form our notions of the happiness of Heaven.[^8]

Scripture’s own images are another matter, because they are revealed, and the Catechism gathers them: “Scripture speaks of it in images: life, light, peace, wedding feast, wine of the kingdom, the Father’s house, the heavenly Jerusalem, paradise.”[^9] A picture can show a city, a feast, a garden and a multitude in white. It cannot show God, who has no shape, though the city, the feast and the multitude are there for the sake of seeing him.

### Heaven is a Person, seen

The Church rightly speaks of heaven as a place; it is also the reward of the just, and a reunion. The Catechism names what comes first: “The beatific vision, in which God opens himself in an inexhaustible way to the elect, will be the ever-flowing well-spring of happiness, peace, and mutual communion.”[^10]

Since the vision cannot be pictured, what the Church asks of her children at the end of her year is less to imagine heaven than to desire it, with the desire of the psalm: “As the hart panteth after the fountains of water; so my soul panteth after thee, O God. My soul hath thirsted after the strong living God; when shall I come and appear before the face of God?”[^11] And when one of her children is carried out of the church to the grave, she sings, asking the angels to lead the soul into paradise: *In paradisum deducant te Angeli*.

## The Vision of God

Scripture sets the promise against a wall. Moses on the mountain asks, “Shew me thy glory,” and is answered: “Thou canst not see my face: for man shall not see me, and live.” He is set in a hole of the rock, covered by the hand of God while the glory passes, and allowed to see only what is behind.[^12] The Apostle, praising the King of kings, says that he “only hath immortality and inhabiteth light inaccessible: whom no man hath seen, nor can see.”[^13] And yet the same Lord who covered Moses with his hand says on the mountain of the Beatitudes that the clean of heart shall see God, and the same Apostle writes: “We see now through a glass in a dark manner: but then face to face. Now I know in part: but then I shall know even as I am known.”[^14]

### The glory of God is a living man

Among the first Fathers to treat both sides of this at length was Irenaeus of Lyons, writing against those who said that the God the prophets saw could not be the invisible Father. He takes up the hardest text directly. That no man shall see God and live is true, he says, of God’s greatness, and he answers it from God’s love:

> But in respect to His greatness, and His wonderful glory, “no man shall see God and live,” for the Father is incomprehensible; but in regard to His love, and kindness, and as to His infinite power, even this He grants to those who love Him, that is, to see God, which thing the prophets did also predict. “For those things that are impossible with men, are possible with God.” For man does not see God by his own powers; but when He pleases He is seen by men, by whom He wills, and when He wills, and as He wills.[^15]

Then he says what the seeing does to those who see, comparing them to people who stand in light:

> For as those who see the light are within the light, and partake of its brilliancy; even so, those who see God are in God, and receive of His splendour. But \[His\] splendour vivifies them; those, therefore, who see God, do receive life.[^16]

From this he draws a conclusion that answers the words spoken to Moses. Exodus says that no man shall see God and live; Irenaeus writes: “Men therefore shall see God, that they may live, being made immortal by that sight, and attaining even unto God.”[^17] A few lines later he defines that life by its object:

> For the glory of God is a living man; and the life of man consists in beholding God.[^18]

Irenaeus adds that the Word reveals God by degrees, “preserving at the same time the invisibility of the Father, lest man should at any time become a despiser of God, and that he should always possess something towards which he might advance.”[^19]

### What the Church confesses

What Irenaeus taught as a Father the Church confessed as dogma. In 1336 Benedict XII, by the apostolic constitution *Benedictus Deus*, defined that the souls of the saints, once nothing remains in them to be purified, see God even before they take up their bodies again and before the general judgment. The constitution’s teaching on the moment after death and on the judgment that follows is set out in *What Death Does Not Finish*. Here only its words about the seeing are needed, taken one phrase at a time:

> *… viderunt et vident divinam essentiam visione intuitiva et etiam faciali, nulla mediante creatura in ratione obiecti visi se habente, sed divina essentia immediate se nude, clare et aperte eis ostendente…*[^20]

The Catechism renders the heart of it: these souls “have seen and do see the divine essence with an intuitive vision, and even face to face, without the mediation of any creature.”[^21]

*Visione intuitiva*: by an intuitive vision. The blessed do not reason their way to God from his works, as we do now, climbing from the creature to the Creator; they see him directly.

*Et etiam faciali*: and even face to face. The word is Saint Paul’s, taken from “then face to face.” Augustine had already explained that “By ‘the face’ of God we are to understand His manifestation, and not a part of the body”.[^22]

*Nulla mediante creatura in ratione obiecti visi se habente*: no creature standing between, in the role of the thing seen. What the blessed see is God himself, not an image of him, nor a glory less than God, nor a messenger.

*Sed divina essentia immediate se nude, clare et aperte eis ostendente*: but the divine essence showing itself to them immediately, plainly, clearly and openly. In this last clause the subject changes: it is no longer the souls who see but the divine essence, which shows itself to them. The vision is God’s gift of himself, and the soul receives it. The Catechism gives the reason: “Because of his transcendence, God cannot be seen as he is, unless he himself opens up his mystery to man’s immediate contemplation and gives him the capacity for it.”[^23]

A century later the Council of Florence confessed the same vision and added two words from the epistle of John: the purified see clearly God himself, three and one, as he is: *sicuti est*. In the same sentence it defined that one sees more perfectly than another, *pro meritorum tamen diversitate*, according to the diversity of merits.[^24]

### The light of glory

Thomas Aquinas asks how a creature can see what exceeds every creature, in the question of the *Summa* on how God is known by us. He states the difficulty in an image of his own: God is in himself supremely knowable, “But what is supremely knowable in itself, may not be knowable to a particular intellect, on account of the excess of the intelligible object above the intellect; as, for example, the sun, which is supremely visible, cannot be seen by the bat by reason of its excess of light.”[^25] The created mind stands to God as the bat to the sun: by its own nature it cannot look at him. And so Thomas concludes, as Irenaeus had, that the seeing must be given: “the created intellect cannot see the essence of God, unless God by His grace unites Himself to the created intellect, as an object made intelligible to it.”[^26]

If the seeing must be given, something must change on the creature’s side. For this Thomas cites a verse of the psalms, “In thy light we shall see light,” and gives an argument:[^27]

> Everything which is raised up to what exceeds its nature, must be prepared by some disposition above its nature; as, for example, if air is to receive the form of fire, it must be prepared by some disposition for such a form. But when any created intellect sees the essence of God, the essence of God itself becomes the intelligible form of the intellect. Hence it is necessary that some supernatural disposition should be added to the intellect in order that it may be raised up to such a great and sublime height. … By this light the blessed are made “deiform”—i.e. like to God, according to the saying: “When He shall appear we shall be like to Him, and \[Vulg.: ‘because’\] we shall see Him as He is.”[^28]

This is the *lumen gloriae*, the light of glory. It strengthens the mind as a habit strengthens a power, and Thomas explains in what sense it can be called a medium:

> This light is required to see the divine essence, not as a similitude in which God is seen, but as a perfection of the intellect, strengthening it to see God. Therefore it may be said that this light is to be described not as a medium in which God is seen, but as one by which He is seen; and such a medium does not take away the immediate vision of God.[^29]

Thomas’s account agrees with the definition of 1336: no creature stands between the soul and God as the thing seen, because the only created thing involved is the soul itself, made able to see. The Council of Vienne in 1312 condemned the contrary error, that the soul *non indiget lumine gloriae, ipsam elevante ad Deum videndum*, does not need the light of glory raising it to see God.[^30] Such a light, he goes on, “cannot be natural to a creature unless the creature has a divine nature; which is impossible.”[^31] The vision is therefore the end to which grace brings us, not the growth of a power we were born with.

### Seeing without exhausting

Thomas teaches that the blessed see God but never comprehend him, and he puts it with a saying he takes from Augustine:

> It is impossible for any created intellect to comprehend God; yet “for the mind to attain to God in some degree is great beatitude,” as Augustine says.[^32]

His reason has two parts. God is infinitely knowable, and the light of glory, being created, is finite, so that no created intellect can know God as far as he can be known: “Hence it is impossible that it should comprehend God.”[^33] The saints see the whole God, for God has no parts; but they do not see him wholly, as he sees himself.

The psalmist prays in words that fit this teaching: “My heart hath said to thee: My face hath sought thee: thy face, O Lord, will I still seek.”[^34] The seeking that begins in faith ends in sight, and the sight never exhausts what it sees.

## Till They Find Rest in Thee

Augustine began the *Confessions* with a sentence addressed to God:

> Thou movest us to delight in praising Thee; for Thou hast formed us for Thyself, and our hearts are restless till they find rest in Thee.[^35]

Restlessness of this kind belongs to a creature made for an end it does not yet possess, and it is the first sign of heaven that each of us carries. Thomas Aquinas, expounding the last article of the Creed, cites this very sentence when he comes to say what everlasting life is. It is, he says, first of all union with God, and that union consists in perfect vision, in the highest praise, and in the perfect satisfying of desire; for nothing created ever satisfies the desire of man: *Deus enim solus satiat, et in infinitum excedit*, God alone satisfies, and exceeds it without end. That is why, he goes on, the Lord says *intra in gaudium domini tui*; and he adds a saying that he gives as Augustine’s, though it is Anselm’s, from the *Proslogion*, and that turns the sentence round: *totum gaudium non intrabit in gaudentes, sed toti gaudentes intrabunt in gaudium*. The whole of that joy will not enter into those who rejoice; those who rejoice will enter, whole, into the joy.[^36]

### Ostia

Once, for a moment, Augustine had been given something of this joy. Not many days before his mother fell sick of her last fever, the two of them stood at a window in a house at Ostia, looking into the garden, resting before a voyage. He tells what they spoke of:

> We then were conversing alone very pleasantly; and, “forgetting those things which are behind, and reaching forth unto those things which are before,” we were seeking between ourselves in the presence of the Truth, which Thou art, of what nature the eternal life of the saints would be, which eye hath not seen, nor ear heard, neither hath entered into the heart of man.[^37]

Their talk climbed. They passed through all bodily things, “and even the heaven itself, whence sun, and moon, and stars shine upon the earth,” and came to their own minds, and went beyond them, toward that Wisdom by whom all things are made; “and while we were thus speaking, and straining after her, we slightly touched her with the whole effort of our heart; and we sighed, and there left bound ‘the first-fruits of the Spirit;’ and returned to the noise of our own mouth, where the word uttered has both beginning and end.”[^38] Then, still speaking together, mother and son reasoned toward heaven. “We were saying, then,” Augustine writes:

> If to any man the tumult of the flesh were silenced,—silenced the phantasies of earth, waters, and air,—silenced, too, the poles; yea, the very soul be silenced to herself, and go beyond herself by not thinking of herself,—silenced fancies and imaginary revelations, every tongue, and every sign, and whatsoever exists by passing away, … and He alone speak not by them, but by Himself, that we may hear His word, not by fleshly tongue, nor angelic voice, nor sound of thunder, nor the obscurity of a similitude, but might hear Him—Him whom in these we love—without these, … If this could be sustained, and other visions of a far different kind be withdrawn, and this one ravish, and absorb, and envelope its beholder amid these inward joys, so that his life might be eternally like that one moment of knowledge which we now sighed after, were not this “Enter thou into the joy of Thy Lord”?[^39]

Augustine’s passage works by subtraction, as the Apocalypse does: everything that passes is silenced, and every created voice falls still, until God speaks “not by them, but by Himself.” And the words they found for what would then be given are the Lord’s own, from the parable of the talents: “Enter thou into the joy of thy lord.”[^40]

Thomas explains why the parable says it that way round. Joy is full when nothing remains to be desired, and in heaven nothing will remain, “so that the joy of the blessed is full to perfection—indeed over-full, since they will obtain more than they were capable of desiring.” And since no creature can hold the joy that God deserves, “it follows that this perfectly full joy is not taken into man, but, on the contrary, man enters into it, according to Matt. 25:21: ‘Enter into the joy of thy Lord.’ ”[^41]

### Rest without weariness

There is a fear that comes to Christians when they think of an endless happiness: that it will be the same thing for ever, and so at last a burden. Benedict XVI named the fear in the encyclical on hope: the word eternal, he wrote, “suggests to us the idea of something interminable, and this frightens us.” His answer was that eternity “is not an unending succession of days in the calendar,” but “a plunging ever anew into the vastness of being, in which we are simply overwhelmed with joy.”[^42] Fathers and saints had answered it long before, each with an image of his own.

Gregory of Nyssa, in his dialogue *On the Soul and the Resurrection*, gives to his sister, whom he calls the Teacher, the teaching that souls were made as vessels for the blessings of God, and that the vessel grows with what it receives:

> The All-creating Wisdom fashioned these souls, these receptacles with free wills, as vessels as it were, for this very purpose, that there should be some capacities able to receive His blessings and become continually larger with the inpouring of the stream. Such are the wonders that the participation in the Divine blessings works: it makes him into whom they come larger and more capacious; from his capacity to receive it gets for the receiver an actual increase in bulk as well, and he never stops enlarging. The fountain of blessings wells up unceasingly…[^43]

Bernard, at the end of his treatise on the love of God, answers it in the language of the Song of Songs, where the Bridegroom bids his friends eat and drink and be inebriated. The fourth degree of love, the love of God for his own sake alone, is reached in its fullness only in the kingdom, and Bernard describes the blessed who have reached it: “From thence comes satiety which is never satisfied; ardour which is insatiable, yet most calm and peaceful; the eternal and incomparable desire to possess, which arises from no want.”[^44] Alphonsus, writing for ordinary Christians, says it plainly: the soul “shall always desire, and always possess that joy. She shall be always satisfied, and always thirsty: for ever thirsty, and always satiated with delights: because the desire of Heaven begets no pain, and its possession produces no tediousness.”[^45] The hunger that goes on while it is fed, which both saints describe, is already in Scripture, where Wisdom says of herself in Ecclesiasticus: “They that eat me, shall yet hunger: and they that drink me, shall yet thirst.”[^46]

The Church has left open how this is to be understood. Gregory speaks of an enlargement that never stops; Thomas teaches that each of the blessed reaches the term appointed for him, so that “nothing further will remain to which he may tend,” and that heaven is no longer a time “for advancing by means of merit to a yet greater reward.”[^47] On either view the blessed both rest and are never wearied: they rest because they possess their end, and they are never wearied because that end is the infinite God, whom they never comprehend.

### The eighth day

Augustine closed the twenty-two books of *The City of God* with the same rest the *Confessions* had longed for on its first page. God himself, he says, will be the reward of virtue, “for, as there is nothing greater or better, He has promised Himself.” And then:

> He shall be the end of our desires who shall be seen without end, loved without cloy, praised without weariness.[^48]

He counts the ages of the world as the days of creation, and sees the saints’ rest as a Sabbath that has no evening, “brought to a close, not by an evening, but by the Lord’s day, as an eighth and eternal day, consecrated by the resurrection of Christ, and prefiguring the eternal repose not only of the spirit, but also of the body.” His last word on the life of heaven is four verbs:

> There we shall rest and see, see and love, love and praise. This is what shall be in the end without end. For what other end do we propose to ourselves than to attain to the kingdom of which there is no end?[^49]

Augustine repeats each verb to begin the next pair, and each step follows from the one before. The restless heart rests because it has found God, and what it has found it sees. To see the supreme Good as he is cannot be anything but to love him, and love that has what it loves overflows in praise. In the same chapter Augustine quotes the psalm: “Blessed are they that dwell in thy house, O Lord: they shall praise thee for ever and ever.”[^50]

The restlessness we carry now, then, is to be kept and turned toward its end rather than quieted with lesser things, as the psalmist kept it: “How lovely are thy tabernacles, O Lord of hosts! my soul longeth and fainteth for the courts of the Lord.”[^51]

## Star Differeth from Star

In the chapter on the resurrection, the Apostle looks up at the night sky and reads in it a law of glory: “One is the glory of the sun, another the glory of the moon, and another the glory of the stars. For star differeth from star in glory. So also is the resurrection of the dead.”[^52] The Church teaches the same inequality of glory. The Council of Florence, in the sentence that confesses the vision of God himself, three and one, as he is, defines that the blessed see him *pro meritorum tamen diversitate alium alio perfectius*: yet according to the diversity of merits, one more perfectly than another.[^53] The Council of Trent, in its decree on justification, anathematizes anyone who denies that the justified man, by good works done through the grace of God and the merit of Jesus Christ, truly merits an increase of grace, eternal life, and *gloriae augmentum*, an increase of glory.[^54] The same canon calls these works both the gifts of God and the merits of the man, so the differences in glory are differences in grace received and used.

### What differs, and what does not

Thomas Aquinas is clear that the object of the vision is the same for all: “the same object will be presented to all—viz. the essence of God.”[^55] No one in heaven has a lesser God, or sees him through a dimmer likeness, or is given a part where another has the whole. What differs is the seeing: the strength of the light of glory in each, by which the one God is seen more or less perfectly. Nor is that strength measured by natural gifts, as though the clever were to see more than the simple; Thomas raises the objection himself and answers that the diversity arises “on the part of the diverse faculty of the intellect, not, indeed, the natural faculty, but the glorified faculty.”[^56]

The Council names merit as the measure. Thomas explains the measure by charity:

> Hence the intellect which has more of the light of glory will see God the more perfectly; and he will have a fuller participation of the light of glory who has more charity; because where there is the greater charity, there is the more desire; and desire in a certain degree makes the one desiring apt and prepared to receive the object desired. Hence he who possesses the more charity, will see God the more perfectly, and will be the more beatified.[^57]

Merit, on this explanation, is not credit laid up to be exchanged at the end for a better seat: a life of charity enlarges the soul, and the enlargement is what shows in glory. Cyprian had tied the reward to desire a thousand years before, at the end of his treatise on the plague, praying that Christ would look upon the eager longing of his people, “He who will give the larger rewards of His glory to those whose desires in respect of Himself were greater!”[^58] Charity is poured into the heart by God and hidden from every eye but his. No one can measure his own, still less his neighbour’s, so the doctrine of degrees gives no ground for ranking anyone on earth; the rank of glory is known only in heaven.

### Degrees without envy

Even a child can raise the objection that an unequal heaven must leave someone sad. Augustine answered it in the last chapter of *The City of God*:

> But who can conceive, not to say describe, what degrees of honour and glory shall be awarded to the various degrees of merit? Yet it cannot be doubted that there shall be degrees. And in that blessed city there shall be this great blessing, that no inferior shall envy any superior, as now the archangels are not envied by the angels, because no one will wish to be what he has not received, though bound in strictest concord with him who has received; as in the body the finger does not seek to be the eye, though both members are harmoniously included in the complete structure of the body. And thus, along with his gift, greater or less, each shall receive this further gift of contentment to desire no more than he has.[^59]

Thomas says the same of joy: each of the blessed reaches the term appointed for him, and “each one’s joy will be full with regard to himself, because his desire will be fully set at rest; yet one’s joy will be greater than another’s, on account of a fuller participation of the Divine happiness.”[^60] Thérèse of Lisieux learned it as a child from her sister Pauline, and told it in her own story:

> One day I expressed surprise that God does not give an equal amount of glory to all the elect in Heaven—I was afraid that they would not all be quite happy. She sent me to fetch Papa’s big tumbler, and put it beside my tiny thimble, then, filling both with water, she asked me which seemed the fuller. I replied that one was as full as the other—it was impossible to pour more water into either of them, for they could not hold it.[^61]

In “the slowest sphere” of the *Paradiso* Dante meets Piccarda, torn from her cloister by violent men, and asks whether the souls placed so low do not desire a higher place. She smiles, and answers:

> Brother, our will is quieted by virtue<br>
> Of charity, that makes us wish alone<br>
> For what we have, nor gives us thirst for more.<br>
> …<br>
> And his will is our peace; this is the sea<br>
> To which is moving onward whatsoever<br>
> It doth create, and all that nature makes.[^62]

And the poet understands: “Then it was clear to me how everywhere / In heaven is Paradise, although the grace / Of good supreme there rain not in one measure.”[^63] Gregory the Great, speaking of the light in which the blessed know one another, uses a phrase that bears on the fear of envy too: they “do in that place with unspeakable brightness (common to all) behold God.”[^64] If the brightness is common to all, no one possesses God at another’s expense.

The doctrine of degrees therefore asks something of the Christian and also consoles him. Every act of charity done now widens the soul for what it will receive. The measure is love, which is within the reach of the least, and every vessel in heaven will be full.

## The Body of Glory

The body, too, is to share in heaven. The Church confesses, in the profession of faith of the Fourth Lateran Council, that all the dead *cum suis propriis resurgent corporibus, quae nunc gestant*: they will rise with their own bodies, the bodies they now bear.[^65] The Apostle, asked how the dead rise and with what body, answers with the seed, which must die to live, and with four contrasts:

> It is sown in corruption: it shall rise in incorruption. It is sown in dishonour: it shall rise in glory. It is sown in weakness: it shall rise in power. It is sown a natural body: it shall rise a spiritual body.[^66]

The pattern of that glory is Christ risen: he “will reform the body of our lowness, made like to the body of his glory.”[^67] In one of us the pattern is already fulfilled. Pius XII defined in 1950 that the Mother of God, “having completed the course of her earthly life, was assumed body and soul into heavenly glory”; and Paul VI, confessing the same faith, adds that she was thereby “likened to her risen Son in anticipation of the future lot of all the just.”[^68]

### Why the body, if the soul already sees

The definition of 1336 raises a question. If the souls of the saints already see God and are *vere beatae*, truly blessed, before the resurrection, what can the body add? Thomas Aquinas answers it. The separated soul, he says, has the whole of its good; yet it is not wholly at rest, because it is made to be the form of a body, and it “desires to enjoy God in such a way that the enjoyment also may overflow into the body, as far as possible.” And so:

> The desire of the separated soul is entirely at rest, as regards the thing desired; since, to wit, it has that which suffices its appetite. But it is not wholly at rest, as regards the desirer, since it does not possess that good in every way that it would wish to possess it. Consequently, after the body has been resumed, Happiness increases not in intensity, but in extent.[^69]

Bernard of Clairvaux, in the last chapter of his treatise on the love of God, comes to the same point in one sentence: “when the soul leaves the body, it is gain, and greater gain when it rejoins it.” And he adds that the soul may “think to obtain increase of glory from its body, because, though infirm and mortal, it has contributed greatly to its merits.”[^70] He describes what the body is to the soul in each of its states:

> The body then is for the soul a faithful companion; if it be a burden, it is also a help; when it ceases to aid, it also ceases to hinder; when again it helps, it is no more a burden. The first state is laborious, but profitable; the second inactive, but in no sense tedious; and the third is glorious.[^71]

The soul that loves God, he says, profits by its poor, weak body “whether living, dead, or risen—living, by bringing forth with her fruits of penance; dead, by resting from its labour; risen, the two together attain the consummation of all joy.”[^72] By this account the fasts of Lent, the kneeling, the weariness of the sick bed and the work of the hands are not lost, because the body that served will share the reward.

### The endowments of glory

The Doctors of the schools gathered what Scripture says of the risen body under four gifts. The Supplement to the *Summa* treats them in order: impassibility, subtlety, agility and clarity. Their names say what they promise: a body that can no longer suffer harm, that is wholly at the service of the spirit, that goes where the spirit wills, and that shines.[^73] This is the common teaching of the Doctors. Thomas traces the gifts to one principle in the mature *Summa*: “these gifts of a glorified body result from an overflow of the soul’s glory on to the body.”[^74] The Supplement states the principle as an order of subjection: “the human body and all that it contains will be perfectly subject to the rational soul, even as the soul will be perfectly subject to God.”[^75]

The body’s glory, then, comes from within: it is the vision of God shining through the flesh, as Christ shone on the mountain of the Transfiguration, and as he promised that “the just shine as the sun, in the kingdom of their Father.”[^76] The Supplement borrows an image from Gregory the Great for that brightness: “in the glorified body the glory of the soul will be known, even as through a crystal is known the color of a body contained in a crystal vessel.”[^77] And because the soul’s glory is measured by charity, so is the body’s.

Augustine, who wrote at length on the risen body, stops in the middle of his praise to confess the limit of what he knows: “What power of movement such bodies shall possess, I have not the audacity rashly to define, as I have not the ability to conceive.” Yet he is sure of the principle: “the body shall forthwith be wherever the spirit wills, and the spirit shall will nothing which is unbecoming either to the spirit or to the body.” And he is sure what the body will be for: “All the members and organs of the incorruptible body, which now we see to be suited to various necessary uses, shall contribute to the praises of God.”[^78]

So the hands that now labour will be lifted in praise; the eyes that now weep will see, in every creature of the new heavens and the new earth, the God who made them; the tongue will sing. “This corruptible must put on incorruption: and this mortal must put on immortality. … O death, where is thy victory? O death, where is thy sting?”[^79] The Christian keeps his body in chastity and offers it in penance because it is to be glorified; and he buries his dead with honour, as the Church does, because they will rise.

## The Company of Heaven and Those We Love

Scripture never shows a solitary blessed soul. On All Saints the Church reads of “a great multitude, which no man could number, of all nations and tribes and peoples and tongues, standing before the throne and in sight of the Lamb, clothed with white robes, and palms in their hands.”[^80] The heavenly city is a bride; the reward is a supper, “the marriage supper of the Lamb”; and the Epistle to the Hebrews tells the baptized that they have already come “to mount Sion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, and to the company of many thousands of angels, and to the church of the firstborn who are written in the heavens, and to God the judge of all, and to the spirits of the just made perfect.”[^81]

The Catechism gives the name heaven to that communion. “This perfect life with the Most Holy Trinity—this communion of life and love with the Trinity, with the Virgin Mary, the angels and all the blessed—is called ‘heaven.’ ”[^82] The vision, as the Catechism teaches, is the well-spring of that communion. Augustine, in the chapter on the beatific vision, says that God will be so present to the saints “that we shall see Him by the spirit in ourselves, in one another, in Himself, in the new heavens and the new earth, in every created thing which shall then exist.”[^83]

### Our dear ones are awaiting us

The first thing a grieving Christian asks is whether he will see his dead again, and the Fathers answered without hesitation. Cyprian of Carthage, writing to his people in a time of plague, when every household had its dead, tells them to lift their eyes from the graves to the homeland:

> We regard paradise as our country—we already begin to consider the patriarchs as our parents: why do we not hasten and run, that we may behold our country, that we may greet our parents? There a great number of our dear ones is awaiting us, and a dense crowd of parents, brothers, children, is longing for us, already assured of their own safety, and still solicitous for our salvation. To attain to their presence and their embrace, what a gladness both for them and for us in common![^84]

Cyprian says three things of the dead in Christ. They are “awaiting us”; they are “longing for us”; and they are “still solicitous for our salvation,” caring for those they left. Then he widens the circle to the whole court of heaven: “There the glorious company of the apostles—there the host of the rejoicing prophets—there the innumerable multitude of martyrs, crowned for the victory of their struggle and passion—there the triumphant virgins …—there are merciful men rewarded, who by feeding and helping the poor have done the works of righteousness.”[^85]

### Knowing and rejoicing in one another

Gregory the Great, asked by Peter, the companion of his *Dialogues*, whether “in heaven the good know the good,” answers from the parable of the rich man and Lazarus, where Abraham knows Lazarus and the rich man knows both. From that mutual knowledge, he says, “the reward likewise to both parts increaseth, for the good do more rejoice, when they behold them also in felicity whom before they loved.” And he adds:

> Beside all this, a more wonderful grace is bestowed upon the Saints in heaven: for they know not only them with whom they were acquainted in this world, but also those whom before they never saw, and converse with them in such familiar sort as though in times past they had seen and known one another … For seeing they do in that place with unspeakable brightness (common to all) behold God, what is there that they know not, that know him who knoweth all things?[^86]

Thomas Aquinas, expounding the last article of the Creed, names the fourth thing in which everlasting life consists, after union with God in vision and praise, the fulfilment of desire, and perfect security: it consists *in omnium beatorum iucunda societate*, in the joyful company of all the blessed, which will be supremely delightful because each will have every good together with all the blessed. His reason is charity: *Nam quilibet diliget alium sicut seipsum; et ideo gaudebit de bono alterius sicut de suo. Quo fit ut tantum augeatur laetitia et gaudium unius, quantum est gaudium omnium.*[^87] Each will love the other as himself, and so will rejoice in another’s good as in his own; and so the joy of each grows by as much as is the joy of all. Alphonsus says the same in his meditation on heaven: “In that kingdom of love, all love each other tenderly, and each rejoices in the good of the others as if it were his own.”[^88]

The bonds of this life remain. In the *Summa* Thomas asks whether the order of charity remains in heaven, and grants that the blessed will love most those who are nearest to God; but he adds: “It will however be possible in heaven for a man to love in several ways one who is connected with him, since the causes of virtuous love will not be banished from the mind of the blessed. Yet all these reasons are incomparably surpassed by that which is taken from nighness to God.”[^89] The mother will still love her son as her son, and the friend his friend; only now every love is ordered in God and made perfect by him.

### Their care for us

The blessed also love those still on earth. Paul VI, in the Credo he proclaimed in 1968, confessed that the multitude gathered around Jesus and Mary in paradise forms the Church of heaven, and the Catechism quotes him: there the blessed see God as he is and “are also, to various degrees, associated with the holy angels in the divine governance exercised by Christ in glory, by interceding for us and helping our weakness by their fraternal concern.”[^90] The Council of Trent teaches it as the Church’s faith: the saints, reigning together with Christ, *orationes suas pro hominibus Deo offerre*, offer their prayers to God for men.[^91] Cyprian’s “still solicitous for our salvation” is the faith of the Church.

Thérèse of Lisieux, in her last illness, made that faith her mission. The epilogue to her story records what she said one evening to Mother Agnes of Jesus: “I will spend my Heaven in doing good upon earth.” And she went on at once to say how: “Nor is this impossible, since from the very heart of the Beatific Vision, the Angels keep watch over us.”[^92]

### What this asks of us now

Alphonsus, imagining the moment a faithful soul enters heaven, sets the meeting with family among its first joys: “What shall be her consolation in rejoining there her relatives or friends, who have been already admitted into Heaven, and in meeting her holy advocates!”[^93] He tells it as a meditation, in the manner of the saints who teach the faithful to picture what they hope for; the hope itself is the hope of Cyprian and Gregory and Thomas. The Christian who buries a parent, a spouse, a child, may say at the graveside what Cyprian said: they are awaiting us.

Two duties follow, and both belong to November. The first is prayer for those of our dead who may still be in need of purification, the mercy by which the saved are made ready to see; the Church gives the month to it, and *What Death Does Not Finish* sets it out. The second is to love now as the blessed love: in God, and for God, and so for ever. If the causes of virtuous love remain in the blessed, as Thomas teaches, then a friendship ordered to God now is one that heaven will keep.

## Without Which No Man Shall See God

The promise of the vision comes with a condition. “Blessed are the clean of heart: for they shall see God.” The Epistle to the Hebrews states the same condition from the other side: “Follow peace with all men and holiness: without which no man shall see God.”[^94] And the Apocalypse, having opened the gates of the city that are never shut, says who will pass through them: “There shall not enter into it any thing defiled or that worketh abomination or maketh a lie: but they that are written in the book of life of the Lamb.”[^95] Those who die in God’s grace but not yet perfectly purified are purified after death, “so as to achieve the holiness necessary to enter the joy of God”;[^96] that mercy shown to the saved is the subject of *What Death Does Not Finish*.

### Heaven would be hell to an irreligious man

John Henry Newman gave the reason for the condition in the first of his *Parochial and Plain Sermons*, preached on that text from Hebrews. He asks why we cannot be saved without holiness, and replies that an unholy man, even if he were let into heaven, could not be happy there. Heaven, he says, is not a place where everyone takes his own pleasure, as on earth:

> Heaven then is not like this world; I will say what it is much more like,—a church. For in a place of public worship no language of this world is heard; there are no schemes brought forward for temporal objects, great or small … Here we hear solely and entirely of God. We praise Him, worship Him, sing to Him, thank Him, confess to Him, give ourselves up to Him, and ask His blessing.[^97]

A man who has never loved God, brought into that presence, would find in it nothing he cared for and everything he had avoided. “Ah! he could not bear the face of the Living God; the Holy God would be no object of joy to him. … None but the holy can look upon the Holy One; without holiness no man can endure to see the Lord.” And then he goes further:

> Nay, I will venture to say more than this;—it is fearful, but it is right to say it;—that if we wished to imagine a punishment for an unholy, reprobate soul, we perhaps could not fancy a greater than to summon it to heaven. Heaven would be hell to an irreligious man.[^98]

“Fire does not inflame iron, but it inflames straw,” Newman says. On his argument holiness is the capacity to enjoy what heaven is, since the presence that is joy and life to the saint would be torment to the soul that hates it. “Outward acts, done on principle, create inward habits,” he adds; every prayer, every act of charity and self-denial, schools the heart for the presence of God.[^99] Thomas said the same in his own terms when he taught that charity measures the light of glory.

### Meditating on paradise

The saints also taught the faithful to meditate on heaven as they meditated on death, judgment and hell, and to draw from the meditation affections and resolutions. Francis de Sales, in the eighth meditation of the *Introduction to the Devout Life*, bids his reader place himself in the presence of God and then consider:

> Represent to yourself a lovely calm night, when the heavens are bright with innumerable stars: add to the beauty of such a night the utmost beauty of a glorious summer’s day,—the sun’s brightness not hindering the clear shining of moon or stars, and then be sure that it all falls immeasurably short of the glory of Paradise.[^100]

Then the company of angels and saints, and then the chief joy: “Consider how they enjoy the Presence of God, Who fills them with the richness of His Vision, which is a perfect ocean of delight.” From the considerations he draws the affections, and the second of them is a reproach to the heart: “Reprove the coldness of your own heart for having hitherto so little sought after that glorious abode. Why have I so long lingered indifferent to the eternal happiness set before me?” The resolution is practical: “Resolve to give up such and such things, which hinder you on the way, and to do such others as will help you thitherwards.”[^101]

Alphonsus ends his consideration on heaven by teaching patience under the cross. “When, therefore, the crosses of this life afflict us, let us animate ourselves with the hope of Heaven to bear them patiently.” He tells of Philip Neri, who, when the dignity of cardinal was offered him, “threw up the cap in the air, exclaiming, Paradise! Paradise!”; and he gives his reader the same short prayer for every trouble: “Let us likewise, when we are afflicted by the miseries of this life, raise up our eyes to Heaven, and console ourselves, saying with a sigh, Heaven! Heaven!” For “if we be faithful to God, all these sorrows, miseries, and fears, shall one day have an end, and we shall be admitted into that blessed country, where we shall enjoy complete happiness as long as God shall be God.”[^102]

### The resolution

The year is ending. The Church prays for her dead and looks for her Lord, and Advent will soon bid her watch. To desire heaven in this season is to do three things.

It is to keep the heart clean, by confession and by guarding the eyes and the will, because only the clean of heart shall see God. It is to grow in charity, in the small and hidden acts that widen the soul, because charity is the measure of the light by which God will be seen. And it is to keep desire alive and turned toward the face it was made for. “And every one that hath this hope in him sanctifieth himself, as he also is holy.”[^103]

Alphonsus closes his meditation with an affection that any Christian may make his own:

> Heaven! Heaven! When, O Lord, shall the day come, on which I shall see you face to face, and embrace you, without fear of ever more losing you? Ah my God, keep your hand upon me, that I may never more offend you.[^104]

## The Last of the Last Things

The Church has long taught her children to keep four things in mind: death, judgment, hell, and heaven. The list has the shape of a road, and it is easy to think of the fourth as the last stage of a journey, the town at the end of it. The four are not alike, though. Death is an event: the debt of nature, paid once by every child of Adam. Judgment is an event: the hour in which what death does not finish is finished, and a life is seen as it is. Hell is a state, the refusal of God made final and suffered for ever, the fire that is not extinguished. Heaven is different in kind: it is the end the creature was made for.

That end is to see God; heaven is a Person, seen. Christ asked it of his Father on the night before he died: “Father, I will that where I am, they also whom thou hast given me may be with me: that they may see my glory which thou hast given me, because thou hast loved me before the creation of the world.”[^105] He promised it to the apostles in the same discourse: “I will come again and will take you to myself: that where I am, you also may be.”[^106] And the Apostle, writing to Christians who were burying their dead, stated the hope and drew its use at once: “and so shall we be always with the Lord. Wherefore, comfort ye one another with these words.”[^107]

The year ends where that hope points. The feast of Christ the King sets the Judge on his throne; in Year A of the Postconciliar Missal its Gospel is the scene of the sheep and the goats, where the King says to those on his right hand: “Come, ye blessed of my Father, possess you the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world.”[^108] The King who speaks that sentence is the Lord who prayed that his own might be where he is and see his glory.

The meditation on judgment and on hell asks for fear; fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom, and the Church does not let her children forget what may be lost. Fear is meant to lead to love, and the meditation on the last things is meant to end in desire: to want God more than anything that could be lost, and to want him so much that sin loses its taste. The saints who meditated most on death and hell were also those who sighed most for heaven. Alphonsus, who wrote the considerations on hell, wrote also: “Heaven! Heaven!”

### Following the bier

When the prayers in the church are finished, says the Roman Ritual, the body is carried to the grave, and while it is carried the clergy sing an antiphon; at the grave the rite goes on, with the blessing of the tomb, the canticle *Benedictus* under the antiphon *Ego sum resurrectio et vita*, and the Lord’s Prayer.[^109] The antiphon sung on the way asks for the dead what the Gospel says was given to one poor man. There was a beggar named Lazarus who lay at a rich man’s gate, full of sores; “And it came to pass that the beggar died and was carried by the angels into Abraham’s bosom.”[^110] The antiphon asks the angels to lead the soul into paradise, the martyrs to receive it and bring it into the holy city, Jerusalem, and the choir of angels to receive it; and it asks that with Lazarus, once a beggar, the soul may have eternal rest. The holy city it names is the one the Apocalypse saw coming down out of heaven from God.[^111]

The meditation on the last things ends, then, on the road to the grave, where the Church sings this prayer over her dead. The holy city she asks for them is the one in which, the Apocalypse says, the blessed shall see the face of God; and those who follow the bier will one day be carried along the same road under the same prayer.

## The Antiphon *In paradisum*

> *In paradísum dedúcant te Angeli: in tuo advéntu suscípiant te Mártyres, et perdúcant te in civitátem sanctam Jerúsalem. Chorus Angelórum te suscípiat, et cum Lázaro quondam paúpere ætérnam hábeas réquiem.*
>
> May the angels lead thee into Paradise; at thy coming may the martyrs receive thee, and bring thee into the holy city, Jerusalem. May the choir of angels receive thee, and with Lazarus, once a beggar, mayest thou have eternal rest.[^112]

## Scope, Method, and Qualifications

##### Reader and purpose.

The fourth and closing volume of *The Four Last Things* is spiritual reading for Catholics at the close of the liturgical year and in the annual preparation for death. It sets out the Church’s hope of heaven, the vision of God, in the words of Scripture, the liturgy, the Fathers, the Doctors and the saints, and moves from that teaching toward desire, resolution and prayer. It is a study aid, not a catechism or a magisterial act.

##### Division with the companion volumes.

The moment of death and the preparation for it belong to *The Debt of Nature*; the particular and general judgment, the full reading of *Benedictus Deus*, purgatory and prayer for the dead belong to *What Death Does Not Finish*; the loss of God and its eternity belong to *The Fire That Is Not Extinguished*. Here those subjects are named in a clause and referred to their volumes.

##### Degrees of teaching.

Each teaching is stated in the body at its degree in a single clause, and Appendix A grades the principal teachings. The graded categories are those of the series: defined; Church teaching; common teaching of the Fathers and Doctors; theological opinion; pious tradition. Where the Church leaves a question open, the body says so once and gives the positions held.

##### Sources and translations.

Scripture is quoted from the Douay–Rheims (Challoner revision), at the Vulgate chapter and verse numbering. The conciliar and papal acts are quoted in Latin from the *Enchiridion symbolorum*; the Catechism, *Spe salvi*, the *Credo of the People of God* and *Munificentissimus Deus* are quoted briefly from the Holy See’s official English, which is copyrighted. The Fathers, Doctors and saints are quoted from public-domain English translations named in the notes and References. Thomas Aquinas’s *Collationes in Symbolum* is quoted in Latin; the English renderings beside it are glosses, not a published translation. Thérèse of Lisieux is quoted briefly from the 1912 Taylor edition, public domain in the United States.

##### Liturgy and received prayer.

The 1962 Missal and the Postconciliar Missal are cited by their own titles and readings; Year A of the Postconciliar Lectionary is so labelled. The closing antiphon is printed in the Latin of the *Rituale Romanum* (Ratisbon, 1872) and in the English of Lasance and Walsh’s *New Roman Missal* (Benziger, 1945), whose copyright was not renewed and which is public domain in the United States. No prayer is composed, translated or adapted.

##### Verification.

Every quotation was read at its locus in the identified witness; the record of those checks, with editions, loci and rights, is kept in the volume’s research file and not in the notes.
