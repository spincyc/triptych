# The Ark of the Covenant: From Sinai to the Woman Clothed with the Sun

*A source-audited study of the ark of the covenant: its canonical journey from Sinai to its disappearance, the theology of presence and holiness it teaches, and the Church's reading of the Virgin Mary as the ark of the new covenant, with every parallel graded by attestation and reception*

## Overture: The Question of the Ark

The ark of the covenant is the most famous lost object in the world, and the modern instinct, on hearing its name, is to ask where it is. The instinct is not foolish. The ark was a real chest of real wood, carried by real poles on real shoulders through a landscape whose towns can still be walked; a thing so concrete invites a concrete question, and the guesses have been concrete accordingly—a cave on Mount Nebo, a chamber under the temple pavement, a chapel in Aksum. This study will report those answers, with their dates and their witnesses, and will hide none of them. But it must say at the outset that the Bible itself, which watches the ark more closely than any other source, declines to end the story that way. The historical books let the ark vanish without an obituary: at some point no writer marks, it is simply no longer there, and the traditions that later arose to fill the silence disagree with one another. And the New Testament names the ark of the covenant only twice: once in the Epistle to the Hebrews, to describe its furniture and decline to expound it, and once, in the canon’s final book, to show it—not found, but seen, in the opened temple in heaven, one verse before the great sign of the woman clothed with the sun.[^1] The canon’s answer to “where did the ark go?” turns out to be an answer of a different kind, because the question itself was of a different kind than it looked. When a king moves his court, the antiquarian hunts for the old throne; the courtier follows the king. The ark was never important as furniture. It was important as the appointed meeting place of God and Israel, and to trace it honestly is to trace not a relic but a Presence.

> **Governing thesis**
>
> The ark’s story is complete only when it is read forward. The chest built at Sinai carries the Presence of God through Israel’s history, vanishes without an obituary, and is seen again in heaven at Apocalypse 11:19, immediately before the sign of the woman clothed with the sun. The Church—in her philology, her Fathers, her liturgy, and her magisterium—reads the Virgin Mary as the ark of the new covenant. This study tells the whole journey, presents that reading with its evidence assessed on two distinct axes—attestation history and ecclesial reception—preserves its disputes, and keeps its devotional application under the primacy of Christ.

The work stands in three panels and a coda, and each panel does one job. The first panel tells the journey entire, from every canonical ark text: the design and construction at Sinai, with the transport law and the meeting promise; the crossing of the Jordan and the seven days at Jericho; the long residence at Silo and the catastrophe that ended it, when the ark was carried into battle like a weapon and taken like a trophy; Dagon on his face in Azotus; the deaths at Bethsames and the parked decades at Cariathiarim; David’s two processions, the first ending in a death and a question, the second in dancing; the temple dedication, when the ark comes to rest and the glory fills the house; then the long silence of the books of Kings, the last sighting under Josias, the word in Jeremias that the ark would neither be remembered nor visited nor spoken of again, the plunder lists of 587 that name the snuffers and not the ark; and finally the ancient traditions of its fate, each told with its own actor and horizon. Where the text of Scripture is itself in dispute—a number at Bethsames, a word at the death of Oza—the dispute is printed, not smoothed. The reader who wants to know what happened to the ark will find here everything the sources actually say, at the confidence the evidence actually supports.

The second panel stops the narrative and asks what the journey taught. It follows the theology the texts themselves build: the glory that the ark’s loss was thought to end and did not; the holiness that refused to be used as a talisman by the very nation that carried it; the golden propitiatory where blood met mercy once a year; the gates commanded to open for the King of glory. It then pays a necessary toll: a short, candid account of what typology is and is not—what the Church means, with the Second Vatican Council and the Catechism, by reading the Old Covenant’s works as prefigurations of the New, and what such reading never claims about the human author or the literal sense. The reader is asked to cross no bridge before it is built.

The third panel presents the Church’s reading of Mary as the ark of the new covenant, and presents it as evidence, not as assertion. It begins with the philology of the Visitation—Luke’s first chapter set beside the ark’s procession in 2 Kings 6 (2 Samuel 6 in modern naming)—at its verified strength and its verified limits, with the scholars who built the case and the scholars who reject it both quoted at their strongest. It then hears the Fathers, each text carrying its own date and its own authenticity verdict; then the liturgy and the magisterium, from the Akathist hymn to the Litany of Loreto to the Assumption definition of 1950 and the present lectionary, dated act by act; and it ends where the canon ends, with Psalm 131, the Assumption, and the opened temple of Apocalypse 11:19 flowing into the sign of the woman. One table near the close—the study’s only summary—grades every parallel on the two axes at once. The coda leaves argument behind and asks what the whole story requires of those who carry Christ within them, as the baptized do.

One promise of method governs all of it, and it is made here once: facts exact, framework Catholic, labels honest. Questions of fact—who wrote a homily, what a verse says, where a word occurs—are settled by evidence, and by nothing else; a pseudonymous sermon is called pseudonymous even when its sentence is beautiful, and a wrong reference is corrected even when it is venerable. Questions of interpretation are not settled by the minimalism of any one school; critics are reported as a school, with their premises stated, and the liturgical and magisterial reception of a reading is treated as real evidence about its truth, since the Church reading her own Scripture in her own worship is not a bystander. Throughout, two things are measured separately and said separately: how anciently a reading is attested, and how the Church has received it—for a reading may be old and never received, or received and explicitly articulated late, and late articulation is what development looks like, not what error looks like. Where this study proposes a synthesis of its own, the proposal is labeled and framed so the reader can see the seam. Resemblance will never be dressed up as exegesis. The reader is owed the evidence at its true weight, and will get it.

That is the whole plan. The journey is long, and it starts where every covenant thing starts, on the mountain where God told a freed people, in cubits and gold, exactly how He wished to be met. Begin at Sinai.

## Sinai: The Ark Commanded

The ark of the covenant enters Scripture as a working drawing. Israel is encamped at the foot of Sinai; the mountain has been smoke and trumpet and a voice so terrible that the people begged not to hear it again; and when Moses goes up into the cloud to receive the pattern of God’s dwelling, the first thing dictated to him is a piece of joinery. “Frame an ark of setim wood,” he is told, “the length whereof shall be of two cubits and a half.”[^2] Setim is the acacia of the desert; the thing specified is a wooden chest with a lid, overlaid with gold, small enough to be carried on two poles. The modern reader, who came for thunder, may fairly feel the anticlimax, and had better feel it honestly. Nothing in the specification is beyond a competent carpenter, and the text does not pretend otherwise. But the order of the blueprint is itself the first lesson. A palace is designed outward from its throne room, and the tabernacle is designed outward from this box: the ark stands at the head of the pattern, and, as readers have long observed, everything else in the sanctuary is arranged around it.

The specification continues. Rings of gold, and staves to run through them: “And they shall be always in the rings, neither shall they at any time be drawn out of them” (25:15) — built, from the first day, to move. Above the chest, “Thou shalt make also a propitiatory of the purest gold” (25:17), and two cherubims of gold “on the two sides of the oracle… covering the oracle… their faces being turned towards the propitiatory wherewith the ark is to be covered” (25:18, 20). Within it, “the testimony which I will give thee” (25:16) — the tables of the law. And then the sentence for which the whole chapter exists: “Thence will I give orders, and will speak to thee over the propitiatory, and from the midst of the two cherubims, which shall be upon the ark of the testimony” (25:22). Where modern versions have God promise to *meet* Israel there, the Douay’s blunter idiom keeps the function on the surface: orders will be given, speech will happen, and the box is the appointed place of it. The ark is not a reliquary, not an idol, not an instrument to be worked. It is furniture built for a voice.

### The words of the thing

This study keeps one English Bible beneath it, the Douay-Rheims of Challoner, and the Douay’s words for the ark’s parts are not the words most readers know. Three of them need fixing now, once, so that every later section can use them without apology.

*Propitiatory.* Where modern versions print “mercy seat,” the Douay prints *propitiatory*: the lid of “the purest gold” laid upon the ark. The Hebrew is **kapporet**, from a root that means at once to cover and to atone; the Greek translators rendered it **hilastērion** — at its first occurrence with a doubled expression, **hilastērion epithema**, “a propitiatory, a cover” — and Jerome’s Latin is **propitiatorium**, whence the Douay’s word.[^3] It is a word with a long future in this study, and Panel II will follow it; for the present it is the golden lid, nothing more.

*Oracle.* The Douay uses this one word for two different things, and the reader must watch it. In Exodus and Leviticus “the oracle” is the zone of the propitiatory itself: the cherubims stand “on the two sides of the oracle”; God will appear “in a cloud over the oracle” (Leviticus 16:2). In the books of Kings the same word names the temple’s whole inner chamber, the **debir**, the windowless room where the ark will one day rest — the Vulgate’s **oraculum**, the place of speaking: “Oraculum autem in medio domus, in interiori parte fecerat, ut poneret ibi arcam foederis Domini” (3 Kings 6:19 = 1 Kings 6:19).[^4] Both senses are named from the same fact, the promise of Exodus 25:22: God speaks there. The context will always show which is meant.

*Covenant, testament, testimony.* In Exodus the chest is “the ark of the testimony,” the testimony being the tables. Elsewhere its running name is “the ark of the covenant of the Lord”; but the Douay also prints, in three places, “the ark of the testament”: “But the ark of the testament of the Lord and Moses departed not from the camp” (Numbers 14:44); the “ark of the testament” of Hebrews 9:4; and — the last time the ark is named in the Bible — “the ark of his testament” seen in the opened temple of heaven (Apocalypse 11:19), a name to remember, since the Bible’s final sight of the ark stands under it.[^5] Covenant and testament translate one and the same phrase; this study says “ark of the covenant” throughout and asks the reader to hear the other two as the same name. The nouns themselves are plain ones. The Hebrew **aron** is an ordinary word for a chest; the Greek Bible’s **kibōtos** is the very noun it uses for Noah’s vessel — “until the day that Noe entered into the ark” (Luke 17:27).[^6] What hallows the box is not its noun but its construct: *of the covenant*, *of the testimony*, *of God*. Finally, the Douay’s book names are used throughout: the books modern Bibles call 1–2 Samuel and 1–2 Kings are here four books of Kings, so that the temple chapters stand in 3 Kings; Paralipomenon is Chronicles; and the psalms carry Vulgate numbers.

### Two makers, two arks?

Exodus assigns the making. God names Bezalel the son of Uri, of the tribe of Juda, fills him with the spirit of God for the work, and sets the ark first in his commission; and in the execution account the blueprint is repeated nearly word for word — **epoiēse Beseleēl tēn kibōton**, “Beseleel made the ark,” as the Greek Bible puts it. He is the only named craftsman of the ark in the Hebrew text.[^7]

Deuteronomy remembers differently. There Moses himself recounts: “thou shalt make an ark of wood”; “And I made an ark of setim wood”; and, of the tables, “…put the tables into the ark, that I had made, and they are there till this present, as the Lord commanded me” (Deuteronomy 10:1, 3, 5).[^8] In Deuteronomy the maker is Moses, the making comes before he ascends again with the blank tables, and the account knows no propitiatory, no cherubims, no gold — a box for the tables and nothing else. Two kinds of explanation hold the field. Harmonists, ancient and modern, have posited a provisional chest later replaced by Bezalel’s finished work, a solution transmitted in rabbinic tradition; critical scholarship instead assigns the plain chest and the golden ark to different literary strata with different theologies of the divine presence, and von Rad influentially read Deuteronomy as deliberately demoting the throne of God to a container for documents.[^9] This study states the tension and leaves it standing: both accounts are canonical, neither is embarrassed, and no verse resolves them.

### What lay within, and what lay before

The prepositions of the Pentateuch are exact, and it will pay to notice them now. *In* the ark goes the testimony: “And thou shalt put in the ark the testimony which I will give thee” (Exodus 25:16). But the omer of manna that Aaron lays up as a memorial is placed *before* the testimony (Exodus 16:33–34), and Aaron’s rod that blossomed is laid up, in the same phrase, before the testimony, as a token against the rebellious (Numbers 17:10).[^10] Before, not in. The precision will matter, because the Epistle to the Hebrews will one day list within the ark “a golden pot that had manna, and the rod of Aaron, that had blossomed, and the tables of the testament” (Hebrews 9:4), where the books of Kings, at the ark’s installation in the temple, will find nothing in it but the tables. That knot is real; this study unties nothing. It is stated here, taken up when the ark enters the temple, and carried openly in the study’s closing records. One more distinction of the same kind: the book of the law that Moses writes at the end of his life is placed at the ark’s side, as a witness against Israel — the tables in the ark, the book beside it.[^11] A people who kept even their furniture’s prepositions straight were not casual about this chest.

### The law of approach

When the tabernacle is at last reared, Moses puts the testimony into the ark, sets the propitiatory above it, carries it within, and hangs the veil; and the sign that the work is accepted follows at once. “The cloud covered the tabernacle of the testimony, and the glory of the Lord filled it. Neither could Moses go into the tabernacle of the covenant, the cloud covering all things and the majesty of the Lord shining, for the cloud had covered all” (Exodus 40:32–33).[^12] Even Moses cannot enter while the glory is there. Yet the promise of Sinai is kept: when he does go in to speak with the Lord, the voice comes to him from above the propitiatory, from between the two cherubims (Numbers 7:89).[^13]

Around this presence Israel’s law builds a whole grammar of handling. The staves stay “always in the rings”: the ark is never to be gripped directly, and never unready to move. On the march it is carried by the sons of Caath — but only after Aaron and his sons have gone in and wrapped it, veil and covering and cloth: “then shall the sons of Caath enter in to carry the things wrapped up: and they shall not touch the vessels of the sanctuary, lest they die” (Numbers 4:15); and again, “Let not others by any curiosity see the things that are in the sanctuary before they be wrapped up, otherwise they shall die” (4:20).[^14] “By any curiosity” is the Douay’s phrase, and worth remembering; it will return in this study at Beth-shemesh. And the porterage is not menial work but a consecration: “At that time he separated the tribe of Levi, to carry the ark of the covenant of the Lord, and to stand before him in the ministry, and to bless in his name until this present day” (Deuteronomy 10:8).

Then there is the approach of the one man on the one day. Leviticus 16 warns Aaron “that he enter not at all into the sanctuary, which is within the veil before the propitiatory, with which the ark is covered, lest he die,” for God says, “I will appear in a cloud over the oracle.” Once in the year the high priest comes within, and comes furnished: with incense, so that its cloud “may cover the oracle, which is over the testimony” (16:13), and with the blood of the bullock and of the goat, sprinkled “towards the propitiatory to the east” (16:14–15).[^15] Blood and cloud, one man, one day: the propitiatory is the one place on earth where atonement and presence are appointed to meet, and the whole theology of Panel II hangs from this chapter. Notice meanwhile what kind of law this is. The Presence is not forbidden to Israel; it is appointed — a way in, a time, a wrapping, a tribe, a day. Holiness here is not a wall but a manner of approach, and the narrative to come will show, more than once, what its breach costs.

### Throne, footstool, container

What was it, then, this gilded chest with its golden lid and its attendant cherubims? The learned literature has returned three answers. Some read the ark as a throne, the seat of the invisibly enthroned Lord — the older German scholarship’s “empty throne,” refined by Haran into a single composite structure of chest, propitiatory, and cherubim, and set by Mettinger within a whole pre-exilic theology of Sion, the King unseen upon the cherubims. Others, with de Vaux — the Dominican whose *Ancient Israel* long served Catholic readers as the standard account — read it as a footstool: the cherubims’ wings form the throne, and the chest lies beneath the feet of the enthroned God. And Deuteronomy itself calls it a container, “an ark of wood” made to hold the tables and nothing else. Seow’s survey observes that the ancient Near East knew a custom joining the last two readings: treaty documents were laid up at the feet of the god, so that a box for covenant tablets and a footstool for the covenant Lord are not two objects but one. The newest full-length study, Eichler’s, rejects throne and footstool alike and argues that ark and cherubim formed a deliberately empty focus, an aniconic guard against picturing God at all.[^16]

The canon will not settle this dispute, because the canon pulls all three ways. Deuteronomy’s box holds the tables; David couples “the ark of the Lord, and the footstool of our God” (1 Paralipomenon 28:2); the one psalm that names the ark bids Israel “adore in the place where his feet stood” (Psalm 131:7 = Hebrew 132:7); and the histories name God as sitting upon the cherubims precisely when they name the ark (1 Kings 4:4; 2 Kings 6:2 = 1 Samuel 4:4; 2 Samuel 6:2).[^17] The scholarly quarrel is really over which function is original and which is reinterpretation — and that question each scholar decides by his prior dating of the sources, not by any datum about the object itself. It is well to say so plainly, and to say also what every school concedes: no physical trace of the ark exists or is expected; a portable shrine of the Lord stands in the earliest strata every school accepts; and the ark was gone before the Second Temple stood. The dispute lives in the stretch between Sinai and the settled land, precisely where the texts are fewest.

Whether the chest that Solomon’s priests carried into the oracle was the chest that Bezalel made is a question of fact, and questions of fact are settled by evidence, not by temperament. The evidence is textual and comparative, for the object has been gone these five-and-twenty centuries, and every party argues from the same documents. One distinction does the necessary work here: *attestation is not existence*. That the ark’s full theology is articulated late is certain; that the object is a late invention is a separate claim, which late articulation cannot by itself establish — development of doctrine about a thing is not evidence against the thing. Scholars who read the narratives as monarchic or exilic composition — Ahlström and van der Toorn may be consulted at their best — conclude from the lateness of the sources to the lateness of the object; the inference is exactly as strong as the premise that nothing in Israel’s memory outran its archives. Egyptologists and comparatists who set the ark beside the gilded barque shrines and pole-borne chests of the Late Bronze age — Kitchen, Noegel, and now Eichler’s full study of the ark and cherubim — find a design at home in the centuries the Bible itself claims for it, and in no later ones.[^18] The Church, for her part, has never defined the carpentry; she receives the ark as Scripture presents it, a real thing really commanded, and reads its whole career — made, carried, lost — as ordered to the One it prefigured. This study therefore reports the range, adopts no school’s unexamined premise, and proceeds on the received history, which no discovered fact contradicts.

### The ark sets out

In the second year the camp breaks up from the mountain. The cloud is “over them by day,” and the ark does not travel in the baggage: it goes first, and Israel’s oldest war-song goes with it.[^19]

> **Witness — Numbers 10:33, 35–36, Douay-Rheims**
>
> …the ark of the covenant of the Lord went before them, for three days providing a place for the camp.
>
> And when the ark was lifted up, Moses said: Arise, O Lord, and let thy enemies be scattered, and let them that hate thee, flee from before thy face.
>
> And when it was set down, he said: Return, O Lord, to the multitude of the host of Israel.

Three days ahead of the people, seeking out their resting place: the rest of this history is that verse enacted. The ark is on the road, and the next thing in its path is a river.

## Into the land: Jordan, Jericho, Ebal, Shiloh

The chapters that carry the ark into the land are the ones in which it looks most like the thing the rest of its story will deny it is. A river halts because a box is carried into it. A walled city falls because a box is carried round it. If a reader concludes that Israel possessed a weapon, he has concluded nothing worse than what Israel’s own elders will one day conclude, with results a later section must narrate; the misreading is not a modern impiety but the oldest mistake in the story. It is worth stating that fairly at the outset, because the book of Josue (the Douay’s name for Joshua) never rebukes the impression directly. What the text does instead is quieter. It hedges its wonders about with distance, title, and order, so that the careful reader comes away having watched not a weapon at work but a King arriving.

### The river and the stones

Israel crosses at the worst possible moment, in the season of harvest when the Jordan runs over his banks — the narrator troubles to say so — and the people are commanded to follow the ark at an interval of some two thousand cubits, something over half a mile, because they had never passed that way before.[^20] The distance is already doing theology. A crowd keeps that kind of interval not around what is useful but before what is sovereign: it is the space cleared for a king, not the radius marked round a hazard — and having said so once we may retire the image. Then comes the title. “Behold the ark of the covenant of the Lord of all the earth shall go before you into the Jordan.”[^21] Nowhere else in the ark’s whole story is it given this name, and it receives it twice, both times at the river (3:11, 13). The God who is about to give one small people one small land is announced at its border as owner of every land, so that the conquest opens with a claim that relativizes it. The crossing itself is told as liturgy rather than stratagem: the priests bear the ark down into the flood, the waters stand and are heaped upstream, “swelling up like a mountain,” while the bearers “stood girded upon the dry ground in the midst of the Jordan” and the nation passed over.[^22]

What Israel is given to keep from the marvel is not a relic but a catechism. Twelve stones are taken from the riverbed where the priests’ feet had stood and set up at Gilgal; Josue sets up twelve more in the midst of the Jordan itself, and the narrator adds that they were there still in his own day. The stones exist to provoke a question from children and to oblige an answer from fathers: when your sons ask what these stones mean, you shall tell them what the Lord did at this river. When the priests come up out of the channel, the waters return to their place.[^23] The first thing the ark does in the land, after the wonder, is to leave behind a discipline of memory. The wonder serves the telling, not the telling the wonder.

### The wall and the mountain of the curse

At Jericho the text is exact about the order of march, and the order is the argument. Armed men go first; then seven priests with seven trumpets — trumpets the Douay names by their festal office, those “used in the jubilee”; then the ark; and “the rest of the common people followed the ark.” So arranged, “the ark of the Lord went about the city once a day” for six days, and seven times on the seventh, and the narrative gives the wall’s fall no mechanism beyond the circuits, the trumpets, and the shout.[^24] The army is present, but as escort; the procession is shaped from its center outward, and the center is the ark. Jericho is not besieged. It is circled, as if in pilgrimage, until it is not there. The walls fell to a liturgy. As for the ground itself: the mound is not in doubt — Jericho is Tell es-Sultan, fixed by its name, its spring, and a century of excavation — but the archaeology of the fall is disputed, the fortified Bronze Age city having been found destroyed well before any plausible date for Josue, and this study reports the dispute without adjudicating it.[^25] And when Israel is beaten at Ai, the ark shows its other face: Josue rends his garments and lies on his face before the ark of the Lord until evening.[^26] The object at whose passage walls fell is also the place where defeat is carried and asked about. In both fortunes, the ark is where Israel goes to be before God.

Then, with the conquest scarcely begun, the campaign stops for a covenant. At Shechem, between the two mountains, all Israel — the stranger as well as the homeborn — stands in two halves, one facing Gerizim for the blessing and one facing Ebal for the curse, and between them, facing both, the Levitical priests carry the ark while the words of the law, blessing and curse together, are read over the whole assembly: the liturgy Moses had commanded in Deuteronomy 27, performed around the box that holds the tables.[^27] The scene is the plainest exposition of the ark in the whole conquest. Jericho showed what the ark’s presence can do; Ebal shows what the ark is for. The nation arranged around it is not an army but a congregation, and what issues from the center is not power but the reading of the law. Covenant, not conquest, is the content of the box.

### Silo

When the land is divided, the tent of meeting is pitched at Silo — Shiloh, in the familiar spelling — in the hill country of Ephraim (Josue 18:1), and there the ark stops for the longest residence of its story, from the days of Josue to the childhood of Samuel. Through the whole age of the Judges it is glimpsed exactly once: at Bethel, during the civil war against Benjamin, with Phinees the son of Eleazar ministering before it — a chronological notice commonly read as setting that grim epilogue early in the period.[^28] Otherwise, silence; and here silence means residence. By the time the story resumes, Silo is a settled sanctuary: a yearly feast, a hereditary priesthood, pilgrim families going up to sacrifice, a lamp burning through the night — and a narrative that has quietly begun to call the tent a temple.

> **Witness — 1 Kings 3:3 (= 1 Samuel 3:3), Douay-Rheims**
>
> Before the lamp of God went out, Samuel slept in the temple of the Lord, where the ark of God was.

The verse earns its frame by what happens next in the chapter: in that room, in the dark, the Lord calls a child by name, and the child learns to answer. This is the promise of Exodus 25:22 being kept — the God who said he would speak from above the propitiatory does speak, in the house where the ark is, to a boy lying near it.[^29] After a river stopped and a wall thrown down, the ark’s deepest work in the land is the quietest: it marks the place where God speaks. That is what it was built for, and at Silo, for once, it is allowed simply to do it.

What became of Silo the Scripture never narrates. It remembers it, twice, from a distance of centuries. The psalmist, recounting Israel’s rebellions, says that God “put away the tabernacle of Silo”[^30] — the verse Panel II will quote in full when it weighs what departed from Israel with the ark. And Jeremias, preaching against a temple grown complacent: “Go ye to my place in Silo, where my name dwelt from the beginning: and see what I did to it for the wickedness of my people Israel.”[^31] And here, for once in this story, the dirt has something to lay beside the text. Shiloh is Khirbet Seilun — an identification as secure as any in the archaeology of Israel, resting on the survival of the name, the precise directions of Judges 21:19, and a century of excavation — and the excavated early Iron Age settlement there ends in a heavy burn layer: ash, collapsed brick, scorched store-jars, and a great deposit of burned animal bones that bespeaks a place of sacrifice, conventionally dated near the middle of the eleventh century before Christ, the right horizon for the catastrophe the next section will reach.[^32] The convergence should be stated exactly, because it is one of the few places in this whole history where text and stratum genuinely meet. No inscription names the destroyer; the attribution to the Philistines is an inference from the narrative, not a find; the date is conventional. What may honestly be said is this: the one sanctuary Scripture remembers as destroyed is the one whose soil shows a destruction, at about the remembered time. The two witnesses do not prove each other. They face each other, and neither blinks.

But burn layer and psalm alike lie far downstream of the point the story has reached. In narrative time Silo is at peace, and the peace is real: the lamp burns, the feast comes round each year, a family climbs the hill country with its offering, a child sleeps beside the ark and is learning to answer when he is called. Only, the same chapters that show us the sleeping child show us the waking priests: the sons of Heli, who treat the Lord’s sacrifice as their own larder, and against whose father’s house a man of God has already pronounced sentence (1 Kings 2).[^33] The reader is not asked to guess; the text has told him. The ark has rested at Silo through the longest age of its history. The next time it goes out, Ophni and Phinees will go with it; and it will not come back.

## The catastrophe: from Aphek to Cariathiarim

Begin by giving the elders of Israel their due. Their army has just been beaten at Aphek, and they ask the right question: why has the Lord struck us today before the Philistines? (1 Kings 4:3 — in the Douay-Rheims numbering this study uses, 1 Kings 4 = 1 Samuel 4). Their answer is not vulgar superstition; it is memory. Let the ark be fetched from Shiloh, they reason, that it may save us out of the hand of our enemies. And the memory is accurate. The ark went first into the Jordan, and the river stood; it circled Jericho, and the wall came down. If the throne of the Lord goes before the army, how can the army fall? The reader who has come this far in the story could sign that argument himself. It is exactly wrong, and the narrative — with no prophet stepping forward to say so — will let the event itself explain why. The precedents the elders remember were never demonstrations of a lever that works when pulled. They were acts of a free Lord who on another day, at Hormah, stayed in the camp while a presumptuous Israel marched without him and was cut to pieces (Numbers 14:44). The elders have remembered the wonders and forgotten the freedom. What they fetch from Shiloh, they think, is a weapon. What arrives is a judge.

### Aphek: the glory departs

So the people send to Shiloh, and there comes into the camp “the ark of the covenant of the Lord of hosts sitting upon the cherubims: and the two sons of Heli, Ophni and Phinees, were with the ark of the covenant of God”[^34] (1 Kings 4:4). The English of the base text is worth a pause: the participle attaches oddly, so that on the surface it is the ark that sits upon the cherubims. The phrase in fact renders the great divine title — the Lord who sits enthroned upon the cherubim, the title that binds the throne to the box — and the study quotes it as printed and reads it so. And the verse’s second clause is as ominous as its first is majestic. Ophni and Phinees are the two priests the reader left at Shiloh: the sons of Heli whose contempt for the offerings of the Lord has already been narrated and already been sentenced (1 Kings 2). The ark comes up to Aphek carried, so to speak, by its own indictment.

The camp of Israel shouts until the earth rings; the Philistines, learning what has arrived, are afraid, remembering what these gods did to Egypt (4:5–8). Israel remembers Jordan; Philistia remembers the plagues; both armies, note, hold the elders’ theology. Then the battle: “And the ark of God was taken: and the two sons of Heli, Ophni and Phinees, were slain”[^35] (4:11). A man of Benjamin runs all day to Shiloh with his clothes rent. Heli, ninety-eight years old, sits waiting by the gate, and the messenger’s report climbs from bad to worse: Israel fled; a great slaughter; your two sons dead; and the ark of God is taken. It is at the ark, not at his sons, that the old priest falls backward and dies (4:17–18). The narrator has graded the losses, and a father’s grief comes second.

The last word of the chapter belongs to a dying woman. The wife of Phinees, hearing that the ark is taken and her husband dead, is brought to labor before her time, and with her last strength she names the child:

> **Witness — 1 Kings 4:21–22, Douay-Rheims**
>
> And she called the child Ichabod, saying: The glory is gone from Israel, because the ark of God was taken… The glory is departed from Israel, because the ark of God was taken.

The name is a theology compressed to three syllables:[^36] it carries the Hebrew word **kabod**, glory — the glory of the visible Presence — and the text itself, twice over, makes the equation the whole rest of this study will need: the ark taken *is* the glory departed. Israel’s own Scripture never treats the loss at Aphek as the loss of a treasured object. It treats it as an eclipse. Panel II will follow that word **kabod** from this deathbed through the Psalter and the prophets; for now it is enough to mark that the equation is not a later devotion but the narrative’s own first-generation verdict, put in the mouth of a woman with nothing left to lose. The glory is departed. That is what the box meant.

### Dagon on his face

“Now the ark of God was in the land of the Philistines seven months”[^37] (1 Kings 6:1) — and those seven months are among the strangest campaigns in the Old Testament, because Israel takes no part in them. The Philistines do what any ancient victor did with a captured god: they carry the trophy into the house of their own god, as a king hangs a captured standard in his hall, to declare whose deity won. The trophy declines the role. In the morning at Azotus — Douay’s name for Ashdod — “behold Dagon lay upon his face on the ground before the ark of the Lord” (5:3); they set him up again, and the second morning “the head of Dagon, and both the palms of his hands were cut off upon the threshold”[^38] (5:4) — the idol prostrate like a courtier, then dismembered like a defeated king, head and hands, before the thing he was supposed to have conquered.

Then the plagues. The Douay prints at 5:6 a double affliction: the Lord “afflicted Azotus… with emerods. And in the villages and fields… there came forth a multitude of mice, and there was the confusion of a great mortality in the city”[^39] (5:6). Here the versions part company, and the study says so rather than smoothing it: the mice of 5:6 stand in the Vulgate and the Septuagint, while the Hebrew keeps only the emerods, the mice entering the story first as golden images in the next chapter. Either way the shape of the episode is the same. The ark is passed like a hot coal from Azotus to Gath to Ekron, plague breaking out at each remove (5:8–12), until the lords of the Philistines are begged by their own people to send it home. No Israelite raid, no ransom, no negotiation: the ark’s captivity is conducted, from first to last, as the Lord’s solo campaign.

Even the sending home is made into evidence. The priests and diviners of Philistia prescribe a guilt offering — golden emerods and golden mice, images of the plagues, five for the five lords (6:3–5) — and then design what can only be called an experiment: a new cart, two milch kine that have never borne the yoke, their calves shut up at home; “if it go up by the way of his own coasts towards Bethsames… but if not, we shall know that it is not his hand hath touched us, but it hath happened by chance”[^40] (6:7–9). Untrained cattle, against every instinct, leaving their calves, taking the straight road up to Israelite territory: the diviners have honestly left the door open for coincidence, and the narrative lets pagan divination be good science. The kine go straight up the highway to Bethsames, lowing as they go, turning neither right nor left (6:12). The test convicts.

### Bethsames: the number as printed

At Bethsames it is wheat harvest, and the reapers lift up their eyes and rejoice; the cart is broken up for altar wood and the kine offered upon it (6:13–14). The ark is home. And then, with no warning the narrative cares to give, the day of joy becomes the section’s hardest verse — hardest in what it reports, and hardest in how its text has come down. The study’s base text prints it thus, and the number must be quoted before it can be discussed:

> **Witness — 1 Kings 6:19, Douay-Rheims**
>
> But he slew of the men of Bethsames, because they had seen the ark of the Lord: and he slew of the people seventy men, and fifty thousand of the common people. And the people lamented, because the Lord had smitten the people with a great slaughter.

Fifty thousand and seventy dead in a harvest village.[^41] The number has troubled readers for as long as the text has had readers, and the honest course is to lay out the witnesses and let them disagree. The Hebrew is itself the first problem: the Masoretic text reads, word for word, “of the people seventy men, fifty thousand men” — two numbers set side by side with no connective, a syntax anomalous in Hebrew counting and wildly out of scale for the place; the Douay’s “seventy men, and fifty thousand of the common people” follows the Vulgate’s attempt to construe that anomaly. The Septuagint keeps both figures and adds a clause of its own — the sons of Jechonias, it says, did not rejoice among the men of Bethsames when they saw the ark of the Lord — as though some family’s irreverence, or refusal of joy, were the offense.[^42] Josephus, retelling the scene in the first century, gives seventy only — the Greek is **hebdomēkonta**, verified at the exact passage — and in Whiston’s old translation the wrath of God “struck seventy persons of the village of Bethshemesh dead, who, not being priests, and so not worthy to touch the ark, had approached to it”; Whiston himself adds in a note that how other copies “come to add such an incredible number as fifty thousand in this one town, or small city, I know not.”[^43] Modern critical Bibles largely follow that instinct: the Revised Standard Version prints “seventy men” and flags it, with its own honest siglum, as a correction of the Hebrew. And one negative must be printed, because the contrary claim circulates: the great Samuel scroll from Qumran, 4QSam-a, preserves verses on either side of 6:19 but not the verse itself, and no other scroll has it. No Dead Sea manuscript reads “seventy”; none reads anything there at all.[^44] The case for seventy rests on Josephus, on the shape of the Hebrew anomaly, and on plausibility — a strong case, but a reconstruction, and this study, which quotes its base text as printed, will not emend silently in either direction.

The Douay tradition has its own reading of the verse, and it deserves to be heard as reception rather than dismissed as apologetics. Challoner’s note on 6:19 glosses “seen”: “And curiously looked into. It is likely this plague reached to all the neighbouring country, as well as the city of Bethsames.”[^45] The second sentence stretches the geography to carry the number; the first sentence is the interesting one, because it is not invented for the occasion. It wires Bethsames back to the transport law of the wilderness and its ban on seeing the holy things unwrapped: “Let not others by any curiosity see the things that are in the sanctuary before they be wrapped up, otherwise they shall die”[^46] (Numbers 4:20). On the Douay’s own terms the men of Bethsames died of the statute the porters of the ark had lived under since Sinai: not for malice, but for treating the holy as a curiosity. Whether that is what the Hebrew’s bare “because they had seen” means, the philology cannot settle; what the deaths mean — what kind of thing holiness is, that joy and irreverence can stand this close together — is Panel II’s question, and it will not be rushed. The men of Bethsames themselves ask it first, in almost those words: who shall be able to stand before this holy Lord? (6:20). Their answer is the practical one. They send messengers up the hill country, to ask someone else to take the ark.

### Cariathiarim: the long parking

The men of Cariathiarim — Kiriath-jearim, the wooded height above the Jerusalem road — come down and take it to “the house of Abinadab in Gabaa: and they sanctified Eleazar his son, to keep the ark of the Lord”[^47] (1 Kings 7:1). Then the narrator closes the whole catastrophe with a sentence of studied quiet: “from the day the ark of the Lord abode in Cariathiarim days were multiplied, (for it was now the twentieth year,) and all the house of Israel rested following the Lord”[^48] (7:2). Two honest difficulties sit in that sentence, and the study states both. The twenty years cannot span the whole interval the story requires: the ark stays in Abinadab’s house through Samuel’s judgeship and the whole reign of Saul, until David’s men come for it — decades on any chronology. The old solutions are two, and both are still on the table: either the twenty years counts only from the ark’s arrival to the national repentance narrated immediately after it (7:3–14), or the verse is the seam where an older ark story was closed and the Saul narratives joined on without the arithmetic ever being reconciled. Report, not resolution. And the mood of the verse divides its witnesses: the Douay’s Israel “rested following the Lord,” while the Hebrew as the moderns read it has Israel *lamenting* after him — a nation either at peace under the parked Presence or homesick for it. The study quotes what its base text prints and leaves both moods standing.

One frame from the scholarship earns its two sentences here. Miller and Roberts, reading these chapters against the ancient Near Eastern literature of captured gods — where the carrying off of a god’s image was never conceded to be the god’s defeat — take the whole Aphek-to-Bethsames narrative as Israel’s masterwork in that genre: the ark taken, and its God thereupon needing no rescue, wrecking Dagon, scourging five cities, and coming home unescorted and undefeated.[^49] On that reading the seven months in Philistia are not the interruption of the story but its thesis. Israel lost a battle; the Lord lost nothing.

So the catastrophe ends not with a restoration but with a parking. The ark does not go back to Shiloh — there is, the reader will learn in time, no Shiloh to go back to — and it does not come to rest in any sanctuary; the old tabernacle drifts separately through the story from now on, surfacing at Nob and later at Gibeon, a tent without its throne (1 Kings 21; 3 Kings 3:4; 2 Paralipomenon 1:3). The ark simply waits, in a hill-town house, one consecrated man keeping it, while the narrative turns its face away: to Samuel’s circuit, to the demand for a king, to Saul’s rise and long ruin. Days were multiplied. Twenty years and more the glory of Israel stands in Abinadab’s house at Gabaa, and a kingdom grows up below it. The next hand laid on the ark will be a king’s.

## The Two Processions

On the road to Jerusalem a man put out his hand to steady the ark of God, and God struck him dead. Every honest reader stumbles at that sentence, and ought to. Oza’s motive, so far as the text lets us see one at all, was to help: the oxen kicked, the ark leaned, and he took hold of it. The instinct that protests on his behalf is not irreligion; it is a sense of justice, and it deserves to be stated at full strength before anything else in this chapter is said. A God who strikes dead the man who steadies the leaning sacred object looks, on a first reading, like caprice.

But the chapter of Scripture in which Oza dies does not contain one procession. It contains two. Three months apart, over the same last miles, with the same ark and the same king, Israel makes the same journey twice — and the first ends in death and the second in dancing. Whatever changed between them is the thing the narrative wants found, and it takes some care to find it, because the story has come down to us in two tellings: the historian’s account in 2 Kings 6 (= 2 Samuel 6, in the Hebrew naming), and the liturgist’s account in 1 Paralipomenon 13 and 15–16 (= 1 Chronicles). This study prints both and harmonizes neither. Where they differ, the differences are themselves evidence.

### The first procession: the new cart

Samuel gives no motive. After the long years at Cariathiarim, with “all the chosen men of Israel, thirty thousand,” David simply acts:

> **Witness — 2 Kings 6:2, Douay-Rheims**
>
> And David arose and went, with all the people that were with him of the men of Juda to fetch the ark of God, upon which the name of the Lord of hosts is invoked, who sitteth over it upon the cherubims.

It is as full a title as the ark bears anywhere in the histories: the Name invoked over it, the throne above it. The narrator states what is being moved in the same breath as the moving of it — and the reader who has come through Sinai and Bethsames already knows what those titles cost.[^50] The Chronicler, writing later and for the temple’s people, supplies the motive Samuel withheld — “let us bring again the ark of our God to us: for we sought it not in the days of Saul” — and widens the muster from an army to a nation, “from Sihor of Egypt, even to the entering into Emath.”[^51]

Then the method. They set the ark of God upon a new cart and brought it “out of the house of Abinadab, who was in Gabaa,” with Oza and Ahio to drive it. A new cart drawn by oxen is exactly how the ark had last travelled — it is the transport the Philistine diviners devised at the end of the seven months, a contrivance for a god whose people were not there to carry him. Israel had another law. The sons of Caath were to bear the covered ark on shoulders, and “they shall not touch the vessels of the sanctuary, lest they die.”[^52] The cart was not a sin of malice; it was a convenience. But a cart is how one moves furniture. Even we do not wheel our dead to the grave while there are shoulders to bear them; some things are carried because carrying is itself the confession of what they are. The narrative does not say any of this at the outset. It lets the wheels turn.

At “the floor of Nachon” — Paralipomenon calls it Chidon, and no two witnesses quite agree on the name[^53] — the oxen kicked, the ark leaned, and “Oza put forth his hand to the ark of God, and took hold of it.” Then the sentence on which the whole difficulty hangs: “and he struck him for his rashness: and he died there before the ark of God” (2 Kings 6:6–7).

### The word that killed him

“For his rashness” reads like an explanation. It is important to know that it is not one — or rather, that it is the Douay’s inheritance of an ancient guess. At this point the received Hebrew has a phrase of famous obscurity, **al-hashshal**, whose sense no one knows; English versions made on the Hebrew have ventured “for his error.” The Vulgate rendered it **super temeritate**, “for the rashness,” and the Douay follows. So the base text of this study prints an interpretation of an obscure word, and the reader should know he is reading one.

The ancient witnesses divide in an instructive way. The Old Greek of Samuel had nothing at all where the phrase stands: in Field’s reconstruction of the Hexapla the column is empty, and the Greek phrase “for the rashness” (**epi tē propeteia**) enters the tradition only as a later addition under Origen’s asterisk, while Aquila rendered the Hebrew “for the folly” (**epi tē eknoia**).[^54] But the oldest Hebrew manuscript of Samuel that survives, the Qumran scroll 4QSam-a — which is extant precisely across these verses — reads a full clause where the received text has the opaque word: God struck him for putting out his hand to the ark, and he died there before God. That is, in substance, the very text of Paralipomenon: “struck him, because he had touched the ark; and he died there before the Lord” (1 Par 13:10). The common critical judgment is that the received Hebrew of Samuel is the damaged remnant of that longer clause; the honest counterweight is that this scroll often agrees with Paralipomenon, so a harmonizing copyist cannot be excluded. Either way, the evidence that the offense was the touch — the hand on the holy thing — is ancient, Hebrew, and older than Christianity; it is not merely the Chronicler’s moral drawn after the fact.[^55]

And the touch had a law. Here the two tellings play their parts. Samuel narrates and refuses to explain: the blow falls, David is angry, then afraid, and the story moves on — the older account tolerates the inexplicable. The Chronicler, a century and more of reflection later, gives the diagnosis: “No one ought to carry the ark of God, but the Levites, whom the Lord hath chosen to carry it, and to minister unto himself for ever”; and, when the procession is finally done rightly, “the sons of Levi took the ark of God as Moses had commanded, according to the word of the Lord, upon their shoulders, with the staves” (1 Par 15:2, 15).[^56] The fault was not a hand; it was a method. The cart put the ark where a stumble of oxen could endanger it and a layman’s reflex could touch it. Obedience would have made the reflex impossible. What that severity means — why the Presence is not managed, and what kind of love the fear of the holy is — belongs to a later panel of this study. Here it is enough to see what the texts themselves insist on: the second procession will change exactly one thing, and it is the thing the law had commanded all along.

David’s response ends where the men of Bethsames ended: in dread, and then in distance — only, in the king, an anger comes first that the narrative records without explaining. “How shall the ark of the Lord come to me?” (2 Kings 6:9). It is a question, not a refusal — the one sentence in the chapter that is still waiting for its full answer, and this study will meet it again, on other lips, before the end.

### Three months in a borrowed house

Unwilling to bring the ark home, David turns it aside: “the ark of the Lord abode in the house of Obededom the Gethite three months: and the Lord blessed Obededom, and all his household” (2 Kings 6:11). The detail is quietly astonishing. The surname is, on its face, the name of Philistine country: the ark that had lately scourged the cities of its captors sits three months under the roof of a man called after Geth, and nothing burns. The same Presence that struck the floor of Nachon fills a private house with blessing. The narrative offers no theory — only the pairing, death on the road and blessing in the house, set side by side and left there. The blessing is what ends the suspension: David goes down to bring the ark up to his own city, and this time everything is done differently.

### The second procession: shoulders and a dance

This time the change of method arrives in a subordinate clause, as though it were the most natural thing in the world: “when they that carried the ark of the Lord had gone six paces, he sacrificed an ox and a ram” (2 Kings 6:13). Carried — the verse’s plainest word is its most important one. No cart appears in the second procession; the ark goes up on men. The six paces can be construed two ways: a single sacrifice after the first six steps, or a sacrifice repeated at every sixth step, a road paved with offerings — and the ancient Greek versions render the verb in a tense that leaves the repeated reading grammatically alive. Paralipomenon describes the day independently: “they offered in sacrifice seven oxen, and seven rams” (1 Par 15:26). The two descriptions should not be merged, and there is a manuscript to keep the editor humble: the same Qumran scroll of Samuel reads seven bulls and seven rams in the Samuel verse itself.[^57]

Then the verse the whole tradition remembers: “And David danced with all his might before the Lord: and David was girded with a linen ephod” (2 Kings 6:14). The king in a priest’s plain linen, whirling before the ark — Paralipomenon, characteristically, robes the dancer: “David was clothed with a robe of fine linen,” like the Levites around him, with the linen ephod over it (1 Par 15:27). And at the window of the palace the procession acquires its one cold spectator: “Michol the daughter of Saul, looking out through a window, saw king David leaping and dancing before the Lord: and she despised him in her heart” (2 Kings 6:16).[^58] Samuel alone preserves what follows: her derision of the king’s self-abasement, his answer, and then the terse notice that closes her story — “Therefore Michol the daughter of Saul had no child to the day of her death” (6:23). Paralipomenon keeps the window and the scorn and drops everything after; the dialogue and the childlessness are not in its account at all.

The day ends with the ark at rest: they “set it in the midst of the tent, which David had pitched for it,” with holocausts and peace offerings; and the Chronicler adds what for him is the whole point, a ministry that does not disband when the procession does — Asaph and his brethren left “before the ark of the covenant of the Lord … to minister in the presence of the ark continually” (1 Par 16:1, 37).[^59] In Samuel a king brings a throne to his capital; in Paralipomenon a liturgy is born.

So the ark stands on Sion at last — but in a tent. David himself is the first to feel the unfinished edge of it: “Dost thou see that I dwell in a house of cedar, and the ark of God is lodged within skins?”[^60] The answer he receives is one of the strangest courtesies in Scripture: God declines the house, recalls that he has walked in a tabernacle and a tent since Egypt, and promises that a son will build it. The king who danced must leave the ark under canvas. A tent on Sion is not a resting place; it is a pause. The house waits for the son.

## Temple and silence

Nothing in the ark’s story is stranger than the way it ends, because it does not end. The most famous lost object in the world has no loss narrative. A reader opening the books of Kings expects either the story of how the ark perished or some claim about where it survives; Scripture gives neither. It gives an arrival told in glory; a silence of nearly four centuries, broken three times, each time more strangely; an inventory that does not mention it; and, from the prophet who watched the catastrophe come, an obituary written before the death. This section follows the texts to the edge of that silence and ends where the canon itself ends the question.

### The house at last

The entry is told in 3 Kings 8 (in modern Bibles, 1 Kings 8). Solomon assembles “the ancients of Israel”; the ark is carried up “out of the city of David, that is, out of Sion”—out of the tent David pitched for it—and set “into the oracle of the temple, into the holy of holies under the wings of the cherubims” (8:6), the inner room prepared for it from the first (3 Kings 6:19), under Douay’s name for that room, the oracle.[^61]

Three details of the installation notice earn their keep. First, the staves: their ends “were seen without in the sanctuary before the oracle,” and “there they have been unto this day” (8:8). The poles are never to be drawn out (Ex 25:15): the ark stands in the house of its rest still rigged for the road, and “unto this day” means the narrator could still point to them—a clause the rabbis would later lean on. Second, the contents: “Now in the ark there was nothing else but the two tables of stone, which Moses put there at Horeb, when the Lord made a covenant with the children of Israel, when they came out of the land of Egypt” (8:9). The parallel at 2 Paralipomenon 5:10 states the same exclusion in a substantially identical formula, the subordinate clause differing—“when the Lord gave the law”—nothing but the tables.[^62] And Solomon’s prayer presses the exclusion to an identity: “And I have set there a place for the ark, wherein is the covenant of the Lord, which he made with our fathers” (8:21). Not the tables of the covenant: the covenant. In Solomon’s mouth the ark’s whole content is a relation between God and Israel.

Third, the cloud.

> **Witness — 3 Kings 8:10–11, Douay-Rheims**
>
> …a cloud filled the house of the Lord, And the priests could not stand to minister because of the cloud: for the glory of the Lord had filled the house of the Lord.

The sign that took possession of the tabernacle at Sinai (Ex 40:32–33, Douay numbering) returns over the ark in the finished house: the cloud, the glory, the ministers driven out by the Presence they serve. The glory whose departure a dying woman had named at the ark’s capture (1 Kings 4:21–22) has come home. It is the ark’s highest moment. It is also its last.

### The long silence

After 3 Kings 8 the ark never again appears in the books of Kings. Not at the schism, not in the temple plunderings and repairs that Kings records across three centuries, not in the reform of Josiah as Kings tells it. Kings never narrates the ark’s removal, destruction, or hiding. In the whole canon that silence is broken three times.

The first is a prayer. In 701 BC, with Sennacherib’s letter spread out before the Lord, Hezekiah prays in the temple: “O Lord God of Israel, who sitteth upon the cherubims, thou alone art the God of all the kings of the earth: thou madest heaven and earth” (4 Kings 19:15 = 2 Kings 19:15).[^63] The title is the ark’s own; Scripture attaches it to the ark verbally at 1 Kings 4:4 and 2 Kings 6:2. Prayed in the temple, it is the strongest canonical indication that the cherubim throne was a living cult reality two full centuries after Shishak of Egypt stripped the temple treasuries about 925 (3 Kings 14:25–26). The old conjecture that the ark left with Shishak must explain why Kings, so ready to record Shishak taking everything, never hints the ark went too, and why the throne-title is still prayed in 701: the conjecture is gratuitous. Honesty adds the calibration: the epithet also occurs in psalmody as a fixed divine title (Ps 79:1; 98:1, Vulgate numbering), so it could be formulaic; and the temple’s own ten-cubit cherubim of olive wood (3 Kings 6:23–28), which no raider could carry, would have stood over an empty floor. The prayer leaves the conjecture with nothing to stand on; it does not strictly refute it.[^64]

The second is an order. In Josiah’s reform, about 622, the king tells the Levites: “Put the ark in the sanctuary of the temple, which Solomon the son of David king of Israel built: for you shall carry it no more: but minister now to the Lord your God” (2 Paralipomenon 35:3 = 2 Chronicles 35:3).[^65] This is the ark’s last appearance in the historical books, and it is strange, for it implies the ark was out of its place and being carried about. Three readings are on record. Either it had been removed under Manasseh—perhaps to make room for the graven image of 4 Kings 21:7—and Josiah restored it; or the Levites had been bearing it in processions; or the notice is the Chronicler’s idealizing construction—Kings’ own account of Josiah has no ark in it—telling us about the Chronicler’s liturgy rather than Josiah’s temple; this last is reported as the majority critical view. The text does not decide among them, and neither shall we. What no reading removes is the plain fact: chronologically, the trail of the ark as an object ends at this verse.

The third is the obituary. In the days of Israel’s increase, Jeremias prophesies, “they shall say no more: The ark of the covenant of the Lord: neither shall it come upon the heart, neither shall they remember it, neither shall it be visited, neither shall that be done any more. At that time Jerusalem shall be called the throne of the Lord: and all the nations shall be gathered together to it” (Jeremias 3:16–17).[^66] This is the only prophetic text that names the ark, and it names it in order to relativize it. The verse presumes an audience for whom the ark’s absence is, or is soon to be, a live wound; and it answers the wound not with recovery but with enlargement—the whole city become “the throne of the Lord,” the nations gathered to it. The loss is absorbed, not mourned. The Bible’s own first theology of the disappearance is already a theology of supersession by something greater.

### The year of the inventory

Then 587, and the lists. When the Chaldeans burned the house, the narrator of 4 Kings 25 (= 2 Kings 25) itemized what they broke up and carried away: the two bronze pillars, the sea and its bases, the pots and shovels, the snuffers and dishes and firepans and bowls—an accounting that descends to the fire-irons of the altar service. The ark is not in the list. It is likewise absent from the fuller parallel list in Jeremias 52, and from the inventory of vessels Cyrus later restored.[^67] Call the argument what it is: an argument from silence. But when a careful clerk inventories a looted house down to the fire-irons and never mentions the one famous thing in it, the omission is itself a datum—though of what kind, the list alone cannot say. Either the ark was already gone before the Chaldeans came, or it perished unrecorded in the burning of the house. Both positions are held by serious scholars. The canon narrates neither.

### The fate traditions

Into that silence tradition spoke, abundantly and not with one voice. Each witness follows with its date, its actor, and its horizon, in its own framing. The earliest is also the canonical one: inside the second letter prefixed to 2 Machabees, the writer appeals to what “is found in the descriptions of Jeremias the prophet” (2 Machabees 2:1):

> **Witness — 2 Machabees 2:4–5, 7–8, Douay-Rheims**
>
> …the prophet, being warned by God, commanded that the tabernacle and the ark should accompany him, till he came forth to the mountain where Moses went up, and saw the inheritance of God. …Jeremias came thither he found a hollow cave: and he carried in thither the tabernacle, and the ark, and the altar of incense, and so stopped the door. …The place shall be unknown, till God gather together the congregation of the people, and receive them to mercy. And then the Lord will shew these things, and the majesty of the Lord shall appear, and there shall be a cloud as it was also shewed to Moses, and he shewed it when Solomon prayed that the place might be sanctified to the great God.

Some who followed came up afterward to mark the way, and could not find it; Jeremias blamed them for the attempt (2:6). Three observations. The passage names its own source—Scripture is here canonizing a tradition and telling the reader that it is doing so. Its geography is Nebo, “the mountain where Moses went up.” And its horizon is not archaeological but eschatological: the place unknown till God gather the congregation of the people; the appointed sign of disclosure the cloud “as it was also shewed to Moses”—the very cloud of Exodus 40 and of 3 Kings 8:10–11, completing the inclusio in the text’s own words. This is consolation with a memory, not a map.[^68]

And it has a family. The *Lives of the Prophets*, a Jewish treatise on the prophets’ deaths and burial places from the first century of our era, transmitted by Christian hands, tells in its Jeremias chapter how the prophet, before the destruction, made the ark be swallowed up in a cliff of rock; how none shall bring it out but Aaron, and none unfold its tables but Moses; and how in the resurrection the ark will rise first of all, come forth from the rock, and be set on Sinai, where the saints will gather to it. The rock lies in the wilderness between the two mountains where Moses and Aaron are buried, and a cloud of fire stands over it by night, for the glory of God will never cease from his law. Here the hiding tradition says aloud what 2 Machabees implies: the hidden ark is an object of resurrection hope.[^69]

The expectation was not confined to Judea, nor even to the ark. Josephus records that in the year 36 a Samaritan—a man, he says, “who thought lying a thing of little consequence”—raised a multitude with exactly this hope: he “bid them to get together upon Mount Gerizzim, which is by them looked upon as the most holy of all mountains, and assured them, that when they were come thither, he would show them those sacred vessels which were laid under that place, because Moses put them there.” Pilate met the crowd with troops and executed the leaders; the massacre cost him his office. Note the precision: the depositor is Moses, the mountain is Gerizim, and Josephus says sacred vessels—not the ark. The words must not be sharpened. The episode proves that holy things hidden in a mountain, awaiting disclosure, were a live and politically explosive expectation in the first century.[^70]

The apocalypses written after the year 70 read 587 through their own catastrophe and pushed the custody one step higher. In 2 Baruch, the night before the Chaldeans breach the walls, it is an angel who descends into the Holy of Holies, takes from it the veil, the holy ark, the mercy-seat, the two tables, and the rest of the holy things, and commands: “Earth, earth, earth, hear the word of the mighty God, and receive what I commit to you, and guard them until the last times, so that, when you are ordered, you may restore them, so that strangers may not get possession of them.”[^71] In 4 Baruch it is Jeremias again, but at God’s own command, who is told to “consign them to the earth,” the earth being charged: “Guard the vessels of the temple service until the gathering of the beloved.”[^72] Destruction becomes divine act; concealment, divine custody; restoration, a promise with a fixed though unknown date.

The rabbis preserved the debate rather than a verdict. The Mishnah records a temple tradition that the ark lay hidden under the wood-store of the temple court, and tells of a priest who noticed that one flagstone there was different from the others and died before he could finish telling it—whereby they knew the ark was hidden there. The Talmud makes Josiah the hider, acting on our very verse, 2 Paralipomenon 35:3, and foreseeing the exile; with the ark, it says, went the jar of manna, the flask of anointing oil, and Aaron’s rod. But on the next folios stands the opposing baraita: Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Simeon ben Yohai held that the ark was carried to Babylon with the vessels; Rabbi Judah ben Lakish, that it was hidden in its place—his prooftext the staves seen “unto this day” of 3 Kings 8:8. Babylon and beneath-the-temple stand side by side in the same tractate, and neither is allowed to erase the other.[^73] And one ancient voice dissents from the whole hiding genre: in the mourning-woman vision of 4 Esdras—a book outside the Catholic canon, an appendix witness—the seer laments the sanctuary laid waste and the ark of the covenant plundered. Despoiled, not hidden.[^74]

The family resemblance is the real find. In nearly every witness the ark is not lost but *kept*, and its return is indexed not to excavation but to God’s regathering of his people. A family that has merely lost its father’s watch says so; this family tells you who is keeping it, and against what day. That is the shape the canon itself gave the question in 2 Machabees 2:7–8, and the honest record keeps the minority report beside it: plundered, says 4 Esdras, and gone.

### The empty room, and the wider world

Meanwhile there was a second temple, and its inmost room was empty. The rabbis say that in it stood only a stone from the days of the early prophets, called the foundation stone, three fingerbreadths above the floor, where the high priest set the censer on the Day of Atonement. Josephus describes the room: “In this there was nothing at all. It was inaccessible and inviolable, and not to be seen by any; and was called the Holy of Holies.” And the pagan witness agrees: Tacitus records that when Pompey entered the temple by right of victory in 63 BC, it became common knowledge that there was **nulla intus deum effigie vacuam sedem et inania arcana**—no image of a god within, the seat empty, the inner mysteries void.[^75] For six centuries the holiest room in Israel was, on the testimony of friend and enemy alike, empty—and was not therefore abandoned. Once a year the high priest still entered, to a bare stone where the ark had been. Israel’s worship had learned to face an absence without consenting to call it one.

The ark’s afterlife reached beyond Israel. The Qur’an enters the ark’s story at its biblical seam—the return in the days of Saul—and makes the ark the sign of Saul’s kingship: “there shall come unto you the ark wherein is peace of reassurance from your Lord, and a remnant of that which the house of Moses and the house of Aaron left behind, the angels bearing it” (Qur’an 2:248, Pickthall’s rendering). Pickthall’s “peace of reassurance” translates **sakina**, a word cognate with **Shekinah**: the Qur’an preserves in Arabic the ark’s theology of indwelt Presence. The classical commentators elaborate the “remnant” as the tablets or their fragments and the relics of Moses and Aaron; and later Islamic eschatology gives the ark a future of its own, in traditions that have the Mahdi bring it forth—from Lake Tiberias, or a cave near Antioch—before the end. Those last are reported leads, not verified texts; but even at one remove they show the same instinct: the ark’s reappearance belongs to the end of days.[^76]

And there is Ethiopia. The claim that the ark rests at Aksum, in the chapel beside the church of Mary of Zion, guarded by one monk and seen by no one, is a present institutional fact of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church. Its charter text, the *Kebra Nagast*, in which Menelik, son of Solomon and the queen of Sheba, carries the ark from Jerusalem, must be dated honestly: it was produced in the early fourteenth century (1314–1322), compiled by Yeshaq of Aksum as the national epic of the Solomonic dynasty restored in 1270—some eighteen centuries after the event it narrates, with no ancient attestation of the Aksum claim behind it. No scholar regards it as history. But as reception it commands respect: Ethiopia did not merely claim the ark; it multiplied it into a living liturgy—the **tabot**, the consecrated ark-tablet without which no Ethiopian altar is an altar, so that every church in that communion houses an ark—a datum of the kind this study calls reception.[^77] The ark’s silence has also fed a modern treasure-hunt literature, from Templar romance to cellar excavation to fiction; this study declines the hunt, and keeps the older company of Jeremias 3:16.

Where, then, is the ark? History answers: unknown—the last sighting is Josiah’s order, and after 587 the record is silence and inference. The traditions answer: kept, in a custody not ours, until God gathers his people. And the canon does not leave the question open: at the far end of the Bible, at the seventh trumpet, John saw it—“the ark of his testament was seen in his temple” (Apocalypse 11:19)—and there this study will finish.[^78]

## The pattern: what the journey taught

The narrative is finished, and it is worth saying plainly what kind of narrative it was. A reader who has walked from Sinai to the silence has not been following the fortunes of a treasure. He has been following a teaching, delivered not in propositions but in itineraries, plagues, processions, and one locked room entered once a year. The second panel of this study steps back from the road and states what the road taught. Four principles carry it. The ark’s story is told as the itinerary of the glory of God among men. The ark’s holiness is the manners God requires of creatures in his presence. The ark’s golden cover is the place where blood and mercy met, and the New Testament gives that place’s own name to Christ. And the ark’s entrances were sung—sung by Israel at the gates of Sion, and sung still, of Christ, at the doors of churches. Each principle in turn.

### The presence: the itinerary of the glory

The first principle is this: the Bible narrates the ark’s end not as the loss of an object but as the departure and return of the glory of God. The canon’s own vocabulary makes the equation before any theologian does. When the ark was taken at Aphek, the wife of Phinees, dying in childbirth, named her son Ichabod, and the narrator has her say it twice: “The glory is departed from Israel, because the ark of God was taken” (1 Kings 4:22; in the Hebrew naming, 1 Samuel 4:22).[^79] Ark and glory, **kabod**, are one loss. Israel’s psalmody remembered Aphek in the same terms:

> **Witness — Psalm 77:60–61 (Hebrew 78:60–61), Douay-Rheims**
>
> “And he put away the tabernacle of Silo, his tabernacle where he dwelt among men. And he delivered their strength into captivity: and their beauty into the hands of the enemy.”

An honest reader will notice that on the surface of this English the ark is nowhere in sight. The Douay, following the Latin psalter’s Greek line, prints “their strength… their beauty,” as though Israel’s own force had gone captive. In the Hebrew the pronoun is God’s: he delivered *his* strength (**oz**) into captivity, *his* beauty or glory (**tiferet**) into the enemy’s hand—and what was carried to Azotus that day was the ark. The identification of the psalm’s “strength” and “beauty” with the ark is therefore a synthesis, grounded in the Hebrew wording and in the Ichabod verses, and the reader should know that it stands on those two supports and not on the English surface.[^80] The glory departed; and the glory came back. At the dedication of Solomon’s temple the sign of Sinai returned: “a cloud filled the house of the Lord,” and the priests could not stand to minister, “for the glory of the Lord had filled the house” (3 Kings 8:10–11 = 1 Kings 8:10–11), the scene quoted in full in the temple section above.[^81]

Then, at the end, the departure was shown to a prophet in slow motion. Ezechiel, a priest among the exiles, watched the glory leave the temple by stations, as if reluctant to go. It rose from the cherub to the threshold of the house, and the house filled with the cloud (Ezechiel 9:3; 10:4); it went out from the threshold and rested over the cherubim at the east gate (10:18–19); at last it went up from the midst of the city and stood upon the mountain east of the city (11:22–23)—the Mount of Olives, as readers ancient and modern have identified that mountain. Of the ark, in all this, not one word. The vision has already outgrown the furniture: the Presence departs, and what is left behind is left to the fire. But the same prophet was shown the return along the same road. The glory of the God of Israel came from the way of the east, entered by the east gate, and filled the house; and the voice named the place his throne and the place of the soles of his feet, where he will dwell in the midst of the children of Israel for ever (43:1–7). And in Ezechiel’s visionary temple there is no ark at all. The throne is now the sanctuary itself.[^82] Jewish memory kept the same shape: a tradition in the Talmud counts ten stages of the Shekinah’s withdrawal from the temple, the last from the city to the Mount of Olives, where it tarried, calling Israel to return.[^83]

This is the frame in which Jeremias’s word about the ark—the only prophetic text that names it—stops sounding like an obituary and starts sounding like theology. The days come, says the prophet, when they shall say no more “The ark of the covenant of the Lord”; it shall not come upon the heart, nor be remembered, nor missed; and in the Hebrew there is a further precision the Douay’s “neither shall that be done any more” carries less sharply—it shall not be *made again*. Why not? Because “At that time Jerusalem shall be called the throne of the Lord” (Jeremias 3:16–17). The ark is not to be replaced, because what it was a vessel of will be present in a larger mode: not a chest in a city but the city itself become the throne. The ancients read the verse exactly this way, and joined it to the verse where this study will end: Tyconius, whose commentary shaped the whole Latin tradition on the Apocalypse, quotes Jeremias 3:16 at Apocalypse 11:19 to say that the ark seen in heaven is the Church—the old vessel surpassed, the figure filled.[^84] Whether that is the last word about the ark of Apocalypse 11:19 is a question for the third panel; what matters here is the shape. The arc even closes where the glory paused. From the Mount of Olives, where Ezechiel saw the glory stand as it left the city, the Glory in flesh wept over Jerusalem, and from that mountain he ascended (Luke 19:37–41; Acts 1:9–12). Let it be said plainly that this arc— presence, departure, return—is a theological reading of the canon’s own architecture, not a report about where a gilded chest was carried; the honest range of historical opinion on that question was given in the first panel and is not altered here. But the architecture is really there. The first panel ended with John’s one sentence about where the ark was finally seen; this arc is the reason the canon could end the story nowhere else.

### The holiness: the Presence is not managed

The second principle: the presence of God is a gift that cannot be conscripted and a holiness that cannot be handled. The narrative taught it in two forms: presumption, which would use the Presence, and familiarity, which would handle it.

It cannot be conscripted. At Hormah the presumptuous attack went up without the sign of the covenant: “But the ark of the testament of the Lord and Moses departed not from the camp” (Numbers 14:44)—and Israel was cut to pieces.[^85] At Aphek the elders reversed the arrangement and fetched the ark *to* the battle, reasoning that the ark’s arrival would save them from their enemies (1 Kings 4:3); the narrative answered with the capture itself. God is not carried into service. The man who finally understood this was David, on the worst day of his life. Fleeing Absalom, with the priests standing ready to bring the ark into exile with him, he sent it back: “Carry back the ark of God into the city: if I shall find grace in the sight of the Lord, he will bring me again, and he will shew me it, and his tabernacle” (2 Kings 15:25).[^86] That sentence is the exact inverse of the elders’ logic at Aphek, and it is the last the reader sees of the ark in the second book of Kings: the king who will not use God is the king God brings home.

It cannot be handled. The two death-scenes of the journey—the men of Bethsames struck for looking into the ark, Oza struck “for his rashness” when he put out his hand to steady it—were narrated above with their textual difficulties, and those difficulties are real and were left standing. But the theology does not hang on the cruxes. In both scenes the offense is the same: a creature treating the throne of the Presence as an object among objects, to be inspected in the one case, managed in the other. The question the men of Bethsames asked afterward—who can stand before the Lord, this holy God? (1 Kings 6:20)—is the narrative’s own summary, and it is a question, not a complaint.[^87] And the law had said all of this in advance, in the dry idiom of transport regulations: the sons of Caath were to carry the holy things already wrapped, “and they shall not touch the vessels of the sanctuary, lest they die,” nor look on them “by any curiosity” before the wrapping (Numbers 4:15, 20). What reads at first like ritual bookkeeping is reverence codified: the statute assumes that the danger is not malice but familiarity—the slow decay by which the holiest thing in the camp becomes, to its porters, luggage.[^88] Between the two lessons the pattern is symmetrical. Presumption treats God as a weapon; irreverence treats him as furniture. The ark’s whole discipline—poles never drawn out, veils, wrappings, shoulders, distance—exists to prevent both, and to train a people in the truth both errors deny: the Presence is a person, and the person is holy.

### The propitiatory: mercy at the center

The third principle: at the dead center of all this holiness, the law placed mercy. The one day a man entered the inner room, he came with blood, and the place he brought it was the ark’s golden cover—the **kapporet**, the Douay’s “propitiatory,” the “mercy seat” of the familiar modern rendering. Leviticus 16 built the room’s whole meaning around that surface: Aaron may not come at all times within the veil, “for I will appear in a cloud over the oracle,” and on the day he comes, the blood of the victims is sprinkled toward and before the propitiatory (Leviticus 16:2, 14–15).[^89] Atonement, in Israel’s central rite, is not transacted at the altar in the court; it is finished at the throne, on the gold between the cherubim, where God had promised to speak.

The word itself then makes a journey no student of the New Testament should be allowed to miss. The Greek translators rendered **kapporet** with **hilasterion**—at its first occurrence with the double phrase **hilasterion epithema**, “a propitiatory, a cover” (Exodus 25:17, Septuagint)—and the Epistle to the Hebrews uses the same word in its inventory of the old sanctuary: “And over it were the cherubims of glory overshadowing the propitiatory: of which it is not needful to speak now particularly” (Hebrews 9:5). And Paul, writing to the Romans, sets Christ forth under this very word: God has put him forward as **hilasterion**, in his blood (Romans 3:25, cited here from the Greek). Whether Paul means the ark’s cover precisely or propitiation generally has been argued by the lexicons for over a century, and the dispute belongs to them; what is not disputed is the word. The vocabulary of the ark’s lid is applied, in the New Testament, to the crucified Lord.[^90]

The Fathers drew the consequence at the empty tomb. St. John alone records that Magdalen, stooping into the sepulchre, saw two angels in white sitting where the body of Jesus had lain, one at the head and one at the feet (John 20:12). St. Gregory the Great, preaching on the scene about the year 591, turned it toward the inner room of the tabernacle. In his reading the two angels are the two Testaments— “Possumus etiam per duos angelos duo Testamenta cognoscere”—and as the two cherubim that cover the propitiatory gaze at one another with their faces turned toward it, so the two Testaments face each other across one center; and then the question that carries the whole figure: “Quid vero per propitiatorium nisi incarnatus Dominus figuratur?” —what is figured by the propitiatory, if not the incarnate Lord? (an editorial paraphrase, not a translation).[^91] Gregory’s figure, note carefully, is an allegory of the Scriptures: two Testaments, one mercy seat, the Lord between them. The more sensory form of the same reading—the slab of the empty tomb itself as the **kapporet**, the two angels as its cherubim flanking the place of the vanished Presence—is early modern and modern, running from Lightfoot’s Hebraic commentary into current scholarship and preaching; it is a legitimate development of Gregory’s insight, but it should wear its date.[^92] Either way the pattern of the third principle holds. The ark’s center was a covering for sin and a meeting place with God; the gospel’s center is a person who is both.

### The gates: the King of glory

The fourth principle: Israel sang the ark’s entrances, and the Church has never stopped singing them—of Christ. Psalm 23 (in the Hebrew numbering 24) stages an entrance as a dialogue at the doors: the gates are commanded to lift up their heads that the King of glory may come in; a voice within asks who this King of glory is; and the answer comes, the Lord strong and mighty, the Lord mighty in battle, the Lord of hosts. The ark is not named in the psalm. The reading that hears in it the liturgy of the ark’s entry into the sanctuary—the Lord entering his house as King, as at 2 Kings 6 and 3 Kings 8—is an interpretation, proposed in antiquity and argued in form-critical dress by the school of Gunkel and Mowinckel, whose reconstruction of a festival procession remains a hypothesis and is reported here as one.[^93]

What is not hypothesis is what Christians did with the psalm, almost from the beginning. Justin Martyr, before the year 165, put the dialogue at the ascension: the rulers in heaven “are commanded to open the gates of heaven, that He who is King of glory may enter in,” and, failing to recognize the ascending Lord in his unglorious flesh, they ask, “Who is this King of glory?”—and the Holy Spirit answers, “The Lord of hosts.” Elsewhere he insists the psalm was spoken neither of Solomon nor of Ezechias but “to this our Christ alone.”[^94] Irenaeus, in the *Demonstration of the Apostolic Preaching*, has the angels who accompany the ascending Christ call the same summons up to the powers above, who ask their astonished question and receive the psalm’s own answer.[^95] And the old descensus tradition turned the same dialogue downward: in the *Gospel of Nicodemus*, a compilation of the fourth or fifth century on older material, the voice like thunder before the doors of Hades cries, “Lift up your gates, O ye rulers… and the King of glory shall come in,” Hades demands to know who this King of glory is, and is told: “The Lord strong and mighty, the Lord mighty in battle.”[^96] The gates of heaven, the gates of hell: antiquity aimed the psalm in both directions, and both readings are pure Christology. Neither names the ark; both assume exactly what the ark’s processions had taught, that when this King approaches a threshold, it is the threshold that must move.

And the entrance liturgy is not a museum piece. In the Byzantine rite, to this day, the bishop consecrating a church carries the relics in procession around the building, halts before its closed doors, and chants the psalm’s summons; the question comes back from within the empty building; the doors are opened, and the King of glory enters his new house. The older Roman pontifical staged the same dialogue at the church door, with the bishop’s staff, at the *Tollite portas*. And once a year the Roman lectionary appoints these very verses as the responsorial psalm of the feast of the Presentation of the Lord, their refrain asking the psalm’s question and answering it of the Lord—sung while the Gospel shows the Lord entering his temple carried in his mother’s arms.[^97] That scene is described here and left where the liturgy leaves it; the third panel will ask its questions in order.

The pattern, then, can be named in one sentence: the ark is what the presence of God among men looks like before Christ—glory that elects a dwelling and cannot be detained; holiness that blesses the reverent house and breaks the presuming hand; mercy seated at the very center of danger; a King whose entrances make the gates themselves stand up. The whole of Israel’s experience of God-with-us was compressed into a box two cubits and a half long, and then the box was taken away, and the prophets said the Presence would come again in a larger mode. The reader is now equipped to ask the only question that remains: what does the same pattern look like after Christ—and did anyone, in the event, look like it? Before answering, the study must pause to say what kind of reasoning can honestly answer such a question. That is the business of the next section.

## Reading Forward: The Rules of Typology

The last section ended with a question: what does the presence of God among men look like after Christ? The third panel of this study will set out the Church’s answer so far as it concerns the ark — her long habit of reading the Virgin Mary as the ark of the new covenant. But before a single parallel is laid on the table, the reader is owed a confession, and it is this: resemblance is cheap. Set any two solemn processions side by side, in any two literatures, and they will share a vocabulary — a going up, a shouting, a carried holy thing, a crowd. Two texts written in the same Greek, steeped in the same Scriptures, drawing on the same small stock of ancient scenes, will echo each other whether or not anyone intended it. Whole libraries of nineteenth-century comparative religion were built on parallels no weaker than some that will appear in the next section, and those libraries are now cautionary tales. A reader who has watched parallel-hunting done badly is right to keep his hand on his wallet. Seeing a likeness is not yet knowing a meaning.

It follows that what a resemblance proves depends on what reading is — and that this must be settled before the resemblances are shown, not negotiated while they are being shown. This section is a toll-gate. The toll is paid once, here, so that the traffic through the last panels can move without stopping at every verse to renegotiate the rules of the road.

### What Typology Is

Typology is a claim about the authorship of history before it is a claim about the art of any writer. In a well-made novel, the thing set quietly on the mantel in the second chapter is aimed at the last chapter, and no analysis of the second chapter alone will explain it; only the author’s one intention, holding both chapters at once, explains it. Typology claims that the two Testaments are such a book — and that its prose is not merely words but events, because its Author disposes events as freely as a novelist disposes sentences.

This is not a private theory of the devout imagination; it is the Church’s stated rule of reading. The Second Vatican Council’s constitution on divine revelation puts the rule in two halves.[^98] The interpreter must first search out what the human authors really intended, with every instrument that serves that search — literary forms, historical setting, the conventions of the age. Nothing in what follows will excuse this study from that duty. But because Scripture is inspired, the same paragraph requires that it be read and interpreted in the same Spirit in which it was written, and it names three tests of such reading: the content and unity of the whole of Scripture; the living Tradition of the whole Church; and the analogy of faith, the coherence of revealed truths among themselves. The Catechism receives this rule and gives the present practice its name: from apostolic times the Church has read the two Testaments as one divine plan by means of typology, which discerns in the works of God under the old covenant prefigurations of what he accomplished in the fullness of time in his incarnate Son.[^99] Two words of that sentence deserve their full weight. Typology *discerns*: the figure is found, not manufactured, and a discernment can be mistaken, which is why evidence will matter at every step. And it discerns in God’s *works*: deeds, persons, objects — a chest of wood as much as a word of prophecy — because the Author of this book writes with events.

From this follows the point on which the whole first panel of this study was staked: typology presupposes the reality of the events it reads. A figure built on nothing signifies nothing. That is why the study spent its opening panel on the box itself — its construction, its journeys, its catastrophes, its disappearance — and settled questions of fact by evidence at every turn. The canon itself models the resulting reticence and the resulting confidence at once: the epistle to the Hebrews inventories the ark’s furniture — the golden pot, the rod, the tables — and then closes the veil with the words “of which it is not needful to speak now particularly” (Hebrews 9:5, Douay-Rheims).[^100] The New Testament’s own author signals that the furniture means more than the letter has yet said, and declines to say it hastily. This study can hardly claim a better warrant for speaking carefully.

### What Typology Is Not

Three confusions, each fatal, must be shut out at the gate. Typology is not literal exegesis. The human author of 2 Kings 6 (= 2 Samuel 6) wrote of David’s procession, and no reading offered in this study will pretend otherwise or overwrite his letter; the Catechism itself warns that the typological reading must never cost the Old Testament its own standing as revelation. Typology is not a claim about a writer’s psychology. Whether Luke consciously wove the ark narrative into his first chapter is a real question — a philological question, and the next section will weigh it with the philologists’ own tools — but the typological claim does not stand or fall with Luke’s private intentions, because it rests on the intention of Another; a demonstrated Lucan design would strengthen the attestation, not create the meaning. And typology is not decoration. A figure, if true, teaches; if false, it deceives; in neither case is it ornament. The stakes are doctrinal, which is exactly why the Church binds the practice with rules and why this study will bind itself with one more.

### The Two-Axis Rule

Every disputed reading in the remaining panels will be handled by one named distinction, stated formally here and nowhere else: **attestation is not reception, and the absence of either is not falsity.** Attestation is the historical trail of a reading — who read the figure this way, when, in what words, under whose name. It is a question of fact, settled by loci, dates, and manuscripts, and this study’s research records settle it without sentiment. Reception is the Church’s own adoption of a reading — in her liturgy, her catechisms, her solemn teaching. It too is a question of fact, settled by dated public acts. The two run on separate axes: a reading may be anciently attested yet never received into worship; it may be received at the highest levels though no Father ever drew it; and a reading poor on both axes may still be true, for late articulation is how development of doctrine ordinarily looks from the outside. Concretely, every reading printed in what follows will be declared in one or more of four states, in any combination: philologically anchored in the verified text; anciently attested, with its witnesses named and their names checked; liturgically or magisterially received, with the act and its date; or the project’s own synthesis, set apart in a labeled frame so that the boundary is structural and not merely verbal. The study will say which, every time.

One instrument more must be declared, because the next section will lean on it from its first page. The philological case to be examined there is cumulative: not one echo but a cluster of them, gathered in the span of a few verses. A cumulative case must be weighed twice over. Each strand is weighed alone, and a strand that fails alone will be printed here as failing; the study’s negatives are findings, not embarrassments. Then the bundle is weighed as a bundle, for the question a cluster raises is not whether any single echo compels but how likely it is that so many should meet by accident in so small a space. Neither weighing can substitute for the other: a rope is not refuted by showing that no one thread would hold the load, and a heap of threads is not yet a rope. Nor is denial the only honest rival of a cumulative case; the stronger rival is a different frame that claims to explain the same bundle more simply, and the reader will meet exactly such a rival frame in the next section, proposed by the critics themselves. All of this is arithmetic that either party to the dispute can accept; where the parties actually part company is the next question, and it is best seen from the ditches.

The rule cuts in both directions, and the reader should watch it cut. There are two ditches beside this road, and the study has met travelers in both.

Consider first the minimalist. Grant his conditional in full: if an allusion is real only when it can be demonstrated — shared words rare enough to signify, in matching order, beyond coincidence — then several of the echoes in the next section fail the test, and the critical school that says so will be quoted there in its own words, at its own loci, with its premises stated fairly.[^101] That work is honest and this study needs it. The ditch lies one step further on: the inference from “the allusion is unproven as the evangelist’s intention” to “the reading is false.” That step silently assumes that the only meaning in Scripture is the human author’s demonstrable meaning — which was precisely the question the toll-gate was built to keep open. Philology can weigh words, and its negative verdicts will be printed here as findings. It cannot weigh the intentions of God. The minimalist who thinks it can has not lost an argument; he has collapsed two axes into one.

Now the enthusiast, and grant his conditional too: if the Church’s reading is true, one would expect the ancients to have seen something — and, in substance, they did, earlier and more widely than the skeptic suspects, as the panel on the Fathers will show. But the enthusiast is not content with the pedigree the record supplies; he improves it. Quotations migrate under greater names; a sermon by no one becomes a sermon by a Doctor of the Church; a translator’s loose clause becomes a third-century witness; an unchecked reading passes from book to book because it ought to be true.[^102] Notice what each embellishment concedes: that only an ancient pedigree could make the reading true — the minimalist’s own axiom, swallowed whole, with forgery added to make up the arrears. The Church does not need the forgery and is not honored by it. Both ditches, in the end, are one error: both make attestation the sole measure of truth. The two-axis rule refuses the axiom, and keeps the evidence.

One piece of the rule remains to be justified: counting reception as evidence at all. The warrant is already in hand, for the council names the living Tradition of the whole Church among the criteria of Spirit-led reading, and nowhere is that Tradition more public, more deliberate, or more precisely datable than in worship. The theologians’ manuals count the liturgy among the **loci theologici** — the established places where theology goes to find its evidence — and this study will use it as exactly that. When the Church appoints a lesson and a psalm to a feast, she is not decorating a ceremony; she is performing an act of interpretation before her whole people, and signing it with a date. A lectionary is a commentary whose author is the reading Church. Such acts can be catalogued like any other witnesses, and the reception panel will catalogue them — act by act, date by date — on the same evidentiary terms as the manuscripts of the Fathers.

The toll is paid. The reader knows what will be claimed and what will not: facts settled by evidence, figures discerned under rule, every reading wearing its state on its sleeve. The bar lifts, and the road beyond it climbs — as it happens, into the hill country of Juda.

## Luke with the Septuagint: The Visitation and the Ark

The claim to be examined in this section is philological, and philological claims can be tested. It is said that St. Luke composed the Visitation narrative on the pattern of the ark’s journey to Jerusalem—that Mary goes up into the hill country of Juda as David went up, that Elizabeth’s cry and her question and John’s leap and the three months’ stay all answer, point for point, the second panel of the story told in section 13. If that is true, it is true in Greek: in the vocabulary of the Septuagint, the Greek Old Testament whose idiom the infancy narrative everywhere speaks, set against the Greek of Luke’s own sentences. The words can be counted, and for this study they have been counted—every concordance claim below rests on a lemma-level search of the whole Rahlfs corpus, run and then adversarially re-run for the study’s research records, not on secondary lists.[^103] One naming note, once: the Greek Bible files the books of Samuel as 1–4 Kingdoms, and the Douay-Rheims files them the same way as 1–4 Kings, so 2 Kings 6 (= 2 Samuel 6) is the chapter in question under either name. Here are the two openings, side by side.

> **Witness — 2 Kings 6:2 (= 2 Samuel 6:2), Douay-Rheims**
>
> And David arose and went, with all the people that were with him of the men of Juda to fetch the ark of God, upon which the name of the Lord of hosts is invoked, who sitteth over it upon the cherubims.

> **Witness — Luke 1:39, Douay-Rheims**
>
> And Mary rising up in those days, went into the hill country with haste into a city of Juda.[^104]

Set side by side, the two sentences already carry the shape of the claim: someone rises and goes, into the country of Juda, toward a house that will hold the holy for a season. But a shape is not a proof. What follows takes the alleged echoes one at a time, each with its Greek skeleton and its honest weight—the strong with their caveats, the weak named as weak, one outright negative printed as a negative. Then the scholarly debate is reported as a debate. What the Church has since done with this reading belongs to the next three sections; here the question is only what stands on the page.

### The overshadowing

“The Holy Ghost shall come upon thee, and the power of the most High shall overshadow thee” (Lk 1:35). The verb beneath “overshadow” is **episkiazein**, and its Old Greek career is short: it occurs exactly four times in the Septuagint. Twice it is the Psalter’s protective shadow (Ps 90:4; 139:8 LXX), once it is wealth’s glory in Proverbs (18:11), and once—the one theologically laden use—it is the cloud at the completion of the tabernacle: Moses could not enter the tent of meeting, because the cloud overshadowed it (**epeskiazen ep’ autēn hē nephelē**) and the tabernacle was filled with the glory of the Lord (Ex 40:35 LXX). The Douay reader should know that this link lives only in the Greek: the English of Douay Exodus reads “The cloud covered the tabernacle… the cloud covering all things” (Ex 40:32–33, the verses numbered 40:34–35 in Hebrew-ordered Bibles), while the English word “overshadow” surfaces at the New Testament end, at Lk 1:35 and again at Heb 9:5, “the cherubims of glory overshadowing the propitiatory.”[^105] And Luke himself tells us what the verb means to him: he uses it again at the transfiguration, where a cloud came and overshadowed the disciples (Lk 9:34, **egeneto nephelē kai epeskiazen autous**)—the theophanic glory-cloud, not mere shelter. That internal control is the strongest single piece of evidence that Lk 1:35 intends Shekinah imagery.

Now the precisions, both of them. First, the object of the overshadowing in Exodus is the tent of meeting, not the ark as such; the ark stands within the tent (Ex 40:20–21). Strictly taken, the figure Lk 1:35 supports is Mary as the overshadowed tabernacle, the place of the glory-cloud; the ark is one step further in. Second, that step is shorter than it once appeared. The adversarial re-run of the whole shadow-verb family for this study found the simple verb **skiazein** sitting directly on the ark itself: at 1 Paralipomenon 28:18 LXX the cherubim spread their wings **skiazontōn epi tēs kibōtou**, overshadowing the ark of the covenant of the Lord—Douay: “spreading their wings, and covering the ark”—and at Ex 38:8 LXX the two cherubim **skiazonta** over the propitiatory, with the veil that shadows the ark for the march at Num 4:5 and the cloud that shall shadow Mount Sion at Isa 4:5.[^106] The shadow-vocabulary of the Greek Bible, in short, is sanctuary furniture vocabulary—cherub wings over the ark, cloud over the tent—though Luke’s own compound **episkiazein** never rests on the ark anywhere in the Septuagint. Both facts belong in print. The soberest critic of this parallel, Marie Isaacs, granted the verb and denied the inference: “it cannot be assumed that Luke is employing a typology whereby Mary represents the tabernacle in whom the Shekinah resides”—her stated ground being that the Shekinah was not confined to the tabernacle or the ark, with the Psalter’s protective usage as her supporting observation.[^107] That is fairly said, and it does not touch Lk 9:34. A rare verb, an uncontested glory-cloud sense in Luke’s own usage, a tent rather than an ark at the Exodus end: that is the exact weight of the first echo.

### The question at the door

When the ark set out for Jerusalem and Oza lay dead, David was afraid, and asked: “How shall the ark of the Lord come to me?” (2 Kings 6:9; LXX **pōs eiseleusetai pros me hē kibōtos kyriou?**). Elizabeth, filled with the Holy Ghost, asks: “And whence is this to me, that the mother of my Lord should come to me?” (Lk 1:43; **pothen moi touto hina elthē hē mētēr tou kyriou mou pros eme?**). Shared: a rhetorical question asked of oneself; a sacred bearer coming—**pros me**, “to me”—and “the Lord” in the genitive at the heart of both phrases. Not shared: the interrogative itself (**pōs** against **pothen moi touto**) and the register, David’s dread against Elizabeth’s joy.

The critics have a rival text, and it deserves its place in the body of this study, not a footnote. Raymond Brown observed that when David comes to Araunah’s threshing floor—the future temple site—Araunah asks, in Brown’s rendering, “What is this, that my lord the king has come to his servant?” (2 Kings 24:21; LXX **ti hoti ēlthen ho kyrios mou ho basileus pros ton doulon autou?**). “This question also resembles Elizabeth’s question,” Brown concludes, “and it does not concern the Ark”; on his reading “the connecting link in the Lucan reminiscences may be David rather than the Ark.”[^108] The rejoinder, pressed by Jan Kozłowski, is about content rather than syntax: Araunah’s question is court courtesy to a superior, while David’s question and Elizabeth’s are the only two shaped as numinous unworthiness—the creature asking how the bearer of the Lord’s presence can enter a private life.[^109] The honest verdict is that this is a genuine motif-parallel with partial verbal support—an allusion at most, never a quotation—and that at this single point the debate is genuinely open.

### Elizabeth’s cry

The philologically strongest item in the cluster is a single word. Luke writes that Elizabeth **anephōnēsen kraugē megalē**—cried out with a loud cry—and blessed Mary among women and the fruit of her womb (Lk 1:42). The verb **anaphōnein** occurs nowhere else in the New Testament, and in the whole Septuagint it occurs exactly five times, every one of them in Paralipomenon, every one of them a Levite’s liturgical acclamation, and four of the five in ark ceremonies: the procession that brings the ark up to Jerusalem (1 Par 15:28), the ministers appointed before the ark (1 Par 16:4–5), the cultic music of Heman and Idithun (1 Par 16:42, the one occurrence with no ark in the verse), and the ark’s installation in Solomon’s temple (2 Par 5:13)—where the acclamation ends with the house filled with the cloud of the glory of the Lord, the same Exodus 40 cluster that stands behind Lk 1:35.[^110] There is a further convergence: the ark goes up to Jerusalem **meta kraugēs**, with shouting (2 Kingdoms 6:15), and the critical text of Lk 1:42 gives Elizabeth precisely a **kraugē**. And Elizabeth is a daughter of Aaron (Lk 1:5), which gives the Levitical color of the verb somewhere to rest.

The caveats are printed with the claim. Five occurrences are a small base on which to declare a technical term; in Greek at large **anaphōnein** is an ordinary verb of crying out, so the Septuagintal distribution is a fact about a corpus, not a property of the word; and a hapax proves that Luke chose a rare word, not why he chose it. The statistic is suggestive, verified, and not a demonstration. That is its weight.

### Three months and a blessed house

The record of the first procession says that “the ark of the Lord abode in the house of Obededom the Gethite three months: and the Lord blessed Obededom, and all his household” (2 Kings 6:11). “And Mary abode with her about three months; and she returned to her own house” (Lk 1:56). The Greek match is exact where it matters—**mēnas treis**, “three months,” verbatim on both sides, Luke adding only “about”—and the Douay English adds a small bonus of its own, the same verb “abode” in both verses. The blessing motif stands beside it: the Lord blessed (**eulogēsen**) all Obededom’s house because of the ark, and Elizabeth pronounces Mary blessed and the fruit of her womb blessed (**eulogēmenē… eulogēmenos**, Lk 1:42)—though the text of Luke never says Zachary’s house was blessed, and honesty keeps that difference visible.

Two deflationary facts are also visible, and the study prints them. The three months are narratively motivated: Mary arrives in Elizabeth’s sixth month (Lk 1:36) and stays until John’s birth is due; no typology is needed to generate the number. And the Book of Judith stands nearby: Israel rejoiced for three months after Judith’s victory (Jdt 16:20), and Elizabeth’s actual sentence of blessing demonstrably tracks Judith’s blessing—blessed art thou, daughter, by the most high God above all women (Jdt 13:18: **eulogētē sy, thygater, tō theō tō hypsistō para pasas tas gynaikas**)—more closely than any ark text does.[^111] But the Judith echoes are polyphony, not rivals. Luke’s manner in these chapters is to layer scriptural voices—Hannah under the Magnificat, Daniel under Gabriel—and a Judith harmony does not silence an ark harmony; indeed Kozłowski’s revival of the whole argument begins from the Judith verse, as we shall see. His judgment on this item is that 2 Kings 6:11 remains the closer parallel to Lk 1:56, because in both a God-bearing presence abides three months in a private Judean house, and blessing attends the stay.

### The leap: an honest negative

Here the study prints a negative finding, because the popular form of this parallel claims more than the texts give. John “leaped” in Elizabeth’s womb—**eskirtēsen** (Lk 1:41, 44)—and David danced before the ark; the apologetic commonplace says they share a verb. They do not. In 2 Kingdoms 6 the Septuagint’s David plays (**paizontes**, v. 5), strikes up on instruments (**anekroueto**, v. 14—the Greek has actually softened the Hebrew’s whirling), and is seen dancing and playing (**orchoumenon kai anakrouomenon**, v. 16). The verb **skirtan** never occurs in the chapter. Its true Septuagintal partner is Gen 25:22: **eskirtōn de ta paidia en autē**—the twins leaping in Rebekah’s womb, in a verse whose question, **hina ti moi touto?**, is formally as close to Lk 1:43 as anything in Kingdoms. Luke may well be interlacing a second typology here—children leaping in the womb before the birth of Israel, and of the new Israel—and Isaacs made the point with relish: “Even the words for ‘dance’ and ‘leap’ are not the same in the greek.”[^112]

Two precisions keep the negative honest in both directions. Laurentin never claimed the same verb: he glossed John’s leap from Malachias and the Psalter—leaping at the coming of the Lord—and listed the joyful bounds of David and of John together as a shared motif; the lexical equation is a popular overreach beyond what he wrote. And the motif equation itself has an ancient, if late, witness. In the second century after Christ the Jewish translator Symmachus rendered David’s whirling at 2 Kings 6:16 with precisely this verb: **skirtōnta kai kanchazonta**, leaping and laughing—the one ancient Greek Bible text in which David does **skirtan** before the ark.[^113] Symmachus is post-Lucan; he cannot be what Luke alluded to, and the verdict for Luke’s own composition stands: no verbal link. What Symmachus shows is something smaller and still real—that an ancient translator, independent of the Church, found “leaping” the natural Greek for what David did before the ark. The equation of the two scenes is not a modern contrivance. It is a hexaplaric footnote, not a proof.

### The hill country

“Arose and went”: the formula **anastasa… eporeuthē** (Lk 1:39), matching **anestē kai eporeuthē Dauid** (2 Kingdoms 6:2), is Septuagintal boilerplate—Abraham rises and goes to Moriah in the same words (Gen 22:3), and dozens of journeys begin so. By itself it carries no allusive weight, and the study assigns it none. What carries weight is the cluster riding on it: arise, go, Juda, the ascent into hill country, a destination house, a sacred burden. And here the Greek Bible offers one curious texture. The Septuagint of 2 Kingdoms 6:2 has lost the place-name: where the Hebrew has David set out from Baale-Juda (that is, Kiriath-jearim), the Greek translator read the noun as a common one, producing **apo tōn archontōn Iouda**—from the rulers of Juda, on the ascent (**en anabasei**)—and the Vulgate, followed by the Douay’s “of the men of Juda,” lost the place-name the same way. A reader of the Greek or Latin Bible thus meets in 2 Kings 6:2 an unnamed Judahite starting point and an ascent—a texture oddly congenial to Luke’s unnamed “city of Juda” in the hill country, though Chronicles keeps the name (1 Par 13:5–6). The ark comes into the house of Obededom (**eis oikon Abeddara**, 6:10–11); Mary enters the house of Zachary (**eisēlthen eis ton oikon Zachariou**, Lk 1:40).

Luke names no town, and the tradition that names one must be dated as tradition. The identification of the Visitation with Ein Karem, west of Jerusalem, rests at its oldest on the pilgrim itinerary of Theodosius (composed between about 518 and 530), which gives a distance only: **De Hierusalem usque ubi habitavit sancta Helisabeth mater domini Ioannis Baptistae milia V**—from Jerusalem to where St. Elizabeth, the mother of John the Baptist, lived, five miles—and no name; one manuscript adds that holy Mary greeted Elizabeth there. The name itself appears in the later liturgical tradition of Byzantine Jerusalem, as reported.[^114] Both Ein Karem and Kiriath-jearim lie in the Judean hill country, and the popular claim that Mary “took the same road as the ark” has no textual basis whatever: Luke names neither town nor route. The tradition is venerable. It is not evidence, and this study does not spend it as evidence.

### Daughter Zion, the companion typology

One more Old Testament pattern stands over Luke 1, and it must be kept distinct before it can be related. “Hail, full of grace, the Lord is with thee: blessed art thou among women” (Lk 1:28, the Douay printing the longer traditional text of the verse). The greeting **chaire**, followed by the announcement that the Lord is coming to dwell, is read by a whole line of scholarship as the Septuagint’s eschatological summons to the Daughter of Zion: **chaire sphodra, thygater Siōn**—rejoice greatly, daughter Sion (Soph 3:14)—with the King of Israel, the Lord, in the midst of thee (3:15) and the Lord thy God in thee (3:17); so too Zach 9:9. On this reading Gabriel greets Mary as the personified Zion whom the Lord enters, and **kecharitōmenē**—a rare verb—names the grace of that election. The minimalist answer is verbal: the only word Lk 1:28 exactly shares with Sophonias is **chaire** itself, which is also the everyday Greek greeting; “the Lord is with thee” belongs to the call-formula spoken to Gedeon and by Booz (Judg 6:12; Ruth 2:4), not to the Zion oracles; and Isaacs judged the verbal parallels “are, in fact, slight,” the womb-for-midst equation “wholly far-fetched”—while allowing that the Daughter-of-Zion personification “may have influenced Luke.”[^115]

The point of printing this here is precision. Daughter Zion and the ark are two different figures: the first makes Mary the city and people whom the Lord indwells, the second makes her the vessel that carries the Presence from place to place. The Church’s own prayer has fused them—the Catechism, in its commentary on the Ave Maria, calls Mary the daughter of Zion in person, the ark of the covenant, the place where the glory of the Lord dwells (CCC 2676), and Ratzinger fuses them deliberately, handing the ark title finally to Christ with Mary as bearer. That fusion is an act of reception, and its story is told in section 32. Here it is enough to say what the texts show: two distinct patterns, each with its own verbal skeleton, laid over the same seventeen sentences.

### The debate as a debate

The modern argument has a genealogy, and stating it plainly costs nothing. Eric Burrows first developed the tabernacle-and-ark reading of Luke 1 in essays published in 1940; Stanislas Lyonnet argued the Daughter-Zion reading of the greeting in 1939; and René Laurentin built both into the classic statement, *Structure et théologie de Luc I–II* (1957), where the Visitation’s chosen traits refer the reader “de façon convergente”—convergently—to the narrative of the ark’s transfer, and Mary is “le type de l’arche d’alliance.”[^116] The argument’s logical form should be noticed: it is cumulative and convergent. No single echo is claimed as proof; the claim is that so many independent contacts within the eighteen verses of one pericope (Lk 1:39–56) are unlikely to be accident.

The critics answer by dismantling the parallels one at a time and offering a rival frame. Brown’s Araunah text has been quoted above; his positive proposal is that the reminiscences are Davidic rather than arkological—Mary enters the story David entered, not the ark’s. Fitzmyer: “But this is subtle. If, indeed, the story may be compared with 2 Sam 24:21, then what connection does it have with the ark?” Schürmann held that the ark narrative aims at Jerusalem’s election, so that Mary shares with the ark only the direction of travel, never the destination, and that the fear-laden three months of the ark are “sehr unpassend”—very unfittingly—compared with Mary’s stay. Bovon was briefest: “Die Symbolik Maria = Lade ist zu weit hergeholt”—the symbolism is too far-fetched. Strauss found it “unlikely… that Luke would be drawing so subtle an allusion here”; Isaacs, that the case “rests upon the flimsiest of correspondences,” and that “it is to go beyond the evidence to suggest that she is a type of the ark of God.”[^117] This is a school with premises, and the study reports the premises with the verdicts: on strict verbal-echo criteria, an allusion must be lexically demonstrable—the same words, not the same shape—or it is not there; and liturgical reception is not admitted as evidence of what Luke meant.

The argument did not end in the commentaries. Kozłowski’s 2017–2018 intervention re-armed Laurentin from an unexpected side: Lk 1:42 blesses Mary and the fruit of her womb in the syntax Judith 13:18 uses to bless a woman and, in the phrase of Kozłowski’s own title, “The Lord God, Creator of Heaven and Earth”—so that the child in the womb is acclaimed in Creator’s language; and if the burden is the Lord, the bearer is what the ark was. To this he added the corrected concordance facts for **anaphōnein** set out above. From the Reformed side, Jacobus de Koning argued the typology in 2020 from 2 Kings 6 with the Chronicles acclamation texts, noting that it is “grootliks afwesig”—largely absent—from the mainstream commentaries, and proposing it as a challenge for Protestant rethinking of Mariology.[^118] And behind both stands an identifiable Catholic school with institutional addresses—Aristide Serra at the Marianum, whose Old Testament prefigurations of Mary include “the tent of the ark of the covenant”; André Feuillet at the Institut Catholique de Paris; Ignace de la Potterie at the Biblicum, for whom the Daughter-of-Zion promises are realized in a definite woman—whose loci this study cites as reported, not as texts opened at first hand.[^119]

Name the divide honestly and it names itself: it is methodological, not confessional. The thesis was born among Jesuits; its sharpest critics are Catholic priests in good standing; its newest defender is Reformed. What separates the parties is what counts as an allusion—strict lexical identity, or convergent pattern—and whether the reading Church’s use of a text is evidence about the text or only about the Church.

### What the philology honestly yields

Count the inventory. One rare verb whose only laden Septuagintal use is the glory-cloud over the completed tabernacle, re-used by Luke himself for the transfiguration cloud—with the tent, not the ark, beneath it. One exact two-word match, “three months,” in the same structural position, with a narrative explanation available and a Judith harmony beside it. One New Testament hapax whose entire Old Greek career is Levitical acclamation, four-fifths of it around the ark—five occurrences in all. One question shaped like David’s question, with a rival Davidic text that lacks the ark. A journey texture congenial to the parallel but proving nothing alone. One honest negative, where the popular claim fails and only a post-Lucan translator supplies the missing verb. And one companion typology, Daughter Zion, distinct in figure, fused with the ark only downstream, in the Church’s prayer.

The verdict this study prints is the one the evidence will bear: the Visitation cluster is a convergent set of which no single strand compels. A reader who requires lexical demonstration, echo by echo, will judge with Brown and Bovon that the allusion is unproven—and on that criterion the judgment is correct. A reader who admits cumulative design in a writer as deliberate as Luke, and who weighs the improbability of five independent contacts in eighteen verses, will judge with Laurentin’s school that the evangelist knew what procession he was describing—and no verified fact above forbids it. The two verdicts follow from two premises, and the study’s named distinction holds the space between them: attestation is not reception, and the absence of either is not falsity. The philology is what it is; what the Church has made of the reading is a separate question with separate evidence, and it occupies the next two sections.

One thing more, and it changes the frame. The whole debate reported above was conducted, on both sides, as if the reading were born with Burrows in 1940. It was not. Around the year 500, a Syriac poet was already preaching the Visitation with the ark—Mary an ark full of mysteries, John’s leap answering David’s dance before it. His name was Jacob of Serugh, and the Fathers are the next section’s business. The philology can carry the reader no further; the Fathers take him up.

## The Fathers: The Making of a Title

The Fathers gave the ark to Christ before they gave it to his Mother; and when at last they gave it to his Mother, they did not take it back from Christ. That is the whole shape of the story this section tells, and it can be told with dates. The philology of the last section ended with a promise: that the Church’s reading of the Visitation is older than the modern concordances that now defend it. The promise is kept here, but it is kept honestly, which means the story must be told as development and not as antiquity flat. Explicit predication — “Mary *is* the ark” — cannot be documented before the fifth century in any language. What can be documented before that is something else, and in some ways something greater: a Christological ark typology reaching back to the third century, in which the Virgin already stands at the ark’s making; and, in Syriac, a genuine fourth-century poetry that lets the figure rest on Mary a full generation before the Council of Ephesus. Every witness below is printed under its true name and its true date; where a famous attribution fails, the failure is stated as a finding, because in this study attestation is a question of fact.

### The Christological ark comes first

The oldest ark exegesis in the Church is about the body of Christ. Hippolytus of Rome (d. c. 235), commenting on Daniel, reads the ark of incorruptible wood as the Lord’s own incorrupt humanity. The reader who has met this Father in the apologetics collections has almost certainly met him saying something slightly — and consequentially — different, and the difference is worth a frame:

> **Witness — Hippolytus, fragment on Daniel, ANF translation (1886)**
>
> “…the Saviour appeared and showed His own body to the world, (born) of the Virgin, who was the ark overlaid with pure gold, with the Word within and the Holy Spirit without.”

In that Victorian English the relative clause fastens to the Virgin: *she* is the ark, and Hippolytus becomes the earliest “Mary is the ark” witness in the language, endlessly reproduced as such. But the sentence is not a stray fragment; it belongs to the genuine, continuously preserved *Commentary on Daniel*, at IV.24.3, and in the modern translation of the full text the ambiguity disappears: the Savior comes from the Virgin and then offers into the world “the Ark, his own body,” gilded in pure gold — within by the Word, without by the Spirit; and the commentary repeats the equation two paragraphs later, the Savior arriving in the world bearing the incorruptible ark that is his own body. The celebrated Marian reading of Hippolytus is a translation artifact. What Hippolytus actually witnesses is in its own way load-bearing: the ark’s incorruptible wood is Christ’s flesh, *and the Virgin is where that flesh came from*. A second fragment, on the Shepherd Psalm, says the same — the ark of imperishable wood is “the Saviour Himself,” sinless in his humanity, “of the virgin and the Holy Ghost inwardly, and outwardly of the word of God, like an ark overlaid with purest gold.”[^120]

The same pattern recurs in fifth-century Alexandria and Constantinople. Among the works transmitted with Cyril of Alexandria is a catena-like compilation on Old Testament texts — of doubtful authenticity, and labeled so here — which glosses the ark that went before Israel with the flat equation **kibōtos ho Christos**, “the ark is Christ”: as the ark of incorruptible wood and pure gold carried the law within it, so Christ’s humanity, incorrupt in respect of sin, held within it the Word of God.[^121] And Proclus of Constantinople, in a Theophany oration transmitted under his name, turns even Noah’s ark the same way: Noah framed an ark of incorruptible wood, but Christ, the spiritual Noah, built **ek tēs aphthorou Marias tēn tou sōmatos kibōton** — from the incorrupt Mary the ark of his own body.[^122] Mark the grammar of all three texts, for the whole development hangs on it: the ark is Christ’s body; Mary is the quarry of the incorruptible wood. She is inside the figure from the beginning — but as source, not yet as subject.

### Ephrem: allusion before Ephesus

The first texts in which the figure comes to rest on Mary herself are not Greek or Latin but Syriac, and they are earlier than almost anyone expects. Ephrem the Syrian (d. 373), in the genuine *Hymns on the Nativity*, sings the Nativity in the language of the ark’s victory over Dagon (1 Kings 5 = 1 Samuel 5): “There rose from the Rib a hidden power, which cut off Satan as Dagon: for in that Ark a Book was hidden that cried and proclaimed concerning the Conqueror.” The ark before which Dagon fell is here the bearer of the proclaiming Book — and the bearer is she. In a second hymn the holy household itself becomes the sanctuary: “Joseph rose to minister before his Lord, Who was in Mary. The priest ministered before Thy ark by reason of Thy holiness. Moses carried the tables of stone…and Joseph bare about the pure Tablet in whom the Son of the Creator was dwelling.”[^123] This is not the later predication-formula; it is allusive typology, woven rather than asserted. But it is genuine Ephrem, it is Mary-referred on the judgment of the standard modern translator of these hymns, and its author died in 373 — fifty-eight years before the Council of Ephesus. The often-quoted “Greek Ephrem” prayers that address Mary as ark and urn of manna are another matter: they belong to a later pseudonymous corpus and prove nothing about the fourth century.[^124]

### The fifth century: Greek and Syriac together

Then comes Ephesus (431), the definition of the Theotokos, and within a generation the thing itself: explicit, public, liturgical predication, in Greek and in Syriac at once.

The Greek series can be dated man by man. Proclus of Constantinople (d. 446/7), whose fifth oration is received as genuine in the modern critical study of his homilies, preaches Mary in a litany of titles: mother, handmaid, cloud, bridal chamber, and ark of the Master — **kibōtos, ou ton nomon bastasasa, alla ton nomothetēn kyophorēsasa**: an ark not carrying the law, but having borne in her womb the Lawgiver (the English renderings of the Greek homilists in this paragraph are editorial, except where Livius’s are credited).[^125] Hesychius, the presbyter-exegete of Jerusalem (d. after 451), whose Marian homilies are dated by the Oxford cult-of-saints researchers to the 410s and 420s, preaches an ark wider, longer, and more glorious than Noah’s: that one an ark of animals, this one of life; that one carried Noah, this one Noah’s Maker; that one had second and third stories, this one the whole fullness of the Trinity. And he is, on present evidence, the first man in Greek to read the ark verse of the psalter — *Arise, O Lord, into thy resting place: thou and the ark, which thou hast sanctified* (Ps 131:8, whose full career waits for the last section of this study) — of the Virgin by name. In Livius’s Victorian rendering: “Arise, Lord, into Thy rest, Thou and the Ark of Thy sanctification, which is very evidently the Virgin.” If you are the pearl, Hesychius argues to his Lord, fittingly is she the ark.[^126] Chrysippus of Jerusalem (d. 479), preaching in all likelihood for the city’s fifteenth-of-August feast, takes up the same psalm verse and answers it: “ark truly royal…ark most precious” — an ark, he goes on, not of all the animal kinds as Noah’s was, nor of the stone tablets as Israel’s desert companion, but the ark whose architect and indweller, pilot and merchant, fellow-traveler and guide is the Maker of all creation.[^127] Around these named preachers stands the anonymous witness that outlived them all: the Akathist hymn, composed in the fifth or sixth century — its popular ascription to Romanos the Melodist is late and unproven, and no ark epithet has been found in Romanos’s genuine corpus — salutes Mary in its twenty-third stanza as tabernacle of God the Word and **kibōte chrysōtheisa tō Pneumati**, ark gilded by the Spirit.[^128]

Two texts often shelved beside these must be moved, with their labels showing. The Annunciation homilies transmitted under Gregory Thaumaturgus (d. c. 270) do contain the full predication — “For the holy Virgin is in truth an ark, wrought with gold both within and without, that has received the whole treasury of the sanctuary” — but they are pseudonymous products of the post-Ephesus homiletic boom, as the ANF’s own editors already flagged; they witness the fifth-century flowering, not the third.[^129] The same disposition governs the celebrated Coptic homily of the Turin papyrus, which apostrophizes the Virgin as ark of the new covenant, clothed with purity instead of gold, containing the golden vessel of the true manna — the flesh in which divinity resides. It circulates everywhere as “St. Athanasius says Mary is the Ark.” It is a homily attributed to Athanasius in a Coptic papyrus; the attribution is disputed, and no scholarly defense of it has been located.[^130]

And then the finding that changes the map. At the very moment the Greek preachers were coining their litanies, a Syriac poet-bishop had the whole Visitation typology — the one the philology section reconstructed echo by echo — explicit and complete. Jacob of Serugh (c. 451–521), in his metrical homilies on the Annunciation and the Visitation, presents Mary abiding in Zachary’s house like “an ark full of mysteries”; regarded by the household as an ark full of fire; carried on the road as an ark full of scriptures, in whom dwelt the interpretation of prophecy’s mysteries. And he does not leave the comparison decorative: the babe exults in Elizabeth’s womb so that Mary’s magnificence might exceed the ark’s — John’s leap answering David’s dance before the ark (2 Kings 6 = 2 Samuel 6); and Mary’s three months in the priest’s house stand where the ark’s three months in Obededom’s house stood, a blessing on the roof that sheltered her.[^131] This is the full Lucan parallel — leap, dance, three months, blessed house — preached in West Syriac around the year 500, centuries before any concordance. The reading the modern critics call contrived was, to a poet with the Peshitta in his memory, simply visible.

### The Latin negatives, printed as findings

The West is another country, and honesty requires saying so plainly: the two greatest Latin Fathers yield no genuine Mary-ark text. Augustine (d. 430), expounding the very psalm verse Hesychius was reading Marially in the same decades, turns it elsewhere: “He is our Head; His ark is His Church: He arose first, the Church will arise also”; and he registers, as other men’s view, that “The Body of Christ, that was born of Mary, has been understood by some to be the ark of sanctification.” Church or Christ’s body — not Mary.[^132] For Ambrose (d. 397) no Marian ark text could be found at all after a directed search: his genuine ark exegesis is Noachic-ecclesial or Christological, and the finding is recorded as unfound-after-search rather than as proof of absence. What circulates everywhere under his name — and, in a second printing, under the name of Maximus of Turin — is one and the same anonymous sermon, printed twice in Migne: “saltavit propheta David ante arcam. Arcam autem quid nisi S. Mariam dixerimus? Siquidem arca intrinsecus portabat testamenti tabulas, Maria autem ipsius testamenti gestabat heredem” — David the prophet danced before the ark; and what shall we call the ark but holy Mary? — for the ark carried within it the tables of the testament, while Mary bore the testament’s own heir; the ark shone within and without with the gleam of gold, holy Mary shone within and without with the splendor of virginity (an editorial paraphrase, not a translation). The sermon is used in this study under its only honest label: a Latin sermon transmitted under both Ambrose’s and Maximus’s names, author unknown, probably of the fifth or sixth century or later.[^133] One more negative belongs beside these, because the lists assert the contrary: Cyril of Alexandria’s famous homily against Nestorius at Ephesus — the litany of the Theotokos as revered treasure of the whole world, unquenchable lamp, crown of virginity, scepter of orthodoxy, temple that cannot be destroyed, place of him whom no place can hold — contains no ark. The word **kibōtos** occurs nowhere in it, nor in the great encomium transmitted under his name.[^134]

### The summit, and the medieval West

The Greek development has a summit, and everyone has always known where it is. John of Damascus (d. c. 749), preaching on the Dormition, opens his second homily with the sentence that would eventually be read at Rome itself — as it circulates in English: “Today the sacred and living ark of the living God, who conceived her Creator Himself, takes up her abode in the temple of God, not made by hands,” as reported at PG 96:723; and the lesson continues that David her forefather exults. The third homily hails the true and living ark of the Lord; the first applies the ark verse of the psalm to her passage into rest.[^135] With the Damascene the title is no longer an epithet in a litany; it is the structural figure of a feast — the living ark carried into the temple not made with hands — and it is exactly this homily that the Roman Breviary would appoint as the Matins lessons of the Assumption from 1568 to 1950, a transmission that belongs to the next section.

And the medieval West? It carried the figure quietly, and mostly did not preach it — a double finding, and both halves matter. The quiet carrier came first: the hymn *Quem terra, pontus, sidera*, ascribed by long tradition to Venantius Fortunatus (d. c. 610) and sung in the office of Our Lady continuously thereafter, confesses in its third stanza: “Beata Mater munere, / Cujus, supernus Artifex, / Mundum pugillo continens, / Ventris sub arca clausus est” — blessed Mother, by whose gift the supernal Artificer, who holds the world in his palm, was enclosed beneath the ark of her womb (an editorial paraphrase, not a translation).[^136] Against that one singing stanza stand the silences, each verified in its sources. The treatise that governed the medieval West’s whole Assumption theology — *Cogitis me*, written c. 860 by Paschasius Radbertus and read for centuries at Matins under Jerome’s name — builds the feast entirely on the Song of Songs; the word **arca** does not occur in it. Bernard’s Assumption sermons contain one ark only, and it is Noah’s, in an ascetic digression. Thomas Aquinas, asking in the *Summa* what the ark signified, answers with the whole Hippolytan inheritance and no Marian clause at all: “He is also signified by the ark: because just as the ark was made of setim-wood, so was Christ’s body composed of most pure members”; the golden pot within is “His holy soul, having manna, i.e. ‘all the fulness of the Godhead.’ ” Across the eleven questions of the *Summa* on Our Lady and the infancy of Christ the word ark never appears.[^137] The chant repertory says the same thing with numbers: in the indexed corpus of the medieval office, the verse *Surge Domine in requiem tuam…* was sung at Paschaltide — of Christ’s rising — and in the rogation processions, and never once at the Assumption; of two hundred seventy indexed **arca** entries, not one belongs to the fifteenth of August. When the medieval Latin liturgy finally did sing Mary as ark, it was late and it was elsewhere: a responsory of the new Presentation feast brought west in 1372 (*Arca dei in qua reconditur* — the ark of God in which the sweet manna is hidden is the Virgin’s womb), and — the one liturgical enactment of the Lucan parallel this study has found — a responsory of the Visitation feast instituted in 1389, *Habitavit arca domini*, whose evident source is the ark’s sojourn in Obededom’s house, though the full text survives in the index truncated, in a single Munich manuscript, and the identification awaits the manuscript itself.[^138]

### What development looks like

Now the pattern can be stated, and it should be stated as a principle: in the Fathers, substance precedes predication, and Christ precedes every other referent. The third century reads the ark of incorruptible wood as the incorrupt flesh of Christ, with the Virgin as its source; the fourth, in Syriac, lets the figure rest on her allusively; the fifth, in Greek and Syriac at once — Proclus before 446, Hesychius and Chrysippus in Jerusalem, Jacob of Serugh by about 500, the anonymous Akathist — says it outright, and says it without ever un-saying the Christological reading, since every one of these texts defines her as ark precisely by what she carried. The West keeps the Christological reading almost undiluted for a millennium, one hymn stanza excepted, until Greek lessons and late feasts carry the Marian figure in. This is not the profile of a legend, which grows loudest where evidence is thinnest and backdates its credentials; the backdated credentials here — Ps.-Gregory, Ps.-Methodius, the Turin homily, the double-named Latin sermon — were removable, and this section has removed them, and the fifth-century flowering stands without their help. It is the profile of development: a figure whose substance was in the Church’s hands from the beginning, articulated explicitly when the Theotokos definition gave the articulation its grammar. What the Fathers achieved was attestation — ancient, datable, cross-lingual. Whether the Church made that attestation her own is a question about reception; and reception belongs to the liturgy, to which the Fathers, in the person of the Damascene read aloud at Roman Matins, now hand the title.

## Reception: Liturgy, Devotion, Magisterium

The Fathers coined the title; it remained for the Church to pray it. The distinction stated formally in the toll-gate section — attestation is not reception, and the absence of either is not falsity — has carried the argument this far; the present section supplies the second axis its evidence. What follows is reception dated act by act, and the kinds of act are kept apart as carefully as the dates: a hymn sung in Lent, a litany approved by a bull, a devotional classic, a Matins lesson, a dogmatic constitution, and a lectionary rubric are different sorts of ecclesial speech, and each is named below for what it is. The whole chain is set out in one table at the end.

### The Byzantine liturgy

The oldest continuous liturgical home of the title is Byzantine. The Akathist hymn, whose ark-line the previous section weighed among the fifth- and sixth-century witnesses, is not a text the liturgy quotes; it is a text the liturgy sings entire, prescribed in the Triodion for the Fridays of Great Lent and, on the fifth of them, celebrated in its own right. In its psi-stanza — the twenty-third of its twenty-four alphabetic stanzas — Mary is hymned as the living temple, **empsychon naon**, and greeted: **chaire, Kibōte chrysōtheisa tō Pneumati** — “Hail, Ark gilded by the Spirit” (the English rendering is editorial). The gilding of Exodus 25 is transposed onto the overshadowing of Luke 1:35; it is the tightest one-line form the typology has ever taken, and the Byzantine rite prays it every Lent.[^139]

At the Dormition (15 August) the ark enters not through the readings — the three Old Testament lessons of Great Vespers are Genesis 28, Ezechiel 43–44, and Proverbs 9, and 2 Kings 6 (= 2 Samuel 6) is read at no point in the feast — but through psalmody and hymnography. The verses appointed with the aposticha at Great Vespers, and the Alleluia verses of the Divine Liturgy, are taken from Psalm 131 (the Hebrew counts it 132): verse 8, in which the Lord is called to arise into his resting place, he and the ark he has sanctified, with verse 11, the oath sworn to David. The first canon of the feast, which the service books ascribe to Cosmas of Maiuma, calls Mary the animate ark, and narrates from the ancient Dormition traditions the episode of Jephonias, the man who laid insolent hands on her funeral bier and lost them — the liturgy composing its own Uzzah antitype, the fear of 2 Kings 6:6–7 re-taught at Mary’s burial. In its sixth ode the same canon hands her the whole furniture of the sanctuary: candlestick of the immaterial Light, golden censer, the jar and the staff, the divinely inscribed tablet, the holy ark, the table of the Word of life. These texts are described here rather than quoted, because the accessible English service books are derivative and their translations unidentified; the study reports their content with the loci and leaves the wording to them. One troparion of this canon — God who kept her virgin in childbirth kept her body incorrupt in the tomb — is the very Byzantine text *Munificentissimus Deus* would quote in 1950 under the citation *Menaei Totius Anni*; the identification of the constitution’s citation with this ode is the study’s own, and it is textually exact in substance.[^140]

At the Entrance of the Theotokos into the Temple (21 November) the Byzantine rite makes its most explicit lectionary use of the ark. As the Greek Archdiocese’s published outline of the feast reports, Great Vespers reads Exodus 40 (the tabernacle erected, the ark installed, the glory filling it), 3 Kings 7:51–8:11 (the ark carried into the holy of holies, the glory filling the house), and Ezechiel 43:27–44:4 (the shut east gate): the child entering the temple is hymned as the true ark entering the true holy of holies. Honesty requires here the same disclosure the Kebra Nagast received in the narrative panel: the feast’s story — Mary dwelling in the temple — rests on the Protoevangelium of James, an apocryphon transmitted under an apostolic name, not on canonical history. The lectionary’s typological act is real reception; the narrative it adorns is not thereby historicized.[^141]

### Loreto, Liguori, and the devotional chain

In the West the title’s chief carrier was not a liturgical book but an approved devotion. The Litany of Loreto sets **Foederis arca** among its symbolic invocations, between the House of Gold and the Gate of Heaven. Its history, so far as it can be established: the earliest known genuine Marian litany, in a twelfth-century Mainz codex, shows no ark invocation in the specimens its historian Angelo De Santi printed; the earliest surviving print of the Loreto form is a Dillingen booklet of about 1558, circulated by Peter Canisius; De Santi’s collations imply — direct inspection of the 1558 print has not been made for this study — that **Foederis arca** stood already in the sixteenth-century core, and no source examined records it as a dated later addition, as **Auxilium christianorum** and the twentieth-century invocations are. Sixtus V approved the litany by the bull *Reddituri* (11 July 1587); a decree under Clement VIII (1601) suppressed all other Marian litanies for public use and so fixed the text. Who coined the invocation, and when, cannot be said; its content simply compresses the Greek hymnographic commonplace into two Latin words.[^142]

The litany did not pray alone. In the 1570s, preaching at Louvain, Robert Bellarmine argued the Assumption from the ark: “And who, I ask, could believe that the ark of holiness, the dwelling place of the Word of God, the temple of the Holy Spirit, could be reduced to ruin?” — words *Munificentissimus Deus* would quote four centuries later (MD 34).[^143] Yet within decades the greatest Catholic commentator of the age shows the other side of the ledger: Cornelius a Lapide’s commentary on Luke 1:39–56, searched for this study in both the English translation and the Latin, contains no ark, no Obededom, no reference to 2 Kings 6; the three months of Luke 1:56 are explained from Ambrose and Origen as ministry to Elizabeth.[^144] The devotional title flourished in a Church whose exegesis of the Visitation knew nothing of the parallel. The two chains are distinct, and neither may be backdated into the other.

Alphonsus Liguori’s *Glories of Mary* (1750) deployed the title three ways. In the Visitation discourse, Obededom’s blessed house supplies the measure: “But with how much greater blessings are those persons enriched who receive some loving visit from this living ark of God, as was the divine mother!” In the second Assumption discourse the angels plead in the words of Psalm 131:8 that the Lord “bring with thee also the living ark of thy sanctification, namely, thy mother, who was the ark sanctified by thee when thou didst inhabit her womb”; and as David brought up the ark with shouting and trumpet, so “with far more splendid and glorious pomp he ordained that his mother should enter into heaven.” And in a novena of meditations on the litany itself he glosses the invocation **Foederis arca**: Mary “an ark more spacious than that of Noe” — **Arca Noe largior** — an attribution to Hesychius made at Liguori’s word. Note carefully what this is and is not. It is pious analogy, not a source-claim about Luke. But the fusion of Psalm 131:8 with the procession of 2 Kings 6 — the exact pairing that the philology of the Visitation section and the definition of 1950 would each make in its own register — stands assembled here, devotionally, two centuries before either.[^145]

The nineteenth century carries the chain into magisterium and dogmatics. *Ineffabilis Deus* (1854) has the Fathers beholding Mary’s privilege “in that ark of Noah, which was built by divine command and escaped entirely safe and sound from the common shipwreck of the whole world,” and celebrating her as “the ark and house of holiness which Eternal Wisdom built” — ark language, echoing the Latin psalm’s **arca sanctificationis**, in the bull of the Immaculate Conception a century before the Assumption definition.[^146] Matthias Scheeben’s dogmatics (1882) treats the ark as “as much a prototype of Mary as of the humanity of Christ,” reads Luke 1:35 as the power of the Most High descending “into Mary as into the true ark of the covenant,” dwelling in her more perfectly than it had dwelt in the cloud over the old ark — and polices the boundary himself: the propitiatory he judges “rather a prototype of Christ.” The Christological priority on which the patristic panel insisted is thus native to the modern dogmatic tradition too.[^147] And one eloquent silence belongs in the record: Newman’s meditations on the litany for the month of May gloss thirty-one titles, and **Foederis arca** is not among them; the word ark never occurs in them, nor in his two Assumption discourses, nor in the Marian sections of the *Letter to Pusey* that were searched. His Marian architecture is the Second Eve. The chain does not need him; the record should show that its most eloquent nineteenth-century English witness chose other figures.[^148]

### The Roman office and the acts of 1950

The Roman rite’s own history is easily misstated, because its Mass and its office diverge. The Assumption Mass before 1950 contains no ark and no Psalm 131:8 anywhere in its propers; its Gospel was Martha and Mary (Luke 10:38–42) — as it happens, the same Gospel the Byzantine Liturgy of the Dormition reads. But the office is another matter. At Matins the Church sang Fortunatus’s hymn *Quem terra, pontus, sidera*, with its sixth-century stanza on the Maker whom the world cannot contain: **ventris sub arca clausus est**, closed beneath the ark of her womb. And from the Pian breviary of 1568, as the standard histories report, down to 1950 — verified for this study in a 1942 printing — the second nocturn of 15 August read John Damascene’s second homily on the Dormition, beginning:

> **Witness — Breviarium Romanum, 1942 printing, 15 August, Matins, lectio iv**
>
> **Hodie sacra et animata arca Dei viventis, quae suum in utero concepit Creatorem, requiescit in templo Domini, quod nullis est exstructum manibus. Et David exsultat ejus parens…** — “Today the sacred and living ark of the living God, she who conceived her Creator in her womb, takes her rest in the temple of the Lord not built with hands; and David her forefather exsults” (an editorial paraphrase, not a translation).

The Greek summit-text of the patristic panel was thus the Roman Church’s own Matins lesson every 15 August for nearly four centuries. In 1950 the revised office kept the Damascene lessons and promoted the ark into the hymnody: the new Matins hymn carries the stanza **Arca non putri fabricata ligno / Manna tu servas, fluit unde virtus, / Ipsa qua surgent animata rursus / Ossa sepulcris** — an ark built of no rotting wood, you keep the manna from which flows the power by which ensouled bones will rise again from their tombs (an editorial paraphrase, not a translation). The new Mass formulary (*Signum magnum*, introduced 1951) set Apocalypse 12:1 at the entrance and the Visitation-Magnificat Gospel at the center — and still contained no explicit ark text, for Apocalypse 11:19 was not read. The ark reached the Roman office centuries before it reached the Roman Mass.[^149]

*Munificentissimus Deus* (1 November 1950) is the chain’s magisterial summit, and precision about what it does is owed. In its paragraph 26, theologians and preachers “following in the footsteps of the holy Fathers” are described as applying to the Assumption the psalm and the type together:

> **Witness — Munificentissimus Deus 26 — AAS 42 (1950) 762–763, with the vatican.va English**
>
> **sunt qui Psaltae sententiam inducant: “Surge, Domine, in requiem tuam, tu et Arca sanctificationis tuae”; atque Arcam foederis, incorruptibili ligno instructam atque in Dei templo positam, quasi imaginem cernant purissimi Mariae Virginis corporis…** — some “have looked upon the Ark of the Covenant, built of incorruptible wood and placed in the Lord’s temple, as a type of the most pure body of the Virgin Mary, preserved and exempt from all the corruption of the tomb and raised up to such glory in heaven.”

Three precisions. First, the constitution’s own note at the words “the holy Fathers” identifies its exemplars: in the Latin of the *Acta Apostolicae Sedis* the note reads **hom. II, 2, 11** — John Damascene’s second Dormition homily at numbers 2 and 11, number 2 being the **sacra et animata arca** passage itself — together with the Encomium “attributed to St. Modestus,” the attribution flagged by the constitution’s own wording. It is a **Cf.** citation: the constitution cites the Damascene ark texts in its apparatus and does not quote them in its body. Second, paragraph 29 reports Anthony of Padua arguing from Isaias 60:13 that the ark of his sanctification “has risen up, since on this day the Virgin Mother has been taken up,” and paragraph 34 gives the Bellarmine sentence quoted above; the word ark occurs in exactly three paragraphs of the constitution — 26, 29, and 34. Third, the dogmatic definition itself, paragraph 44, uses no ark language at all. The type stands in the argument, not in the defined formula — reception at the highest level, exercised with the tradition’s own restraint.[^150]

Fourteen years later the most authoritative conciliar text on Mary passed the title over entirely: the word **arca** does not occur in *Lumen Gentium*, in Latin or in English. Chapter VIII prefers “temple of the Holy Spirit” (LG 53), and its list of invoked titles is Advocate, Auxiliatrix, Adjutrix, Mediatrix (LG 62). The finding is printed because popular literature often implies conciliar endorsement of the title; there is none to cite. By the two-axis rule the silence subtracts nothing — but it is a silence, and the record keeps it.[^151]

### From Marialis Cultus to the Catechism

*Marialis Cultus* (1974) restored the title to papal speech, twice. At MC 6, on the restored solemnity of the Annunciation, the liturgies celebrate the Virgin who, by receiving the one Mediator into her womb, “became the true Ark of the Covenant and true Temple of God” — in the Latin, **vera Foederis Arca**, the litany’s exact phrase, capitalized. At MC 26, reflecting on Luke 1:35, the Fathers are said to have seen in the Spirit’s intervention what transformed her into “the ‘Ark of the Covenant’ or ‘the Ark of Holiness’” (**Foederis vel Sanctificationis Arcam**); and the note appended there is a papally curated patristic dossier in miniature — Severus of Antioch, Hesychius of Jerusalem, Chrysippus, Andrew of Crete, John Damascene, with the Akathist’s psi-stanza cited in the companion note — the magisterium doing in an apparatus what the patristic panel of this study did at length.[^152]

The postconciliar lectionary (1969) then did what no Roman Mass formulary had done. Its selections are described here by reference, as the rights of the liturgical translations require and as the argument in any case only needs: the reception datum is the Church’s choice and pairing of texts, not their modern English wording. At the Vigil of the Assumption (Lectionary 621) the first reading is 1 Paralipomenon 15:3–4, 15–16; 16:1–2 — David bringing the ark up to the tent he had pitched for it — and the responsorial psalm is 131 (the lectionary counts it 132), with a refrain drawn from verse 8, the resting-place-and-ark verse; the Gospel is Luke 11:27–28. At the Mass of the day (Lectionary 622) the entrance antiphon keeps *Signum magnum* (Apocalypse 12:1); the first reading begins at Apocalypse 11:19a — the verse before the medieval chapter division, the ark seen in the opened temple — and runs into 12:1–6a and 10ab; and the Gospel is Luke 1:39–56, the Visitation. For the first time the Roman Mass of the Assumption reads the ark narrative, prays the ark psalm, and joins the ark verse to the Woman and to the Visitation within a single formulary. What that splice means is the business of the study’s final panel; that it was made, and made in 1969 and not before, is the datum here. The current Liturgia Horarum matches it at Lauds, where the intercessions pray: **Verbum aeternum, quod Mariam habitationis tuae arcam incorruptibilem elegisti — libera nos a corruptione peccati** — Eternal Word, who chose Mary as the incorruptible ark of your dwelling: free us from the corruption of sin (an editorial paraphrase, not a translation).[^153]

The Catechism gathers the whole into one clause. Commenting on the angel’s “the Lord is with thee,” CCC 2676 reads: Mary “is the daughter of Zion in person, the ark of the covenant, the place where the glory of the Lord dwells” — in the Latin, **est ipsamet filia Sion, Foederis Arca, locus in quo Domini gloria commoratur**, the litany’s capitalized phrase once more. Here the two typologies that the Visitation section kept deliberately distinct — Daughter Zion, the city and people whom the Lord indwells, and the ark, the vessel that carries him — are fused; and the fusion is itself reception, not ancient exegesis. Elsewhere the Catechism receives the Daughter-Zion reading in so many words — “It was quite correct for the angel Gabriel to greet her as the ‘Daughter of Zion’: ‘Rejoice.’ ” (CCC 722; cf. 489) — and Joseph Ratzinger had made the fusion in his own voice, Marian devotion being “the praise of him…whom she bears, the true, incorruptible, indestructible Ark of the Covenant”: the ark predicated of Christ first, and of Mary as his bearer. One precision: the ark clause of CCC 2676 stands under the Annunciation greeting, not under Elizabeth’s; it is the next article, 2677, that opens with Luke 1:43 — “whence is this to me, that the mother of my Lord should come to me” — the classic echo of 2 Kings 6:9, which the Catechism quotes without citing 2 Kings 6. The juxtaposition is the Catechism’s; the identification remains the exegetes’.[^154]

John Paul II shows the same two-axis texture within a single pontificate. His Angelus address of 10 July 1983, in the series on the Loreto invocations, is a full ark catechesis: the title, he says, **viene da molto lontano** — comes from very far back — and from the Annunciation onward **il suo grembo diviene l’arca della Nuova alleanza**, her womb becomes the ark of the new covenant, the sanctuary where the incarnate Presence of God came to dwell (editorial paraphrases of the quoted Italian). Yet *Redemptoris Mater* (1987), his Marian encyclical, never uses the word, working instead with the daughter of Zion; and his Visitation catechesis of 1996 builds Elizabeth’s “mother of my Lord” on Psalm 109:1, not on 2 Kings 6. Reception is act by act; it was never a uniform policy.[^155]

The table reads two ways, and both readings are true. The title is ancient: a sixth-century Latin hymn, an anonymous Byzantine masterpiece, a Greek homily read at Roman Matins from 1568 — no one invented the ark of the covenant as a name for Mary in modern times. The liturgy’s embrace of the texts is largely modern: 1 Paralipomenon 15–16, the Psalm 131:8 refrain, and Apocalypse 11:19 entered the Roman Mass only in 1969, and the exegetical case for reading Luke 1 through 2 Kings 6 found no commentator before the twentieth century in this study’s audit, though Liguori had assembled its devotional raw materials by 1750. And the Church knew what she was doing both times: when she prayed the title for centuries without a philological warrant, and when, the philology in hand, she finally set the old texts into the feast itself. Development has exactly this shape. One text remains, the one the lectionary’s splice points at: the ark seen in the opened temple, and the Woman clothed with the sun. To that text, and to the master table of the whole study’s evidence, the last panel now turns.

## The Assumption and the Woman Clothed with the Sun

Two promises were made earlier and both fall due here. The narrative panel ended with one sentence held in reserve: at the far end of the Bible, John saw the ark. The reception panel ended with a splice pointed at one text: since 1969 the Roman Church has read the ark verse and the Woman clothed with the sun as a single lesson on the day she celebrates Mary’s Assumption. The principle governing this last examination can be stated in one plain sentence: the canon answers the question *where is the ark?* not with a place but with a vision of heaven, and the Church answers the question *where is Mary?* from the same page. Whether those two answers are one answer is precisely what must now be weighed, and weighed by the rule this study has used throughout: attestation first, reception second, neither confused with the other, the absence of either never mistaken for falsity.

### The psalm of the resting place

Begin with the verse that has followed the ark since Solomon’s dedication and now stands over Mary’s feast. Psalm 131 (the Hebrew counts it 132) prays the ark into its rest; and the Douay Bible, by an accident of its own textual inheritance, lets the reader see the verse’s two ancient forms without leaving the base text. The psalter follows the Gallican and Septuagint line; the Chronicler’s quotation of the same prayer follows the Hebrew:

> **Witness — Psalm 131:8 and 2 Paralipomenon 6:41, Douay-Rheims**
>
> Arise, O Lord, into thy resting place: thou and the ark, which thou hast sanctified. *(Psalm 131:8)*
>
> Now therefore arise, O Lord God, into thy resting place, thou and the ark of thy strength: let thy priests, O Lord God, put on salvation, and thy saints rejoice in good things. *(2 Paralipomenon 6:41)*

The ark of thy strength; the ark, which thou hast sanctified. One Bible, both traditions. The Greek psalter reads **hē kibōtos tou hagiasmatos sou**, the ark of thy holiness or sanctification, and the Latin of the Church’s use renders it as a noun-phrase, **arca sanctificationis tuae** — the form the Assumption liturgy and the constitution of 1950 employ, native to the study’s Latin edition though not to its English, whose relative clause (“which thou hast sanctified”) says the same thing more quietly.[^156]

Before the verse was Mary’s it was Christ’s, and the evidence for that priority is older than any Marian use of it. Primasius, bishop of Hadrumetum in the mid-sixth century, commenting on the very verse of the Apocalypse this section will reach, quotes the psalm and applies it to the incarnate Lord: the ark seen in heaven is **mysterium incarnationis Christi**, the mystery of Christ’s Incarnation, and **Exsurge, domine, in requiem tuam, tu et arca sanctificationis tuae** is the Lord arising into his rest with the ark of his own sanctified flesh — an editorial paraphrase, not a translation, of a Latin text read for this study in a transcription based on the critical edition.[^157] The medieval Latin office agrees with him: as the patristic panel’s chant-database audit recorded, the one responsory verse built on Psalm 131:8 was sung in Paschaltide — the Lord risen with the ark of his body — and never, in the indexed manuscripts, at the Assumption.[^158] The oldest typological career of the Assumption proof-verse is paschal and Christological.

Its Marian career has already been dated, act by act, in the two preceding sections, and is only gathered here: Hesychius of Jerusalem, in the decades around 420, the first man in this study’s evidence to read the verse of the Virgin by name; Anthony of Padua in 1231, the earliest Western application to the Assumption that the audit could find; Liguori in 1750, fusing the psalm with the procession of 2 Kings 6 (= 2 Samuel 6) in a devotional classic; *Munificentissimus Deus* in 1950, quoting the verse twice on its way to the definition; the Roman Vigil Mass since 1969, singing verse 8 as its refrain while the ark procession is read.[^159] The verse’s history is the study’s whole lesson in miniature: a Christological antiquity, a Marian development, a modern liturgical embrace — and, by the rule paid at the toll-gate, the lateness of the last two subtracts nothing from their reality as acts of the reading Church, provided no one backdates them. No one here will.

### The ark and the Woman: Apocalypse 11:19–12:1

The text itself must be seen whole, because the numbers that divide it are a thirteenth-century convenience and the vision knows nothing of them. At the sounding of the seventh trumpet, heaven’s temple opens:

> **Witness — Apocalypse 11:19–12:1, Douay-Rheims**
>
> And the temple of God was opened in heaven: and the ark of his testament was seen in his temple, and there were lightnings, and voices, and an earthquake, and great hail. And a great sign appeared in heaven: A woman clothed with the sun, and the moon under her feet, and on her head a crown of twelve stars:

“ark of his testament” is Douay’s rendering of the same construct elsewhere printed “ark of the covenant”; the terminology section of the first panel owns that note. The reader who has followed this study wants to know one thing: did the ancients read these two sentences together?[^160]

The answer has two halves, and both must be printed, because a popular sentence and its popular refutation are each half wrong. The ancient commentators were checked at this verse, Greek and Latin, for this study. Victorinus of Pettau, the earliest Latin commentator (d. c. 304), reads the opened temple as “a manifestation of our Lord,” the ark’s appearance as “the preaching of the Gospel and the forgiveness of sins, and all the gifts whatever that came with Him,” and the woman as “the ancient Church of fathers, and prophets, and saints, and apostles.” Oecumenius, whose sixth-century work is the earliest full Greek commentary and the first to read the woman of 12:1 directly as Mary — “the mother of our Savior,” pure in soul and body, of the same substance as we are — reads the ark of 11:19 as something else entirely: the hidden good things of the age to come, the things eye has not seen (1 Corinthians 2:9), revealed to the saints. The verdict sentence writes itself: the one ancient commentator who held both pieces in his hands did not join them. Andrew of Caesarea (c. 611) makes the ark the good things concealed in Christ, “in whom all the fullness of divinity dwelt bodily” (Colossians 2:9), reports that “some” had understood the woman to be the Theotokos, and sides with Methodius: she is the Church. Cassiodorus’s summary skips the ark of 11:19 altogether while touching the woman partly Marially; the anonymous Greek scholia pass over ark and woman in silence. So the first half of the finding, hardened by the audit: *no ancient commentator reads the ark of Apocalypse 11:19 as Mary* — not even the one who read the very next verse of her.[^161]

The second half overturns what apologetic and anti-apologetic literature alike assume. An ancient commentator *does* join the ark and the woman — the most influential of them all. Tyconius, the Donatist whose commentary of about 380 shaped the whole Latin tradition, writes at 11:19 that “it is understood that the church is the Ark of the Covenant,” and quotes Jeremias 3:16 at the verse as his warrant — the prophecy, weighed in the second panel, that the old ark would neither be remembered nor remade; and at 12:1, by his rule of genus and species, he makes heaven, the temple in heaven, and the woman clothed with the sun “one and the same thing,” the Church. He even supplies the structural claim the modern lectionary needs: at 11:19, he says, the vision recapitulates from the nativity of the Lord, and chapter 12 restates the same content more extensively. Primasius repeats the genus-species chain for temple, heaven, and woman — “the church clothed with Christ” — though he lifts the ark out of it and gives it, as quoted above, to the Incarnation; and Bede, in Marshall’s Victorian English, carries the incarnational ark to its most finished form: in the Church, “the ark of His Incarnation is laid open to the whole world,” the veil rent, and “like the heavenly manna in clean gold is the Divinity in a holy body.” Elsewhere Bede can say simply “the ark of the Lord’s body.”[^162] So the corrected finding, in full: the structural continuity of 11:19 with 12:1 — one movement, ark and woman a single referent — is anciently attested; what has no ancient witness is the *Marian* identification of either term at this verse. The ancient ark of the Apocalypse, where it is not the Church, is Christ’s flesh — the flesh, Primasius adds in the same breath, of one begotten of the inviolate Virgin in a new birth. The ancient reading stands one explicit step short of Mary, and no ancient took the step.

Two more facts frame the verse, and the study concedes both in full. First, within the Apocalypse the ark belongs to a furnished heavenly sanctuary that the book keeps steadily in view: the altar of 6:9 and 8:3–5, the ark of 11:19, the temple opening again at 15:5 under its wilderness name, the tent of the testimony. Oecumenius and Andrew both read this architecture as the archetype of Bezalel’s tent — the pattern shown to Moses, of which the tabernacle was the copy — and Bede expressly ties 15:5 back to 11:19. Second, the modern critical account of the verse’s function: the storm formula that closes each septet of the book grows by one member at each recurrence — 4:5, 8:5, 11:19, 16:18–21 — so that, as Bauckham’s analysis has it, the theophany of 11:19 marks the climactic close of the trumpets and encompasses what follows; the ark’s appearance is covenant theophany in the manner of Sinai, and the woman of 12:1, for the critical mainstream as for the patristic majority, is the people of God, with the Marian sense as reception history. This is a school’s verdict resting on that school’s premises — but here, uniquely in this study, the school and the Fathers substantially coincide, and honesty says so plainly.[^163]

Nothing in what the Church now does with the verse requires denying any of that. It requires only seeing what she did. The Assumption constitution itself used the Woman of chapter 12 guardedly and never cited 11:19; the 1950 Mass put Apocalypse 12:1 at the entrance and still read no ark text. It was the postconciliar lectionary, in 1969, that began the day’s first reading at 11:19a and ran it into the vision of the Woman, then closed the formulary with the Visitation Gospel — so that in one Mass the Church reads the ark seen in the opened temple, the Woman crowned with stars, and Elizabeth’s question at the door, the question this study traced to 2 Kings 6:9. The splice is deliberate, datable, and hers. On the principles this study adopted at the toll-gate — the liturgy as a privileged act of the reading Church — that selection is not decoration; it is the Church saying, in the idiom proper to her, where she finds Mary in the canon’s last vision. The reading is received; it is not, and should never be printed as, an exegesis the Fathers drew.[^164] One adjacent thread deserves its footnote and no more: the “hidden manna” promised to the conqueror at Apocalypse 2:17, which modern exegesis hears against the hidden ark-and-urn of the Maccabean and apocalyptic tradition; the ancient commentators heard the Bread come down from heaven — Victorinus glossing immortality, Andrew the Bread of Life — and only the modern instruments connect the urn. The two readings converge on the same Christ; only the second is ancient.[^165]

What remains is the study’s own act, and it is labeled as such.[^166]

> **Source-grounded synthesis — the project’s reading**
>
> The second letter of 2 Machabees left the hidden ark under a promise with a horizon: the place unknown “till God gather together the congregation of the people, and receive them to mercy,” and then disclosure — “the Lord will shew these things, and the majesty of the Lord shall appear, and there shall be a cloud,” the cloud of Moses and of Solomon. The Apocalypse’s seventh trumpet sounds precisely such a horizon: the moment the loud voices in heaven proclaim that the kingdom of this world has passed to the Lord and to his Christ (Apocalypse 11:15), the temple opens and the ark is seen. The project reads the one text as answering the other: the disclosure promised for the ingathering, granted at the ingathering — not a relic produced from a cave, but the reality the relic guarded, shown at last where it always belonged. At this same verse the ancients stood at a crossroads. Tyconius turned toward the Church: the ark superseded, Jerusalem become the throne (Jeremias 3:16 quoted at the verse). The lectionary of 1969, and this study following it, turn the other way at the same crossroads: toward the Mother of God, the living ark whom the next sentence of the vision shows crowned in heaven — the ark of the Assumption. And the two turns are not rivals. The Church and the Mother of God are not competitors for the figure; the Fathers who read the Woman as the Church and the liturgy that reads her as Mary are reading one mystery at two distances, for Mary is what the Church will be, and the ark that went up is the first of the vessels of the Presence to reach its resting place. This reading is the project’s own synthesis: its verbal skeleton is verified above, its structural continuity is anciently attested in Tyconius, its Marian form is attested by no ancient commentator and received in the Church’s lectionary.

The table should be read the way the study was written. The attestation column is honest to the point of severity: most rows show no ancient witness for the Marian reading, and where antiquity speaks at all it speaks of Christ first — his body the gilded ark, his flesh the ark of the Incarnation, his resurrection the psalm’s arising. The reception column is equally real: a catechism, a dogmatic constitution’s argument, two rites’ liturgies. The named distinction holds the two columns apart and holds them both: attestation is not reception, and the absence of either is not falsity. A tradition that put Christ first and let his Mother’s title grow from his — ark as bearer, figure from figure — is not a tradition caught inventing; it is a tradition developing in the only order sound doctrine allows.[^167]

Where, then, is the ark? The question opened this study, and it can now be answered three times over. History answers: unknown — a last sighting under Josiah, then silence. The canon answers: seen — in the opened temple of heaven, at the last trumpet, when the kingdom is the Lord’s. The liturgy answers: assumed — the living ark carried into the temple not made with hands, while the Church sings the psalm of the resting place. And the three answers end in one sentence, which is the whole of this study’s finding and the only map it offers: the ark is with God, carried into his rest by the One it carried.

## Coda: Carrying the Presence

What the ark teaches is not, in the end, information about a box. It is the manners of creatures before the Presence. Three panels of evidence have gone before this page — a journey, a pattern, and a reading — and this coda adds nothing to any of them. It asks the question the sources themselves keep forcing on anyone who reads them slowly: granted all of that, how am I to behave? For the doctrine on which every panel rested is not an antiquarian one. The God who spoke from between the cherubims has not withdrawn from the earth. He makes his abode with the man who loves him (John 14:23); Paul has to remind the Corinthians that their bodies are temples of the Holy Ghost (1 Corinthians 6:19); and every communicant walking back from the altar is, for those minutes, a bearer of Christ in a sense more literal than any figure this study has printed. The modes of presence differ, and theology keeps them carefully distinct; the manners they ask of us are of one family. And the first grammar of those manners is Israel’s long schooling before the ark.

### The Visitation as the pattern of apostolate

The principle first, stated flatly: works are the overflow of a presence carried, or they are noise. The old masters of the interior life put this in a figure the study may restate in its own words: a cistern serves the valley below it only because it is first full, while a bare channel passes water all day, keeps none, and runs dry the hour its source is cut. The apostle is meant to be a cistern, not a channel.[^168]

The first apostolic journey of the new covenant shows what that means, because it was made by a woman newly with child. Mary, overshadowed by the power of the most High (Luke 1:35), “rising up” went “into the hill country with haste” (Luke 1:39). She brought Elizabeth no gift and no program; she brought only what she carried, and at her plain greeting the house filled with the Holy Ghost — Elizabeth cried out, and “the infant in my womb leaped for joy” (Luke 1:44). Then the visit of the Presence became service: she “abode with her about three months” (Luke 1:56), the last months of an old woman’s difficult waiting, and her one recorded utterance in that house is praise, the Magnificat (Luke 1:46–55).[^169] The order of the narrative is itself the doctrine. Luke sets the overshadowing before the journey: she is filled before she is sent, and she does not go until she carries. Even her haste, the one detail a busy man reads with relief, will not serve as his alibi — it is not the haste of someone escaping an empty house, but the overflow of a fullness that cannot wait to become service. And Luke, who records Elizabeth’s cry to the syllable, records not one word of the greeting that provoked it (Luke 1:40–41). What entered that house with Mary was not eloquence.[^170] The preacher’s extension — that the house which receives the bearer of Christ is blessed, as Obededom’s house was blessed in the ark’s three months there (2 Kings 6:11 = 2 Samuel 6:11) — is homiletics rather than demonstrated typology, and the project’s records say so plainly; but a coda is the lawful place for homiletics, and as homiletics it states nothing beyond the principle of this paragraph: where Christ is carried in, blessing follows him.

Then the examination writes itself, and it must be made in the first person or not at all. I have reason to fear that in me the channel has outrun the cistern: if I speak about God more fluently than I speak to him; if my works have multiplied in the same years in which my prayer has shortened; if I go everywhere with haste and could not say, on arriving, that I carry anything; if the last soul who left my company went away impressed by me and unchanged toward my Master. The figures this study has watched — Levites under the poles, David counting six paces, a young woman on the hill road into Juda — never confused the carrying with the thing carried. Let the whole of Panel I condense to one question, small enough to take through any doorway on any errand: *whom do I carry when I go?*

### Reverence, and the fear of the holy

Again the principle first: the fear of the holy is not the opposite of love; it is love’s grammar. This study did not soften Uzzah or Beth-shemesh (sections on the catastrophe and the processions), and it declines now to turn them into threats; the narratives are not blackmail, and Panel II drew their lesson exactly — the Presence is not managed. The law had said it before either incident, and said it to the very men whose office was carrying: the sons of Caath were to bear the holy things only after the veiling, forbidden to look on them “by any curiosity… otherwise they shall die” (Numbers 4:20).[^171] The severity is Scripture’s own; the study’s part is only to refuse to explain it away. What God has taken for his own use is not afterward common. That is the whole grammar, and love speaks it or speaks something else.

But notice what the same law provided. The chapter that forbade the look prescribed the veiling; the sentence that threatened the Caathites appointed them their poles and their shoulders. The severity and the tenderness are one provision: God did not leave Israel to guess how to come near, but taught them, in cubits and coverings and an appointed manner of carrying, how the unbearable could dwell in the middle of the camp and no one be destroyed. Reverence, in the canon, is not the distance love keeps from God; it is the nearness God makes possible for love. The survivors of Bethsames asked who could stand before so holy a God (1 Kings 6:20 = 1 Samuel 6:20), and the question was better than they knew; the rest of this study has been the long answer, which ends with the Presence carried in a girl’s body and laid in a manger. The holiness never lessened. The provision widened.

The Church never outgrew that grammar; she set it to music. The Byzantine office of the Dormition, which hymns Mary as the living ark, carries in its canon the old Transitus episode of Jephonias, the insolent man who laid hands on the Virgin’s funeral bier and lost them — the liturgy’s own Uzzah, sung each fifteenth of August; and on the eve of the same feast the Church reads Ezechiel’s vision of the east gate kept shut because the Lord had entered by it (Ezechiel 44:1–2).[^172] That shut gate is the Fathers’ figure for consecrated things. Ambrose asks, in his work on the instruction of a virgin, what that gate is if not Mary, shut because virgin; and it is there — at the gate, not at Uzzah’s cart — that the old tradition anchors what it says of St. Joseph’s continence.[^173] The modern devotional argument — that Joseph held back from his wife because he knew what befell those who touched the ark — must be labeled honestly: no ancient witness for it could be found, and pressed as an argument it proves too much, since Elizabeth embraced Mary unharmed and touch as such was never the ark’s offense — the Caathites were commanded to carry it, and the Chronicler locates Uzzah’s fault in transport against the law (1 Paralipomenon 15:13–15).[^174] It is a modern devotional development, consonant with the attested grammar of reverence but resting on none of the ancient texts. The ancient anchor is the shut gate, and it is enough.

And then the grammar turns on us, who assault no biers and are in little danger of Uzzah’s zeal, being rarely zealous enough to steady anything. The examination, again, in the first person: my genuflection — has it worn down to the bob of a man glancing at his watch, or is it still the body’s confession of what the lamp means? My communions — do I prepare for, and give thanks after, the reception of him whom the propitiatory only prefigured, with as much recollection as Israel spent on a gilded chest it was forbidden to see? My speech about holy things — has familiarity made it glib, the insider’s patter of a man handling sacred vessels like stock? My silence in church — do I keep any? These are not scruples. They are manners; and no one who has followed the ark from Sinai will mistake the manners of creatures before the Presence for etiquette.

### Mary, bearer

Mary is in this study for one reason, and it is the reason she is in the Gospel: she carried Christ. The oldest ark typology the project verified is Christological — Hippolytus’s ark of imperishable wood is the Lord’s own body, its incorrupt timber from the Virgin — and when the explicitly Marian title flowers in the fifth century it flowers as a way of saying that same thing about her Son. John Damascene, on the day of her passing, called her the sacred and living ark of the living God, the ark that had conceived its own Maker.[^175] A modern abbot, writing for apostles, closed his book before a Byzantine icon of the Mother of God and read the whole interior life off its geometry: Jesus her center, her breath, her life; she carrying him, magnifying his voice, holding him out to be seen — his word for her was the monstrance.[^176] The study may put the image in its own terms: a monstrance is precious metal shaped entirely around what it holds; empty, it is a curiosity in a case; and no one who kneels before it kneels to the gold. Everything the Church says of Mary under the ark’s figure has exactly that shape, and the Church says so in her own voice: Mary’s maternal office adds nothing to the one mediation of Christ and takes nothing from it, but shows its power; whatever she carries, and whatever grace her carrying brings near, is his.[^177] Israel’s reverence ran through the ark to the Presence between the cherubims; the Church’s runs through Mary to her Son. A devotion to her that stopped at her would be the figure emptied — the gold without the manna.

One practice, then, and this book has done its work if the reader takes nothing else from it. The next time I enter a church I will look first for the small flame beside the tabernacle. Israel had a cloud over the tent by day and “a fire by night” — and that “throughout all their mansions,” the Douay’s old word for every stopping-place of the pilgrimage; and when the camp moved, the ark went before it, “for three days providing a place for the camp” (Exodus 40:36 Douay = 40:38; Numbers 10:33).[^178] The lamp keeps that watch still, two steps from the door of any parish church, at the end of the long road this study has walked from Sinai. I will genuflect like a man who has read the first panel; keep a moment of silence like a man who has read the second; and go out carrying what I have received, as she went into the hill country. The ark still goes before the camp.

## Scope, Status, and Qualifications

**Question and coverage.** This study answers: where the ark of the covenant went, so far as Scripture, tradition, and scholarship can say; what its journey teaches about presence, holiness, and the propitiatory; and how the Church came to read the Virgin Mary as the ark of the new covenant — in philology, Fathers, liturgy, and magisterium — with every parallel graded separately for attestation and reception, ending at Apocalypse 11:19–12:1 and the Assumption. It is a source-audited study under the repository’s Mariology profile: the narrative panel follows the repository’s evidence discipline for historical accounts, and the philological panel reads each witness at its own locus and preserves disagreement. It is not a general Mariology, a liturgical book, an apparition study, or a guide to recovering the object. Excluded or bounded: contested archaeology is reported at its scholarly confidence and not adjudicated (Jericho’s event archaeology; the Mount Ebal structure); the *Kebra Nagast* and the Aksum claim are treated as fourteenth-century reception history; modern pseudo-archaeology is declined in a sentence; Islamic reception is bounded to one paragraph on Qur’an 2:248; source hypotheses about the ark narratives (Rost and successors) are noted without adjudication, the narrative standing on the canonical text; and no mediation title beyond the sense of *Lumen Gentium* 60–62 is advanced.

**Authority classes, kept distinct.** (1) Scripture supplies the whole substance: every canonical ark pericope from Exodus 25 to Apocalypse 11:19 is treated, quoted from the Douay-Rheims base text; the study adds nothing to public Revelation, and reads the unity of the two Testaments under *Dei Verbum* 12 and CCC 128–130, the rule stated in the toll-gate section. (2) Defined dogma touched here: the divine motherhood (Ephesus, 431) and the Assumption (1950). The Mary-ark typology itself belongs to no defined formula: *Munificentissimus Deus* deploys the type in its argument (nn. 26, 29, 34) and defines without ark language (n. 44), and the study claims nothing more. (3) Conciliar and papal magisterium, cited act by act: *Ineffabilis Deus* (1854); *Munificentissimus Deus* with its *Acta* text and notes; *Marialis Cultus* 6 and 26; CCC 489, 722, 2676–2677; *Lumen Gentium* 60–62 governing everything said of Mary, with the verified negative that the constitution never uses the title; John Paul II’s 1983 Angelus catechesis beside the silence of *Redemptoris Mater*. (4) Liturgical reception, treated as real evidence on the warrant stated in the toll-gate section: dated acts of two rites, from the Akathist and the Dormition Menaion through the Litany of Loreto (approved 1587, fixed 1601), the Damascene Matins lesson of the Roman office (1568–1950, verified here in a 1942 printing), the revised office of 1950, and the postconciliar Lectionary 621/622 with the Liturgia Horarum. (5) Patristic witness, carried with per-text authenticity classes: genuine and dated witnesses (Hippolytus, Ephrem, Proclus’s fifth oration, Hesychius, Chrysippus, Jacob of Serugh, John Damascene, and Augustine’s contrary exegesis); pseudonymous or disputed texts used only under honest labels (Ps.-Gregory Thaumaturgus, Ps.-Methodius, the Turin homily attributed to Athanasius, the sermon transmitted under both Ambrose’s and Maximus’s names, author unknown); and verified negatives printed as findings (no genuine Augustine or Ambrose Marian ark text; no ark in Cyril’s Ephesus homily; the silences of *Cogitis me*, Bernard, and Aquinas; the chant-index negative for the Assumption). (6) Historical scholarship: the ark-origins literature (de Vaux, Haran, Mettinger, Seow, Eichler, Noegel, and the compositional school), the excavation records of Shiloh and Jericho, Miller and Roberts, and the modern Apocalypse commentators (Bauckham, Aune, Beale) are reported as schools with premises; where a work was consulted at one remove, the note says so. (7) The Luke debate is presented as genuinely open and methodological: Burrows, Lyonnet, and Laurentin against Brown, Fitzmyer, Schürmann, Bovon, and Isaacs, with the Kozłowski and de Koning revival — both sides quoted at their strongest, no verdict imposed beyond what the Visitation section prints. (8) Editorial synthesis, labeled where it occurs: the study’s one framed synthesis box, in the Assumption section, joins 2 Machabees 2:7–8 to the seventh trumpet; the identification of Psalm 77:60–61’s “strength” and “beauty” with the ark is labeled source-grounded synthesis in the prose of the pattern section; and the master table’s prose grades, the fate-traditions table, and the coda’s homiletic extensions are project constructions grounded in the cited sources and labeled at their places.

**The two axes.** One promise of method governs the study, made in the overture and stated formally at the toll-gate: facts exact, framework Catholic, labels honest. Questions of fact are settled by evidence; questions of interpretation are not settled by any school’s minimalism; liturgical and magisterial reception counts as evidence; and every disputed reading is declared in one or more of four states — philologically anchored, anciently attested, liturgically or magisterially received, or the project’s own labeled synthesis — under the named distinction that attestation is not reception, and the absence of either is not falsity.

**Edition and as-of bounds.** English Scripture is quoted throughout from the public-domain Douay-Rheims (Challoner) text as printed at drbo.org, with its book names (1–4 Kings, 1–2 Paralipomenon, Josue, Jeremias, 2 Machabees, Apocalypse) and Vulgate psalm numbers, dual citations given where confusion threatens. Septuagint claims follow Rahlfs numbering and were verified in a machine-readable derivative of Rahlfs 1935, independently re-run in an adversarial pass; a spot-check against the printed Rahlfs–Hanhart remains open below. New Testament Greek counts use the SBL Greek New Testament. The print contains no Greek or Hebrew script: Greek is transliterated in italics with macrons, Hebrew in plain unpointed transliteration; the exact scripts stand in the research records. All web witnesses were read 2026-08-15 unless a note states another date (the chant-index data were retrieved in August 2026). Mutable claims — the current lectionary selections, the Liturgia Horarum text served by derivative electronic witnesses, the Triodion rubric reported from modern liturgical resources — are stated as of those witnesses and dates and must be rechecked before reliance.

**Translation status.** Scripture in English is Douay-Rheims only. Patristic and ancient English quotations use identified public-domain translations, named at each locus: the Ante-Nicene and Nicene/Post-Nicene series translators, Morris (1847) for Ephrem, Livius (1893), Allies (1898) for the Damascene (an identification noted as not confirmed line by line), Marshall (1878) for Bede, Whiston for Josephus, Charles for 2 Baruch, Kraft and Purintun for 4 Baruch, Pickthall for the Qur’an. Texts with no public-domain English — the Turin homily, Jacob of Serugh, Gregory the Great’s homily, the Latin hymns and the Breviary lesson, Primasius, the Greek homilists where no public-domain rendering is credited — are given in labeled editorial paraphrase, each marked as a paraphrase and not a translation, or are described with their loci. Rabbinic, Menaion, and current lectionary texts are described or paraphrased with exact references; their modern English translations are not quoted. No prayer or liturgical text is offered for recitation, and the leaf therefore keeps no prayer-text audit.

**Textual cruxes carried.** The base text is quoted as printed and never silently emended; where the witnesses divide, the division is printed. The cruxes, each at its section: the Bethsames number, 1 Kings 6:19 (fifty thousand and seventy quoted as printed; the Masoretic anomaly, Josephus’s seventy, the Revised Standard Version’s flagged emendation, and the negative that 4QSam-a does not preserve the verse — the catastrophe section); the mice of 1 Kings 5:6 (Vulgate and Septuagint against the Hebrew — the same section); the mood and arithmetic of 1 Kings 7:2 (“rested” against the Hebrew’s lamenting; the twenty years — the same section); the word that killed Oza (Douay “for his rashness” as the Vulgate’s construal of the obscure **shal**, with 4QSam-a and 1 Paralipomenon 13:10 reading the touch — the processions section); Nachon against Chidon, and the sacrifices of the six paces (an ox and a ram against seven bulls, the scroll siding with Paralipomenon — the same section, tabled, not harmonized); the ark’s contents (nothing but the tables at 3 Kings 8:9 against the fuller inventory of Hebrews 9:4 — stated at Sinai, restated at the temple, untied nowhere); and the pronouns of Psalm 77:60–61 (the Douay’s “their” against the Hebrew’s “his” — the pattern section, with the ark identification labeled as synthesis).
