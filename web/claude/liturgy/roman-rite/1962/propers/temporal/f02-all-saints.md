# All Saints: A Study of the Proper in the 1962 Roman Missal

*Expansive study of the 1962 Missale Romanum proper of the feast of All Saints, as said on Sunday 1 November 2026 with the commemoration of the Twenty-third Sunday after Pentecost: appointed texts, each text in its setting, three interpretations of the whole Mass with their four senses, a comparison, and the scriptural date and location of its passages*

**All Saints**

Missale Romanum 1962 · Sanctorale, 1 November, I classis · Sunday 1 November 2026

The sealed and the uncounted, the way of the Beatitudes, and the just in the hand of God:<br>

## The Feast of All Saints and Its Texts {#sec:opening}

The Mass of All Saints opens with words of the Church, not of Scripture. Its Introit antiphon, in the English of the Lasance missal, is “Let us all rejoice in the Lord, celebrating a feast in honor of all the saints, in whose solemnity the angels rejoice and join in praising the Son of God.” Its verse is the first of Psalm 32 (33): “Rejoice in the Lord, O ye just: praise becometh the upright.” The Collect says that God has granted us to venerate the merits of all his saints *sub una celebritate*, in one celebration, and asks that, *multiplicatis intercessoribus*, with the intercessors multiplied, he grant the abundance of his mercy that we desire.

The Lesson is John’s vision in the seventh chapter of the Apocalypse. An angel rising from the east with “the sign of the living God” tells the four angels who have power to hurt the earth and the sea to hurt nothing “till we sign the servants of our God in their foreheads.” John hears the number of the sealed, “an hundred forty-four thousand … of every tribe of the children of Israel,” and hears it again tribe by tribe, twelve thousand from each of twelve. Then he sees “a great multitude, which no man could number, of all nations and tribes and peoples and tongues, standing before the throne and in sight of the Lamb, clothed with white robes, and palms in their hands,” crying “Salvation to our God, who sitteth upon the throne, and to the Lamb.” The angels fall on their faces before the throne and give God a sevenfold praise. The Lesson ends there, at verse 12, before an elder asks John who the multitude are.

The Gradual is from Psalm 33 (34): “Fear the Lord, all ye his saints: for there is no want to them that fear him.” Of the next verse it sings only the second half, *Inquirentes autem Dominum, non deficient omni bono*, that those who seek the Lord shall lack no good; the first half, “The rich have wanted, and have suffered hunger,” is not sung. The Alleluia is the Lord’s invitation in Mt 11:28, “Come to me all you that labor and are burdened, and I will refresh you.” The Gospel is the opening of the Sermon on the Mount (Mt 5:1–12a). Jesus sees the multitudes, goes up into a mountain, sits, and teaches: blessed are the poor in spirit, the meek, those who mourn, those who hunger and thirst after justice, the merciful, the clean of heart, the peacemakers, and those who suffer persecution for justice’ sake; and blessed are the disciples when they are reviled and persecuted for his sake, “for your reward is very great in heaven.”

The Offertory takes three verses of Wisdom and changes them: “The souls of the just are in the hand of God, and the torment of malice shall not touch them: in the sight of the unwise they seemed to die, but they are in peace. Alleluia” (Lasance). The Secret offers “the gifts of our devotion” and asks that they be pleasing “for the honor of all the just” and profitable to those who offer them. The Communion sings again three of the beatitudes the Gospel has just given: the clean of heart, the peacemakers and the persecuted. The Postcommunion asks that God grant his faithful people “ever to rejoice in the veneration of all Thy saints, and to be defended by their unceasing prayers.”

The Missal prints this Mass in the Sanctorale under the heading *Die 1 novembris*, *In festo omnium Sanctorum*, of the first class (printed pp. 702–703, nos. 3974–3983). In 2026 the first of November is a Sunday, the Twenty-third after Pentecost. A feast of the first class of the universal Church outranks a Sunday of the second class (RG 91, rows 11 and 15; RG 16), and the Sunday is not resumed on another day (RG 14). Because All Saints is not a feast of the Lord, the Sunday is not simply set aside: it is commemorated, as a privileged commemoration made in every Mass of the day (RG 108, 109 a, 111 a; RGMR 434 a, 436–437). After the feast’s Collect, Secret and Postcommunion the priest therefore says the Sunday’s Collect *Absolve, quaesumus, Domine*, its Secret *Pro nostrae servitutis augmento* and its Postcommunion *Quaesumus, omnipotens Deus*, each under its own conclusion. Nothing else of the Sunday is said. The colour is white (RG 120 d); the Gloria and the Credo are said (RGMR 431; 475 a and b), and the last Gospel is the beginning of the Gospel according to St John (RGMR 509–510). The feast has no Preface of its own. The general rubrics admit two answers, the Preface of the Most Holy Trinity and the common Preface, and the grounds of each are given with the appointed texts.

The texts raise three questions, and each of the three interpretations that follow takes up one. The first is who and how many the saints are. The Lesson counts the sealed exactly and then shows a multitude that nobody could count. St Bede takes the number to signify the whole Church, which is beyond counting; St Augustine, quoting the same multitude, says that the saints, few beside the lost, are many in the company of the angels, and says elsewhere that their number is known to the Creator and not to us. The second is why the Gospel of this feast is the Beatitudes, which bless the poor, the mourning, the hungry and the persecuted, and in what sense their promises are already given. St Augustine says that the promises can be fulfilled in this life; St Thomas Aquinas says the rewards are begun on the way and given perfectly in the homeland; St Hilary of Poitiers places the vision of God after the change to immortality. The third is how the Gradual’s promise can be true, when saints have gone hungry and died at persecutors’ hands, and what the Offertory means by the just in the hand of God. St Basil the Great answers that the good they cannot lack is God himself, and that the goods promised are not bodily ones, since the apostles sought the Lord and lacked them; St Thomas and St Robert Bellarmine keep, beside the spiritual sense, a promise of help in bodily need.

## The Appointed Texts {#sec:appointed-texts}

The Latin below is that of the 1962 typical edition, *Missale Romanum* (Typis Polyglottis Vaticanis), printed pp. 702–703, nos. 3974–3983, for the feast, and pp. 416–417, nos. 1720, 1726 and 1728, for the three orations of the Twenty-third Sunday after Pentecost, read on the page images of the CMAA facsimile. Every word of the feast’s ten texts stands in the Tours printing of the *Missale Romanum* (Mame, 1922), pp. 748–750, and every word of the Sunday’s three orations in the Ratisbon printing (Pustet, 1862), pp. 357–358. Those printings differ from the 1962 page in orthography (the Mame writes *Joannes*, *Jesus*, *justi*), in pointing, and in the length of some conclusion cues; no word that is said differs. The rubrics of the 1962 book are described here, not reproduced.

The English of the scriptural texts is the Douay–Rheims Bible in Bishop Challoner’s revision, which translates the Clementine Vulgate in the Missal’s own numbering. It is given at the canonical verses: where a chant takes part of a verse, the Douay words for that part are given and an ellipsis marks what the Missal leaves out, and each place where the Missal’s form departs from the Vulgate is named beneath the text. The English of the Introit antiphon and of the six orations is that of the Lasance *New Roman Missal* in the Benziger revision of 1945, pp. 1265 and 1268 for the feast and pp. 733 and 735–736 for the Sunday. The Lasance prints this Mass under the rubrics in force before 1960, as a double of the first class with commemorations arranged differently; only its English is used here, and none of this English is an approved liturgical translation of the 1962 Missal. Psalms are cited in the Missal’s numbering, with the Hebrew numbering in parentheses.

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Lasance, The New Roman Missal (1945), All Saints, Introit</span>
>
> Let us all rejoice in the Lord, celebrating a feast in honor of all the saints, in whose solemnity the angels rejoice and join in praising the Son of God. Rejoice in the Lord, ye just: praise becometh the upright. V. Glory.

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Ps 32:1, the verse</span>
>
> … Rejoice in the Lord, O ye just: praise becometh the upright.

The antiphon is not taken from Scripture; it is a text of the Church, and the Missal gives it no citation. The verse is the first of Psalm 32. The Douay prints the psalm’s inscription, “A psalm for David,” inside its verse 1, and the Missal does not sing it. After the *Gloria Patri* the antiphon is repeated (RGMR 427).

### Collect {#proper-collect}

*Oratio*. Missal p. 702, no. 3975.

> *Omnípotens sempitérne Deus, qui nos ómnium Sanctórum tuórum mérita sub una tribuísti celebritáte venerári: quǽsumus; ut desiderátam nobis tuæ propitiatiónis abundántiam, multiplicátis intercessóribus, largiáris. Per Dóminum.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Lasance, The New Roman Missal (1945), All Saints, Collect</span>
>
> Almighty, eternal God, Who hast granted us to venerate the merits of all Thy saints in one solemnity, vouchsafe to us, we beseech Thee, through the multitude of our intercessors, that abundance of Thy mercy for which we ever have hoped. Through our Lord.

The Latin’s *sub una … celebritate*, “in one celebration,” is the Lasance’s “in one solemnity”; its *multiplicatis intercessoribus*, an ablative absolute in which the intercessors are multiplied, is the Lasance’s “through the multitude of our intercessors.” The prayer is addressed to the Father, and the Missal’s cue *Per Dominum* stands for the full conclusion through the Son.

### Commemoration Collect {#proper-commemoration-collect}

*Oratio* of the Twenty-third Sunday after Pentecost. Missal p. 416, no. 1720.

> *Absólve, quǽsumus, Dómine, tuórum delícta populórum: ut a peccatórum néxibus, quæ pro nostra fragilitáte contráximus, tua benignitáte liberémur. Per Dóminum nostrum Iesum Christum, Fílium tuum: Qui tecum vivit et regnat in unitáte.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Lasance, The New Roman Missal (1945), Twenty-third Sunday after Pentecost, Collect</span>
>
> Remit, we beseech Thee, O Lord, the sins of Thy people, that by Thy kindness we may be delivered from the trammels of our sins, in which, through our frailty, we have become entangled. Through our Lord.

The priest says this prayer after the feast’s Collect, as a commemoration of the Sunday, under its own conclusion (RG 108, 109 a, 111 a; RGMR 434 a, 437). The Latin’s *populorum* is plural, “of thy peoples”; the Lasance gives the singular.

Et audívi númerum signatórum, centum quadragínta quátuor mília signáti, ex omni tribu filiórum Israel. Ex tribu Iuda duódecim mília signáti. Ex tribu Ruben duódecim mília signáti. Ex tribu Gad duódecim mília signáti. Ex tribu Aser duódecim mília signáti. Ex tribu Néphtali duódecim mília signáti. Ex tribu Manásse duódecim mília signáti. Ex tribu Símeon duódecim mília signáti. Ex tribu Levi duódecim mília signáti. Ex tribu Issachar duódecim mília signáti. Ex tribu Zábulon duódecim mília signáti. Ex tribu Ioseph duódecim mília signáti. Ex tribu Béniamin duódecim mília signáti. Post hæc vidi turbam magnam, quam dinumeráre nemo póterat, ex ómnibus géntibus, et tríbubus, et pópulis, et linguis: stantes ante thronum, et in conspéctu Agni, amícti stolis albis, et palmæ in mánibus eórum: et clamábant voce magna, dicéntes: Salus Deo nostro, qui sedet super thronum, et Agno. Et omnes Angeli stabant in circúitu throni, et seniórum, et quátuor animálium: et cecidérunt in conspéctu throni in fácies suas, et adoravérunt Deum, dicéntes: Amen. Benedíctio, et cláritas, et sapiéntia, et gratiárum áctio, honor, et virtus, et fortitúdo Deo nostro in sǽcula sæculórum. Amen.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Apoc 7:2–12</span>
>
> And I saw another angel ascending from the rising of the sun, having the sign of the living God. And he cried with a loud voice to the four angels to whom it was given to hurt the earth and the sea, Saying: Hurt not the earth nor the sea nor the trees, till we sign the servants of our God in their foreheads. And I heard the number of them that were signed. An hundred forty-four thousand were signed, of every tribe of the children of Israel. Of the tribe of Juda, twelve thousand signed: … Ruben … Gad … Aser … Nephthali … Manasses … Simeon … Levi … Issachar … Zabulon … Joseph … Benjamin … After this, I saw a great multitude, which no man could number, of all nations and tribes and peoples and tongues, standing before the throne and in sight of the Lamb, clothed with white robes, and palms in their hands. And they cried with a loud voice, saying: Salvation to our God, who sitteth upon the throne and to the Lamb. And all the angels stood round about the throne and the ancients and the four living creatures. And they fell down before the throne upon their faces and adored God, Saying: Amen. Benediction and glory and wisdom and thanksgiving, honour and power and strength, to our God, for ever and ever. Amen.

For each of the twelve tribes after Juda the Douay repeats “twelve thousand signed”; the repetitions are shortened here to the tribe’s name. The Missal’s opening, *In diebus illis: Ecce ego Ioannes vidi alterum Angelum*, stands for the Vulgate’s *Et vidi alterum angelum*, the Douay’s “And I saw another angel.” The Lasance renders the Missal’s opening “In those days, behold, I, John, saw another angel ascending from the rising of the sun.” Every other word of verses 2–12 agrees with the Clementine text. The Lesson stops before verse 13, where one of the ancients asks John who the white-robed multitude are and answers that they “are come out of great tribulation” (7:13–14).

### Gradual {#proper-gradual}

*Graduale*, *Ps. 33, 10 et 11* (Heb. 34). Missal p. 703, no. 3977.

> *Timéte Dóminum, omnes Sancti eius: quóniam nihil deest timéntibus eum. ℣. Inquiréntes autem Dóminum, non defícient omni bono.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Ps 33:10–11</span>
>
> Fear the Lord, all ye his saints: for there is no want to them that fear him. … but they that seek the Lord shall not be deprived of any good.

The Gradual’s wording is not the Clementine Vulgate’s. For *nihil deest*, “nothing is lacking,” the Clementine reads *non est inopia*, which the Douay translates “there is no want”; for *non deficient*, “shall not fail,” it reads *non minuentur*, “shall not be diminished,” which the Douay translates “shall not be deprived.” The verse leaves out the first half of verse 11, *Divites eguerunt, et esurierunt*, “The rich have wanted, and have suffered hunger.” St Augustine’s psalter reads *nihil deest* at verse 10, as the Gradual does, and *non minuentur* at verse 11, as the Clementine does. The Vulgate and the Douay count the psalm’s title as verse 1, as the Hebrew does, so these verses are 33:10–11 in both; English Bibles that leave the title unnumbered call them 34:9–10.

### Alleluia {#proper-alleluia}

Alleluia verse, *Matth. 11, 28*. Missal p. 703, no. 3978.

> *Allelúia, allelúia. ℣. Veníte ad me, omnes qui laborátis et oneráti estis: et ego refíciam vos. Allelúia.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Mt 11:28</span>
>
> Come to me all you that labor and are burdened, and I will refresh you.

The verse is the whole of Mt 11:28 and agrees with the Clementine text. It is the Lord’s invitation after his thanks to the Father, who has hid these things “from the wise and prudent” and revealed them “to little ones” (11:25); it goes on, “Take up my yoke upon you, and learn of me, because I am meek, and humble of heart” (11:29). The feast never falls between Septuagesima and Easter or in Paschaltide, so no Tract or paschal Alleluia is printed.

Beáti mites: quóniam ipsi possidébunt terram. Beáti qui lugent: quóniam ipsi consolabúntur. Beáti qui esúriunt et sítiunt iustítiam: quóniam ipsi saturabúntur. Beáti misericórdes: quóniam ipsi misericórdiam consequéntur. Beáti mundo corde: quóniam ipsi Deum vidébunt. Beáti pacífici: quóniam fílii Dei vocabúntur. Beáti qui persecutiónem patiúntur propter iustítiam: quóniam ipsórum est regnum cælórum. Beáti estis cum maledíxerint vobis, et persecúti vos fúerint, et díxerint omne malum advérsum vos, mentiéntes, propter me: gaudéte et exsultáte, quóniam merces vestra copiósa est in cælis.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Mt 5:1–12a</span>
>
> And seeing the multitudes, he went up into a mountain, and when he was set down, his disciples came unto him. And opening his mouth he taught them, saying: Blessed are the poor in spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. Blessed are the meek: for they shall possess the land. Blessed are they that mourn: for they shall be comforted. Blessed are they that hunger and thirst after justice: for they shall have their fill. Blessed are the merciful: for they shall obtain mercy. Blessed are the clean of heart: for they shall see God. Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God. Blessed are they that suffer persecution for justice’ sake: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. Blessed are ye when they shall revile you, and persecute you, and speak all that is evil against you, untruly, for my sake: Be glad and rejoice for your reward is very great in heaven. …

The Missal’s opening, *In illo tempore: Videns Iesus turbas*, stands for the Vulgate’s *Videns autem Jesus turbas*, the Douay’s “And seeing the multitudes”; the Lasance renders it “At that time, Jesus seeing the multitudes, went up into a mountain.” The reading ends at the first half of verse 12, *in caelis*, and leaves out the second, “For so they persecuted the prophets that were before you.” Every other word agrees with the Clementine text. The Missal prints the cue *Credo* after the Gospel.

### Offertory {#proper-offertory}

*Antiphona ad Offertorium*, *Sap. 3, 1, 2 et 3*. Missal p. 703, no. 3980.

> *Iustórum ánimæ in manu Dei sunt, et non tanget illos torméntum malítiæ: visi sunt óculis insipiéntium mori: illi autem sunt in pace, allelúia.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Wis 3:1–3</span>
>
> … the souls of the just are in the hand of God, and the torment of death shall not touch them. In the sight of the unwise they seemed to die: … but they are in peace.

The antiphon is a received liturgical form, not the verses as the Vulgate prints them, and the Douay cannot render it word for word. Against the Clementine it leaves out the opening *autem*, “But”; reads *tanget* for *tangent*, a difference that does not show in English; reads *tormentum malitiae*, “the torment of malice,” for *tormentum mortis*, “the torment of death”; leaves out “and their departure was taken for misery: And their going away from us, for utter destruction” (3:2b–3a); and adds *alleluia*. The Lasance gives the antiphon its own English: “The souls of the just are in the hand of God, and the torment of malice shall not touch them: in the sight of the unwise they seemed to die, but they are in peace. Alleluia.”

The antiphon’s wording has a history of its own. As a chant of martyrs’ Masses the verses were sung with *malitiae* before the printed Missal: so the Gregorian Antiphonary printed in Migne’s *Patrologia Latina* gives them, as a Communion and as a Gradual; so do the chant excerpts from the Fonte Avellana codices; and so does the Sarum Missal in its common of many martyrs, with the same omission as the antiphon, while the lesson from Wisdom in the same common reads *mortis*. The Venice printing of the Roman Missal of 1570 reads *tormentum mortis* at All Saints and has no *alleluia*, though the same book’s Masses of martyrs read *malitiae*. The Roman printings of 1604, 1862 and 1889, and Fromage’s text of 1909, read *malitiae* at the feast and have no *alleluia*. The earliest printing at hand that has the *alleluia* is the Mame of 1922, conformed to the typical edition of 1920.

### Commemoration Secret {#proper-commemoration-secret}

*Secreta* of the Twenty-third Sunday after Pentecost. Missal p. 417, no. 1726.

> *Pro nostræ servitútis augménto sacrifícium tibi, Dómine, laudis offérimus: ut, quod imméritis contulísti, propítius exsequáris. Per Dóminum nostrum Iesum Christum, Fílium tuum: Qui tecum vivit et regnat in unitáte.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Lasance, The New Roman Missal (1945), Twenty-third Sunday after Pentecost, Secret</span>
>
> We offer Thee, O Lord, the sacrifice of praise for the increase of our service, that Thou mayest graciously complete that which, for no merit of ours, Thou hast granted unto us. Through our Lord.

It is said secretly after the feast’s Secret, and its last words, *Per omnia saecula saeculorum*, are said aloud to begin the Preface (RGMR 481). On the Sunday’s own page the Missal directs a Preface after this prayer; that direction belongs to the Mass of the Sunday, and a commemoration does not bring a Preface of its own into the Mass in which it is made (RGMR 483).

### Preface {#proper-preface}

No Preface is printed or directed with the feast. Either the Preface of the Most Holy Trinity, Missal p. 293, no. 1082, or the common Preface, p. 297, no. 1086.

The feast has no Preface of its own, and its page names none, so neither Preface’s text is printed here. The general rubrics state that a Mass takes its own Preface; that a Mass without one takes the Preface of the season (*de Tempore*); and that otherwise the common Preface is said (RGMR 482). The question is whether the Sunday’s seasonal Preface reaches a feast of the first class that has no Preface of its own and is said on a Sunday of the second class.

One answer gives the Preface of the Most Holy Trinity. RGMR 494 b appoints that Preface as the seasonal Preface on Sundays of the second class outside Christmastide and Paschaltide, and the feast, lacking its own, would take the seasonal one under RGMR 482. The Missal’s direction at the Vigil of the Nativity, which gives the common Preface but the Trinity Preface when the Vigil falls on a Sunday (p. 16), is then an instance of the general rule. The Vigil can fall only on the Fourth Sunday of Advent, which RGMR 494 b names in a clause of its own; the Twenty-third Sunday after Pentecost falls under the clause for the other Sundays of the second class, so the instance reaches All Saints only if the two clauses work alike. The Ordo of the Institute of Christ the King Sovereign Priest for 1 November 2026 directs the Trinity Preface; that is one institute’s direction, not a rubric of the Missal.

The other answer gives the common Preface. RGMR 494 b speaks of the Mass of the Sunday and not of a feast said on a Sunday. The rubrics for Lent, Passiontide, Easter, the Ascension and Pentecost extend their Prefaces in express words to the other Masses of the season that lack a Preface of their own (RGMR 486 b, 487 b, 489 b, 490 b, 493 b), and 494 b has no such words. The Sunday is present only by its commemoration, and a commemoration brings no Preface into the Mass (RGMR 483); the common Preface is said in Masses that have no Preface of their own and are not to take the seasonal one (RGMR 498). On this answer the Vigil’s direction is an exception, printed because the general rule did not reach the Vigil. The Missal’s words admit both answers.

## Each Text in Its Setting {#sec:each-element}

Each appointed text is set here in its own place: the chapter or psalm it comes from, the words the Missal keeps and leaves out, its earlier use where that is known, and what the Fathers and later commentators say of its words. The three interpretations that follow take up the larger questions.

### Introit: the Church’s antiphon and the psalm of the just {#sec:element-introit}

The antiphon was not written for this feast. Blessed Ildefonso Schuster notes that *Gaudeamus* is an ancient Introit first assigned to the feast of St Agatha on 5 February (*The Sacramentary* V, p. 209), and William Durandus, in the thirteenth century, gives *Gaudeamus omnes in Domino* to St Agatha (*Rationale* VII.47). On this feast the antiphon names, in place of one martyr, *Sanctorum omnium*, and keeps the angels who rejoice at the solemnity and praise the Son of God. The Gregorian Antiphonary printed by Migne already gives the Introit *Gaudeamus omnes in Domino* for All Saints, with the verse *Exsultate justi in Domino, rectos decet collaudatio*. Lucien Fromage, continuing Dom Prosper Guéranger’s *L’Année liturgique*, comments on the antiphon’s angels; his comment is given with the first interpretation below.

The verse opens Psalm 32 (33), a hymn in which the just are bidden to rejoice and to praise on the harp, and which goes on to the word and works of the Lord and his counsel that stands for ever. St Augustine reads the first verse as a warning against a joy that rests on oneself: *exsultate justi, non in vobis, non enim hoc tutum est; sed in Domino*, “rejoice, you just, not in yourselves, for that is not safe, but in the Lord”; and only those praise the Lord *qui se subdunt Domino*, “who submit themselves to the Lord” (*Enarr. in Ps.* 32, en. 1). In his second exposition he asks who the upright are, and answers, *Qui dirigunt cor secundum voluntatem Dei; et si eos perturbat humana fragilitas, divina consolatur aequitas*: those who direct the heart by the will of God, and whom, if human frailty troubles them, God’s justice consoles (en. 2, s. 1).

St Basil the Great tells the just to exult *non cum res domi vobis prospere succedunt … sed quod Dominum habetis*, not when things go well at home, but because they have the Lord (*Hom. in Ps.* 32, n. 1). St Thomas Aquinas draws a meaning from the prefix of *collaudatio*: those whose will is conformed to God’s *gaudent in prosperis et adversis; et ideo dicit Collaudatio, quia de omnibus laudant, non in aliquibus tantum. Item unanimiter*, “rejoice in prosperity and adversity, and so he says *collaudatio*, because they praise in all things, not in some only; and also with one mind” (*In Ps.* 32). St Robert Bellarmine reads the second half of the verse as a special invitation to the just, with whom God is always pleased. An extract under the name of Cassiodorus in St Bruno of Würzburg’s commentary on the Psalms says the same of the prefix in another way: *Collaudatio vero multorum ore una laudatio est, propter unitatem Ecclesiae*, “praise together is one praise from the mouths of many, because of the unity of the Church.”

### Collect: one celebration and many intercessors {#sec:element-collect}

The Collect is older than the feast. The Leonine Sacramentary, in its prayers for the feast of Ss Peter and Paul in June, and the Gelasian Sacramentary, among its evening prayers after the same Masses, give it as a prayer for all the apostles: *qui nos omnium apostolorum merita sub una tribuisti celebritate venerari*. Neither book has a Mass of All Saints. Where they ask for the abundance of mercy *celerem* or *celeriter*, “swift” or “swiftly,” the feast’s Collect reads *desideratam*, “desired,” the reading that Wilson’s edition reports from a later hand of a St Gall manuscript and from Gerbert’s edition. Schuster says the Collect is Gelasian and belonged to a collective feast of the Apostles (p. 210).

The Collect’s last words exist in two forms. The apostles’ prayer in both sacramentaries, the Gilbertine and Sarum Missals, and the Roman Missal from 1570 to 1962 read *multiplicatis intercessoribus*, “the intercessors being multiplied.” The Gregorian Sacramentary in Hugues Ménard’s codex, the eleventh-century sacramentary of Monte Suavicino, and Durandus’s *Rationale* read *multiplicatis intercessionibus*, “the intercessions being multiplied.” The age of the books does not show which reading came first, since the Leonine and Gelasian books give the apostles’ prayer and not its adaptation to all the saints. Ménard says only that his codex’s reading, though the Roman Missal reads *intercessoribus*, *non est contemnenda*, “is not to be despised.”

Both forms make the petition rest on many asking. Durandus gives four causes for which the feast was instituted: the dedication of the Pantheon; the supplying of the feasts of saints who have none; the making good of negligence in keeping them; and the easier granting of petitions, for which he quotes this Collect (*Rationale* VII.34; his reason is given in the first interpretation below). St Bernard, in a sermon for the feast, says that our infirmity is helped by the saints’ intercession, *cum eorum intercessione juvetur infirmitas nostra* (*In festo omnium Sanctorum*, sermo 2, n. 1).

### Commemoration Collect: the bonds of sins {#sec:element-commemoration-collect}

The Sunday’s Collect is said because the feast has displaced the Sunday, after the feast’s Collect and under its own conclusion. It asks that God absolve the sins of his peoples, so that by his kindness they may be freed from the bonds that their frailty has contracted. It was composed for the Twenty-third Sunday after Pentecost, not for this feast, and no commentator on the feast speaks of it. In the order in which the priest says them, the feast’s prayer asks for mercy through the many saints and the Sunday’s asks release from the sins of those who pray.

### Lesson: the sealing and the multitude {#sec:element-lesson}

The seventh chapter of the Apocalypse stands between the opening of the sixth seal and the seventh. Four angels stand at the four corners of the earth, holding the four winds (7:1). The angel from the rising of the sun forbids them to hurt the earth or the sea or the trees until the servants of God are sealed on their foreheads (7:2–3). John hears the number, a hundred and forty-four thousand, twelve thousand from each tribe, and then sees a multitude “which no man could number,” from all nations, in white robes and with palms. The angels, the ancients and the four living creatures stand round the throne, and the angels fall on their faces and adore God with a sevenfold praise. The Lesson stops before the elder’s question in 7:13 and his answer, that these “are come out of great tribulation” (7:14).

Schuster takes the Lesson as a sign of the feast’s origin, since the same Lesson served for the dedication of the Pantheon on 13 May (p. 210; he prints chapter viii for vii). Durandus gives the same Lesson, *Ecce ego Ioannes*, in his account of the feast, and Sicard of Cremona says why it suits the day: *Huic festo bene congruit epistola de numero signatorum, et evangelium de numero beatitudinum*, “to this feast there fits well the epistle on the number of the sealed and the gospel on the number of the beatitudes” (*Mitrale* IX.49). The Fathers’ answers to who the sealed and the multitude are belong to the first interpretation below.

### Gradual: fear and want {#sec:element-gradual}

Psalm 33 (34) is an alphabetical psalm. Its title sets it at the time when David “changed his countenance before Achimelech, who dismissed him, and he went his way” (33:1; compare 1 Kings 21:10–15). In the verses before the Gradual’s, the angel of the Lord encamps round those who fear him and delivers them, and the psalm says, “O taste, and see that the Lord is sweet” (33:8–9). The Gradual takes up at the next verse, “Fear the Lord, all ye his saints,” and leaves out the first half of verse 11, “The rich have wanted, and have suffered hunger.” The Gregorian Antiphonary already gives the Gradual for All Saints.

The psalm’s own word for the faithful, *Sancti eius*, “his saints,” is the word of the feast. St Bernard, preaching on the feast, speaks of the verse as it was sung in the night office, and his sermon is given in the third interpretation below. The promise attached to the verse, *nihil deest timentibus eum*, meets the objection St Augustine puts in the mouth of a man who seeks the Lord daily and has nothing good, while the wicked rich die in their riches; Augustine, St Basil, St Thomas and St Robert Bellarmine answer it in the third interpretation below.

### Alleluia: the invitation {#sec:element-alleluia}

The verse is the Lord’s invitation in Mt 11:28. Before it he has upbraided Corozain, Bethsaida and Capharnaum (11:20–24), and thanked the Father for hiding “these things from the wise and prudent” and revealing them “to little ones” (11:25–27); the place where he speaks is not named. After it he says, “Take up my yoke upon you, and learn of me, because I am meek, and humble of heart … For my yoke is sweet and my burden light” (11:29–30). Schuster calls the verse a preparation for the Gospel (p. 210).

The Fathers differ on what burden the invitation lifts. St John Chrysostom says it calls “not this or that person, but all that are in anxiety, in sorrows, in sins,” and that “sin too hath labor, and a burden” (*Hom. in Matt.* 38.3). St Augustine, preaching on the verse, says that all labour “except that we are mortal men, frail creatures and infirm, bearing about vessels of clay,” and asks, “What then does He mean by, ‘Come unto Me, all ye that labour,’ but that ye may labour no more?” (*Sermon* XIX \[LXIX\], n. 1). St Hilary of Poitiers reads the labour as the difficulties of the Law and the burden as the world’s sins (*Legis deinde difficultatibus laborantes, et peccatis saeculi oneratos*), and says the Lord promises to take them away if they take his yoke, that is, his commandments, and come to him *sacramento crucis*, by the sacrament of the cross (*In Matt.* XI, n. 15). St Jerome gives two answers and does not choose: the burden is sin, as Zacharias and the psalmist testify (Zach 5; Ps 37 (38)), *vel certe*, “or else,” the heavy yoke of the Law, from which the Lord calls to the grace of the Gospel (*In Matt.* II); Rabanus Maurus takes the comment over nearly word for word in his own commentary. St Gregory the Great applies the verse to those of Judaea who “were weighed down with the heavy yoke of the Law” (*Moralia* I, n. 24), and St Thomas reports Hilary’s sentence to show that the New Law is a lighter burden than the Old (*ST* I-II q. 107 a. 4).

### Gospel: the Beatitudes {#sec:element-gospel}

The Gospel is the opening of the Sermon on the Mount. Jesus has gone “about all Galilee,” and crowds have followed him from Galilee, Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea and beyond the Jordan (4:23–25). Seeing the multitudes, he goes up into a mountain that Matthew does not name, sits, and teaches his disciples (5:1–2). The eight beatitudes follow (5:3–10), and then he applies the last of them to the disciples themselves: they are blessed when they are reviled and persecuted for his sake, and their reward is very great in heaven (5:11–12a). The Missal stops before “For so they persecuted the prophets that were before you.” The first and the eighth beatitude end with the same promise, “for theirs is the kingdom of heaven,” in the present tense; the six between promise in the future.

Schuster says that on this feast the Gospel could only be that of the Beatitudes, since all are included in it, and that “those who have least according to the world receive a larger share of the heavenly gift” (pp. 210–211). Durandus and Sicard give the same Gospel, and Sicard sets its number beside the Lesson’s. St Bernard’s first sermon for the feast is an exposition of these verses. The second interpretation below takes up Bernard and the Fathers on the order of the beatitudes and the time of their rewards.

### Offertory: in the hand of God {#sec:element-offertory}

The Book of Wisdom gives first the speech of the wicked, who reckon life short and death the end, and resolve, “Let us oppress the poor just man … Let us condemn him to a most shameful death” (Wis 2:10, 20). Chapter 3 answers them: the souls of the just are in the hand of God; to the unwise they seemed to die, but they are in peace; “their hope is full of immortality” (3:4). The antiphon keeps the first clause, the appearance of death and the peace, and leaves out the unwise men’s judgement that the departure of the just was misery and destruction.

Schuster reads the antiphon of the martyrs, and St Alphonsus Liguori of every just soul; St Augustine and St Bonaventure gloss its first clause; their readings are given in the third interpretation below. Fromage connects the antiphon to the Gospel just read, as the second interpretation sets out. Other Fathers use the verse without expounding it. St John Chrysostom cites “the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God” to deny that the souls of the dead wander the earth (*Hom. in Matt.* 28, n. 3), and St Gregory the Great applies the following clause to the martyr whose body is bereft of life “amidst the hands of the persecutors” (*Moralia* XII, n. 6).

### Secret: the honour of all the just {#sec:element-secret}

The Secret offers “the gifts of our devotion” and asks two things of them: that they be pleasing to God *pro cunctorum … honore iustorum*, for the honour of all the just, and that by his mercy they be for our salvation. The saints are named here by the word the Offertory has just sung, *iustorum*. The prayer stands in the Monte Suavicino sacramentary with the same word; Ménard’s Gregorian Sacramentary reads *honore sanctorum*, “the honour of the saints.” Schuster translates the prayer and adds nothing to it (p. 211). It asks that the offering honour the saints and benefit those who make it, and it asks nothing for the saints themselves.

### Commemoration Secret: the sacrifice of praise {#sec:element-commemoration-secret}

The Sunday’s Secret offers *sacrificium … laudis*, the sacrifice of praise, “for the increase of our service,” and asks God to bring to completion what he has given *immeritis*, to the undeserving. Like the Sunday’s Collect it was composed for another Mass and is said here because the Sunday is commemorated. Its closing words are said aloud to begin the Preface.

### Preface {#sec:element-preface}

The feast has no Preface of its own, and the two Prefaces that the rubrics admit are set out with the appointed texts. Either of them ends with the praise of the angels and leads into the *Sanctus*.

### Communion: three beatitudes again {#sec:element-communion}

The Communion repeats the sixth, seventh and eighth beatitudes, the clean of heart, the peacemakers and those persecuted for justice, word for word. Fromage gives a reason for the choice of these three, which the second interpretation below quotes. Schuster calls the Beatitudes “as it were the decalogue of happiness” and paraphrases the three: the clean of heart shall see God, the peaceable shall be known as true sons of God, and the persecuted will receive endless life and joy for what they gave up (p. 211).

### Postcommunion: veneration and protection {#sec:element-postcommunion}

The Postcommunion asks that the faithful peoples ever rejoice in the veneration of all the saints and be defended by their unceasing prayer. It stands in Ménard’s Gregorian Sacramentary as a second Postcommunion and in the Monte Suavicino book as the only one. Schuster dwells on its name for the Church on earth, *fideles populi*: “the people journeying towards eternity with the eyes, and in the light of faith” (p. 211). St Bernard asks what our praise does for the saints, and answers that they need none of our honours: *quod eorum memoriam veneramur, nostra interest, non ipsorum*, “that we venerate their memory concerns us, not them.” The memory, he says, kindles in us a desire first for their company, to be gathered and to rejoice together in the communion of all the saints (sermo 5, n. 5).

### Commemoration Postcommunion: divine things and human dangers {#sec:element-commemoration-postcommunion}

The Sunday’s Postcommunion asks that those to whom God gives to rejoice in sharing divine things, *divina … participatione*, he not allow to be subject to human dangers. It is the last proper prayer of the Mass, said after the feast’s Postcommunion, so that the feast’s prayer to be defended by the saints’ prayer is followed by the Sunday’s prayer against human dangers.

## One City of Angels and Men, Numbered by God {#lane-sealed-number-uncounted-crowd}

The Lesson counts the servants of God who are sealed, a hundred and forty-four thousand, and then shows a multitude that no one could count. St Bede takes the number for the whole Church and the seal for the sign of the cross. St Augustine calls the uncounted multitude the mass of the saints, and says that their number is known to God and not to us. Taken with the angels of the Introit and the many intercessors of the Collect, the Lesson shows one company, numbered by God and beyond our count, gathered with the angels before the throne.

### A number, and a multitude without number {#sec:sealed-question}

John hears a number before he sees anything: *centum quadraginta quatuor millia signati, ex omni tribu filiorum Israel*, and the number is then given again tribe by tribe, twelve thousand from each. Immediately after it he sees *turbam magnam, quam dinumerare nemo poterat*, “a great multitude, which no man could number,” from all nations and tribes and peoples and tongues. Read as a count, the number either contradicts the multitude or confines the sealed to Israel and leaves the nations outside the seal. Two further questions follow from the same verses. If the number is a figure, is there a number at all, and who knows it? And if no man could count the multitude, does its size mean that salvation is easily reached?

### St Bede: the seal, the number and the multitude {#sec:sealed-bede}

St Bede comments on the Apocalypse verse by verse. The four angels at the corners of the earth are the four kingdoms, or the four winds (on 7:1). The angel from the rising of the sun is “the Lord incarnate, Who is the Angel of the great counsel,” who “has visited us, ‘the day-spring from on high,’ bearing the ensign of the cross, with which to seal His own in their foreheads” (on 7:2; Lk 1:78). The command not to hurt the earth he reads of what followed the Passion: “to this end was the empire of the nations broken up, that the face of the saints might be freely marked with the seal of faith, which these had resisted” (on 7:3). The seal is faith, marked with the cross, on the forehead where it is seen.

On the number he is explicit: “By this definite number is signified the innumerable multitude of the whole Church, which is descended from the patriarchs either by the lineage of nature, or the imitation of faith.” His warrant for counting the Gentiles among the tribes is St Paul’s “if ye be Christ’s, then are ye Abraham’s seed” (Gal 3:29). Twelve multiplied by twelve is raised to thousands, “the cube of the number ten, by which is represented the enduring life of the Church” (on 7:4). The number is a figure of the Church’s completeness, and the twelve tribes include those who are Abraham’s children by faith.

At verse 9 he gives two accounts of the multitude, in this order. First, the vision “announces the glory of those who are to overcome the wickedness of the last persecution.” Then, “that which follows, ‘From all nations, and tribes, and peoples, and tongues,’ may also be thus understood, that after enumerating the tribes of Israel, to whom the Gospel was first preached, he desires to make mention of the salvation of the Gentiles as well.” The white robes are baptism and the palms “the triumph of the Cross” (on 7:9). Bede does not say in so many words that the number and the multitude are one people seen twice. But if the number signifies the whole Church, as he says at verse 4, and the multitude adds the Gentiles to the tribes, as his second account of verse 9 has it, the Lesson shows one Church twice: under a number, in its completeness, and beyond number, in its extent.

The rest of the Lesson he reads as the Church’s praise. The multitude’s loud voice is “great devotion, an unceasing praise, that on the throne, namely, in the Church, there reign the Father and the Son; the Holy Spirit, nevertheless, reigning together with them” (on 7:10). When the angels fall on their faces before the throne, “In all the angels he has represented the persons of the great multitude worshipping the Lord,” though he allows that the verse may be taken of the angels themselves (on 7:11). The sevenfold praise of verse 12 is the Church’s: “The Church offers the sevenfold praise of excellence unto the Lord, and in each of its members confesses to have received this from Him” (on 7:12).

Two other early commentators differ from Bede on the angel, and one of them on the number. An anonymous commentary printed among Augustine’s works takes the angel from the east for the Church itself, *eamdem Ecclesiam catholicam … ab ortu solis, a passione et resurrectione Domini*, “the Catholic Church, from the rising of the sun, that is, from the Passion and Resurrection of the Lord,” and says of the number, *Centum quadraginta quatuor millia, omnis omnino Ecclesia est*, “the hundred and forty-four thousand are the whole Church altogether” (*Expositio in Apocalypsim*, hom. 6). St Victorinus of Pettau, in a text whose transmission is uncertain, takes the angel for Elias, who comes before Antichrist “for the restoration and establishment of the churches,” and says that “the number of those that shall believe, of the Jews and of the nations, is a great multitude which no man was able to number”; the multitude of verse 9 is “the number of the elect out of all believers” (on 7:2–9). Elsewhere he places the 144,000 among those who believe at Elias’s preaching in the last times (on 12:6), so that for him the two companies are two moments of one history.

### St Augustine: the mass of the saints and the number God knows {#sec:sealed-augustine}

St Augustine quotes the Lesson’s multitude in a sermon on the question put to the Lord, “Are there few that be saved?”, and on his answer, “Strive to enter by the strait gate” (Lk 13:23–24). The Lord, he says, “confirmed what He heard”: “few then are saved in comparison of the many that shall perish. For these same ‘few’ will constitute in themselves a great mass.” The mass is the wheat on the threshing floor, which the Winnower lays up in his garner: “So large a mass is to come out of this floor, as to fill the garner of heaven.” Then he names the multitude: “the ‘few’ are themselves the ‘many;’ ‘few’ in comparison of the lost, ‘many in the society of the Angels.’ Hearken, dearly Beloved. The Apocalypse hath this written; ‘After this I beheld of all languages, and nations, and tribes, a great multitude, which no man can number, coming with white robes and palms.’ This is the mass of the saints.” When the floor has been winnowed and the grain separated from the ungodly and the false Christians, “the purified mass” stands at the right hand to reign with Christ (*Sermon* LXI). In Augustine’s sentence “the mass of the saints” is the winnowed grain, and the uncounted multitude is that grain seen in heaven.

In the *Enchiridion* he says what the number completes. Restored mankind fills “the gap which the rebellion and fall of the devils had left in the company of the angels,” and the heavenly city “shall perhaps reign over even a more abundant population.” He adds at once: “We do not know the number either of the saints or of the devils,” but “the number of the citizens” is “present to the thoughts of the great Creator” (*Ench.* 29). What Bede calls innumerable, Augustine calls unknown to us and known to the Creator.

Others say otherwise what the number completes. St Gregory the Great holds that the heavenly city is made of angels and men, and that as many men ascend to it as there were elect angels who remained (*tantum credimus humanum genus ascendere, quantos illic contigit electos angelos remansisse*); the different ways of life of the elect answer to the nine orders of angels (*Hom. in Ev.* 34, n. 11). Augustine fills the place of the fallen; Gregory matches the number of those who stood. St Thomas Aquinas reports three opinions, that as many men are saved as angels fell, as many as remained, or as many as were created, and sets all three aside: the number of the predestined is certain to God, and “it is, however, better to say that, ‘to God alone is known the number for whom is reserved eternal happiness,’” in the words of a Secret of the Missal, *Deus, cui soli cognitus est numerus electorum in superna felicitate locandus* (*ST* I q. 23 a. 7). That Secret stands among the Missal’s prayers for various needs and is not said on this day.

### The angels’ feast and the many who pray {#sec:sealed-elements}

The Introit’s antiphon keeps the feast of all the saints *de quorum solemnitate gaudent Angeli, et collaudant Filium Dei*: at their solemnity the angels rejoice and join in praising the Son of God. Fromage reads the angels’ joy on this feast as that of spirits *qui voient se combler les vides de leurs chœurs*, who see the gaps in their own choirs filled: the gap that, in Augustine’s *Enchiridion*, restored mankind fills. The psalm verse that follows bids the just praise, *rectos decet collaudatio*, and Aquinas reads the prefix of *collaudatio* as praise given with one mind (see the Introit in Each Text in Its Setting). If the antiphon’s angels and the psalm’s just praise with one mind, as Aquinas reads *collaudatio*, theirs is the praise of one city. Either Preface that the rubrics admit ends with the praise of the angels and the *Sanctus*.

The Collect asks for mercy *multiplicatis intercessoribus*, the intercessors being many; the form Durandus quotes, *multiplicatis intercessionibus*, makes the intercessions many. In either form the petition rests on the number of those who pray. Durandus counts the easier granting of petitions among the four causes for which the feast was instituted, *quia impossibile est multitudinem non exaudiri*, “because it is impossible that a multitude should not be heard,” and quotes this Collect for it (*Rationale* VII.34). Fromage, on the same prayer, says that with so many intercessors the whole of heaven prays, and asks what the whole of heaven will not obtain, if the prayer of one of its citizens is powerful. The Postcommunion asks the same help at the end of the Mass: that the faithful peoples be defended *perpetua supplicatione*, by the saints’ unceasing prayer. Between the two, the Secret offers the gifts *pro cunctorum … honore iustorum*, for the honour of all the just together.

The Gradual calls the faithful *omnes Sancti eius*, all his saints, and bids them fear. St Bernard says that the verse, as it was sung in the night office of the feast, is addressed to us and not to the saints in glory, who no longer fear (third interpretation below). In the same sermon he calls those still fighting holy *juxta solam adhuc praedestinationem*, “so far only by predestination,” a holiness that *latet … penes Deum*, “lies hidden with God” (sermo 5, n. 2). He does not comment on the Mass Gradual. Read with the Lesson, the Gradual’s saints are the servants of God still to be sealed, holy by a number that God knows and they do not.

The Alleluia’s verse calls *omnes qui laboratis*, all who labour, and Chrysostom says that it calls not this or that person but all (see the Alleluia in Each Text in Its Setting). If the multitude is the Church from every nation, this is the call that gathers it. The Gospel blesses the poor in spirit, the meek and the rest, and the Communion sings three of its beatitudes again. Sicard of Cremona, who says that the Lesson and the Gospel fit the feast together, speaks of the number of the sealed and the number of the beatitudes. The Offertory says that the souls of the just are in God’s hand and at peace. The Sunday’s three prayers, said after the feast’s own, ask pardon for sins contracted through frailty, the completion of a gift given to the undeserving, and protection from human dangers; no commentator on the feast speaks of them.

### Does the multitude mean that salvation is easy? {#sec:sealed-difficulty}

The uncounted multitude can be taken as an argument from number to ease, and that inference has an ancient precedent. St Cyprian, exhorting to martyrdom, says that the ancient examples “may be numbered,” but that “the Christian martyrs cannot be numbered, as the Apocalypse testifies,” and quotes Apoc 7:9–15. He concludes: “let no one think it a hard or a difficult thing to become a martyr, when he sees that the crowd of martyrs cannot be numbered” (*Ad Fortunatum* 11). His conclusion concerns martyrdom only. Schuster, on this Lesson, draws the wider one: “It is not then so hard to be saved, as St John writes that he could not count the number of the elect” (p. 210). The Lesson’s words are *quam dinumerare nemo poterat*, that no one could number it; Schuster puts the failure to count in John, and neither he nor the Lesson speaks of God’s count, which Augustine and Aquinas say is known.

Augustine takes the same verse the other way. The saved are many, many in the company of the angels, and the multitude shows it; but they are few beside the lost, and the Lord’s answer to the question how many are saved was “Strive to enter by the strait gate.” Augustine and Schuster agree that the saints are many. They differ over what follows: for Schuster, that salvation is not hard; for Augustine, that the few who pass the narrow gate are many in themselves, while the gate stays narrow. Schuster himself adds a condition at once: the seal is the Tau that Ezechiel saw set on the foreheads of those who sigh and mourn (Ezech 9:4), and he reads it with St Paul’s “as you are partakers of the sufferings, so shall you be also of the consolation” (2 Cor 1:7).

Literal.

: John sees an angel from the east forbid the angels of destruction to hurt the earth until the servants of God are sealed on their foreheads; he hears their number, twelve thousand from each of twelve tribes; he then sees an uncounted multitude from every nation in white robes with palms before the throne and the Lamb, and the angels fall on their faces and adore God.

Allegorical.

: The angel from the east is Christ, the Angel of great counsel, who seals his own with the cross, and the hundred and forty-four thousand are the whole Church, descended from the patriarchs by nature or by faith (Bede); the anonymous *Expositio* takes the same number for the whole Church altogether.

Moral.

: The holiness of those still fighting lies hidden with God, so we are to praise those whose victory is already certain, *quorum jam certa victoria est* (Bernard, sermo 5, nn. 2–3), and venerating them profits us and not them (n. 5); the narrow gate still stands, though those who pass it are many (Augustine).

Anagogical.

: The heavenly city of angels and men is complete in a number that God knows (Augustine, Gregory, Aquinas), and the winnowed mass of the saints is laid up in the garner of heaven (Augustine).

## The Beatitudes, the Way the Saints Walked {#lane-beatitudes-way-of-the-saints}

On the feast of all the saints the Mass reads the eight beatitudes, and at the Communion it sings the last three of them again. St Augustine reads the beatitudes as steps that rise from humility, and St John Chrysostom as a golden chain in which each precept makes way for the next. St Thomas Aquinas says that their rewards are begun on the way and perfect in the homeland; Rabanus Maurus, in words he marks as his own, that the clean of heart see God here by grace and hereafter face to face; St Hilary of Poitiers reads the promises of those who have been changed by immortality. If the beatitudes are an ordered way, the Gospel read on this feast describes the road the saints walked.

### Why the Beatitudes, and in what tense {#sec:beatitudes-question}

The Gospel does not praise the saints for what they now enjoy. It calls blessed the poor in spirit, the meek, those who mourn, those who hunger and thirst, those who are persecuted and reviled: people who lack, grieve and suffer. Its promises raise a second difficulty, of time. The first and the eighth beatitude say *ipsorum est regnum caelorum*, “theirs is the kingdom”; the six between say that the blessed shall possess the land, be comforted, have their fill, obtain mercy, see God, be called the children of God; and the last verse says *merces vestra copiosa est in caelis*, “your reward is very great in heaven.” If every promise waits for the life to come, the saints on earth had none of what was promised; if every promise is given now, the mourners are comforted already, which their mourning does not show. A third question is the Communion: why the Mass, having read eight beatitudes, sings three of them again.

### St Augustine: seven steps and an eighth {#sec:beatitudes-augustine}

St Augustine reads the eight sayings as stages in order, rising from humility (*De serm. Dom.* I.3.10). The poor in spirit are the humble and those who fear God; the soul that has submitted to God becomes meek, learns in what evils it is held and mourns, hungers for justice, shows mercy, is purified in heart, and at the seventh step reaches wisdom, the peace of the whole man. “The eighth, as it were, returns to the starting-point, because it shows and commends what is complete and perfect: therefore in the first and in the eighth the kingdom of heaven is named.” He then sets the seven steps beside the seven operations of the Holy Ghost in Isaias, counted upward from the fear of the Lord (Isa 11:2–3): fear answers to the poor in spirit, piety to the meek, knowledge to the mourners, fortitude to the hungry, counsel to the merciful, understanding to the clean of heart, and wisdom to the peacemakers (I.4.11). Augustine puts the fear of the Lord at the first step, beside the poor in spirit; if the Gradual’s *Timete Dominum* is that fear, the Gradual sings the first step.

The rewards, he says, are one reward, the kingdom of heaven, variously named according to the stages, and the eighth “goes back to the starting-point, and makes manifest the perfect man,” as circumcision was on the eighth day and the Lord rose on the day after the Sabbath (I.4.12). Of the time of the rewards he writes: “And those promises can indeed be fulfilled in this life, as we believe them to have been fulfilled in the case of the apostles. For that all-embracing change into the angelic form, which is promised after this life, cannot be explained in any words.” Preaching on the Alleluia’s verse and the one after it, “learn of me, because I am meek, and humble of heart,” he tells his hearers where such a way begins: “Thou wishest to be great, begin from the least,” and “first think of the foundation of humility” (*Sermon* XIX \[LXIX\], n. 2).

### St John Chrysostom: a golden chain, and comfort here and there {#sec:beatitudes-chrysostom}

St John Chrysostom also reads the beatitudes in order. The Lord, he says, “by the former precept making way for the following one, He hath woven a sort of golden chain for us”: the humble man will mourn for his sins; the mourner will be meek, righteous and merciful; the merciful will be pure in heart and a peacemaker; and the man who has attained all these is armed against dangers and is not troubled when evil is spoken of him (*Hom. in Matt.* 15.9). He asks where the mourners shall be comforted, and answers, “Both here and there” (15.4). Of the meek who inherit the earth he says that the Lord speaks “not as limiting the rewards to things present, but as joining with these the other sort of gifts also” (15.5).

On the Alleluia’s verse he makes the link to the Gospel himself. The meekness and lowliness of which the Lord speaks in the next verse, Mt 11:29, are “the mother of all strictness of life. Wherefore also, when beginning those divine laws, with this He began,” that is, with the Beatitudes; and of the rest promised there to the meek and lowly, “even before the things to come, He gives thee here thy recompense” (*Hom. in Matt.* 38.3). Chrysostom and Augustine both begin the way in humility, and both read the Alleluia’s call with the verse that follows it.

### St Thomas and Rabanus Maurus: begun in the way, perfect in the homeland {#sec:beatitudes-thomas}

St Thomas Aquinas gives the distinction in two words. The rewards are had *in via*, on the way, as a beginning (*secundum inchoationem*), and *in patria*, in the homeland, perfectly (*Super Matt.* 5, Venice printing, p. 67). Of the peacemakers he says that their peace *hic inchoatur*, “is begun here” (p. 72), and the eight rewards are *unum praemium*, one reward under different names. On the eighth he reports Chrysostom: *Non dicit a paganis*, the Lord does not say “by the pagans,” since persecution for justice is suffered not only from pagans. The Leonine text of the same lectures adds that the kingdom is promised to the poor in hope and to the martyrs in fact.

Rabanus Maurus compiles his commentary on Matthew from earlier writers and marks many of his sources in the running text: “(Hieron.)” for Jerome, “(Aug.)” for Augustine, “(MAURUS.)” for himself. From Augustine he takes the poor in spirit as those who fear God, *Initium autem sapientiae timor Domini*, “the beginning of wisdom is the fear of the Lord,” and, at 5:9–10, Augustine’s sentence that the promises can be fulfilled in this life and that the eighth returns to the start; at the Lord’s Prayer he repeats Augustine’s matching of the seven petitions to the beatitudes. Under his own name he says of the clean of heart: *Dominum enim mundi corde vident hic in aenigmate per gratiam, in futuro autem facie ad faciem*, “the clean of heart see the Lord here in a dark manner by grace, and in the future face to face” (on 5:8, with 1 Cor 13:12). St Bernard, preaching on the feast, says the same of the vision: *Videbunt quidem in fine facie ad faciem; videbunt etiam nunc, sed per speculum in aenigmate*, “they will see at the end face to face; they see even now, but through a glass in a dark manner” (sermo 1, n. 13), and he calls the lesser kingdom given now a pledge, *velut in arrham*, of the greater (n. 9).

### St Hilary: the precepts of the heavenly life {#sec:beatitudes-hilary}

St Hilary of Poitiers reads the promises chiefly of the state the saints now possess. The Lord on the mountain, *in paternae scilicet majestatis positus celsitudine*, “set, that is, in the height of the Father’s majesty,” lays down *caelestis vitae praecepta*, the precepts of the heavenly life, for he would not have handed on the laws of eternity had he not stood in eternity (*In Matt.* IV, n. 1). The earth the meek inherit is the body the Lord assumed, and *claritate clarificati ejus corporis vestiemur*, “we shall be clothed with the glory of his glorified body” (n. 3). Consolation and fullness are *in caelo*, in heaven (nn. 4–5). The clean of heart see God because *non enim nisi spiritu perfecti, et immortalitate immutati … hoc quod in Deo est immortale cernemus*, “only when we are perfected in spirit and changed by immortality shall we behold what in God is immortal” (n. 7). The persecuted are *gloriosi promissorum Dei martyres*, the glorious martyrs of God’s promises (n. 9).

### Joy, peace, and three beatitudes sung again {#sec:beatitudes-elements}

The Introit’s verse bids the just rejoice, and St Basil, on that verse, sets their joy beside ours: the joy God sometimes, rarely, lets fall on us is a small taste that reminds us of what we lack, while *justo perpetua est divina ac caelestis laetitia, quod semel in ipso inhabitat Spiritus*, “the just man has a divine and heavenly joy that does not end, because the Spirit dwells in him once for all” (*Hom. in Ps.* 32, n. 1).

The Offertory says of the just, *illi autem sunt in pace*, “but they are in peace.” St Bonaventure, on these words, reports the *Glossa*: *Perpetuae quietis, nunc in spe, tandem in re*, “of perpetual rest, now in hope, at last in fact.” St Alphonsus Liguori, on the same verses, says that the saints at death “begin to feel that peace which they shall afterwards fully enjoy in Heaven.” Fromage, on the Offertory, joins it to the Gospel just read: the Beatitudes raise man above torments and above death itself, which *n’ébranle pas la paix des justes, mais la consomme*, does not shake the peace of the just but consummates it, and that, he says, is what the Offertory sings.

The Communion sings the clean of heart, the peacemakers and the persecuted, word for word as the Gospel has just given them. Fromage gives the reason for the three: *Echo de la lecture évangélique, l’Antienne de Communion, ne pouvant énumérer à nouveau la série entière des Béatitudes, rappelle les trois dernières, et, ce faisant, les rapproche toutes avec raison du Sacrement divin où elles s’alimentent.* The antiphon echoes the Gospel; since it cannot list the whole series again, it recalls the last three, and in doing so brings them all, rightly, to the divine Sacrament in which they are nourished. The Fathers above comment on the same three verses as part of the Gospel. In Augustine’s order they are the sixth and seventh steps and the eighth that returns to the first. St Bernard’s first sermon for the feast expounds the Gospel’s beatitudes as reconciliations: the first three of the soul with itself, the next two with its neighbour, the sixth with God; in the seventh it reconciles others, and the eighth is the martyrs’ prerogative (sermo 1, nn. 14–15).

The Lesson’s multitude wears white robes and has palms in its hands. Bede reads them first as those who overcome the last persecution (first interpretation above); if they are, they are the persecuted of the eighth beatitude, whose reward is the kingdom of heaven. The feast’s three orations venerate the saints’ merits and ask mercy through their many intercessors, offer the gifts for the honour of all the just, and ask that the faithful ever rejoice in venerating the saints. The Sunday’s Collect asks release from sins contracted *pro nostra fragilitate*, through our frailty; its Secret, that God complete what he has given to the undeserving; its Postcommunion, protection from human dangers. Either Preface ends with the praise of the angels.

### Present and future {#sec:beatitudes-difficulty}

The tenses of the Gospel are the hardest point. The first and the eighth beatitude are in the present, *est*, and the six between in the future. The Fathers do not divide the rewards by those tenses. Augustine calls the eight rewards one reward, the kingdom of heaven, variously named, and Aquinas calls them *unum praemium*; what they say of time, they say of all the rewards together.

Augustine, Chrysostom, Aquinas and Rabanus place the rewards in this life, as fulfilment, comfort, beginning or vision by grace. Augustine says that the promises can be fulfilled here, as they were in the apostles; Chrysostom, that the mourners are comforted here as well as there, and that the promise of the earth does not limit the rewards to the present; Aquinas, that peace is begun here; Rabanus, that the clean of heart see the Lord here by grace. Bernard’s pledge and mirror say the same. Hilary places the rewards in heaven, for those changed by immortality. The difference is real, and it changes what the Gospel describes: on Hilary’s account chiefly the state the saints now possess, on the others’ the life they already lived. Augustine’s own next sentence keeps a change after this life that no words can explain, so between him and Hilary the difference is one of emphasis.

Bernard gives a reason why the first and the eighth have the same promise. The kingdom is promised to the poor and to the martyrs alike, *quia vere martyrii genus paupertas voluntaria est*, because voluntary poverty is truly a kind of martyrdom: the kingdom *paupertate quidem emitur, sed in passione pro Christo absque omni dilatione percipitur*, “is bought by poverty, but received without delay in suffering for Christ” (sermo 1, n. 15).

Literal.

: Jesus, seated on a mountain, teaches his disciples that the poor in spirit, the meek, the mourners, those who hunger for justice, the merciful, the clean of heart, the peacemakers and the persecuted are blessed, and tells the disciples that when they are reviled and persecuted for his sake their reward is great in heaven.

Allegorical.

: Christ teaches from the height of the Father’s majesty the precepts of the heavenly life, and the earth of the meek is his glorified body, with whose glory the saints will be clothed (Hilary).

Moral.

: The beatitudes are steps rising from humility and the fear of the Lord, answering to the gifts of the Holy Ghost (Augustine), and a golden chain in which each precept makes way for the next, from the humble to the persecuted (Chrysostom); they reconcile the soul with itself, with its neighbour and with God (Bernard).

Anagogical.

: The one reward, begun on the way, is given perfectly in the homeland (Aquinas), to those perfected in spirit and changed by immortality, who see what in God is immortal (Hilary); the change into the angelic form is promised after this life (Augustine).

## No Want to Them That Fear Him, and the Just in God’s Hand {#lane-no-want-just-in-gods-hand}

The Gradual of this Mass promises that nothing is lacking to those who fear the Lord, and its Offertory says that the souls of the just are in the hand of God and only seemed to die. Both statements meet an obvious objection: the saints the feast honours went hungry, were persecuted and were put to death. St Basil the Great says that the good the fearer cannot lack is God himself; St Augustine, that the goods are spiritual; St Thomas Aquinas and St Robert Bellarmine add that God helps those who seek him in bodily need. Augustine also glosses the Offertory’s first words: the just in God’s hand dwell under his help. If the good the fearer cannot lack is God himself, the just in God’s hand have it.

### A promise that seems false {#sec:no-want-question}

The Gradual sings *Timete Dominum, omnes Sancti eius: quoniam nihil deest timentibus eum*, and then, *Inquirentes autem Dominum, non deficient omni bono*. St Augustine states the difficulty as an objector would. In the psalm the promise follows the words *Divites eguerunt et esurierunt*, “the rich have wanted and have suffered hunger,” and *Si ad litteram accipias, videtur te fallere*: “if you take it according to the letter, it seems to deceive you.” The objector has seen many wicked rich men die in their riches, and says of himself, *Quotidie ad basilicam surgo, quotidie genuflecto, quotidie inquiro Dominum, et nihil boni habeo*, “Daily I rise for the church, daily I kneel, daily I seek the Lord, and I have nothing good” (*Enarr. in Ps.* 33, s. 2, n. 14). The Mass sings the promise without the half-verse about the rich, but the singers still know saints who were poor.

The Offertory takes its verses from the chapter in which Wisdom answers the wicked who said, “Let us condemn him to a most shameful death” (Wis 2:20). The antiphon says that the just *visi sunt oculis insipientium mori*, seemed to die in the eyes of the unwise, and that *non tanget illos tormentum malitiae*, the torment of malice shall not touch them. The just whose death the wicked brought about are said to be untouched. Two questions follow: what good the fearer is promised, if it is not bread; and what it means to be in God’s hand, if it is not to be kept from death.

### St Basil: the fear that lacks nothing {#sec:no-want-basil}

St Basil, preaching on Psalm 33 (34), begins from what fear does. Unless fear has ordered our life, holiness cannot be gained in the body, and he quotes *Confige timore tuo carnes meas*, “Pierce thou my flesh with thy fear” (Ps 118:120; Hebrew 119:120). Then: *Timenti itaque non est inopia; hoc est, qui per timorem ab omni indecora et absurda actione arcetur, ei nihil deest ad quamlibet virtutem, sed perfectus est*, “to him who fears there is no want; that is, he who is kept by fear from every unseemly and unreasonable act lacks nothing for any virtue, but is perfect” (*Hom. in Ps.* 33, n. 6).

On the next verse he offers two accounts of the rich who lacked. The words may teach contempt of bodily riches; or the rich are Israel, to whom belonged the adoption and the promises (Rom 9:4–5), and who, having killed the Bread of life, suffer the famine of hearing the word. Those from the nations who seek the Lord *non minuentur omni bono. Perfectum omnino bonum Deus ipse est, quo non minuentur quotquot exquirunt ipsum*: “shall not be lessened in any good. The wholly perfect good is God himself, of whom none who seek him shall be lessened.” He then puts the objection as a dilemma. Whoever takes bodily goods for the good the psalm promises must say either that the apostles did not seek the Lord, since they lacked bodily goods, or that Scripture is false. Neither can be said: *Paulus utique et inquirebat Dominum, et nihil ei defuit bonorum*, “Paul certainly sought the Lord, and nothing of the goods was lacking to him,” though he was scourged, stoned and shipwrecked (n. 7). On Basil’s account the promise is kept in the one who fears, because what he cannot lack is God.

### St Augustine: the spiritual goods {#sec:no-want-augustine}

St Augustine answers his own objection by the kind of good the psalm means: the goods are spiritual, *in bonis spiritualibus* (n. 15). The rich who lack, he says, lack the bread that says *Ego sum panis vivus*, “I am the living bread,” and he adds the beatitude of those who hunger and thirst after justice (Mt 5:6). That gloss is his comment on the half-verse about the rich, which the Gradual does not sing; what he says of the seekers’ good is his comment on the half-verse it does sing. Of peace he says, on a later verse of the psalm that the Mass does not use, that it is had *ex parte hic … ex toto illic*, “in part here, wholly there” (n. 19, on v. 15).

### St Thomas and St Robert Bellarmine: help in need {#sec:no-want-thomas-bellarmine}

St Thomas Aquinas begins where Basil does: *Timete … sancti, quia nullus potest esse sanctus nisi sit timens*, “Fear, you saints, because no one can be a saint unless he fears,” and those who remain in holiness still need fear (*Super Ps.* 33, n. 10). He expounds *non est inopia* three ways: of spiritual goods, since the fear of the Lord is the treasure of Isa 33:6; of bodily want, since the man content with what he has is not needy (Phil 4:11–12); and *Item Deus quaerentibus se subvenit in necessitate*, “God also helps those who seek him in their need.” He meets the objection of St Paul, “Even unto this hour we both hunger and thirst” (1 Cor 4:11), with the physician who withholds food from the sick for the sake of their health: God sometimes sends want and sometimes gives riches, as it serves salvation.

St Robert Bellarmine reads the promise in two senses. In the higher, to feel no want “is the essence of perfection,” and those who seek the Lord shall lack no good “because, as they cling to the Supreme Good, they possess all that is good.” In a humbler sense, the same words teach that God will supply his servants with temporal things and not desert them in need (*Explanatio in Psalmos*, on Ps 33, his verses 9–10). He says nothing of the vision of God.

Basil and Augustine put the promised good in God and the goods of the spirit; Aquinas and Bellarmine add providence in bodily need. The four agree that the fearer lacks nothing that matters. They disagree whether bodily need is included, and the disagreement stands: Basil excludes bodily goods by the apostles’ example, and Aquinas meets the same example by the physician.

### The just in the hand of God {#sec:no-want-offertory}

St Augustine glosses the Offertory’s first clause in a sentence: *Justorum animae in manu Dei sunt, hoc est, habitant in adjutorio Altissimi, et in protectione Dei coeli commorantur*, “the souls of the just are in the hand of God, that is, they dwell in the help of the Most High, and abide in the protection of the God of heaven” (*Enarr. in Ps.* 87, n. 5, citing Ps 90:1; Hebrew 91:1). He says it of the just among the dead before their resurrection, and adds that those cast out of God’s hand are the ones who believed Christ had been cast out of it.

St Bonaventure, in his commentary on Wisdom, glosses the same words the same way and goes further. The souls are first those of the martyrs, after the *Glossa*, *quod etiam verum est de aliis iustis*, “which is true also of the other just”; the verse says souls, not bodies, for the body is given into the hands of the wicked; and *in manu Dei sunt, id est in protectione, et ideo sunt securae*, “they are in God’s hand, that is, under his protection, and therefore they are safe,” with the same psalm verse and the Lord’s “no man shall pluck them out of my hand” (Jn 10:28). His text reads *tormentum mortis*, and the torment that does not touch them is eternal death, for temporal death is *quasi umbra mortis … quidam transitus ad vitam*, “as it were a shadow of death, a passage to life.” What he says of the torment is said of the Vulgate’s *mortis*, not of the antiphon’s *malitiae*, on which none of these commentators speaks. St Alphonsus Liguori, meditating on the same verses with the antiphon’s omission, asks: “If God holds fast in his hands the souls of the just, who can snatch them from him?”, and says that at the hour of death God multiplies his help where the danger is greater (*Preparation for Death*, Ninth Consideration).

They differ over whose souls are meant. Blessed Ildefonso Schuster reads the antiphon of the martyrs: “The persecutor believes that the lives of the martyrs are in his hands, but they are in the hands of God” (*The Sacramentary* V, p. 211). Alphonsus reads it of the death of every just soul, whose enemy is the last temptation; Bonaventure holds both, the martyrs specially and all the just truly. On Schuster’s account the Offertory names the martyrs among the saints of the feast; on Alphonsus’s it names every saint.

### Joy in the Lord they have {#sec:no-want-elements}

The Introit’s verse bids the just exult *in Domino*, in the Lord, and its commentators (see the Introit in Each Text in Its Setting) agree on what that joy is not: for Augustine, joy in oneself; for Basil, joy in prosperity; for the extract under Cassiodorus’s name, joy *in terrenis delectationibus*, in earthly delights. Aquinas puts it in four words, *in Domino, non in mundo*, “in the Lord, not in the world.” Basil gives the reason the just rejoice, that they have the Lord; and on the Gradual’s psalm he says that the good the seeker cannot lack is God himself. On both psalms he names one good, the Lord whom the just have.

The Gospel blesses those who hunger and thirst after justice, for they shall be filled. Augustine sets that beatitude beside the half-verse of Ps 33 the Mass leaves out, and Basil sets it beside the verse before the Gradual’s, *Gustate*, “O taste.”

The Collect asks for the abundance of mercy that is desired, *desideratam … propitiationis abundantiam*, and the Secret that the gifts be for our salvation, *nobis salutaria*. In the Lesson the angel from the east forbids the angels of destruction to hurt the earth until the servants of God are sealed. The Alleluia promises those who labour *ego reficiam vos*, “I will refresh you,” and the Communion promises the clean of heart that they shall see God. The Postcommunion asks that the faithful be *muniri*, defended, by the saints’ prayers. Of the Sunday’s prayers, the Collect asks release from the bonds of sin, the Secret calls what God has given a gift *immeritis*, to the undeserving, and the Postcommunion asks that those who share divine things be not left *humanis … periculis*, subject to human dangers. Either Preface ends with the praise of the angels.

### Does the promise include bread? {#sec:no-want-difficulty}

The strongest objection is Augustine’s: taken by the letter, the verse seems to deceive. The four Fathers meet it in different ways, and the difference is not only of emphasis. If Basil is right, the Gradual promises the saints God himself and the virtues, and the hunger of the apostles is no exception to it. If Aquinas and Bellarmine are right, it also promises help in need, and the hunger is God’s medicine.

St Bernard answers from the command rather than the promise. Preaching on the feast, he speaks of the verse as it was sung in the night office: *Cantavimus sanctis hac nocte, dicentes: Timete Dominum, omnes sancti ejus; sed non istis*, “we sang to the saints this night, Fear the Lord, all ye his saints; but not to these,” that is, not to the saints in glory, who no longer fear. To us it is rightly said, for *Tota interim beatitudo nostra est timere Deum*, “meanwhile our whole beatitude is to fear God,” while the saints’ festivity is in exultation and praise (sermo 5, n. 3).

Literal.

: The psalmist, whom the title sets before Achimelech, promises those who fear the Lord no want and those who seek him no lack of any good; Wisdom’s just seemed to the unwise to die, and they are in peace.

Allegorical.

: The rich who lacked are Israel, which killed the Bread of life and suffered a famine of the word, and those who seek the Lord are the nations, who lack no good (Basil).

Moral.

: Fear trains the life and pierces the flesh, and the fearer lacks nothing for any virtue (Basil); no one is holy without fear (Aquinas); our beatitude meanwhile is to fear God (Bernard); the just rejoice in the Lord and not in themselves (Augustine).

Anagogical.

: God himself is the perfect good (Basil); peace is had wholly there (Augustine); the souls of the just dwell in God’s help and protection, and are safe (Augustine, Bonaventure).

## Where the Three Interpretations Agree and Differ {#sec:comparison}

The three interpretations answer the three questions set out at the opening. Each begins from its own passage of the Mass.

|  | **One city, numbered by God** | **The way of the Beatitudes** | **No want to them that fear him** |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Question | Who are the 144,000 sealed, and who is the multitude that no one could number? | When are the rewards of the beatitudes given: in this life, or only in the next? | What does the Gradual promise to those who fear the Lord, and whose souls does the Offertory place in God’s hand? |
| Starting passage | The Lesson, Apoc 7:2–12 | The Gospel, Mt 5:1–12a | The Gradual, Ps 33:10–11, with the Offertory, Wis 3:1–3 |
| What the Fathers say of it | Bede: the number signifies the whole Church. Augustine: the multitude is the mass of the saints, whose number God knows and we do not | Augustine: the beatitudes are steps, and their promises can be fulfilled in this life. Chrysostom: a golden chain; comfort here and there. Aquinas: the rewards are begun on the way. Rabanus: the clean of heart see God here by grace. Hilary: the rewards belong to those changed by immortality | Basil: the good the fearer cannot lack is God himself. Augustine: the goods are spiritual. Aquinas and Bellarmine: God also helps in bodily need |
| The interpretation’s answer | The counted and the uncounted are one Church, whose number God knows and men do not | The beatitudes are the way the saints walked, its rewards begun in this life and perfected in the next | Nothing is lacking to the God-fearing because God himself is their good, and the just in his hand have it |
| Where they disagree | Augustine and Gregory on what the number completes; Cyprian and Schuster against Augustine on whether the multitude shows that salvation is easily reached | Hilary against the others on when the rewards are given | Basil against Aquinas and Bellarmine on bodily goods; Schuster, Alphonsus and Bonaventure on whose souls are in God’s hand |
| Reached by inference | The Introit, as one praise of angels and men; the Gradual’s saints, as the sealed still on earth; the Alleluia’s call, as the call that gathers the multitude | The Gradual, as the first step; the Lesson, through the eighth beatitude | The Offertory, as holding the good the Gradual promises |

Each interpretation gives some texts of the Mass a reason that the other two do not. The first alone accounts for the Lesson’s double vision, a number sealed and a multitude beyond counting, and with Durandus and Fromage for the Collect’s many intercessors; it says little of how a man becomes one of the saints, beyond Augustine’s narrow gate. The second gives the Gospel and the Communion their own weight and finds in the feast a way with a first step and an end; it reaches the Lesson only through the eighth beatitude. The third gives the Gradual and the Offertory an argument of their own and keeps a real dispute over bodily need; it names the Lesson and the Communion without drawing on them.

They look at the saints from different places. The first looks at them already before the throne, with the angels, and reads back from there to the sealing on earth. The second follows the road the saints walked, from the poverty of spirit at its start to the persecution at its end. The third looks at the condition of the just in this life, where fear is still commanded and want and death still come, and asks what the promise means there.

Two of the interpretations meet in the Offertory. For the second, the words *illi autem sunt in pace* name a peace held now in hope and at last in fact, as Bonaventure reports the *Glossa*. For the third, the hand of God is the help and protection under which the just dwell, as Augustine glosses the first clause.

St Augustine and St Thomas Aquinas stand behind all three, and they argue differently in each. In the first Augustine says that the few who enter by the narrow gate are many in the company of the angels, and both he and Aquinas say that the number of the saved is known to God. In the second Augustine says that the promises of the beatitudes can be fulfilled in this life, and Aquinas that their rewards are begun here. In the third Augustine puts the promised goods in the spiritual order and the just under God’s help, while Aquinas admits also the help God gives in bodily need. The three answers can be held together, since each answers a different question; the disagreements that remain lie within each interpretation, among the authorities it relies on.

### Psalms 32 (33) and 33 (34): the Introit verse and the Gradual {#sec:date-psalms}

The two psalms share the first and third rows of the table. Neither is dated by an occasion of its own. Psalm 32 has no title in the Hebrew; the Clementine Vulgate and the Douay print “A psalm for David” inside its first verse, and the Missal’s verse begins after it. Psalm 33 is titled “For David, when he changed his countenance before Achimelech, who dismissed him, and he went his way”; the title answers to 1 Kings 21:10–15, where David, fleeing Saul, comes to Achis, king of Geth, and feigns madness before him. The attribution to David is the titles’ own; the table marks it disputed and gives David’s reign in the usual chronology from the *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s article “David” (Corbett, vol. 4). The reign is the reference point for that attribution, not a date of the psalms’ composition.

For Psalm 32 the chronology record has no historical setting, and the second row says so. For Psalm 33 the table dates the episode the title names. The figure is the year of the world that Haydock’s Douay commentary gives at 1 Kings 21, reporting Ussher’s reckoning; it is Ussher’s date for the flight to Achis, not an independent dating of the psalm. The title calls the king Achimelech, while 1 Kings 21 calls him Achis; the episode is the same, and the table follows the book of Kings for the place.

The composition date is the bound that the introduction to the Psalms in the *New American Bible, Revised Edition* sets for the whole Psalter, in the same sentence in which it holds that no individual psalm can be dated securely. It dates the book, not these psalms.

### Wisdom 3:1–3: the Offertory {#sec:date-wisdom}

The Book of Wisdom narrates no dated event in these verses; chapter 3 answers the speech of the wicked in chapter 2. The two composition dates are those that Francis Gigot’s “Book of Wisdom” in the *Catholic Encyclopedia* (vol. 15) records for the dispute over the book’s date: in or after the time of Ptolemy IV Philopator, and in or after the time of Ptolemy VII Physicon. Both are marked disputed, and the table gives neither as preferred. The dates are dates of the book, and the antiphon is an adaptation of its verses, not the verses as the Vulgate prints them.

### Matthew 5:1–12a, 5:8–10 and 11:28: the Gospel, the Communion and the Alleluia {#sec:date-matthew}

The Sermon on the Mount is not dated in the chronology record, and the row says so. Matthew places it on “a mountain” that he does not name, after Jesus has gone about all Galilee and the multitudes have followed him from Galilee, Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea and beyond the Jordan (4:23–25). The Alleluia verse and the Communion are drawn from the same Gospel and share its composition row. The six composition dates are those of Eugène Jacquier’s “Gospel of St. Matthew” (vol. 10) and Alfred Durand’s “The New Testament” (vol. 14) in the *Catholic Encyclopedia*; all are marked disputed, and the table keeps the range from the early to the later dates rather than choosing one. They are dates of the book, not of the sermon.

### Apocalypse 7:2–12: the Lesson {#sec:date-apocalypse}

The two composition dates are those of Christian Van den Biesen’s “Apocalypse” (vol. 1), which places the book in the reign of Domitian, and of Alfred Durand’s “The New Testament” (vol. 14), which gives an approximate decade; both are marked disputed. John writes that he was “in the island which is called Patmos” when he saw the visions (1:9). The sealing and the multitude of chapter 7 are a vision and have no date of their own.

## Appendix: Scope and Qualifications {#sec:scope}

**Edition, Mass and occurrence.** The study treats the Mass *In festo omnium Sanctorum*, *Die 1 novembris*, I classis, of the *Missale Romanum*, editio typica, Typis Polyglottis Vaticanis 1962, printed pp. 702–703, nos. 3974–3983, as said on Sunday 1 November 2026 in the universal 1962 calendar and no particular calendar, with the commemoration of the Twenty-third Sunday after Pentecost, pp. 416–417, nos. 1720, 1726 and 1728. The precedence of the feast over the Sunday, the commemoration and its order, the colour, the Gloria, the Credo and the last Gospel rest on the Missal’s own general rubrics (RG 14, 16, 91, 108, 109 a, 111 a, 120 d; RGMR 431, 434 a, 436–437, 475, 480–481, 505, 509–510), as `research/context.md` records. The feast has no Preface of its own. The general rubrics (RGMR 482, 483, 494 b, 498) and the Missal’s direction at the Vigil of the Nativity (p. 16) admit both the Preface of the Most Holy Trinity and the common Preface; no registered rubrical commentary read treats a feast of the first class said on a Sunday of the second class, and the dated Ordo of the Institute of Christ the King Sovereign Priest, which directs the Trinity Preface, is one institute’s direction. Neither Preface’s text is printed. The *Asperges*, the Ordinary and the Office of the feast are not treated.

**Text control.** Every Latin form was read on page images of the CMAA facsimile of the 1962 typical edition and collated word for word with the Tours printing of 1922 (Mame) for the feast’s ten texts and with the Ratisbon printing of 1862 (Pustet) for the Sunday’s three orations; the collation and the comparison with the Clementine Vulgate are in `propers/verified.md`. The rubrics of the 1962 book are described and not reproduced. The history of the Offertory’s wording rests on the chant books printed in PL 78 and PL 151 and the Sarum Missal, on the text layers of the Roman printings of 1570, 1604, 1862 and 1889 and of Fromage’s volume of 1909, and on the page images of the Mame printing of 1922. No printing between 1909 and 1922 was read, so the entry of the Offertory’s *alleluia* is bounded only by those two dates. The history of the Collect’s *intercessoribus* and *intercessionibus* rests on the sacramentaries and missals named in the References; none of them shows which reading came first.

**English.** The scriptural English is the Douay–Rheims in Challoner’s revision at the canonical verses, with the Missal’s departures named beside each text. The English of the Introit antiphon and of the six orations is the Lasance *New Roman Missal* of 1945, whose rubrics are those before 1960. The Lasance English of the Lesson’s and the Gospel’s liturgical openings and of the Offertory is quoted on the Lasance’s attribution only; it has no row in the repository’s register of translations. None of this English is an approved liturgical translation of the 1962 Missal. Patristic and later authors are quoted in the nineteenth-century English of the editions named in the References, or in their Latin or French; where an edition named has no English, the English beside the quotation is the study’s own rendering, and no English is composed for a liturgical text.

**Reception.** The interpretations rest on the loci named in the text and recorded, with editions and bounds, in `research/scope.md` and `research/interpretations.md`. The reading is bounded by the corpora named there and is not complete. Among the commentaries not read are Cassiodorus, Theodoret and St Athanasius on the psalms, held only in remote volumes that were not fetched; St Jerome on Mt 5; St Thomas Aquinas on Mt 11; St Gregory of Nyssa on the Beatitudes, which the library does not hold; and the *Glossa ordinaria*, known here only as St Bonaventure reports it. Each interpretation’s principal authorities are Fathers and saints with a row in the repository’s register of authorial standing. St Bernard, St Bonaventure, St Alphonsus Liguori, St Victorinus of Pettau, St Cyprian and St Bruno of Würzburg have no row there and are cited in support only. Blessed Ildefonso Schuster, Lucien Fromage, William Durandus and Sicard of Cremona are read on this Mass as a Mass; Fromage, Durandus and Sicard are ecclesiastical writers, and none of the four is a principal authority for an interpretation; Durandus’s Mass of the feast had another Alleluia, *O quam gloriosum*. St Rabanus Maurus is a compiler: only the words his commentary marks as his own are credited to him, and his comment on Mt 11:28 is cited as St Jerome’s. Every connection drawn between texts of the Mass belongs to the study’s interpretations unless an author is named as making it, and the orations of the commemorated Sunday are read only for what they say where they stand in this Mass. St Augustine’s *Enarrationes* is read in the Latin of the Migne text, which keeps the Vulgate numbering; his sermons, *De sermone Domini in monte* and *Enchiridion* in NPNF. St Robert Bellarmine is read in O’Sullivan’s abridged English, which numbers Psalm 33 one verse lower than the Vulgate; St Basil in the Latin version printed in PG 29; St Thomas on Matthew in the Venice printing of 1745, cited by page, with the lecture number of the Corpus Thomisticum text, and on the Psalms in the Parma printing of 1863; St Bonaventure in the Quaracchi edition; St Bernard in PL 183.
