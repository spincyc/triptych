# All Saints: A Study of the Proper in the 1962 Roman Missal

*Expansive study of the 1962 Missale Romanum proper of the feast of All Saints, as said on Sunday 1 November 2026 with the commemoration of the Twenty-third Sunday after Pentecost: appointed texts, each text in its setting, three interpretations of the whole Mass with their four senses, a comparison, and the scriptural date and location of its passages*

**All Saints**

Missale Romanum 1962 · Sanctorale, 1 November, I classis · Sunday 1 November 2026

The sealed and the uncounted, the way of the Beatitudes, and the just in the hand of God:<br>

## The Feast of All Saints and Its Texts {#sec:opening}

The Mass of All Saints opens with words of the Church, not of Scripture. Its Introit antiphon, in the English of the Lasance missal, is “Let us all rejoice in the Lord, celebrating a feast in honor of all the saints, in whose solemnity the angels rejoice and join in praising the Son of God.” Its verse is the first of Psalm 32 (33): “Rejoice in the Lord, O ye just: praise becometh the upright.” The Collect says that God has granted us to venerate the merits of all his saints *sub una celebritate*, in one celebration, and asks that, *multiplicatis intercessoribus*, with the intercessors multiplied, he grant the abundance of his mercy that we desire. The Lesson is John’s vision in the seventh chapter of the Apocalypse. An angel rising from the east with “the sign of the living God” tells the four angels who have power to hurt the earth and the sea to hurt nothing “till we sign the servants of our God in their foreheads.” John hears the number of the sealed, “an hundred forty-four thousand … of every tribe of the children of Israel,” and hears it again tribe by tribe, twelve thousand from each of twelve. Then he sees “a great multitude, which no man could number, of all nations and tribes and peoples and tongues, standing before the throne and in sight of the Lamb, clothed with white robes, and palms in their hands,” crying “Salvation to our God, who sitteth upon the throne, and to the Lamb.” The angels fall on their faces and give a sevenfold praise. The Lesson ends there, at verse 12, before an elder asks John who the multitude are.

The Gradual is from Psalm 33 (34): “Fear the Lord, all ye his saints: for there is no want to them that fear him.” Of the next verse it sings only the second half, *Inquirentes autem Dominum, non deficient omni bono*, that those who seek the Lord shall lack no good; the first half, “The rich have wanted, and have suffered hunger,” is not sung. The Alleluia is the Lord’s invitation in Mt 11:28, “Come to me all you that labor and are burdened, and I will refresh you.” The Gospel is the opening of the Sermon on the Mount (Mt 5:1–12a). Jesus sees the multitudes, goes up into a mountain, sits, and teaches: blessed are the poor in spirit, the meek, those who mourn, those who hunger and thirst after justice, the merciful, the clean of heart, the peacemakers, and those who suffer persecution for justice’ sake; and blessed are the disciples when they are reviled and persecuted for his sake, “for your reward is very great in heaven.” The Offertory takes three verses of Wisdom and changes them: “The souls of the just are in the hand of God, and the torment of malice shall not touch them: in the sight of the unwise they seemed to die, but they are in peace. Alleluia” (Lasance). The Secret offers “the gifts of our devotion” and asks that they be pleasing “for the honor of all the just” and profitable to those who offer them. The Communion sings again three of the beatitudes the Gospel has just given, the clean of heart, the peacemakers and the persecuted. The Postcommunion asks that God grant his faithful people “ever to rejoice in the veneration of all Thy saints, and to be defended by their unceasing prayers.”

The Missal prints this Mass in the Sanctorale under the heading *Die 1 novembris*, *In festo omnium Sanctorum*, of the first class (printed pp. 702–703, nos. 3974–3983). In 2026 the first of November is a Sunday, the Twenty-third after Pentecost. A feast of the first class of the universal Church outranks a Sunday of the second class (RG 91, rows 11 and 15; RG 16), and the Sunday is not resumed on another day (RG 14). Because All Saints is not a feast of the Lord, the Sunday is not simply set aside: it is commemorated, as a privileged commemoration made in every Mass of the day (RG 108, 109 a, 111 a; RGMR 434 a, 436–437). After the feast’s Collect, Secret and Postcommunion the priest therefore says the Sunday’s Collect *Absolve, quaesumus, Domine*, its Secret *Pro nostrae servitutis augmento* and its Postcommunion *Quaesumus, omnipotens Deus*, each under its own conclusion. Nothing else of the Sunday is said. The colour is white (RG 120 d); the Gloria and the Credo are said (RGMR 431; 475 a and b), and the last Gospel is the beginning of the Gospel according to St John (RGMR 509–510). The feast has no Preface of its own, and the Missal’s general rubrics can be read for either of two Prefaces, that of the Most Holy Trinity or the common Preface; the appointed texts below set out both readings of the rule without choosing between them.

These texts raise three questions, and each of the three interpretations that follow takes up one. The first is who the sealed are. The Lesson counts them exactly, and then shows a multitude that nobody could count. St Bede answers that “by this definite number is signified the innumerable multitude of the whole Church,” sealed by the Lord with the sign of the cross; St Augustine, on the Introit’s verse, bids the just rejoice “not in yourselves, for that is not safe; but in the Lord.” Read together, the saints the feast honours are those God has sealed and gathered, from Israel and from every nation. The second is why the Gospel of this feast is the Beatitudes, which bless the poor, the mourning, the hungry and the persecuted, and why the Communion repeats three of them. St Augustine sets the eight sayings out as seven steps, from the fear of the Lord to wisdom, with an eighth that returns to the first; St John Chrysostom calls them a golden chain, each linked to the next, with humility as their foundation. The third is how the Gradual’s promise can be true. The Mass sings “there is no want to them that fear him,” though saints have gone hungry and died at persecutors’ hands, and its Offertory says that the just only “seemed to die.” St Basil the Great answers that perfect fear lacks nothing because the perfect good is God himself; St Robert Bellarmine distinguishes a higher sense of the promise, in which the friend of God feels no want, from a lower, in which God provides for his servants.

## The Appointed Texts {#sec:appointed-texts}

The Latin below is that of the 1962 typical edition, *Missale Romanum* (Typis Polyglottis Vaticanis), printed pp. 702–703, nos. 3974–3983, for the feast, and pp. 416–417, nos. 1720, 1726 and 1728, for the three orations of the Twenty-third Sunday after Pentecost, read on the page images of the CMAA facsimile. Every word of the feast’s ten texts stands in the Tours printing of the *Missale Romanum* (Mame, 1922), pp. 748–750, and every word of the Sunday’s three orations in the Ratisbon printing (Pustet, 1862), pp. 357–358. Those printings differ from the 1962 page in orthography (the Mame writes *Joannes*, *Jesus*, *justi*), in pointing, and in the length of some conclusion cues; no word that is said differs. The rubrics of the 1962 book are described here, not reproduced.

The English of the scriptural texts is the Douay–Rheims Bible in Bishop Challoner’s revision, which translates the Clementine Vulgate in the Missal’s own numbering. It is given at the canonical verses: where a chant takes part of a verse, the Douay words for that part are given and an ellipsis marks what the Missal leaves out, and each place where the Missal’s form departs from the Vulgate is named beneath the text. The English of the Introit antiphon and of the seven orations is that of the Lasance *New Roman Missal* in the Benziger revision of 1945, pp. 1265 and 1268 for the feast and pp. 733 and 735–736 for the Sunday. The Lasance prints this Mass under the rubrics in force before 1960, as a double of the first class with commemorations arranged differently; only its English is used here, and none of this English is an approved liturgical translation of the 1962 Missal. Psalms are cited in the Missal’s numbering, with the Hebrew numbering in parentheses.

> <span class="smallcaps">Lasance, The New Roman Missal (1945), All Saints, Introit</span>
>
> Let us all rejoice in the Lord, celebrating a feast in honor of all the saints, in whose solemnity the angels rejoice and join in praising the Son of God. Rejoice in the Lord, ye just: praise becometh the upright. V. Glory.

<!-- end of quotation -->

The antiphon is not taken from Scripture; it is a text of the Church, and the Missal gives it no citation. The verse is the first of Psalm 32. The Douay prints the psalm’s inscription, “A psalm for David,” inside its verse 1, and the Missal does not sing it. After the *Gloria Patri* the antiphon is repeated (RGMR 427).

### Collect {#proper-collect}

*Oratio*. Missal p. 702, no. 3975.

> *Omnípotens sempitérne Deus, qui nos ómnium Sanctórum tuórum mérita sub una tribuísti celebritáte venerári: quǽsumus; ut desiderátam nobis tuæ propitiatiónis abundántiam, multiplicátis intercessóribus, largiáris. Per Dóminum.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Lasance, The New Roman Missal (1945), All Saints, Collect</span>
>
> O almighty, eternal God, Who hast granted us to venerate the merits of all Thy saints in one solemnity, vouchsafe to us, we beseech Thee, through the multitude of our intercessors, that abundance of Thy mercy for which we ever have hoped. Through our Lord.

The Latin’s *sub una … celebritate*, “in one celebration,” is the Lasance’s “in one solemnity”; its *multiplicatis intercessoribus*, an ablative absolute in which the intercessors are multiplied, is the Lasance’s “through the multitude of our intercessors.” The prayer is addressed to the Father, and the Missal’s cue *Per Dominum* stands for the full conclusion through the Son.

### Commemoration Collect {#proper-commemoration-collect}

*Oratio* of the Twenty-third Sunday after Pentecost. Missal p. 416, no. 1720.

> *Absólve, quǽsumus, Dómine, tuórum delícta populórum: ut a peccatórum néxibus, quæ pro nostra fragilitáte contráximus, tua benignitáte liberémur. Per Dóminum nostrum Iesum Christum, Fílium tuum: Qui tecum vivit et regnat in unitáte.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Lasance, The New Roman Missal (1945), Twenty-third Sunday after Pentecost, Collect</span>
>
> Remit, we beseech Thee, O Lord, the sins of Thy people, that by Thy kindness we may be delivered from the trammels of our sins, in which, through our frailty, we have become entangled. Through our Lord.

The priest says this prayer after the feast’s Collect, as a commemoration of the Sunday, under its own conclusion (RG 108, 109 a, 111 a; RGMR 434 a, 437). The Latin’s *populorum* is plural, “of thy peoples”; the Lasance gives the singular.

Et audívi númerum signatórum, centum quadragínta quátuor mília signáti, ex omni tribu filiórum Israel. Ex tribu Iuda duódecim mília signáti. Ex tribu Ruben duódecim mília signáti. Benedíctio, et cláritas, et sapiéntia, et gratiárum áctio, honor, et virtus, et fortitúdo Deo nostro in sǽcula sæculórum. Amen.*

> <span class="smallcaps">Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Apoc 7:2–12</span>
>
> And I saw another angel ascending from the rising of the sun, having the sign of the living God. And he cried with a loud voice to the four angels to whom it was given to hurt the earth and the sea, Saying: Hurt not the earth nor the sea nor the trees, till we sign the servants of our God in their foreheads. And I heard the number of them that were signed. An hundred forty-four thousand were signed, of every tribe of the children of Israel. Of the tribe of Juda, twelve thousand signed: … Ruben … Gad … Aser … Nephthali … Manasses … Simeon … Levi … Issachar … Zabulon … Joseph … Benjamin … After this, I saw a great multitude, which no man could number, of all nations and tribes and peoples and tongues, standing before the throne and in sight of the Lamb, clothed with white robes, and palms in their hands. Benediction and glory and wisdom and thanksgiving, honour and power and strength, to our God, for ever and ever. Amen.

For each of the twelve tribes after Juda the Douay repeats “twelve thousand signed”; the repetitions are shortened here to the tribe’s name. The Missal’s opening, *In diebus illis: Ecce ego Ioannes vidi alterum Angelum*, stands for the Vulgate’s *Et vidi alterum angelum*, the Douay’s “And I saw another angel.” The Lasance renders the Missal’s opening “In those days, behold, I, John, saw another angel ascending from the rising of the sun”. Every other word of verses 2–12 agrees with the Clementine text. The Lesson stops before verse 13, where one of the ancients asks John who the white-robed multitude are and answers that they “are come out of great tribulation” (7:13–14).

### Gradual {#proper-gradual}

*Graduale*, *Ps. 33, 10 et 11* (Heb. 34). Missal p. 703, no. 3977.

> *Timéte Dóminum, omnes Sancti eius: quóniam nihil deest timéntibus eum. ℣. Inquiréntes autem Dóminum, non defícient omni bono.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Ps 33:10–11</span>
>
> Fear the Lord, all ye his saints: for there is no want to them that fear him. … but they that seek the Lord shall not be deprived of any good.

The Gradual keeps the older Roman wording of the psalter, not the Clementine Vulgate’s. For *nihil deest*, “nothing is lacking,” the Clementine reads *non est inopia*, which the Douay translates “there is no want”; for *non deficient*, “shall not fail,” it reads *non minuentur*, “shall not be diminished,” which the Douay translates “shall not be deprived.” The verse leaves out the first half of verse 11, *Divites eguerunt, et esurierunt*, “The rich have wanted, and have suffered hunger.” The Vulgate and the Douay count the psalm’s title as verse 1, as the Hebrew does, so these verses are 33:10–11 in both; English Bibles that leave the title unnumbered call them 34:9–10.

### Alleluia {#proper-alleluia}

Alleluia verse, *Matth. 11, 28*. Missal p. 703, no. 3978.

> *Allelúia, allelúia. ℣. Veníte ad me, omnes qui laborátis et oneráti estis: et ego refíciam vos. Allelúia.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Mt 11:28</span>
>
> Come to me all you that labor and are burdened, and I will refresh you.

The verse is the whole of Mt 11:28 and agrees with the Clementine text. It is the Lord’s invitation after his thanks to the Father, who has hid these things “from the wise and prudent” and revealed them “to little ones” (11:25); it goes on, “Take up my yoke upon you, and learn of me, because I am meek, and humble of heart” (11:29). The feast never falls between Septuagesima and Easter or in Paschaltide, so no Tract or paschal Alleluia is printed.

> <span class="smallcaps">Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Mt 5:1–12a</span>
>
> And seeing the multitudes, he went up into a mountain, and when he was set down, his disciples came unto him. And opening his mouth he taught them, saying: Blessed are the poor in spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. Blessed are the meek: for they shall possess the land. The Missal prints the cue *Credo* after the Gospel.

### Offertory {#proper-offertory}

*Antiphona ad Offertorium*, *Sap. 3, 1, 2 et 3*. Missal p. 703, no. 3980.

> *Iustórum ánimæ in manu Dei sunt, et non tanget illos torméntum malítiæ: visi sunt óculis insipiéntium mori: illi autem sunt in pace, allelúia.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Wis 3:1–3</span>
>
> … the souls of the just are in the hand of God, and the torment of death shall not touch them. In the sight of the unwise they seemed to die: … but they are in peace.

The antiphon is a received liturgical form, not the verses as the Vulgate prints them, and the Douay cannot render it word for word. Against the Clementine it leaves out the opening *autem*, “But”; reads *tanget* for *tangent*, a difference that does not show in English; reads *tormentum malitiae*, “the torment of malice,” for *tormentum mortis*, “the torment of death”; leaves out “and their departure was taken for misery: And their going away from us, for utter destruction” (3:2b–3a); and adds *alleluia*. The Lasance gives the antiphon its own English: “The souls of the just are in the hand of God, and the torment of malice shall not touch them: in the sight of the unwise they seemed to die, but they are in peace. Alleluia.”

The antiphon’s wording has a history of its own. The 1570 Missal’s Offertory for All Saints reads *tormentum mortis* and has no *alleluia*; the same Missal’s Masses of martyrs already read *malitiae*. The printings of 1604, 1862 and 1889 that were read read *malitiae* and have no *alleluia*, which first appears, among the printings read, in the Mame of 1922. When it entered was not established. Blessed Ildefonso Schuster notes that the antiphon is the same as that of the Mass of St Hippolytus on 13 August (*The Sacramentary* V, p. 211).

### Commemoration Secret {#proper-commemoration-secret}

*Secreta* of the Twenty-third Sunday after Pentecost. Missal p. 417, no. 1726.

> *Pro nostræ servitútis augménto sacrifícium tibi, Dómine, laudis offérimus: ut, quod imméritis contulísti, propítius exsequáris. Per Dóminum nostrum Iesum Christum, Fílium tuum: Qui tecum vivit et regnat in unitáte.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Lasance, The New Roman Missal (1945), Twenty-third Sunday after Pentecost, Secret</span>

> We offer Thee, O Lord, the sacrifice of praise for the increase of our service, that Thou mayest graciously complete that which, for no merit of ours, Thou hast granted unto us. Through our Lord.

It is said secretly after the feast’s Secret, and its last words, *Per omnia saecula saeculorum*, are said aloud to begin the Preface (RGMR 481). On the Sunday’s own page the Missal directs a Preface after this prayer; that direction belongs to the Mass of the Sunday, and a commemoration does not bring a Preface of its own into the Mass in which it is made (RGMR 483).

### Preface {#proper-preface}

No Preface is printed or directed with the feast. Either the Preface of the Most Holy Trinity, Missal p. 293, no. 1082, or the common Preface, p. 297, no. 1086.

The feast has no Preface of its own, and its page names none. Which Preface is said on 1 November 2026 is not settled here, and neither text is given. The general rubrics state that a Mass takes its own Preface; that a Mass without one takes the Preface of the season (*de Tempore*); and that otherwise the common Preface is said (RGMR 482). The question is whether the Sunday’s seasonal Preface reaches a feast of the first class that has no Preface of its own and is said on a Sunday of the second class.

One reading gives the Preface of the Most Holy Trinity. RGMR 494 b appoints that Preface as the seasonal Preface on Sundays of the second class outside Christmastide and Paschaltide, and the feast, lacking its own, would take the seasonal one under RGMR 482. The Missal’s direction at the Vigil of the Nativity, which gives the common Preface but the Trinity Preface when the Vigil falls on a Sunday (p. 16), is a similar case. It concerns one Mass, however, and may be read either as an instance of the general rule or as an exception to it.

The other reading gives the common Preface. RGMR 494 b, on this reading, speaks of the Mass of the Sunday and not of a feast said on a Sunday; the Sunday is present only by its commemoration, and a commemoration brings no Preface into the Mass (RGMR 483); and the common Preface is said in Masses that have no Preface of their own and are not to take the seasonal one (RGMR 498). Neither reading is settled by the rubrics cited here.

## Each Text in Its Setting {#sec:each-element}

Each appointed text is set here in its own place: the chapter or psalm it comes from, the words the Missal keeps and leaves out, its history where that is known, and what the Fathers and later commentators say of it. The three interpretations that follow take up the larger questions.

### Introit: the Church’s antiphon and the psalm of the just {#sec:element-introit}

The antiphon was not written for this feast. Blessed Ildefonso Schuster notes that *Gaudeamus … sub honore* is an ancient Introit “originally assigned to the feast of St Agatha on February 5” (*The Sacramentary* V, p. 209), and William Durandus, in the thirteenth century, already gives *Gaudeamus omnes in Domino* to St Agatha, explaining it by her going to prison and to her passion with joy (*Rationale* VII.47). On this feast the antiphon names, in place of one martyr, *Sanctorum omnium*, and keeps the angels who rejoice at the solemnity and praise the Son of God.

The verse opens Psalm 32, a hymn: the just are bidden to rejoice and to praise on the harp (32:1–3), the psalm recounts the word and the works of the Lord (32:4–9) and his counsel that stands against the counsels of the nations (32:10–11), and it calls blessed “the nation whose God is the Lord” (32:12). St Augustine reads the first verse as a warning: “rejoice, O ye righteous, not in yourselves, for that is not safe; but in the Lord. ‘For praise is comely to the upright’: these praise the Lord, who submit themselves unto the Lord” (*En. in Ps.* 32 \[NPNF “Psalm XXXIII”\], 1). St Robert Bellarmine takes the rejoicing to include praise, and the praise “becometh the upright” because it is “the special duty of the just” (*Explanatio*, Ps 32, v. 1).

### Collect: one celebration and many intercessors {#sec:element-collect}

Schuster finds the Collect already in the Gelasian Sacramentary, where it belongs to a collective feast of the Apostles within the octave of Ss Peter and Paul (p. 210); on this feast its *omnium Sanctorum* widens it. The prayer gives two reasons together. God has given us to venerate the merits of all the saints in one celebration; and the intercessors, being many, are the ground of the petition. Durandus, giving the four causes for which the feast was instituted, names both. The second is that many saints have no feast of their own, and on this day all are honoured together. The fourth is that petitions are more easily granted, “because it is impossible that a multitude should not be heard” (*quia impossibile est multitudinem non exaudiri*), and he quotes this Collect for it, in a form that reads *multiplicatis intercessionibus*, “with the intercessions multiplied,” where the 1962 Missal reads *intercessoribus*, “intercessors” (*Rationale* VII.34). The third interpretation below returns to this cause.

### Commemoration Collect: the bonds of sins {#sec:element-commemoration-collect}

The Sunday’s Collect enters only because the feast has displaced the Sunday, and it is said after the feast’s own, as the opening sets out. It asks that God absolve the sins of his peoples, so that by his kindness they may be freed from the bonds that their frailty has contracted. Said one after the other, the feast’s prayer asks for mercy through the many saints, and the Sunday’s asks release from the sins of those who pray.

### Lesson: the sealing and the multitude {#sec:element-lesson}

The seventh chapter of the Apocalypse stands between the sixth seal and the seventh. Four angels stand at the four corners of the earth, holding the four winds (7:1). The angel from the rising of the sun forbids them to hurt the earth or the sea or the trees until the servants of God are sealed on their foreheads (7:2–3). Schuster connects the seal with the sign of Ezechiel’s vision, where the Lord commands, “mark Thau upon the foreheads of the men that sigh, and mourn for all the abominations” committed in Jerusalem (Ezech 9:4). He quotes it as *Signa Tau super frontem virorum gementium et dolentium* and reads it as “a sign of membership or conformity with the Eternal Father and his Christ” (p. 210). John then hears the number, a hundred and forty-four thousand, twelve thousand from each tribe. The list of tribes is not that of Jacob’s sons: Juda is put first, Dan is missing, and Manasses, Joseph’s son, is counted with Joseph. After the number John sees a multitude “which no man could number,” from all nations, in white robes and with palms, and all the angels, standing round the throne, the ancients and the four living creatures, fall on their faces and adore God. The Lesson ends at the angels’ sevenfold praise. It does not read the elder’s question in 7:13 or his answer, that these “are they who are come out of great tribulation and have washed their robes … in the blood of the Lamb” (7:14).

Schuster calls the Lesson “significant of the origin of the feast,” because it is the same as for the dedication of the Pantheon on 13 May (p. 210; his printed reference to chapter viii is an error for vii). Durandus gives the same Lesson, *Ecce ego Ioan.*, in his account of the feast (VII.34). How the number and the multitude are related is the question of the first interpretation below.

### Gradual: fear and want {#sec:element-gradual}

Psalm 33 is an alphabetical psalm. Its title sets it at the time when David “changed his countenance before Achimelech, who dismissed him, and he went his way” (33:1; compare 1 Kings 21:10–15). In the verses before the Gradual’s, the angel of the Lord encamps round about those who fear him and delivers them, and the psalm says, “O taste, and see that the Lord is sweet” (33:8–9). The Gradual takes up at the next verse, “Fear the Lord, all ye his saints,” and it leaves out the first half of verse 11, “The rich have wanted, and have suffered hunger.” Schuster notes that the same Gradual is sung on 8 August for St Cyriacus (p. 210).

The psalm’s own word for the faithful, *Sancti eius*, “his saints,” is the word of the feast. The promise attached to it, *nihil deest timentibus eum*, raises the difficulty that saints have gone hungry and been put to death. St Augustine raises it himself, and St Basil the Great and St Robert Bellarmine answer it; the third interpretation follows them.

### Alleluia: the invitation {#sec:element-alleluia}

The verse is the Lord’s invitation in Mt 11:28. Before it he thanks the Father for hiding “these things from the wise and prudent” and revealing them “to little ones,” and says that no one knows the Father but the Son and he to whom the Son will reveal him (11:25–27). After it he says, “Take up my yoke upon you, and learn of me, because I am meek, and humble of heart … For my yoke is sweet and my burden light” (11:29–30).

St John Chrysostom notes whom the invitation reaches: “Not this or that person, but all that are in anxiety, in sorrows, in sins.” He notes too what it promises: “He said not, ‘I will save you,’ only; but … ‘I will place you in all security’” (*Hom. in Matt.* 38.3). St Augustine, preaching on the same verse, asks why the Lord calls the labouring to him, “but that ye may labour no more?”, and tells his hearers to “first think of the foundation of humility” if they would build high, the height of that building reaching “even to the Vision of God” (*Sermo* 69, 1–3). Schuster calls the verse “a preparation for the Gospel”: “Jesus invites those who faint beneath the cross to come to him and he will give them refreshment” (p. 210). Durandus, in his account of the feast, gives a different Alleluia, *O quam gloriosum*, so the Mass he knew agreed with this one in the Gospel but not in this verse.

### Gospel: the Beatitudes {#sec:element-gospel}

The Gospel is the opening of the Sermon on the Mount. Seeing the multitudes, Jesus goes up into a mountain, sits, and teaches his disciples (5:1–2). The eight beatitudes follow (5:3–10), and then he applies the last of them to the disciples themselves: they are blessed when they are reviled and persecuted for his sake, and their reward is very great in heaven (5:11–12a). The Missal stops before “For so they persecuted the prophets that were before you.” St Augustine calls the Sermon “a perfect standard of the Christian life” (*De serm. Dom.* I.1), and the first and the eighth beatitude end with the same promise, “for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.”

Schuster says that on this feast “the Gospel can be no other than that of the Beatitudes,” because all are included in it, and those who have least according to the world receive the larger share; the first beatitude is promised “to the humble and poor in spirit” (pp. 210–211). Durandus gives the same Gospel, *Videns Iesus turbas* (VII.34). The second interpretation below takes up why the Mass reads the Beatitudes for the saints, and how its present and future promises are to be read.

### Offertory: in the hand of God {#sec:element-offertory}

The Book of Wisdom gives first the speech of the wicked, who reckon life short and death the end, and resolve: “Let us oppress the poor just man … Let us condemn him to a most shameful death” (Wis 2:10, 20). Chapter 3 answers them. The souls of the just are in the hand of God; to the unwise they seemed to die, but they are in peace; “their hope is full of immortality” (3:4); and “The just shall shine” (3:7). The antiphon keeps the first clause, the appearance of death and the peace, and leaves out the two clauses on the departure taken for misery and destruction.

The Fathers quote the verse often. St John Chrysostom uses “the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God” to deny that the souls of the dead wander the earth (*Hom. in Matt.* 28). St Augustine, on “My soul is continually in my hands” (Ps 118:109), explains the reading “in thy hand” by it, since most copies read so: “For ‘the souls of the righteous are in God’s hand’” (*En. in Ps.* 118, on v. 109). St Gregory the Great applies the next clause to the just man whose body is bereft of life “amidst the hands of the persecutors,” for, “according to the words of Wisdom, In the sight of the unwise they seemed to die” (*Moralia* XII.v.6, on Job 14:8–9). Schuster notes that the antiphon is that of St Hippolytus on 13 August, and reads it of the martyrs: “The persecutor believes that the lives of the martyrs are in his hands, but they are in the hands of God”; the wicked are instruments God uses “to perfect his works of art,” and “the divine artist and his living masterpiece” are at peace (p. 211). The third interpretation returns to the antiphon’s “seemed to die.”

### Secret: the honour of all the just {#sec:element-secret}

The Secret offers “the gifts of our devotion” and asks two things of them: that they be pleasing to God *pro cunctorum … honore iustorum*, for the honour of all the just, and that by his mercy they be for our salvation. The saints are named here by the word the Offertory has just sung, *iustorum*. Schuster quotes the prayer whole and adds nothing to it (p. 211). The prayer asks that the offering honour the saints and benefit those who make it; it does not ask anything for the saints themselves.

### Commemoration Secret: the sacrifice of praise {#sec:element-commemoration-secret}

The Sunday’s Secret offers *sacrificium … laudis*, the sacrifice of praise, “for the increase of our service,” and asks God to bring to completion what he has given *immeritis*, to the undeserving. Said after the feast’s Secret, it offers the sacrifice for the increase of the service of those present, and names what they have received as given without merit.

### Preface {#sec:element-preface}

The feast has no Preface of its own, and which Preface is said on this day is not settled; the appointed texts give the two readings of the rubrics.

### Communion: three beatitudes again {#sec:element-communion}

The Communion repeats the sixth, seventh and eighth beatitudes, the clean of heart, the peacemakers and those persecuted for justice. Schuster says that at this point “the Eternal Truth teaches men how to attain” happiness, setting out on the Mount “as it were the decalogue of happiness,” and paraphrases the three: the clean of heart shall see God, the peaceable shall be known as true sons of God, “the author of peace,” and the persecuted “will receive endless life and joy in exchange for the life and joy they have sacrificed here” (p. 211). St Augustine, as the second interpretation shows, places the vision of God and the divine sonship at the top of his seven steps and reads the eighth beatitude as a return to the first.

### Postcommunion: veneration and protection {#sec:element-postcommunion}

The Postcommunion asks that the faithful peoples ever rejoice in the veneration of all the saints and be defended by their unceasing prayer. Schuster dwells on its name for the Church on earth, *fideles populi*, “the faithful people”: “the people journeying towards eternity with the eyes, and in the light of faith” (p. 211). The prayer asks for two things that it holds together: the joy of venerating the saints, which the Introit began, and their continual intercession, which the Collect asked for.

### Commemoration Postcommunion: divine things and human dangers {#sec:element-commemoration-postcommunion}

The Sunday’s Postcommunion asks that those to whom God gives to rejoice in sharing divine things, *divina … participatione*, he not allow to be subject to human dangers. It is the last proper prayer of the Mass, said after the feast’s Postcommunion. The feast’s prayer asks for the saints’ defence, and the Sunday’s asks for protection from human dangers.

## One Church, Sealed and Uncounted {#lane-sealed-number-uncounted-crowd}

The Lesson of this Mass counts the servants of God who are sealed, a hundred and forty-four thousand, and then shows a multitude that no one could count. This interpretation follows St Bede, who expounds the chapter verse by verse, and St Augustine, who speaks to its number and its crowd in three passing loci. Bede reads the number as the whole Church and the seal as the sign of the cross; Augustine says the number signifies the whole body of the saints and calls the uncounted crowd the mass of the saints, and on the Introit’s psalm he bids the just rejoice in the Lord and not in themselves. Victorinus of Pettau, who comments on the chapter’s opening and on its crowd, agrees with them that the multitude is the elect out of all believers and disagrees with Bede on the angel and on the number, and St Cyprian reads the crowd as the martyrs, the reading Bede rejects. Read together, the saints the feast honours are those God has sealed and gathered, from Israel and from every nation.

### The question: a number, and a multitude without number {#sec:sealed-question}

John hears a number before he sees anything: *centum quadraginta quatuor millia signati, ex omni tribu filiorum Israel*, and the number is then given again tribe by tribe, twelve thousand from each. Immediately after it he sees *turbam magnam, quam dinumerare nemo poterat*, from all nations and tribes and peoples and tongues. Read as a count, the number contradicts the multitude, or restricts the sealed to Israel and leaves the nations outside the seal. The list of tribes adds a second difficulty. It is not the list of Jacob’s sons in the order of their birth: Juda stands first, Dan is missing, and Manasses is counted beside his father Joseph. A third difficulty is the seal itself. The angel forbids the four angels to hurt the earth *quoadusque signemus servos Dei nostri in frontibus eorum*, but the Lesson does not say what the seal is, or what it does for those who bear it.

### St Bede: the number is the whole Church {#sec:sealed-bede-number}

St Bede, commenting on the Apocalypse verse by verse, takes the angel from the rising of the sun to be the Lord incarnate, “the day-spring from on high” (Lk 1:78), “bearing the ensign of the cross, with which to seal His own in their foreheads” (*Explanatio Apocalypsis* I, on 7:2). The command not to hurt the earth he reads of what followed the Passion, when the dominion of the enemy and of worldly power was broken: “to this end was the empire of the nations broken up, that the face of the saints might be freely marked with the seal of faith, which these had resisted” (on 7:3). The angel from the east is not read as the Lord by every Father: Victorinus of Pettau reads him as Elias, in his own subsection below.

On the number he is explicit: “By this definite number is signified the innumerable multitude of the whole Church, which is descended from the patriarchs either by the lineage of nature, or the imitation of faith.” His warrant for counting the Gentiles among the tribes is St Paul’s “if ye be Christ’s, then are ye Abraham’s seed” (Gal 3:29). Twelve is multiplied by twelve and raised to thousands, “the cube of the number ten, by which is represented the enduring life of the Church” (on 7:4).

The order of the tribes he explains in the same way. John begins with Juda, “from which tribe our Lord sprang,” and leaves out Dan, from whom, “as it is said, Antichrist is to be born,” citing Jacob’s blessing, “Let Dan become an adder in the way” (Gen 49:17). John “has not proposed to set forth the order of earthly generation, but according to the interpretation of the names the powers of the Church, which from present confession and praise is hastening to the right hand of eternal life.” Juda means “confession” or “praise,” and comes first, “for before the first step of confession no one reaches the height of good works”; Benjamin, “the son of the right hand,” comes last, because after death is destroyed the elect are given the eternal inheritance at Christ’s right hand (on 7:5–8). Between them Bede reads each name as a virtue in which the faithful make progress, from the works of Reuben to the martyrdom of Zabulon and the unwearied prayer of Benjamin, and concludes that “from the merits of individuals, should the most perfect beauty of the Church, as the sum of a hundred and forty-four thousand, be made up.” On his reading the twelve thousand of each tribe are not twelve groups of people but twelve kinds of holiness, each sealed “in his own profession,” which together make up one Church.

### St Bede: the multitude before the throne {#sec:sealed-bede-multitude}

At verse 9 Bede gives two readings of the multitude. First, the vision “announces the glory of those who are to overcome the wickedness of the last persecution.” Then, “that which follows, ‘From all nations, and tribes, and peoples, and tongues,’ may also be thus understood, that after enumerating the tribes of Israel, to whom the Gospel was first preached, he desires to make mention of the salvation of the Gentiles as well” (on 7:9). Bede does not say that the number and the multitude are the same people seen twice. He says that the number signifies the whole Church (on 7:4), and, in his second reading, that the multitude adds the Gentiles to the tribes. Taken together, Bede’s two comments give the number as the Church’s completeness and the multitude as its extent.

The robes, he writes, signify baptism, and the palms “the triumph of the Cross,” and “in Christ they have overcome the world.” The loud voice is “great devotion, an unceasing praise” (on 7:10). When the angels fall down before the throne, “In all the angels he has represented the persons of the great multitude worshipping the Lord,” though Bede allows that the verse may also be read of the angelic spirits themselves (on 7:11). The sevenfold praise of verse 12 is the Church’s: “The Church offers the sevenfold praise of excellence unto the Lord, and in each of its members confesses to have received this from Him” (on 7:12).

Two of Bede’s comments on verses that the Lesson does not read bear on whom the feast honours. St Cyprian, exhorting Fortunatus to martyrdom, quotes the uncounted multitude in white robes, who have washed their robes and made them white in the blood of the Lamb, and concludes that “the Christian martyrs cannot be numbered” (*Ad Fortunatum* §11): on his reading the crowd before the throne is the crowd of the martyrs. On the white robes washed in the blood of the Lamb (7:14) Bede writes, “He speaks not of martyrs alone. They are washed in their own blood. But the blood of Jesus, the Son of God, cleanses the whole Church from all sin.” The reading Cyprian gives the vision is the one Bede here refuses. And after expounding the end of the chapter as the life to come, he adds that the vision of the white-robed multitude “may also be understood of the present time, when ‘we are saved in hope’” (Rom 8:24; on 7:17). The feast was first a feast of martyrs: Schuster traces it to a collective feast of martyrs in fourth-century Syria and to the feast of the martyrs that Boniface IV instituted when he consecrated the Pantheon as a church (*The Sacramentary* V, pp. 208–209). On Bede’s reading of 7:14 the white robes are not the martyrs’ alone.

### St Augustine: the whole body of the saints, rejoicing in the Lord {#sec:sealed-augustine}

St Augustine nowhere expounds this chapter; what he says of its number and its crowd he says in passing, inside other arguments. Setting out Tichonius’s fifth rule, the rule of the times, he comes to the numbers Scripture favours: “twelve into twelve gives one hundred and forty-four, which last number is used in the Apocalypse to signify the whole body of the saints,” and that number, he adds, “has not reference to times, but to men” (*De doctrina christiana* III.35.51). The application stands in his account of Tichonius’s rule, which the text does not separate from his own. Writing to Vincentius that the one Church is few beside the wicked and many in herself, he instances this vision: “in the Apocalypse, many thousands ‘which no man can number,’ from every tribe and tongue, are seen clothed in white robes, and with palms of victory” (*Letter XCIII*, §30). Preaching on the few and the many, he tells his hearers that the few who are saved are “many in the society of the Angels,” and quotes the great multitude “coming with white robes and palms”: “This is the mass of the saints” (*Sermon LXI*, 1). None of the three expounds the chapter, and none speaks of the seal or of the angel from the east; they are short applications, and on the two points the Lesson presses they stand with Bede: the number signifies the whole body of the saints, and the uncounted crowd is the one Church.

His word to the Mass’s own verses is on its psalms. On the Introit’s verse he writes, “rejoice, O ye righteous, not in yourselves, for that is not safe; but in the Lord. ‘For praise is comely to the upright’: these praise the Lord, who submit themselves unto the Lord” (*En. in Ps.* 32 \[NPNF “Psalm XXXIII”\], 1). The Introit antiphon bids all rejoice in the Lord on the feast of all the saints, and the verse, read with Augustine, says in whom the just rejoice: not in themselves.

Later in the same psalm, outside the verse the Mass sings, Augustine reads “Blessed is the nation whose God is the Lord” of “one nation … belonging to the heavenly city,” which “not of itself, but by the gift of God, hath been chosen” (32:12; §12). The Lesson’s multitude is *ex omnibus gentibus*; to hear Augustine’s one nation beside it is this interpretation’s inference, not his. What his comment gives is the point this interpretation draws from Bede as well: those who are counted are counted by God’s choice and gift.

On the Gradual, too, Augustine is a witness. Its verse sings that those who seek the Lord shall lack no good, and he asks what good is meant. “When thou art filled with spiritual riches, canst thou be poor?” The riches are “such jewelry of virtues, justice, truth, charity, faith, endurance,” and of the promise, “what manner of good, I have already said” (*En. in Ps.* 33 \[NPNF “Psalm XXXIV”\], 14). Augustine names those goods as the virtues, justice, truth, charity, faith, endurance; they are close to the virtues Bede reads in the tribes’ names.

### Victorinus of Pettau: the angel as Elias, the number in the last times {#sec:sealed-victorinus}

One other independent Latin commentary on the Apocalypse reaches this chapter among the witnesses read: Victorinus of Pettau’s, and it reads the chapter differently from Bede. Victorinus comments on two of the Lesson’s verses. Of the angel ascending from the east he writes, “He speaks of Elias the prophet, who is the precursor of the times of Antichrist,” sent, as Malachias says, “to recall the Jews to the faith of the people that succeed them” (Mal 4:5–6; *Commentary on the Apocalypse*, on 7:2). Where Bede sees the Lord incarnate bearing the cross, Victorinus sees the forerunner of the last persecution. He says nothing of the number at the Lesson’s verse; when his commentary reaches the woman fleeing into the wilderness, he names it: “in the last times a hundred and forty-four thousands of men should believe on the preaching of Elias” (on 12:6). The definite number, on his reading, counts the believers of the last times, not the whole Church’s completeness.

On the crowd he stands with Bede. The multitude no man could number shows “the number of the elect out of all believers, who, being cleansed by baptism in the blood of the Lamb, have made their robes white, keeping the grace which they have received” (on 7:9), and the believers are “of the Jews and of the nations” (on 7:2). The elect gathered out of all believers, and the robes as baptism in the Lamb’s blood, are Bede’s own readings; the keeping of the grace received adds a note Bede does not sound, that the white robe is a gift to be kept. Victorinus also warns that the Apocalypse does not tell its events in their order — “Nor must we look for order in the Apocalypse; but we must follow the meaning of those things which are prophesied” — so that on his reading the sealing and the crowd belong to the same last times.

His witness stands under a caution. The text of the commentary is not secure: the editors of the English edition report that “the manuscripts must have been much corrupted,” that the mention of Genseric “proves the subsequent interpolation of his works,” and that what survives may be “full of the corrections of some pious disciple of St. Augustine who lived much later,” with the author’s own identity in doubt. His reading is given here under that caution, and with its gaps: he passes over the sealing itself and the tribes. What he gives this interpretation is a second developed reading of the chapter, one that agrees with Bede on whom the crowd is and on what its robes mean, and disagrees with him on the angel and on what the number counts.

### The sealed in the other texts of the Mass {#sec:sealed-elements}

The Introit and the Collect speak of the saints as one company. The antiphon keeps the feast *sub honore Sanctorum omnium*, and the Collect thanks God for giving us to venerate the merits of all the saints *sub una … celebritate* and asks for mercy *multiplicatis intercessoribus*. Durandus explains the one celebration by the saints’ number: so many could not each be given a feast (*Rationale* VII.34). The Gradual calls them *Sancti eius*, his saints, and names them by their fear of the Lord; in the psalm’s preceding verse, which the Mass does not sing, “the angel of the Lord shall encamp round about them that fear him: and shall deliver them” (Ps 33:8). That angel, set beside the Lesson’s angel who holds back the four angels from the earth, is this interpretation’s comparison, not the psalm’s or Bede’s.

The Alleluia calls *omnes*, all who labour and are burdened, and Chrysostom remarks that the invitation reaches “not this or that person, but all that are in anxiety, in sorrows, in sins” (*Hom. in Matt.* 38.3); the multitude that no one could number answers to that word. The Gospel the feast reads with this Lesson blesses the poor in spirit, the mourning and the hungry, and those reviled and persecuted for the Son’s sake, and promises that their reward is very great in heaven. Bede’s first reading of the multitude is that the vision “announces the glory of those who are to overcome the wickedness of the last persecution” (on 7:9): the persecuted whom the Gospel blesses are the crowd the Lesson shows in glory. On this reading the Gospel describes the sealed on earth, those who bear the cross openly under the world’s hatred, with their reward in heaven.

The Offertory sings of the just in death. The souls of the just are in the hand of God and “seemed to die”; Bede’s seal is set on those who “in the midst of adverse things are faithful confessors of His Name” (on 7:14). The Secret asks that the gifts be pleasing “for the honor of all the just”: the sacrifice is offered for the honour of the whole numbered company at once. The Communion sings again the sixth, seventh and eighth beatitudes: the clean of heart shall see God, the peacemakers shall be called the children of God, the persecuted for justice possess the kingdom of heaven. Those promises are the ones the Lesson’s crowd is shown to enjoy, standing before the throne and in sight of the Lamb; on this interpretation the assembly that hears them sung over the Sacrament is the same Church as that multitude, still on the way. The Postcommunion asks that the *fideles populi* be defended by the saints’ unceasing prayer. Schuster reads those peoples as the Church on earth, “journeying towards eternity with the eyes, and in the light of faith” (p. 211); on this interpretation they are the sealed who have not yet come to stand before the throne. The Sunday’s Collect, said after the feast’s, asks God to loose the sins of his *populi*; its Secret asks him to bring to completion what he has given the undeserving; and its Postcommunion asks that those who share in divine things be not left subject to human dangers. These prayers belong to the Sunday and say nothing of the seal; the peoples who ask for the saints’ prayer also ask to be absolved.

### The strongest difficulty: a number that limits {#sec:sealed-difficulty}

The strongest objection is the plain sense of the Lesson’s own words. The number is exact, the tribes are Israel’s, and John says he *heard* the number of the sealed before he *saw* the multitude; a reading that takes the number for the whole Church may seem to drop *centum quadraginta quatuor millia* in order to reach the crowd. Bede’s answer is to keep the number and to read it, giving his reasons: Paul’s seed of Abraham by faith, the arithmetic of twelve and ten, and the order of the tribes that is not the order of birth. The tribes and the twelve thousands remain in his reading, as the powers of the Church from confession to the right hand.

The disagreement on these answers is older than the reception of this Mass. On the number, Bede reads the whole Church’s completeness, and Augustine the whole body of the saints; Victorinus reads a company of the last times, those who believe at Elias’s preaching. On the crowd, Bede, Augustine and Victorinus read the elect gathered out of all believers, from Israel and the nations; Cyprian reads the martyrs, a crowd that cannot be numbered. On the angel from the east, Bede reads the Lord incarnate, and Victorinus reads Elias, the precursor of Antichrist’s times. This interpretation follows Bede on all three, with Augustine on the number and the crowd: against Victorinus on the number, against Cyprian on the crowd, and with Victorinus’s Elias standing beside Bede’s Lord, for the Fathers do not speak with one voice on this chapter.

A different reading of the same verses is held by Lucien Fromage, who continued Dom Prosper Guéranger’s *Liturgical Year*. He takes the Lesson as a second census. At his first coming the God-Man, using Caesar Augustus, had the world enrolled, and now, Fromage writes, *l’heure a sonné d’un autre recensement, qui doit consigner au livre de vie le résultat des opérations du salut*: the hour has struck for another census, which is to record in the book of life the outcome of the work of salvation. He cites St Gregory the Great’s Christmas homily on the census, and adds that because many withdrew by their own fault from the first enrolment, which comprised all men in the redemption, a second and definitive one was needed to cut the guilty off from it (*L’Année liturgique*, vol. 15, p. 94; Gregory, *Hom. in Ev.* 8). Bede reads the number as the whole Church made up of its members’ virtues; Fromage reads the sealing as the last reckoning, which enrols the saved and strikes out the rest. On Fromage’s reading the multitude is still uncounted, but the census that precedes it is also a judgment.

Schuster draws the opposite conclusion from the size of the multitude: “Not only the hundred and forty-four thousand predestined sons of Abraham, but a turba magna of every age, sex, and condition” enter heaven, and “It is not then so hard to be saved, as St John writes that he could not count the number of the elect” (p. 210). He adds at once one condition, the seal of the Tau that Ezechiel saw set on the foreheads of those who sigh and mourn, and he reads it with St Paul’s “as you are partakers of the sufferings, so shall you be also of the consolation” (2 Cor 1:7). Schuster’s 144,000 are sons of Abraham, and his multitude adds others to them; Bede’s 144,000 are already the whole Church.

Literal.

: John sees an angel from the east forbid the angels of destruction to hurt the earth until the servants of God are sealed on their foreheads; he hears their number, twelve thousand from each of twelve tribes; he then sees an uncounted multitude from every nation in white robes with palms before the throne and the Lamb, and the angels’ sevenfold praise.

Allegorical.

: The angel is Christ incarnate, the seal his cross, and the hundred and forty-four thousand the whole Church, descended from the patriarchs by nature or by faith (Bede, with Augustine on the number as the whole body of the saints; Victorinus reads the angel as Elias and the number as the believers of the last times). The multitude is the victors over the last persecution or, in Bede’s second reading, the Gentiles gathered after Israel, washed in the blood of the Lamb — the elect out of all believers (Bede, with Victorinus; Cyprian reads the crowd as the martyrs).

Moral.

: The seal is the faith confessed openly on the forehead, and the tribes are the virtues of the faithful, from confession to perseverance in prayer (Bede). The white robe is the grace of baptism, to be kept (Victorinus). The just rejoice in the Lord and not in themselves, and the goods they are promised are justice, truth, charity, faith and endurance (Augustine).

Anagogical.

: The elect stand at last at the right hand of Christ, which Benjamin’s place at the end of the list signifies, and give God unceasing praise; the vision is also true now of those who are saved in hope (Bede).

## The Beatitudes, the Way the Saints Walked {#lane-beatitudes-way-of-the-saints}

On the feast of all the saints the Mass reads the eight beatitudes, and at the Communion it sings the last three of them again. This interpretation follows St Augustine, who reads them as one ordered way, beginning in humility and the fear of God and ending in persecution for justice, and St John Chrysostom, who reads them as a golden chain with humility as its foundation, with St Thomas Aquinas and St Hilary of Poitiers on what the promises give and when. None of the four comments on this feast. Read together here, the Gospel describes how the saints became saints, and the promises are given to the same people whose way it describes.

### The question: why the Beatitudes, and in what tense {#sec:beatitudes-question}

Schuster writes that on this feast “the Gospel can be no other than that of the Beatitudes,” because “all are included in it and to each is granted a special blessing” (*The Sacramentary* V, p. 210). The Gospel calls blessed the poor in spirit, the meek, those who mourn, those who hunger and thirst, those who are persecuted and reviled: people who lack, grieve and suffer. The promises raise a second difficulty, of tense. The first and the eighth beatitude say *ipsorum est regnum caelorum*, “theirs *is* the kingdom”; the six between say that the blessed *shall* possess the land, be comforted, have their fill, obtain mercy, see God, be called the children of God. If the promises are all future, the saints the feast honours do not yet have what the Gospel promises; if they are all present, the mourners are comforted already, which experience does not show. A third question is the Communion: why the Mass, having read eight beatitudes, sings three of them again.

### St Augustine: seven steps and an eighth {#sec:beatitudes-augustine}

St Augustine opens his commentary by calling the Sermon on the Mount “a perfect standard of the Christian life” (*De serm. Dom.* I.1). He contrasts the mountain with Sinai: the lesser precepts were given “to a people who as yet required to be bound by fear,” the greater to a people “now become suitable to set free by love” (I.2). He then reads the eight sayings as a sequence (I.3.10). The poor in spirit are “the humble and God-fearing,” for “the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom,” as “pride” is “the beginning of all sin” (I.3). The soul that has submitted to God becomes meek before the Scriptures, learns from them in what evils it is held and mourns, labours to tear itself free and so hungers for justice, receives counsel and shows mercy, is purified in heart to contemplate the highest good, and at the seventh step reaches wisdom, which makes peace in the whole man. “The eighth, as it were, returns to the starting-point, because it shows and commends what is complete and perfect: therefore in the first and in the eighth the kingdom of heaven is named.”

He sets the seven steps beside the sevenfold operation of the Holy Ghost in Isaias 11, counted upward from the fear of the Lord: fear answers to the poor in spirit, piety to the meek, knowledge to the mourners, fortitude to the hungry, counsel to the merciful, understanding to the clean of heart, and wisdom to the peacemakers (I.4.11). Rabanus Maurus, compiling his commentary on Matthew, takes Augustine’s reading over: he quotes him by name at 5:11–12, and in his comment on the Lord’s Prayer he joins the gifts to the beatitudes again, beginning with *timor Dei, quo beati sunt pauperes spiritu* (*Expositio in Matthaeum*, on Mt 5–6).

The rewards are one reward under eight names: “the one reward, which is the kingdom of heaven, is variously named according to these stages” (I.4.12). The meek receive an inheritance, the mourners comfort, the hungry their fill, the clean of heart the sight of God, the peacemakers the likeness of God. Augustine adds that “those promises can indeed be fulfilled in this life, as we believe them to have been fulfilled in the case of the apostles,” while “that all-embracing change into the angelic form, which is promised after this life, cannot be explained in any words.” The eighth beatitude, which returns to the beginning, he finds figured in circumcision on the eighth day and in the Lord’s resurrection on the day after the Sabbath, which is “the eighth, and at the same time the first day” (I.4.12).

### St John Chrysostom: humility, blessing and the golden chain {#sec:beatitudes-chrysostom}

St John Chrysostom begins in the same place. The poor in spirit are “the humble and contrite in mind,” and the Lord blesses first those “who by choice humble and contract themselves”; he says “poor” rather than “humble” because he means “them who are awestruck, and tremble at the commandments of God” (*Hom. in Matt.* 15.2). Humility comes first because pride is “the root and fountain of all wickedness,” and the Lord “laid this law first as a strong and safe foundation” (15.3). He notes the form of the sayings: the Lord speaks “not … by way of advice or of commandments, but by way of blessing,” so that “though thou be a slave, a beggar, in poverty, a stranger, unlearned, there is nothing to hinder thee from being blessed” (15.3).

Chrysostom also reads the eight as connected, and in an order of his own: “He hath woven a sort of golden chain for us. Thus, first, he that is ‘humble,’ will surely also ‘mourn’ for his own sins: he that so ‘mourns,’ will be both ‘meek,’ and ‘righteous,’ and ‘merciful’; … and he that hath attained unto all these, will be moreover arrayed against dangers” (15.9). The two Fathers differ on the link between the steps. For Augustine it is an ascent of knowledge and of the Spirit’s gifts toward contemplation; for Chrysostom it is a chain of dispositions, each producing the next, and its end is readiness for persecution. They agree that humility comes first and that persecution comes only to one already formed by the rest. Chrysostom reads the peacemakers’ reward as a share in the Son’s own work: “this became the work of the Only Begotten, to unite the divided, and to reconcile the alienated” (15.7). On the eighth, he observes that the Lord “hath set two limitations; when it is for His sake, and when the things that are said are false” (15.7), the *propter me* and *mentientes* of the Gospel’s last verse.

### St Thomas and St Hilary: in the way and in the homeland {#sec:beatitudes-thomas-hilary}

St Thomas Aquinas answers the question of tense with a distinction. The rewards can be had *perfecte & consummate, & hoc in patria tantum: & secundum inchoationem, & imperfecte; & sic in via*, perfectly and in their completion only in the homeland, and in their beginning and imperfectly on the way. So *Beati pauperes spiritu, non spe tantum, sed etiam re*: the poor in spirit are blessed not only in hope but in fact, for “the kingdom of God is within you” (Lk 17:21). On the clean of heart he records that some place the vision of God in contemplation in this life, and holds that the Lord promises it for the homeland. The eighth beatitude *designat perfectionem omnium praecedentium*, signifies the perfection of all that precede it, because persecution tests them, as “the furnace trieth the potter’s vessels” (Ecclus 27:6); and *persecutio non facit beatum, sed eius causa*, persecution does not make a man blessed, but its cause, *propter iustitiam* — here quoting Chrysostom: *Non dicit a paganis, et pro fide, sed propter iustitiam*, he does not say “by pagans, and for the faith,” but “for justice” (*Super Matt.* cap. 5, lect. 1).

St Hilary of Poitiers reads the promises more strictly of the life to come. The Lord on the mountain, *in paternae majestatis positus celsitudine*, set in the height of the Father’s majesty, lays down *caelestis vitae praecepta*, the precepts of the heavenly life. The land the meek inherit is *ejus corporis, quod ipse Dominus assumpsit habitaculum*, the body which the Lord himself took as his dwelling. The clean of heart see God because *non enim nisi spiritu perfecti, et immortalitate mutati … quod in Deo est immortale cernemus*: only when we are perfected in spirit and changed by immortality shall we behold what in God is immortal (*In Matt.* IV). Chrysostom reads the land differently: the meek are promised “a sensible prize,” for “with the things spiritual” the Lord “hath mingled the sensible” (15.5); and of the mourners he asks, “Where shall they be comforted! tell me. Both here and there” (15.4). Yet he too concludes that “although He give different names to the rewards, yet He brings all into His kingdom” (15.7).

### The way in the other texts of the Mass {#sec:beatitudes-elements}

The Lesson shows the way at its end. A multitude that no one could number stands before the throne and in sight of the Lamb, “clothed with white robes, and palms in their hands,” and Bede reads the robes as baptism and the palms as “the triumph of the Cross,” for “in Christ they have overcome the world” (*Explanatio Apocalypsis* I, on 7:9). Read beside the Gospel, the crowd is the way’s company arrived: those persecuted for justice’ sake hold the palms, and the poor in spirit stand in the kingdom that was theirs. That reading is this interpretation’s own inference; Bede’s comment is on the Apocalypse, not on Matthew.

The Alleluia verse comes before the Gospel and speaks to people who labour. Its context in Matthew names the disposition with which the Beatitudes begin: “learn of me, because I am meek, and humble of heart” (Mt 11:29). St Augustine, preaching on that verse, tells his hearers, “first think of the foundation of humility,” and says the building reaches “even to the Vision of God” (*Sermo* 69, 2–3), the reward of the sixth beatitude. The Gradual’s *Timete Dominum, omnes Sancti eius* names the fear that Augustine puts at the first step; and the Introit’s verse, which Augustine reads as rejoicing “not in yourselves … but in the Lord,” asks for the joy of those who are not puffed up.

The Offertory’s just who “seemed to die” have reached the eighth beatitude: Wisdom’s just man is the one the wicked resolve to “condemn … to a most shameful death” (Wis 2:20). The Collect speaks of the saints’ merits, and on this interpretation those merits are the way walked. The Secret asks that the gifts be pleasing for the honour of all the just and salutary to those who offer them: the honour to those who have finished the way, the profit to those still on it. The Communion sings the clean of heart, the peacemakers and the persecuted: in Augustine’s order the sixth and seventh steps and the eighth that returns to the first, in Aquinas’s the vision of God and the perfection of all the rest. Schuster calls the passage “as it were the decalogue of happiness” (p. 211). Why the Mass, having read all eight, sings these three again at the Communion, the witnesses do not say; the continuation remarks only that the Church receives in the Sacrament the three beatitudes she has just heard, “the three last beatitudes.” The three are the beatitudes whose promises name the way’s end: the clean of heart shall see God, the peacemakers shall be called the children of God, and theirs, as the poor in spirit’s, is the kingdom of heaven. That the Mass sings the vision, the sonship and the kingdom where the assembly receives the Sacrament is this interpretation’s inference, drawn from the two orders and from the Communion’s place in the Mass. The Postcommunion asks that the faithful peoples ever rejoice in the veneration of all the saints and be defended by their unceasing prayer: those who have walked the way now defend those who walk it. The Sunday’s Collect, said after the feast’s, asks for release from sins contracted *pro nostra fragilitate*; its Secret asks God to bring to completion what he gave the undeserving, and its Postcommunion that those who share in divine things be not left subject to human dangers. The same Mass that reads the way of the saints asks pardon for frailty.

### The strongest difficulty: present and future {#sec:beatitudes-difficulty}

The strongest difficulty for this reading is the tense of the promises. If every promise is postponed to the last day, the present *est* of the first and the eighth beatitude is ignored, and so is the feast, which honours saints who already reign. If every promise is made present, the mourners are already comforted and the hungry already filled, and the Gospel’s “your reward is very great in heaven” is ignored.

The Fathers answer the difficulty in different ways, and not by the text’s division of tenses: that two promises stand in the present and six in the future is this study’s observation, not theirs. Augustine holds that the promises can be fulfilled in this life, “as we believe them to have been fulfilled in the case of the apostles,” and that the change “into the angelic form” comes after it. Aquinas says the rewards are begun on the way and completed in the homeland, and the poor in spirit have the kingdom *re*, in fact, as the kingdom of God is within them. Chrysostom says the mourners are comforted “both here and there.” Hilary holds that the vision of God belongs to those perfected in spirit and changed by immortality. The difference between Augustine and Hilary is real: what Augustine believes fulfilled in the apostles, Hilary reserves to the perfected. On the feast, the present tense of the first and the eighth beatitude is true of the saints in heaven in its completion and of the Church on earth in its beginning.

Literal.

: Jesus, seated on a mountain, teaches his disciples that the poor in spirit, the meek, the mourners, those who hunger for justice, the merciful, the clean of heart, the peacemakers and the persecuted are blessed, and tells the disciples that when they are reviled and persecuted for his sake their reward is great in heaven.

Allegorical.

: The Lord gives the new law from the mountain as the old was given from Sinai, the greater precepts to those set free by love (Augustine); seated in the height of the Father’s majesty, he lays down the precepts of the heavenly life, and the land of the meek is the body he took (Hilary).

Moral.

: The way begins in humility and the fear of God and rises through meekness, mourning, hunger for justice, mercy and purity of heart to peace, each disposition linked to the next, and ends in readiness to suffer for justice’ sake (Augustine, Chrysostom); persecution blesses only for its cause (Aquinas).

Anagogical.

: The one reward, the kingdom of heaven, is begun on the way and completed in the homeland (Aquinas); the clean of heart, perfected in spirit and changed by immortality, see God (Hilary).

## No Want to Them That Fear Him {#lane-no-want-just-in-gods-hand}

The Gradual of this Mass promises that nothing is lacking to those who fear the Lord, and its Offertory says that the just only seemed to die. Both statements meet an obvious objection: the saints the feast honours went hungry, were persecuted and were put to death. This interpretation follows St Basil the Great and St Robert Bellarmine, who read the promise of the psalm, with St Augustine and St Thomas Aquinas, who state the objection against it and answer it, and the Fathers who read the Offertory’s verses of the just in the hand of God.

### The question: a promise that seems false {#sec:no-want-question}

The Gradual sings *Timete Dominum, omnes Sancti eius: quoniam nihil deest timentibus eum*, and then, *Inquirentes autem Dominum, non deficient omni bono*. St Augustine states the difficulty as plainly as any objector. Many will not fear God for fear of going hungry. And when the psalm goes on, “The rich do lack and suffer hunger, but they that seek the Lord shall not want any good thing,” then “if thou receive it according to the letter, He seemeth to deceive thee, for thou seest that many rich men that are wicked die in their riches.” The man who has seen such a rich man buried says, “the Scripture has deceived me,” and adds, “Daily I rise up to Church, daily I bend the knee, daily I seek the Lord, and have nothing good” (*En. in Ps.* 33 \[NPNF “Psalm XXXIV”\], 13).

Two features of the Mass bear on the difficulty. The Gradual leaves out the words about the rich, *Divites eguerunt, et esurierunt*, which stand at the head of verse 11 and on which all the commentators below build part of their answer; the Mass sings the promise without the contrast. The Offertory takes its verses from the chapter in which Wisdom answers the wicked who said, “Let us condemn him to a most shameful death” (Wis 2:20). The antiphon says that the just *visi sunt oculis insipientium mori*, seemed to die in the eyes of the unwise; the death that seemed to be the end was a death that the wicked inflicted.

### St Basil: the fear that lacks nothing {#sec:no-want-basil}

St Basil, preaching on Psalm 33, begins from what fear does. In the Latin version printed in Migne: *Nisi timor instituerit vitam nostram, sanctimonia in corpore comparari non potest*, unless fear has ordered our life, holiness cannot be gained in the body. He quotes “Pierce thou my flesh with thy fear” (Ps 118:120): as those transfixed with nails have members that cannot move to act, so those whose soul is seized by divine fear escape every disturbance that comes from the passions of sin. *Timenti itaque non est inopia*: he who is kept by fear from every unseemly act *nihil deest ad quamlibet virtutem, sed perfectus est*, lacks nothing for any virtue, but is perfect. And he who has received perfect fear *nihil peccabit*, will sin in nothing, and will not experience want, because fear is always with him (*Hom. in Ps.* 33, PG 29, cols. 365–368).

On the next verse Basil offers a reading of the rich who lacked. Perhaps the psalm calls rich the people of Israel, “to whom belongeth the adoption as of children … and the promises” (Rom 9:4), and they, *ob peccatum in Dominum admissum eguerunt*, came into want through the sin committed against the Lord. After they killed the bread of life (Jn 6:48), famine came upon them, a famine “not of bread, nor a thirst of water, but of hearing the word of the Lord” (Amos 8:11). Those from the nations who learned to seek the Lord are not diminished in any good, and *Perfectum omnino bonum Deus ipse est*, the wholly perfect good is God himself. He then puts the objection as a dilemma. Whoever counts bodily goods as the good the psalm promises must say either that the apostles did not seek the Lord, since they did not obtain bodily goods, or that Scripture is false. Neither can be said. Paul sought the Lord, *et nihil ei defuit bonorum*, and nothing good was lacking to him, though he was thrice beaten with rods, once stoned, thrice shipwrecked, and was in hunger and thirst to the last hour (2 Cor 11:25–27).

### St Robert Bellarmine: the higher sense and the humbler {#sec:no-want-bellarmine}

St Robert Bellarmine (whose English numbers these verses one lower than the Vulgate) reads the fear of the Gradual as “the pure and filial fear that is the characteristic of the saints and of the perfect”: “it is by fear we are made perfect, not by servile fear,” for “the perfect lover fears vehemently lest he may offend his beloved in any way.” The promise then has two senses. In “the higher meaning,” “the essence of perfection is to feel no want. And, what want can the friend of God, who owns everything, feel, when the property of friends is common; and if the just appear sometimes to be in want, they really are not so, because they get patience, better than any riches, to bear it.” The same words, “understood in an humbler sense,” teach the simple “that God will supply his servants with the temporal things of the world, and will not desert them in time of necessity” (*Explanatio*, Ps 33, his v. 9). On the next verse he gives the higher meaning again: those who seek the Lord “shall not be deprived of any good,” “because, as they cling to the Supreme Good, they possess all that is good” (his v. 10).

Bellarmine and Basil agree that the promise is kept in the good that the one who fears possesses, God himself. They differ in what they make of the rich. For Basil the rich may be Israel, impoverished of the word; for Bellarmine, in the plain sense, they are the wicked rich reduced to poverty, and in the higher sense the covetous, who “always hunger and need.” And Bellarmine keeps a lower sense of temporal provision that Basil does not state.

### St Augustine and St Thomas: riches and the physician {#sec:no-want-augustine-thomas}

St Augustine’s answer to his own objection is that the goods the psalm promises are not those the rich man had. “When thou art filled with spiritual riches, canst thou be poor? And was he therefore rich, because he had a bed of ivory; and art thou poor who hast the chamber of thy heart filled with such jewelry of virtues, justice, truth, charity, faith, endurance?” The rich who lack, he says, lack bread, and he names the bread from the Gospel, “I am the Living Bread which came down from Heaven,” and with it the beatitude, “Blessed are they which do hunger and thirst after righteousness: for they shall be filled” (§14). That gloss is his comment on the half-verse about the rich, which the Gradual does not sing; what he says of the seekers’ good, “what manner of good, I have already said,” is his comment on the half-verse it does sing.

St Thomas Aquinas expounds *non est inopia* first of spiritual goods, since “the fear of the Lord is his treasure” (Isa 33:6), then of bodily goods: it may happen that one who fears God has little, but not that he is needy, for *Inops est qui se deficientem reputat*, he is needy who counts himself lacking, and those who fear God are content with what they have (Phil 4:11–12); and he adds that God comes to the help of those who seek him in their need, *Item Deus quaerentibus se subvenit in necessitate*. He then states the objection in Augustine’s name from St Paul: “Even unto this hour we both hunger and thirst and are naked” (1 Cor 4:11). His answer is that *Deus est nutritor et medicus*, God is both nourisher and physician: the physician withholds food from the sick man, because it helps his health, and so God, as it helps our salvation, *quandoque inopiam immittit, quandoque divitias confert*, sometimes sends want and sometimes gives riches (*Super Psalmos* 33, nn. 10–11). For Aquinas, the want of the God-fearing is given for their health.

### The just in the hand of God {#sec:no-want-offertory}

The Offertory carries the question from want to death. St John Chrysostom quotes its first clause, “the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God,” to deny that the souls of the dead wander the earth (*Hom. in Matt.* 28); St Augustine quotes it to explain the reading “My soul is continually in thy hand” (*En. in Ps.* 118, on v. 109); and St Gregory the Great applies the next clause to the just man whose body is bereft of life “amidst the hands of the persecutors” (*Moralia* XII.v.6). Schuster, on this antiphon, states the contrast in terms of hands: “The persecutor believes that the lives of the martyrs are in his hands, but they are in the hands of God” (*The Sacramentary* V, p. 211).

The 1962 antiphon differs from the Vulgate in ways that bear on this reading. It reads *tormentum malitiae*, the torment of malice, where the Vulgate reads *tormentum mortis*, the torment of death — a wording whose history among the Missal printings is given with the appointed texts — and it leaves out the verses in which the departure of the just “was taken for misery.” What the 1962 form says is that the torment of malice will not touch the just.

### The promise in the other texts of the Mass {#sec:no-want-elements}

The Introit begins in joy, before the difficulty is heard: the just are bidden to rejoice in the Lord, and Bellarmine takes the rejoicing to include the praise that is “the special duty of the just” (*Explanatio*, Ps 32, v. 1). The Lesson shows the promise where it is kept: a multitude that no one could number stands before the throne and in sight of the Lamb, clothed in white robes, crying “Salvation to our God.” Those who feared the Lord want nothing there; what the psalm promises and the assembly sings, the vision shows as fact. The Alleluia verse invites those who labour and are burdened, and St John Chrysostom notes that it calls “all that are in anxiety, in sorrows, in sins” (*Hom. in Matt.* 38.3). The Gospel blesses those who hunger and thirst after justice, the verse Augustine cites inside his exposition of the Gradual’s psalm. The Collect asks for the abundance of mercy that is desired, *multiplicatis intercessoribus*, and Durandus gives as the fourth cause of the feast that petitions are more easily granted when all the saints intercede together, *quia impossibile est multitudinem non exaudiri* (*Rationale* VII.34). The Secret asks that the offering be salutary to those who make it, *te miserante*, by God’s mercy. The Communion sings the clean of heart, who shall see God, the peacemakers, who shall be called the children of God, and the persecuted, whose is the kingdom of heaven: the good that is not lacking, named as the vision of God himself, which Bellarmine’s higher sense makes the whole of the promise, since those who cling to the Supreme Good possess all that is good. The Postcommunion asks that the faithful peoples be defended by the saints’ unceasing prayer.

The commemoration’s orations are the Sunday’s. Said in this Mass, its Collect asks to be loosed from the bonds of sin contracted through frailty, its Secret calls what has been received a gift to the undeserving, *immeritis*, and its Postcommunion asks that those who share in divine things be not left subject to human dangers.

### The strongest difficulty: the promise read as comfort {#sec:no-want-difficulty}

The strongest difficulty is the one Augustine voices: the promise read as the assembly hears it seems false. Saints have starved and been killed, and the same Mass that sings *nihil deest* asks, in the Sunday’s prayer, to be freed from the bonds of sins. Augustine says that taken “according to the letter” the verse “seemeth to deceive thee,” and he turns the promise to the goods within; Basil says that whoever takes bodily goods for the psalm’s good must accuse the apostles or Scripture. Two of the witnesses do keep a temporal sense, each in a bounded form: Bellarmine gives the words “an humbler sense,” for the simple, in which God supplies his servants with the temporal things of the world and does not desert them in necessity, and Aquinas says that God comes to the help of those who seek him in their need. Neither makes temporal provision the promise’s meaning: Bellarmine sets his humbler sense beside the higher one, in which the just who appear to be in want “really are not so,” and Aquinas answers the hungering apostles with the physician who withholds food for the sick man’s health.

Their answers differ in what they say is promised: God himself (Basil, Bellarmine), the virtues (Augustine), and the want that serves salvation (Aquinas). The Gradual’s omission of *Divites eguerunt* does not remove the difficulty, because the singers still know saints who were poor. It does mean that what the Mass sings is the promise alone, and what the Fathers say of the rich is said about a half-verse the Mass leaves out.

Literal.

: A psalm of David bids the Lord’s saints fear him, because those who fear him lack nothing and those who seek him lack no good; the Book of Wisdom answers the wicked who put the just man to death, saying that the souls of the just are in the hand of God, that they seemed to die, and that they are in peace.

Allegorical.

: The rich who lacked may be Israel, which killed the bread of life and suffered a famine of the word, while the nations who sought the Lord lack no good (Basil); the just in God’s hand are the martyrs, whose lives were not in the persecutor’s hands (Schuster).

Moral.

: Filial fear, which fears to offend the one it loves, orders the life and keeps it from sin, and he who has it lacks nothing for any virtue (Basil, Bellarmine); want is borne with patience, and God sends it, as a physician withholds food, for the soul’s health (Bellarmine, Aquinas).

Anagogical.

: The perfect good is God himself, and those who seek him are not deprived of the goods laid up in eternal rest (Basil); the just are in the hand of God and in peace.

## Where the Three Interpretations Agree and Differ {#sec:comparison}

The three interpretations take up the three questions set out at the opening.

|  | **One Church, sealed and uncounted** | **The way of the Beatitudes** | **No want to them that fear him** |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Question | Who are the sealed, counted at 144,000 and then shown as a multitude no one could number? | Why does the feast read the Beatitudes, which bless the poor and the persecuted, and how are their present and future promises to be read? | How can the Gradual promise that nothing is lacking to those who fear the Lord, and the Offertory say the just only seemed to die? |
| Where its question presses hardest | the Lesson’s counted number and uncounted multitude | the Gospel’s blessings, three of them sung again at the Communion | the Gradual’s promise and the Offertory’s “seemed to die” |
| Answer | The number signifies the whole Church, sealed with the cross; the multitude is the victors of the last persecution or the Gentiles added to Israel | The beatitudes are one way, from humility and fear to persecution for justice, whose one reward is begun on earth and completed in heaven | What the God-fearing are promised is God himself and the virtues; want, when it comes, is sent for their health; the just dead are in God’s hand |
| Principal authors | Bede, with Augustine on the Lesson and the psalms | Augustine, Chrysostom; Aquinas, Hilary | Basil, Bellarmine; Augustine, Aquinas |
| What it asks of the faithful | To confess the faith openly and rejoice in the Lord, not in themselves | To begin in humility and the fear of God and be ready to suffer for justice | Filial fear, and patience in want |
| Strongest difficulty | The exact number and Israel’s tribes, read as a limit | The tenses: two promises present, six future | The promise read as temporal comfort, which saints’ lives contradict |
| Where its witnesses differ | Bede and Augustine read the number as the whole Church, Victorinus as the believers of the last times at Elias’s preaching; Bede, Augustine and Victorinus read the crowd as the elect out of all believers, Cyprian as the innumerable martyrs; Bede reads the angel from the east as the Lord incarnate, Victorinus as Elias; in the reception, Fromage reads the sealing as a second, definitive census, and Schuster takes the 144,000 as sons of Abraham with others added | Augustine’s ascent of knowledge and gifts against Chrysostom’s chain of dispositions; Hilary’s land as the Lord’s body against Chrysostom’s sensible prize | Basil reads the rich as Israel, Bellarmine as the wicked rich and the covetous; a temporal sense is kept by Bellarmine, as the humbler meaning, and by Aquinas, as God’s help in necessity |

All three rest on the fear of the Lord and on humility, though they come to it from different texts. In the first, the just rejoice “not in yourselves” but in the Lord, and the seal is set on those who confess. In the second, the poor in spirit are the humble and God-fearing, and Augustine’s way begins with the gift of fear. In the third, the Gradual’s *Timete Dominum* is the subject itself: for Basil the one who fears lacks nothing for any virtue, and the perfect good is God himself; for Bellarmine the filial fear of the perfect feels no want, because the friend of God possesses all. In each interpretation holiness begins in what God gives: the seal, the humility of the first beatitude, the fear the Gradual commands.

They attend to different times. The first looks at the saints already before the throne and reads back from there to the sealing on earth. The second follows the way the saints walked, from its first step to its last. The third stands where the assembly stands, with the promise still being tested by hunger and death. Bede’s remark that the vision of the white-robed multitude “may also be understood of the present time, when ‘we are saved in hope’ ” and Aquinas’s distinction between the rewards begun on the way and completed in the homeland, give the first two a bridge to the third’s present.

The three meet in the same texts and read them differently. The first and the third both read the Lesson and the Offertory. For the first, the multitude with palms is the Church that has overcome, and the Offertory’s just are the sealed who have passed through death. For the third, the Offertory answers the scandal of the just man’s death, and the Lesson’s crowd before the throne is the Gradual’s promise shown as kept. The second reads the same crowd as the way’s end, its robes baptism and its palms the cross’s triumph. The second and the third share the Gospel’s fourth beatitude, the hunger and thirst after justice, which Augustine cites inside his exposition of the Gradual’s psalm; for the second it is a step on the way, for the third it names the want that is blessed.

Each question is worked out where its text presses hardest. The first explains the Lesson’s number and its list of tribes with Bede’s reasons: the seed of Abraham by faith, the arithmetic of twelve and ten, and the tribes in their strange order read as the Church’s powers. The second explains why the Gospel is the Beatitudes and how its present and future promises stand. The third meets the objection that the Gradual’s promise is false, and says what the Mass sings of Psalm 33 and what it leaves out. The witnesses still disagree on the number, the crowd and the angel, on the census, on the land of the meek and on the rich who lack.

### Psalms 32 (33) and 33 (34): the Introit verse and the Gradual {#sec:date-psalms}

The two psalms share the first and third rows of the table. Neither is dated by an occasion of its own. Psalm 32 has no title in the Hebrew; the Clementine Vulgate and the Douay print “A psalm for David” inside its first verse, and the Missal’s verse begins after it. Psalm 33 is titled “For David, when he changed his countenance before Achimelech, who dismissed him, and he went his way”; the title answers to 1 Kings 21:10–15, where David, fleeing Saul, comes to Achis, king of Geth, and feigns madness before him. The attribution to David is the titles’ own; the table marks it disputed and gives David’s reign in the usual chronology from the *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s article “David” (Corbett, vol. 4). No reign is used to date either psalm.

For Psalm 32 the chronology record has no historical setting, and the second row says so. For Psalm 33 the table dates the episode the title names. The figure is the year of the world that Haydock’s Douay commentary gives at 1 Kings 21, reporting Ussher’s reckoning; it is Ussher’s date for the flight to Achis, not an independent dating of the psalm. The title calls the king Achimelech, while 1 Kings 21 calls him Achis; the episode is the same, and the table follows the book of Kings for the place.

The composition date is the bound that the introduction to the Psalms in the *New American Bible, Revised Edition* sets for the whole Psalter, in the same sentence in which it holds that no individual psalm can be dated securely. It dates the book, not these psalms.

### Wisdom 3:1–3: the Offertory {#sec:date-wisdom}

The Book of Wisdom narrates no dated event in these verses; chapter 3 answers the speech of the wicked in chapter 2. The two composition dates are those that Francis Gigot’s “Book of Wisdom” in the *Catholic Encyclopedia* (vol. 15) records for the dispute over the book’s date: in or after the time of Ptolemy IV Philopator, and in or after the time of Ptolemy VII Physicon. Both are marked disputed, and the table gives neither as preferred. The dates are dates of the book, and the antiphon is an adaptation of its verses, not the verses as the Vulgate prints them.

### Matthew 5:1–12a, 5:8–10 and 11:28: the Gospel, the Communion and the Alleluia {#sec:date-matthew}

The Sermon on the Mount is not dated in the chronology record, and the row says so. Matthew places it on “a mountain” that he does not name, after Jesus has gone about all Galilee and the multitudes have followed him from Galilee, Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea and beyond the Jordan (4:23–25). The Alleluia verse and the Communion are drawn from the same Gospel and share its composition row. The six composition dates are those of Eugène Jacquier’s “Gospel of St. Matthew” (vol. 10) and Alfred Durand’s “The New Testament” (vol. 14) in the *Catholic Encyclopedia*; all are marked disputed, and the table keeps the range from the early to the later dates rather than choosing one. They are dates of the book, not of the sermon.

### Apocalypse 7:2–12: the Lesson {#sec:date-apocalypse}

The two composition dates are those of Christian Van den Biesen’s “Apocalypse” (vol. 1), which places the book in the reign of Domitian, and of Alfred Durand’s “The New Testament” (vol. 14), which gives an approximate decade; both are marked disputed. John writes that he was “in the island which is called Patmos” when he saw the visions (1:9). The sealing and the multitude of chapter 7 are a vision and have no date of their own.

## Appendix: Scope and Qualifications {#sec:scope}

**Edition, formulary and occurrence.** The study treats the Mass *In festo omnium Sanctorum*, *Die 1 novembris*, I classis, of the *Missale Romanum*, editio typica, Typis Polyglottis Vaticanis 1962, printed pp. 702–703, nos. 3974–3983, as said on Sunday 1 November 2026 in the universal 1962 calendar and no particular calendar, with the commemoration of the Twenty-third Sunday after Pentecost, pp. 416–417, nos. 1720, 1726 and 1728. The precedence of the feast over the Sunday, the commemoration and its order, the colour, the Gloria, the Credo and the last Gospel rest on the Missal’s own general rubrics (RG 14, 16, 91, 108, 109 a, 111 a, 120 d; RGMR 431, 434 a, 436–437, 475, 480–481, 505, 509–510), as `research/context.md` records. The Preface is not settled: the feast has none of its own, and the general rubrics (RGMR 482, 483, 494 b, 498) can be read for the Preface of the Most Holy Trinity or for the common Preface. No other rubrical source searched for the question, as `research/scope.md` lists them, settles it. The study names both, asserts neither, gives neither text, and builds nothing on either. The *Asperges*, the Ordinary and the Office of the feast are outside the formulary and are not treated.

**Text control.** Every Latin form was read on page images of the CMAA facsimile of the 1962 typical edition and collated word for word with the Tours printing of 1922 (Mame) for the feast’s ten texts and with the Ratisbon printing of 1862 (Pustet) for the Sunday’s three orations; the collation and the comparison with the Clementine Vulgate are in `propers/verified.md`. The rubrics of the 1962 book are described and not reproduced. The history of the Offertory’s wording rests on the 1570 text layer read at the feast and at two Masses of martyrs, and on the text layers of the 1604, 1862 and 1889 printings; when its *alleluia* entered is not established.

**English.** The scriptural English is the Douay–Rheims in Challoner’s revision at the canonical verses, with the Missal’s departures named beside each text. The English of the Introit antiphon and of the seven orations is the Lasance *New Roman Missal* of 1945, whose rubrics are those before 1960. The Lasance English of the Lesson’s and the Gospel’s liturgical openings and of the Offertory is quoted on the Lasance’s attribution only; it has no row in the repository’s register of translations. None of this English is an approved liturgical translation of the 1962 Missal. Patristic and later witnesses are quoted in the nineteenth-century English of the editions named in the References, or in their Latin or French, and otherwise described; no English is composed for a liturgical text.

**Reception.** The interpretations rest on the loci named in the text and recorded, with editions and bounds, in `research/scope.md` and `research/interpretations.md`. The sweep is bounded by the corpora named there and is not complete; St Jerome on Matthew, St Bernard’s sermons for the feast, St Gregory of Nyssa on the Beatitudes and the Greek commentators on the psalms beyond Basil were not read. No commentary on the Lesson’s chapter by St Augustine was used, and he is not presented as interpreting it; his three loci on the Lesson’s number and crowd (*De doctrina christiana* III.35.51, *Letter XCIII* §30, *Sermon LXI* 1) are short applications inside other arguments. Victorinus of Pettau’s commentary on the chapter is quoted from the Ante-Nicene Fathers text under its editors’ recension caution — corrupted and interpolated manuscripts, a possible later corrector, an uncertain author — and St Cyprian’s martyrs-only reading of the crowd is kept as the reading Bede rejects; Victorinus and Cyprian have no rows in the author-standing register, and neither carries an interpretation. Blessed Ildefonso Schuster’s *The Sacramentary* V (English, 1930), Lucien Fromage’s continuation of Guéranger’s *L’Année liturgique* (vol. 15, 1909) and Durandus’s *Rationale* are the commentaries read on this Mass as a Mass; Fromage and Durandus are ecclesiastical writers and carry no interpretation. Rabanus Maurus is cited as a compiler of Augustine. Every connection drawn between texts of the Mass belongs to the study’s interpretations unless a witness is named as making it, and the orations of the commemorated Sunday are read only for what they say where they stand in this Mass. Bellarmine is read in O’Sullivan’s English, which numbers Psalm 33 one verse lower than the Vulgate; Basil in the Latin version printed in PG 29; Aquinas in the Venice (1745) and Parma (1863) printings, on their text layers.
