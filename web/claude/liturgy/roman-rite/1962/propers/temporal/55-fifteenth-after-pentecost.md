### The four senses

| **Sense** | **Synthesis** |
|:-----------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| Literal | A psalm asks God to bend down and hear one poor man crying all day long <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>. Paul instructs the spiritual how to correct a brother, sets a burden to be shared against one that cannot be, and closes on sowing and reaping <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>. At the gate of Naim Christ meets a funeral, is moved with mercy, touches the bier and gives a dead son back alive to his widowed mother <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>. |
| Allegorical | **Ambrose reads the widow as holy Church**, recalling the younger people from the pomp of the funeral by the tears she is forbidden to weep, *quae flere prohibetur eum cui resurrectio debeatur*; and the bier is wood, so that the dead man *spem resurgendi habebat, quia ferebatur in ligno* <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>. **Bede makes the widowed mother the Church**, *licet e multis collecta personis, una sit perfecta et immaculata virgo*, her motherhood that of Gal. 4:19, and each of the faithful her only son; the gate is one of the bodily senses. |
| Moral | **Augustine reads the three raisings as three grades of sin** <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>: the girl within the house is sin consented to in the heart, the young man carried out of the gate is sin gone out into the deed, and Lazarus under the stone is habit, *vis dura consuetudinis*. Jerome and Augustine make Gal. 6:1 a discipline of the corrector before a remedy for the corrected; Chrysostom makes it an economy of complementary weaknesses inside one body <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>. |
| Anagogical | **Cyril of Alexandria makes the raisings a pledge**, the three raised standing as “a pledge of the hope prepared for us of a resurrection of the dead” <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>, and the flesh that touched the bier “the body of life” <span class="nodecor">(*Comm.*)</span>. The lesson reaches as far: *de spíritu metet vitam ætérnam*, at a harvest *témpore … suo* <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>. |

## The Propers: Themes and Movement {#triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

The three prayers of this Mass deny the Church and the communicant any standing of their own, twice over: she cannot stand safe without God, and what must go before in us is not our own sense but the working of a heavenly gift. The Secret between them asks only that the sacraments guard and defend, so that not one of the three credits the assembly with anything of its own. Everything the day sings and reads is the motion that answers that denial — mercy moving first in four grammars, a harvest whose seed was given, a dead man handed back alive at a town gate, and flesh given at the end of it for the life of the world.

### 1. Mercy moves first, and the formulary says so four times in four grammars

The word is a petition at the Introit, *miserére mihi, Dómine, quóniam ad te clamávi tota die*; a divine attribute at the Collect, *miserátio continuáta mundet et múniat*; the thing to be proclaimed at the Gradual, *ad annuntiándum mane misericórdiam tuam*; and at the Gospel the motion in Christ out of which the miracle comes, *misericórdia motus super eam*.

**At the Gospel the initiative is Christ’s, and Cyril says so.** Cyril of Alexandria sets Naim against Capharnaum: there Christ was present by invitation, here nobody summons him and he comes to raise the dead man on his own motion, as the Gospel subsection sets out in Cyril’s own words (Payne Smith, vol. 1, pp. 132–133). The widow of the pericope asks nothing, says nothing, and is credited with no faith; everything that happens at the gate begins on the other side. What the scene draws from those who watch it is a verdict about God and not about the young man, *Deus visitávit plebem suam*, and the appointed text leaves that sentence with them and not with the Evangelist.

**The first and last orations of the Mass say the same thing negatively, and their grammar says it too.** The Collect’s semicolon puts the Church’s incapacity, *quia sine te non potest salva consístere*, ahead of the petition it grounds; the Postcommunion makes the same denial of the communicant in the first person plural, *ut non noster sensus in nobis, sed iúgiter eius prævéniat efféctus*, and puts its own subject last of all, both grammars being set out at the Collect and Postcommunion subsections. Augustine, on the Introit’s own psalm, makes the incapacity the ground on which the petition is heard — the ear bends to the neck that is not stiffened — in the sentence the Introit subsection prints (*En. Ps.* 85.2). *Misericordia* and *miseratio* are different nouns, so what runs through the four elements is one sense and not one word, and *praeveniat* is a plain verb of precedence. The Gradual pairs the mercy with truth, *misericórdiam tuam et veritátem tuam*, the fixed collocation whose psalms the Gradual subsection counts through the psalter. Four grammars, and in none of them does the motion begin on the assembly’s side.

### 2. The lesson runs an economy, and its currency is itself given

The Epistle is unembarrassedly commercial — *Opus autem suum probet unusquísque*, *Quæ enim semináverit homo, hæc et metet*, *témpore enim suo metémus* — while the orations allow the Church nothing of her own. The tradition’s resolution is not that the economy is figurative but that its currency is a gift.

Augustine, expounding the Gradual’s own psalm, draws the consequence for any progress at all: *quia munera ipsius sunt, non merita tua* (*En. Ps.* 91.3); the Collect asks that the Church be governed *tuo semper múnere*. **Aquinas finds the same doctrine in the lesson’s own grammar**, reading the asymmetry of the possessives at Gal. 6:8: *cum agit de seminatione carnis, dicit in carne sua … sed cum loquitur de semine spiritus, non dicit suo, quia spiritus non est nobis a nobis, sed a Deo* (*Super Galatas* cap. 6 lect. 2) — and the asymmetry is in the appointed Latin, *qui séminat in carne sua* against *qui autem séminat in spíritu*, a possessive at the flesh and none at the spirit. At 6:5 he makes even the sheaves measured grace, the burden each man carries to judgement being *mensuram gratiae suae … tamquam manipulos bonorum operum*. *Munere* in the Collect and *munera* in Augustine do similar work, so the lesson’s commerce and the orations’ denial are one doctrine and not two: what the day tells the assembly it will reap was first given it to sow.

### 3. Flesh sown, flesh carried out, flesh given

*Caro* turns twice inside one Mass. The Epistle makes flesh the ground that yields corruption, *de carne et metet corruptiónem*; the Gospel carries a corpse out through a gate; the Communion gives flesh as the world’s life, *caro mea est pro sǽculi vita*. The lesson carries its burdens with the bier-bearers’ own verb, *ónera portáte* at 6:2 and *onus suum portábit* at 6:5 standing in the same Mass as *hi autem, qui portábant* at the gate of Naim. And the lesson’s other half reaps out of the spirit what the flesh cannot yield — *qui autem séminat in spíritu, de spíritu metet vitam ætérnam* — so the turn the noun makes across the three elements is already answered inside the element that begins it.

**One Father turns the noun in both places, and he does it from the Gospel side.** Asked why Christ touched the bier when a word would have sufficed, Cyril answers that the touch is itself the teaching: what the flesh of the Word touches it makes alive, because that flesh is the Word’s own, and the Gospel subsection prints his sentence (Payne Smith, vol. 1, p. 135). Aquinas draws the same inference at the Communion’s verse, naming John Damascene for it and closing on Christ’s healing of the sick by his own touch (*Super Ioannem* cap. 6 lect. 6), so the bier and the bread stand side by side on a checked witness’s own sentence; Augustine supplies the ecclesial half at the same verse, where none but the body of Christ lives by Christ’s Spirit (*Tract. in Io.* 26.13), so that the life the antiphon offers is had by being made a member of the one whose flesh it sings. Cyril’s Luke commentary reaches this guide through an 1859 English of an ancient Syriac version, and Augustine’s and Aquinas’s lemmata at that verse read *dabo* and *pro mundi vita*, so their expositions belong to the verse and not to the antiphon as it is sung.

### 4. One voice through every change of person

The formulary changes person constantly: first singular at the Introit and the Offertory, first and second plural in the Epistle and its second singular at *consíderans teípsum* in Gal. 6:1, no speaking person in the Gradual or the Alleluia, Christ’s own singular at the Communion, first plural in the Secret and the Postcommunion. **Augustine expounds both of this Mass’s psalm-antiphons on a single principle.** At the Introit’s psalm the one Christ is head and members together, and the Introit subsection prints his sentence (*En. Ps.* 85.1); at the Offertory’s psalm he settles the question of its speaker on the same principle, and the Offertory subsection carries his answer (*En. Ps.* 39.5). Cassiodorus expounds both psalms on the same principle in his own words, so two Latin commentators state the reading: at Ps. 85 the speaker is Christ in the form of a servant and at Ps. 39 the Church he speaks in, each subsection printing his Latin (PL 70). Head at the one antiphon and body at the other, and the Mass sings both of them in the first person singular.

**The orations move with it.** Their objects narrow from *Ecclésiam tuam* to *nos* to *mentes nostras et córpora*, while their verbs go from cleansing and fortifying to guarding and defending to possessing and going before; so the singular of the chants and the plural of the prayers are not two speakers alternating but one body addressed at closer range as the rite proceeds. Objects narrowing across a Mass is a common euchological shape, and this formulary is one instance of it rather than its inventor. And at five places in this Mass’s two psalm-antiphons Augustine’s lemma is not the chant’s, three at Ps. 85 and two at Ps. 39, so there what he expounds is the psalm and not the sung text; Cassiodorus’s lemma departs from them at *miserere mei* alone.

### 5. The day’s subject is an interval to be endured

The vocabulary of duration is unusually dense — *tota die* (Int.), *mane … per noctem* (Grad.), *exspéctans exspectávi* (Off.), *témpore enim suo metémus* and *dum tempus habémus* (Ep.), and the stock euchological *continuáta*, *semper* and *iúgiter* in the three orations, which carry little on their own. **Every load-bearing one of the scriptural markers has a reading behind it that stretches it to a lifetime.** Augustine reads *tota die* as the whole age in which the body of Christ groans under pressure, running to the end of the world, and Cassiodorus as the span of one life, both at the Introit subsection (*En. Ps.* 85.5; PL 70). At the Gradual’s versicle Augustine divides the day between the time when it goes well with us and the time of tribulation, giving the psalm’s mercy to the one and its truth to the other (*En. Ps.* 91.4); Cassiodorus on the same versicle takes the two words for the whole of a life, *Mirabiliter vita nostra duobus est sermonibus conclusa*. Ambrose on the Offertory’s doubled verb makes perfection not the waiting but the having waited (*In Ps. XXXIX* §1), on the strength of Matt. 10:22, the verse Jerome and Augustine likewise reach for where the lesson says *non deficiámus*; and Aquinas glosses *dum tempus habémus* as this life, which is the time of sowing. Chrysostom, on the same verse, makes the interval the measure of mercy itself: “As it is not always in our power to sow, so neither is it to show mercy.”

**The one element that could have said *today* is the one whose today is left unsung.** The Alleluia sings Ps. 94 from inside the summons to worship and leaves unsung the oracle of warning in which the psalm’s own *hodie* stands, as the Alleluia subsection of the commentary sets out. What this Mass sings is the waiting; the *hodie* that would cut the waiting short is the half the chant does not reach.

## The Appointed Formulary in Full

Everything the 1962 *Missale Romanum* prints for this Mass, in the order it prints it: the Latin of the Vatican *editio typica* exactly as the controlling facsimile has it, each element under the printed heading, scriptural reference and marginal number the book gives it, and beneath it the English of an identified public-domain witness.

**Formulary.** *DOMINICA DECIMA QUINTA post Pentecosten*, *II classis*, printed pp. 396–397, marginal nos. 1582–1591. It opens in the lower half of the left column of p. 396, below the Postcommunion of the Fourteenth Sunday (no. 1581), and ends at the foot of the left column of p. 397, the right column of that page already carrying the Sixteenth Sunday. It appoints no Tract, Sequence, blessing or ritual text, and no second or third Collect, Secret or Postcommunion.

**The English below.** Scriptural elements are the Douay–Rheims as revised by Bishop Challoner — the English of the Clementine Vulgate the missal prints — in the Project Gutenberg e-text of that revision, with the psalm loci resolved in the missal’s own Vulgate numbering. The three orations are the anonymous English of *The Roman Missal translated into the English language for the use of the laity*, first revised edition (Philadelphia: Eugene Cummiskey, 1861), at the three lines the tracked transcription of that book’s temporal orations carries for this formulary, physical lines 155–157, reproduced with its own nineteenth-century spelling and its abbreviated conclusion “Thro’.”

### 1. Introit <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span> {#proper-introit}

> ***Ant. ad Introitum* — Ps. 85, 1 et 2-3 — marginal no. 1582**
>
> *Inclína, Dómine, aurem tuam ad me, et exáudi me: salvum fac servum tuum, Deus meus, sperántem in te: miserére mihi, Dómine, quóniam ad te clamávi tota die. <span class="nodecor">Ps. ibid., 4</span> Lætífica ánimam servi tui: quia ad te, Dómine, ánimam meam levávi. ℣. Glória Patri.*

> **English: Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Ps. 85:1–4 printed whole, with the portions the chant sings in bold**
>
> **v. 1** A prayer for David himself. **Incline thy ear, O Lord, and hear me:** for I am needy and poor. **v. 2** Preserve my soul, for I am holy: **save thy servant, O my God, that trusteth in thee.** **v. 3** **Have mercy on me, O Lord, for I have cried to thee all the day.** *Ps.* **v. 4** **Give joy to the soul of thy servant, for to thee, O Lord, I have lifted up my soul.** ℣. *Glória Patri.*

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** The antiphon is a three-part cento, not a quotation: v. 1a as far as *exáudi me*, then v. 2b from *salvum fac*, then v. 3 entire, with the psalm’s own inscription and the halves of vv. 1 and 2 that mention need, poverty and holiness all unsung. Two of its words are not the Clementine’s. *Ad me* is added at v. 1 and the Douay’s “Incline thy ear, O Lord, and hear me” has no counterpart for it; *miserére mihi* stands at v. 3 where the Bible text reads *miserere mei*, and the Douay’s “Have mercy on me” happens to answer both. The doxology cue *Glória Patri* belongs to the Ordinary and not to the proper.

### 2. Collect <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span> {#proper-collect}

> ***Oratio* — marginal no. 1583**
>
> *Ecclésiam tuam, Dómine, miserátio continuáta mundet et múniat: et quia sine te non potest salva consístere; tuo semper múnere gubernétur. Per Dóminum.*

> **English: Cummiskey (Philadelphia, 1861), Collect of this formulary, printed p. 428**
>
> May thy continual mercy purify and defend thy church: and since without thee it cannot be safe, may it always be directed by the influence of thy grace. Thro’.

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** *Tuo semper múnere* becomes “by the influence of thy grace,” so the noun *munus* — the gift the Church is governed by, and the word Augustine uses at the Gradual’s psalm to deny that any progress is a merit — does not survive into the English, and *semper* is carried by the adverb of the verb rather than of the gift. The semicolon after *consístere* is the prayer’s hinge; the uncorrected optical layer of the Benziger 1962 shows it too, and those of the Pustet 1862, the Venice 1570 and the Vatican *typica* of 1604 show a comma there.

### 3. Epistle <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span> {#proper-epistle}

> ***Léctio Epístolæ beáti Pauli Apóstoli ad Gálatas.* — Gal. 5, 25-26; 6, 1-10 — marginal no. 1584**
>
> *Fratres: Si spíritu vívimus, spíritu et ambulémus. Non efficiámur inánis glóriæ cúpidi, ínvicem provocántes, ínvicem invidéntes. Fratres, et si præoccupátus fúerit homo in áliquo delícto, vos, qui spirituáles estis, huiúsmodi instrúite in spíritu lenitátis, consíderans teípsum, ne et tu tentéris. Alter altérius ónera portáte, et sic adimplébitis legem Christi. Nam si quis exístimat se áliquid esse, cum nihil sit, ipse se sedúcit. Opus autem suum probet unusquísque, et sic in semetípso tantum glóriam habébit, et non in áltero. Unusquísque enim onus suum portábit. Commúnicet autem is qui catechizátur verbo, ei qui se catechízat, in ómnibus bonis. Nolíte erráre: Deus non irridétur. Quæ enim semináverit homo, hæc et metet. Quóniam qui séminat in carne sua, de carne et metet corruptiónem: qui autem séminat in spíritu, de spíritu metet vitam ætérnam. The two burdens of 6:2 and 6:5 are one noun in Latin and in English, *ónera* and *onus*, where Paul writes two different Greek words. The sowing clause is numbered differently in the two languages: what the Clementine and the Douay print at the head of v. 8 stands at the end of Greek v. 7.

## The Propers: Detailed Commentary

### The typical edition against the Benziger printing

The Vatican *editio typica* controls every word printed above. Against the Benziger *editio iuxta typicam* of the same year, whose text reaches this guide only in an uncorrected optical layer, and allowing for that layer’s accent substitutions, the two agree, apart from the differences enumerated next, through the Introit, the Collect, the body of the Epistle, the Alleluia, the Offertory, the body of the Secret and the Communion — including *ad me* and *miserére mihi*, the semicolon after *consístere*, *super omnem terram*, *respéxit me*, *hymnum*, *dédero* and *sǽculi*.

**Where they differ, one difference is a word and the rest are pointing, abbreviation and cue length.** At Lk. 7:14 the typical edition prints *Aduléscens* with *u* and Benziger *Adolescens* with *o*; the Clementine reads the *o*, and so do the uncorrected optical layers of the Venice 1570 and the 1604 *typica*, so the *u* is the 1962 typical edition’s own form. At the same verse the stop stands outside the closing parenthesis here, *stetérunt).*, and inside it there, where the Clementine agrees with Benziger. Benziger adds two commas at Gal. 6:6 and one after *Mentes nostras*, prints the antiphon-repetition cue *Inclína* after the Introit’s *Glória Patri.*, and abbreviates both the Secret’s and the Postcommunion’s conclusions to *Per Dóminum.* where the typical edition writes them out to *in unitáte.* — the reverse of the Fourteenth Sunday’s direction, where it is Benziger that prints the longer cue.

#### Where the appointed text is not the Bible’s text

The table prints departures at six elements. Nine of them are word-level: *ad me* and *miserére mihi* at the Introit, with *quia* for *quoniam* in its psalm verse; *super omnem terram* at the Alleluia; *respéxit me*, *deprecatiónem meam* and *hymnum* at the Offertory; *dédero* and *sǽculi* at the Communion. The eight outside the psalm verse stand also in the uncorrected optical layers of the Pustet Ratisbon 1862, the Venice 1570 and the Vatican *typica* of 1604, so none of the eight is a 1962 act. The rest of the table is matter of another kind — four extents cut, the lesson’s prefixed address, the Gospel’s substituted incipit, capitalisation, and the stop that moves outside the parenthesis at Lk. 7:14 — with one word form that runs the other way: the uncorrected optical layers of the Venice 1570 and the 1604 *typica* show *Adolescens* with the Clementine, and the *u* of *Aduléscens* is the 1962 typical edition’s own.

Green is not printed at this Mass: it follows RG 127 b of the 1960 *Rubricae generales*, whose September Ember exception is a rule about *feriæ* and does not reach a Sunday. That the formulary prints one Collect, one Secret and one Postcommunion follows inside the 1960 code from RG 434 b with RG 435; the instrument that abolished the seasonal second and third orations themselves is not the *Rubricae generales* but *Variationes in Breviario et Missali Romano* cap. IV n. 18, *Orationes pro diversitate Temporum abolentur*, at *AAS* 52 (1960) printed p. 709. The same promulgation carries a *Rubricae generales* n. 18 at printed p. 599, on transferring the impeded Sundays after the Epiphany, so the bare numeral names two different instruments.

### How this Mass came to be this Sunday’s

#### The three orations descend as a unit, and their wording has moved twice

All three composed orations stand together, as one Mass and in liturgical order, in the Gregorian Hadrianum as Wilson prints it: section XXXIIII, heading *DOMINICA .XVI. POST PENTECOSTEN.*, printed p. 174, with the manuscript folio mark *\[fo. 168v\]*. The same three stand in the Old Gelasian, Vat. Reg. lat. 316, at Wilson’s Book III sect. XI, printed p. 230, under the heading *Item alia Missa* and with no Sunday number at all. **Between the eighth-century books and 1962 the wording moves in two places, and the two ancient books do not move together**: both of them read *contra diabolicos tueantur semper incursus* where the 1962 Secret reads *contra diabólicos semper tueántur incúrsus*, and at the Postcommunion the Old Gelasian sets the vocative differently, *possideat, Domine, quaesumus*, where the Hadrianum and 1962 read *possídeat, quǽsumus, Dómine*. So the Hadrianum stands apart from 1962 at one place and the Old Gelasian at two, and the two ancient witnesses differ from each other at the second.

Two further variants are transmitted at the Postcommunion. Wilson’s apparatus records Pamelius reading *iugiter aevi praeveniat* and the S(2) and Gerbert witnesses *iugiter eius proveniat* against the *iugiter eius praeveniat* of the received text, and notes that the Vatican manuscript’s *sunt* for *sed* is a scribal error corrected by Tommasi.

#### The Sunday number, and witnesses that disagree by one

Five further sacramentary witnesses number this Mass, beyond the Hadrianum’s *DOMINICA .XVI.* above, and they do not agree. Wilson’s apparatus at Gelasian III.XI records that “This Missa is assigned by R. S. Gerb. to the seventeenth Sunday after Pentecost: see the Missa for the sixteenth Sunday in Pam., and that for the seventeenth Sunday in Men.,” with the marginal citations *R. S. / Gerb. 175. / Pam. 411. / Men. 179.* — **so Wilson reports Rheinau, S. Gallen, Gerbert and Menard at two Sundays later than 1962 and Pamelius at one, where the Hadrianum’s heading already stands.** The page image settles what the optical layers lose: the second Collect of the Gelasian Mass, *Da, quaesumus, Domine, hanc mentem populo tuo*, carries only *R. S. / Gerb. 175.* in its margin, so Pamelius and Menard do not have it, and the three prayers all five of them carry are exactly the three the 1962 formulary keeps. The same one-Sunday split reproduces at the adjoining section, so the offset is a numeration base and not a local accident.

The Liber Comitis heads the Mass *Dominica mensis vi* at PL 30 cols. 521–522, its count from the feast of St Lawrence having stopped one Sunday earlier, and Schuster calls this one *Quinta post Sancti Laurentii*, the last of the Lawrence Sundays; the two reckonings differ by one. What Migne prints there under Jerome’s name — its date, its provenance, its textual authority, and whether the *mensis vi* and *mensis viii* headings with no *mensis vii* between them are the book’s own or Migne’s arrangement — is unsettled, so the Epistle-and-Gospel pairing here is what the Liber Comitis prints.

#### Honorius’s Fifteenth Sunday, and the chants that did not move

Honorius Augustodunensis, writing c. 1120–1130, has this Sunday’s Collect where 1962 has it: at *Gemma animae* IV cap. LXXVIII, *Dominica decima quinta, \<Inclina:\> sub lege*, PL 172 cols. 719–720, he writes *cui Oratio, Ecclesiam tuam, Domine, concordat, quam ut Deus mundet, muniat, rogat*, and repeats the prayer at cap. LXXIX. His Sixteenth offers *Absolve, Domine, tuorum vincula populorum, vel in Oratione, Tua nos, Domine*, so this Secret’s incipit stands there as an alternative oration. **His Alleluias are not this one, and he says so himself**: *Alleluia, Domine, exaudi … Alii cantant Alleluia, Confitemini Domino … Alii cantant Alleluia, Paratum cor meum*. The Alleluia 1962 sings here stands at his Fourteenth. Honorius’s reading of the whole Mass off the September historia of King Josiah is his own scheme applied to a twelfth-century German use.

In the margins of Ottobonianus 313 the chant series and the oration series run two Sundays apart across five consecutive sections, uniformly. The four-element Mass standing under this Sunday’s Introit in that margin — Introit *Inclina domine aurem*, respond *Bonum est confidere*, Offertory *Expectans expectaui*, Communion *Qui manducat* — agrees with Honorius at three slots of four, and is not the 1962 four. Whether that margin’s *Bonum est confidere* under this Introit is a respond shared across two consecutive Sundays or a copying error is unsettled; the doubling on Wilson’s p. 173 is real, his own index printing “173 (bis).” Delisle assigns the manuscript to the second half of the ninth century and Ebner follows him, though Ebner’s later judgment favoured an earlier date; Wilson reports both and adopts neither.

The gregorien.info index of the six earliest Mass antiphonaries Hesbert collected already places this Introit, Gradual and Offertory at *Dominica XV post Pentecosten*, AMS 187, but gives that Mass the Communion *Qui manducat carnem meam* and no fixed Alleluia at all; *Panis quem ego dedero* stands there at the Fourteenth Sunday. **So against that record the move runs one way: the Communion this Mass sings stands at the Fourteenth Sunday in AMS, and the Communion AMS gives the Fifteenth has not taken its place at the 1962 Fourteenth.** Within the tracked registry that Sunday sings *Primum quaerite* at Mt. 6:33, while the 1962 book’s own *Qui manducat meam carnem*, Jn. 6:57, stands at the Ninth Sunday. Every antiphonary figure here is the gregorien.info database’s report of Hesbert’s printed pages.

Guéranger’s 1909 volume shows the formulary before the 1960 change, printing the three seasonal orations by cross-reference — “The other Collects, as on page 120” at printed p. 345, “The other Secrets, as on page 130” and “The other Postcommunions, as on page 131” at p. 355 — and names the day “the Sunday of the widow of Naim.” He prints at p. 356, under Vespers, the Magnificat antiphon *Propheta magnus surrexit in nobis, et quia Deus visitavit plebem suam*; that antiphon is his book’s. Schuster’s three pages name no station church for this Sunday.

### Introit <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>

The antiphon is a three-segment centonisation: v. 1a as far as *exáudi me*, then v. 2b from *salvum fac*, then v. 3 entire, with v. 4 as the psalm verse. **The clause it drops first is the psalm’s ascription**, and that ascription is unique: the ascriptions of Vulgate Pss. 72–88 in the tracked Clementine give Asaph at 72–82, *filiis Core* at 83, 84, 86 and 87, Eman the Ezrahite at 87 as well, Ethan the Ezrahite at 88, and *Oratio ipsi David.* at 85 alone. Ps. 85 is the single Davidic prayer in the whole of Book III, which the tracked text bounds by the doxologies at Ps. 71:19–20 and Ps. 88:53. What the cento sings after that omission is a psalm addressed by a servant to his God with no name attached to it.

> **Augustine, *Enarratio in Psalmum* 85 §§1–6 (PL 37, 1081–1090)** This is the locus classicus of the *totus Christus*, and the whole of the Introit’s reception runs through it. The Word is head of men and men his members, so that there is one Christ *qui et oret pro nobis, et oret in nobis, et oretur a nobis. Orat pro nobis, ut sacerdos noster; orat in nobis, ut caput nostrum, oratur a nobis, ut Deus noster*. *Inclina, Domine, aurem tuam* is therefore spoken *ex forma servi* and by the whole Body; *clamavi tota die* is not one day but *omni tempore … Unus homo usque in finem saeculi extenditur*; and the ground of the petition is that *Inclinat aurem, si tu non erigas cervicem: humiliato enim appropinquat; ab exaltato longe discedit* (§2). At v. 4 he turns the psalm verse into an exhortation: *Certe recte admonet membra sua ut sursum cor habeant … cor ut levetur, voluntatem mutat.*

> **Cassiodorus, *Expositio Psalmorum* on Ps. 85 (PL 70)** A second Latin witness stating the same reading in his own words: *Ex forma servi (sicut praefati sumus) Christus omnipotenti supplicat Patri … quod dicit: Inclina, ostendit quia se ad ipsum extendere non poterat humana conditio.* On *tota die*, *totius vitae tempus ostenditur, ut per multa tempora annorumque curricula quasi unius diei continuus clamor esse monstretur*. At v. 4 he reads the verse of the servant’s own part: *Iucundari petit animam suam fons hilaritatis et origo laetitiae, ab illa scilicet parte qua servus est.* His text was read on an optical layer of the Migne volume whose recognition is poor. His numbering runs level with the Vulgate’s here, Ps. 85 counting its inscription inside v. 1, so his *Vers. 1* is the Vulgate’s v. 1.

**Neither commentator’s lemma is the antiphon’s at every point, and the difference reaches the citation.** Augustine’s v. 1 has no *ad me* and runs on into *quoniam egenus et inops ego sum*, the clause the cento does not sing and on which his §§2–3 hang; his v. 3 reads *Miserere mei* where the missal prints *mihi*; and his v. 4 reads *Iucunda* where the missal prints *Lætífica*, his §6 commenting on *iucunda*. Cassiodorus reads *ad me* and *Laetifica* with the missal but *miserere mei* against it. So the *ad me* the antiphon adds is older than the Missal and stands in a sixth-century Latin lemma, while *miserere mihi* stands in no Bible witness and in no patristic lemma opened here: Swete’s Greek at Ps. 85:1 has no pronoun answering *ad me*, and *eleeson me* at v. 3 is a bare accusative that cannot decide between a Latin genitive and a dative, so *mihi* for *mei* is a question of Latin construction and not of what the psalm says.

Within the tracked 1962 registry — a repository derivative and not the printed book — Ps. 85:3 opens the Sixteenth Sunday’s Introit, which sings v. 5 with it and takes Ps. 85:1 as its psalm verse, while Ps. 85:2 *Salvum fac servum tuum* is the Gradual of the Ember Friday in the first week of Lent — the head of the psalm here and its continuation a week later; C3 of the synthesis sets the pair beside the two Offertories’ Ps. 39.

### Collect <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span>

*Ecclésiam tuam, Dómine, miserátio continuáta mundet et múniat: et quia sine te non potest salva consístere; tuo semper múnere gubernétur.* The prayer’s hinge is its semicolon, which divides the causal clause from the single petition it grounds, so that the two verbs of the first limb stand on one side of it and the one verb of the second on the other. The uncorrected optical layer of the Benziger 1962 shows that semicolon too, and those of the Pustet 1862, the Venice 1570 and the 1604 *typica* show a comma in its place.

Three things in it are load-bearing for the rest of the Mass. *Miserátio continuáta* makes mercy an attribute and not a request, and the participle is the first word of duration in the three orations. *Sine te non potest salva consístere* denies the Church any standing of her own; the first unit of the thematic movement takes that denial together with the Postcommunion’s. *Tuo semper múnere* makes the Church’s governance a gift; Augustine, expounding the Gradual’s own psalm, uses the same noun to deny that any progress is a merit, as the second unit of the thematic movement sets out in his own words.

No Father or Doctor comments on this prayer’s text in any source opened here. Its reception is liturgical, and Honorius’s *quam ut Deus mundet, muniat, rogat* is the nearest thing to an exposition of it.

### Epistle <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>

#### Where the lesson is cut from, and what the cuts carry in

The lesson opens on a conclusion whose premises it does not print: Gal. 5:25 *Si spíritu vívimus, spíritu et ambulémus* draws together the flesh-and-Spirit antithesis of 5:16–24, and the pericope closes the same figure at 6:8 in agricultural terms, so the excerpt is bracketed at both ends by an argument only one end of which is heard stated. Three threads carry in from outside it. The *invicem* chain runs 5:13, 5:15 and the lesson’s own 5:26. The law thread runs 5:14, 5:18, 5:23 and the lesson’s 6:2 *adimplébitis legem Christi*. And the lesson stops at 6:10, one verse before Paul takes the pen at 6:11.

**Both of the lesson’s boundaries fall just clear of the cross.** The lesson begins one verse after 5:24 *Qui autem sunt Christi, carnem suam crucifixerunt*, and ends four verses before 6:14 *Mihi autem absit gloriari, nisi in cruce Domini nostri Jesu Christi*. So the Mass raises vainglory as a problem and does not read Paul’s answer to it, and opposes flesh to spirit without reading the crucifixion of the flesh that grounds the opposition.

#### Four lexical results

**The two burdens are one word in Latin and two in Greek.** The apparent self-contradiction between *Alter altérius ónera portáte* at 6:2 and *Unusquísque enim onus suum portábit* at 6:5 is an artefact of the Latin: the Greek reads *baros* at 6:2 and *phortion* at 6:5, and the Vulgate renders both by one noun. The Douay follows the Latin and says “burdens” and “burden.”

**The sowing axiom is a commonplace of the Latin Bible** — 2 Cor. 9:6, Prov. 22:8, Jer. 12:13, Eccles. 11:4 — and its verse is numbered differently in the two languages, the sowing clause standing at the end of Greek v. 7 and the start of Clementine v. 8.

**One word in the lesson names a church office, and the English does not carry it.** *Catechizátur* and *catechízat* at 6:6 transliterate the Greek verb rather than rendering it, and the Douay reads “instructed” and “instructeth,” so the Latin and English columns part company there. *Domésticos fidei* at 6:10 is the household metaphor of Eph. 2:19, *domestici Dei*.

**And the lesson’s verb of carrying is the bier-bearers’ verb.** The Greek *bastazo* occurs twenty-seven times in the New Testament, four of them in Galatians and two of those inside this lesson, and the appointed text prints *portáte*, *portábit* and *qui portábant* within one Mass. The Latin does not distinguish them and neither does the English.

#### What the commentators argue

> **Jerome, *Commentariorum in Epistolam ad Galatas* lib. III, on Gal. 5:26–6:10 (PL 26, cols. 423–432)** At 5:26 he confesses that vainglory infects almsgiving, long prayer, the pallor of fasting, chastity in every state and martyrdom itself — *martyrium ipsum, si ideo fiat ut admirationi et laudi habeamur a fratribus, frustra sanguis effusus est* (col. 424). At 6:1 he observes that Paul calls the offender *homo* and does not call the corrector one, and that Paul wrote *ne et tu tenteris* and not *ne tu cadas*: *Vinci quippe vel vincere, nonnumquam in nostra est potestate: caeterum tentari, in potestate tentantis est* (col. 426). At 6:2 he grounds the burden on Ps. 37:5 and closes *Quae Christi lex est? Hoc est mandatum meum, ut diligatis invicem* (col. 427). **At 6:5 he reconciles the two burdens by moving one of them to the tribunal**: in this life we may help each other by prayers and counsel, but before the judgement seat *non Job, non Daniel, nec Noe rogare posse pro quoquam, sed unumquemque portare opus suum* (col. 429). At 6:6 he refutes Marcion’s reading of the verse as being about prayer; at 6:7 he ties *Deus non irridetur* to v. 6 against the disciple who pleads a failed harvest, *Excusatio verisimilis hominem potest utcumque placare, Deum non potest fallere* (col. 430); at 6:8 he answers the Encratite Julius Cassianus, who read sowing in the flesh as a condemnation of marriage; at 6:10 he makes the time of sowing the present life (col. 432). **His lemmata agree with the missal at the points that matter** — *Deus non irridetur*, *considerans teipsum* — and the Vulgate Galatians is his own revision, so his lemma and the appointed text are one text. The Latin was read on page images with no text layer and transcribed by eye.

> **Augustine, *Expositio epistulae ad Galatas* §§52–61 (PL 35, 2145–2148)** Correction of a brother is for Augustine the one test of a spiritual man, and it may be undertaken only after examining one’s own conscience: *Nihil autem sic probat spiritalem virum quam peccati alieni tractatio*; *Numquam itaque alieni peccati obiurgandi suscipiendum est negotium, nisi cum internis interrogationibus examinantes nostram conscientiam liquido nobis coram Deo responderimus dilectione nos facere … Dilige et dic, quod voles* (§§56–57). At 6:2 the law of Christ is the law of charity, whence *Eadem igitur Scriptura et idem mandatum, cum bonis terrenis inhiantes premit servos, Testamentum Vetus; cum in bona aeterna flagrantes erigit liberos, Testamentum Novum vocatur* (§58). At 6:3–5 the burden nobody can share is the burden of conscience: *Non ergo laudatores nostri minuunt onera conscientiae nostrae; atque utinam non etiam accumulent* (§59). At 6:7–10, *Vident enim seminationem operum suorum, sed messem non vident … Bene autem seminare, id est bene operari, facilius est quam in eo perseverare* (§61). §52 distinguishes the two vices of 5:26, *aemulatio* healed by peace and *invidia* by meekness. **Augustine reads a pre-Vulgate Latin Galatians, and his lemmata differ from the missal’s at nearly every appointed verse**: *spiritu et sectemur* for *spíritu et ambulémus*, the two participles of 5:26 in reverse order, *in spiritu mansuetudinis intendens teipsum* for *in spíritu lenitátis, consíderans teípsum*, *et tunc in seipso* for *et sic in semetípso*, *proprium onus portabit* for *onus suum portábit*, *Deus non subsannatur* for *Deus non irridétur*, and *non infirmemur; proprio enim tempore metemus infatigabiles* for *non deficiámus: témpore enim suo metémus, non deficiéntes*. His §54 argument depends entirely on his own verb *sectemur*.

> **Chrysostom, *Commentary on the Epistle to the Galatians*, chapter 6 (PG 61, 673–680), read in the nineteenth-century English of the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers** Chrysostom does not make the passage a discipline of the corrector’s conscience but an economy of complementary weaknesses inside one body. On 6:1: “He says not, in meekness, but, in a spirit of meekness, signifying thereby … that to be able to administer correction with mildness is a spiritual gift,” and he arraigns “the malice of the devil rather than the remissness of the soul.” At 6:2 the Church is a building whose stones hold different positions, and the verb is really “complete” — “make it up all of you in common, by the things wherein you bear with one another.” **At 6:6 he reads a deliberate institution rather than a rule about stipends**: Christ imposed on the teacher the necessity of requiring aid from his disciples, in order to repress his spirit. At 6:10 grace “invites both land and sea to the board of charity, only it shows a greater care for its own household.” He is read here in nineteenth-century English, so his distinction between “fulfil” and “complete” is his argument about the Greek.

> **Aquinas, *Super Epistolam ad Galatas lectura* cap. 6 lect. 1–2** Aquinas reads the lesson as an ordered instruction in how superiors treat inferiors (6:1), equals equals (6:2) and inferiors superiors (6:6). *Praeoccupatus* means overtaken by surprise and so the more deserving of pardon; *in aliquo* excludes the habitual sinner, against whom severity is right; and Paul wrote *instruite* and not *corrigite* because he speaks of men overtaken. He gives three modes of bearing another’s burden, the third of them *pro poena sibi debita satisfaciendo, orationibus et bonis operibus* — **a development beyond anything in Jerome, Augustine or Chrysostom on this verse, and his and not the tradition’s**. At 6:8 he reads the asymmetry of the possessives, and at 6:10 *dum tempus habemus, id est in hac vita, quae est tempus seminandi*. His second exposition of 6:7 is Jerome’s argument almost word for word, so the two are one continuous tradition and not two independent witnesses.

**Jerome and Augustine make Gal. 6:1–2 a discipline of the corrector’s own conscience; Chrysostom makes it an economy of complementary weaknesses inside one body.** On 6:5, Jerome moves the unshareable burden to the tribunal, Augustine identifies it with conscience, Chrysostom with self-regard, Aquinas with the rendering of account. Four expositors, four answers to one verse.

### Gradual <span class="nodecor">(*Grad.*)</span>

The chant sings the psalm’s exordium and stops. What it does not sing runs from the sabbath inscription at v. 1 through the wicked springing up as grass at vv. 7–8 to *Justus ut palma florebit; sicut cedrus Libani multiplicabitur* (v. 13), *Plantati in domo Domini … florebunt* (v. 14) and *Adhuc multiplicabuntur in senecta uberi* (v. 15) — the harvest imagery that would have answered the Epistle’s sowing, and that nobody hears. The appointed text departs from the Clementine only in pointing.

**The chant’s first word answers the lesson’s last *bonum*.** At Gal. 6:10 the lesson reads *Ergo dum tempus habémus, operémur bonum ad omnes* and closes immediately on the five words *máxime autem ad domésticos fidei*, the catchword standing three words before *máxime*; the responsory printed directly beneath it on p. 396 begins *Bonum est confitéri Dómino*. Two things hold the catchword down. In the Greek the lesson uses *kalon* at 6:9 and *agathon* at 6:10, so the triple repetition is partly the Latin’s and the chant answers only the second; and the further pairing with the psalm’s palm and cedar is drawn from verses the chant does not sing.

*Misericórdiam tuam et veritátem tuam* is a fixed collocation, standing in the tracked Clementine at Ps. 39:11, Ps. 87:12 and Ps. 91:3 and nowhere else — two of the three in psalms this Mass sings from.

> **Augustine, *Enarratio in Psalmum* 91 §§1–4 (PL 37, 1171–1178)** The sabbath of the title is interior: *Intus est, in corde est sabbatum nostrum … Cui autem bona est conscientia, tranquillus est*, and against a merely bodily observance, *Melius est enim arare, quam saltare*. **Confessing is two-sided**, and this is the reading the Gradual’s first word carries: *In utraque re, et in peccato tuo, quia tu fecisti, et in bono facto, confitere Domino, quia ipse fecit*, with the excuses of Satan and of the stars each refused. *Psallere nomini tuo* is distinguished from singing — *Opus nostrum, psalterium nostrum est … Canta ore, psalle operibus* — and the singing seeks *gloriam Dei, non tuam; nomen ipsius, non tuum*, which is in praise exactly what the lesson forbids at Gal. 5:26. On the versicle: *Mane dicitur, quando nobis bene est: nox dicitur, quando tristitia tribulationis est … quando bene est, lauda misericordiam; quando male, lauda veritatem: quia peccata flagellat, non est iniquus.* His lemma matches the Gradual’s wording at both verses, including *Altissime*, differing only in the comma the 1962 book drops.

> **Cassiodorus, *Expositio Psalmorum* on Ps. 91 (PL 70)** The same two-sided confession stated in his own words: confession is of sins and of benefits, *nec meritorum nostrorum esse dicimus quod ipsius miseratione praestatur*; *Psallere enim significat piis operibus Domini mandata peragere, ut sicut psalterium de superioribus sonat, ita operatio nostra ad aures Divinitatis ascendat*. On the versicle: *Mane significat gaudium, nox autem cognoscitur indicare tristitiam … Per noctem vero, id est per tribulationem … veritatem ipsius similiter annuntiare debemus: quia merito patimur quod delictis gravantibus sustinemus.*

> **Hilary of Poitiers, *Tractatus de titulo psalmi XCI* §§1–3 (CSEL 22, pp. 339ff.)** **What survives of Hilary on this psalm is a tractate on its title, not a commentary on its verses**, and he has nothing on *Bonum est confitéri Dómino* or on *Ad annuntiándum mane misericórdiam tuam* — the two clauses the Gradual actually sings. What he argues is that the sabbath the title names cannot be the seventh day of Genesis, because the psalm says nothing about that rest: *nulla hic diei septimi requies, nullum humanorum operum otium decantatur*. The rest meant must therefore be sought at Ps. 94:11, and the Lord himself loosed the seventh-day sabbath when he said that the priests in the temple profane it and are blameless. Zingerle’s preface records the tractates on the titles of Pss. 9 and 91 as transmitted *solis codicibus Vr*. **Hilary’s Latin reaches this guide through a poor optical layer of a scan of Zingerle’s edition**, whose running heads and apparatus are badly mangled and whose page sequence is unreliable.

Within the tracked registry the same antiphon and verse range stands at the Saturday of the second week of Lent, and Ps. 91:2 at Septuagesima as an Offertory; Schuster states the Lenten identity independently at printed p. 139 of volume III of *The Sacramentary*.

### Alleluia <span class="nodecor">(*All.*)</span>

Ps. 94 is a summons to worship in vv. 1–7 and an oracle of warning in vv. 8–11, the turn falling at v. 7/8; the chant’s verse stands inside the summons as the first of its reason clauses, four verses before the turn, so the chant carries in the summons and not the warning. The warning half is what Hebrews quotes at length, at Heb. 3:7–11, 3:15, 4:3, 4:5 and 4:7, three of the five returning to *Hodie si vocem ejus audieritis*. The Fourteenth Sunday’s Alleluia is *Venite, exsultemus*, Ps. 94:1, so this chant continues the previous Sunday’s psalm — Schuster calls it “a continuation” and not the next verse, and it is not: 94:1 to 94:3 skips v. 2.

**No checked witness expounds the predicate this chant substitutes.** What is proper to this element is that the two expositions of Ps. 94:3 opened here are built on the word the chant removes, so that neither survives it; and the three composed orations have no expositor in any source opened here. The chant reads *et Rex magnus super omnem terram* where every Bible witness opened here reads *above all gods* — Swete’s *epi pantas tous theous*, the Clementine’s *super omnes deos*, the Douay’s “above all gods.”

> **Augustine, *Enarratio in Psalmum* 94 §§5–6 (PL 37, 1217–1234)** Augustine’s lemma is *et rex magnus super omnes deos*, quoted three times in §§5–6 and again at the close of §6, and he builds two whole developments on the word the chant removes. First, with 1 Cor. 8:5–6 and Ps. 95:5, the gods of the nations are demons, over whom it would be small praise for God to be great: *Parum enim erat quia terribilis super omnia daemonia Deus: quid magnum, esse super omnia daemonia?* Second, the *gods* are men made gods by participation: *Hic accipe homines deos … Deos dixit participatione, non natura; gratia, qua voluit facere deos … Deus verus facit deos credentes in se.* **Neither development survives the chant’s substitution**, so both are exposition of Ps. 94:3 as the Latin Bible reads it and not of the Alleluia as sung. His §8 reading of the neighbouring *in manu eius fines terrae*, v. 4, of Christ joining two peoples is adjacent in sense to the chant’s wording and belongs to a verse the chant does not sing.

> **Cassiodorus, *Expositio Psalmorum* on Ps. 94 (PL 70)** Read after Augustine and on the same verse, Cassiodorus also has *super omnes deos* and expounds the gods the same two ways: *Deos frequenter legimus ab hominibus confictos, ut Iovem, Martem, Saturnum … Legimus etiam deos, sanctos viros a Domino constitutos, ut est illud: Deus stetit in synagoga deorum.* So two Latin commentators expound this verse, and both expound the predicate the chant does not sing.

**The chant’s predicate is the Latin Bible’s own phrase.** It stands verbatim at Ps. 46:3, *quoniam Dominus excelsus, terribilis, rex magnus super omnem terram*, the one place in the tracked Clementine where *rex magnus* and *super omnem terram* collocate.

In the older chant record the verse is cued at eight places in the antiphonaries Hesbert collected, none of them a Sunday after Pentecost later than the Eighth, and the Ottobonianus margins cue it at a Paschal Friday and at another Mass. **Neither phrase the English Bibles carry renders this chant**: “a great King above all gods” is the Bible text the missal departs from, and “a great king over all the earth” is the Douay’s Ps. 46:3, whose Latin the chant’s predicate reproduces. An identified English of the chant’s own reading exists outside the Bibles: the 1861 Philadelphia hand missal prints the versicle at printed p. 428 as “a great King over all the earth,” beside the Latin *Quóniam Deus magnus Dóminus, et Rex magnus super omnem terram*. Within the 1962 temporal registry the chant occurs at this Mass and nowhere else.

### Gospel <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>

#### Seven results from the text itself

**The missal’s incipit cuts the connective.** Luke’s *Et factum est: deinceps ibat* makes the raising the next thing in order after the centurion’s servant, and *In illo témpore: Ibat Iesus* replaces it while supplying the subject the sentence needs. Two consequences follow: the pair Luke makes — a servant healed at a distance for a Gentile who asks, then a son raised at close quarters for a widow who does not — is severed; and *mortui resurgunt* at Lk. 7:22, six verses after the pericope ends, has no antecedent in the Third Gospel but this. *Naim* is a hapax in the tracked Clementine.

**The narrative is told in the words of Elias at Sarepta, and the identity is exact only in the Greek.** The clause *kai edoken auton te metri autou* stands at LXX 3 Kingdoms 17:23 and is letter for letter the clause Luke 7:15 ends with; the Greek verse runs on past it into Elias’s own words. The Vulgate breaks the link, reading *tradidit matri suæ* at 3 Kings and *dedit illum matri suæ* at Luke, so a reader of the Latin column cannot see the quotation, and the claim that Luke is quoting is a claim about the Greek. Two weaker supports stand beside it: the gate-and-widow agreement at 3 Kingdoms 17:10 against Luke 7:12 uses cognate but different nouns, and 4 Kings 4 shares the raising and the restoring but no verbal formula. The strongest text-internal support for the Elias figure is Luke’s own, at 4:25–26, where the Sarepta widow is the programmatic example of the Nazareth sermon.

***Deus visitávit plebem suam* answers the Benedictus.** The verb and object repeat Zachary’s *quia visitavit, et fecit redemptionem plebis suæ* at Lk. 1:68. *Visitare* with God as its subject stands at Lk. 1:68, 1:78, 7:16 and 19:44 *eo quod non cognoveris tempus visitationis tuæ* and nowhere else in Luke, the Latin stem and the Greek lemmata agreeing verse for verse. The word enters Luke twice in the Benedictus, is spoken once by the crowd at Naim, and returns once over Jerusalem. **It is in the mouth of the crowd and not of the Evangelist.**

***Filius únicus* renders *monogenes*, and the Latin hides the connection.** *Monogenes* stands nine times in the Greek New Testament — Lk. 7:12, 8:42, 9:38; Jn. 1:14, 1:18, 3:16, 3:18; Heb. 11:17; 1 Jn. 4:9 — and the Latin split is complete with no exception: *unicus* in Luke, *unigenitus* everywhere else.

***Misericórdia motus* renders *splanchnizomai*, which Luke uses exactly three times**: here, of the Samaritan at 10:33, and of the prodigal’s father at 15:20. The cognate noun occurs once in Luke, at 1:78 *per viscera misericordiæ Dei nostri* — the same Benedictus that supplies the visitation language, and there the Latin does keep the anatomy. So the Naim pericope answers the Benedictus twice, at v. 13 and at v. 16, and only the second is visible in the Latin. *Noli flere* and its plural return in Luke exactly three times: 7:13, 8:52 and 23:28.

**Lk. 7:13 is the first place in the Third Gospel where the narrator’s own voice calls Jesus *Dóminus***, and the appointed text keeps it: before 7:13 the word is God in narration or in cited Scripture, or a character’s vocative, or inside a character’s speech.

***Prophéta magnus* is not a stock biblical title.** In the tracked Clementine it stands only at Ecclus. 48:25, of Isaias, and at this verse. *Surge* at v. 14 and *surréxit* at v. 16 are one Greek verb in two forms; the Vulgate’s promise of a prophet at Deut. 18:15 stands on a different Latin verb, *suscitabit*.

#### What the commentators argue

> **Ambrose, *Expositio Evangelii secundum Lucam* V.88–92 (CSEL 32 pars 4, pp. 216–218; PL 15, 1376B–1377A)** Three moves at once. **The widow is holy Church**, who *populum iuniorem a pompa funeris atque supremis sepulchri suarum reuocet ad uitam contemplatione lacrimarum, quae flere prohibetur eum cui resurrectio debeatur*, and ringed by the crowd of peoples she seems *plus … esse quam feminam* (§89). **The bier is wood, and because it is wood the dead man already has hope of rising**: *spem resurgendi habebat, quia ferebatur in ligno … ut esset indicio salutem populo per crucis patibulum refundendam* (§90). And the bearers who halt are the mortal fluxes of material nature — *cum uel ignis inmodicae cupiditatis exaestuat uel frigidus umor exundat uel pigra quadam corporis habitudine uigor habetatur animorum … hi sunt nostri funeris portitores*. At §§91–92, *quis iste est tumulus tuus nisi mali mores? tumulus tuus perfidia est, sepulchrum tuum guttur est* (Ps. 5:11), and *fleat pro te mater ecclesia, quae pro singulis tamquam pro unicis filiis uidua mater interuenit*. At VI.64, outside the pericope’s own place, he explains why this raising is public where Jairus’s daughter’s is private and reads the two as two peoples: *est etiam forma sapientiae in uiduae filio cito ecclesiam credituram, in archisynagogae filia credituros quidem Iudaeos, sed ex pluribus pauciores* — marked with *puto* and *est etiam forma*, offered as a reading and not as a demonstration. The critical text was read as an optical layer whose body text is generally sound and whose apparatus lines are mangled.

> **Augustine, *Sermo* 98 (PL 38, 591–595)** **The three raisings are three grades of sin.** The girl inside the house is sin consented to in the heart; the young man carried out of the gate but not yet buried is sin gone out into the deed; Lazarus, buried under the stone, is sin hardened into habit — *Moles illa imposita sepulcro, ipsa est vis dura consuetudinis, qua premitur anima, nec surgere, nec respirare permittitur* (§5). The four-step analysis behind it stands at §6: *Prima est enim quasi titillatio delectationis in corde; secunda, consensio; tertium, factum; quarta, consuetudo.* At §2 the widowed mother is mother Church rejoicing at each raising; at §6 the loosing of Lazarus is the office given to the disciples while the raising stays Christ’s. **The sermon stops at v. 15**, one verse short of the appointed pericope, and nothing in it comments on v. 16.

> **Cyril of Alexandria, *Commentary on the Gospel of Saint Luke*, Sermon XXXVI (Payne Smith, Oxford 1859, vol. 1, pp. 132–135)** Christ comes uninvited: at Capharnaum he was present by invitation, but at Naim “He draws near without being invited. For no one summoned Him to restore the dead man to life, but He comes to do so of His own accord.” The Evangelist supplies two proofs of a real raising, that the dead man began to speak and that Christ gave him to his mother; the three raised are “a pledge of the hope prepared for us of a resurrection of the dead,” confirmed from Is. 26:19 and Ps. 103(104):29–30. **And the touching of the bier teaches the life-giving flesh**: it was done “that thou mightest learn that the holy body of Christ is effectual for the salvation of man. For the flesh of the Almighty Word is the body of life … For consider, that iron, when brought into contact with fire, produces the effects of fire,” so that it “annihilates the influence of death and corruption.” The words reach this page in an English translation of an ancient Syriac version of a Greek original that survives elsewhere only in catena fragments.

> **Bede, *In Lucae evangelium expositio* lib. II (PL 92, cols. 417–420)** Bede takes Augustine’s moral reading and works it through the pericope’s furniture. At v. 12 the dead man carried out is sin no longer hidden in the heart’s chamber but published abroad, *quasi per suae civitatis ostia propalantem*, and the widowed mother is mother Church, one perfect and immaculate virgin though gathered from many persons, *licet e multis collecta personis, una sit perfecta et immaculata virgo, mater Ecclesia*, each of the faithful her only son, with Gal. 4:19 quoted for her motherhood; the gate is one of the bodily senses, closing with *Obsecro, Domine Jesu, cunctas meae civitatis portas justitiae facias* (Ps. 117:19). Against Novatian, who *veramque matrem Ecclesiam … negat consolari debere*. At v. 14 the bier is *male secura desperati peccatoris conscientia* and its bearers *vel immunda desideria … vel lenocinia blandientium … venenata sociorum*; at v. 15 *Redditur matri, cum … per sacerdotalis decreta judicii communioni sociatur Ecclesiae*. **Bede is the only witness located who comments on v. 16**: *Quia Deus visitavit plebem suam. Non tantummodo verbum suum semel incorporando, sed etiam … semper in corda mittendo.* Two of his moves are his own and not Ambrose’s or Augustine’s: the anti-Novatianist application, and the reading of the restoration to the mother as sacramental reconciliation. His text was read on an optical layer of Migne whose two-column setting interleaves badly and whose individual words are corrupt in places.

> **Gregory of Nyssa, *De hominis opificio* 25.6–11 (PG 44, 213–217), read in the nineteenth-century English of the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, second series vol. 5** The raising is fourth in a graded pedagogy by which Christ accustomed human weakness to believe in resurrection — the fever, the nobleman’s son, the ruler’s daughter, the young man on his way to the tomb, four-day Lazarus — and the widow’s only son is “the stock of her race, the shoot of its succession, the staff of her old age.”

**Four expositors, four questions.** Augustine reads the raisings morally, Cyril eschatologically, Ambrose ecclesially, Gregory of Nyssa as a pedagogy of increasing difficulty. None denies another and they answer different questions. On the widow the two Latin readings are not identical either: Ambrose’s Church weeps and intercedes for each sinner as for an only son and is forbidden to weep because resurrection is owed to her; Augustine’s rejoices. Grief and joy, not one doctrine.

#### What the commentators do not supply

Gregory the Great has nothing on this pericope. Within Luke 7 every heading of his in the *Catena aurea*, bare *Gregorius* or *Gregorius in Evang.*, stands at lect. 6, on the sinful woman of Lk. 7:36–50, and none at lect. 2; the *Gregorius Nyssenus* headings are another Gregory’s. On the standard account of their contents, no edition of them having been opened for this formulary, Gregory’s forty *Homiliae in Evangelia* treat Luke 7 only at Homily 33, on the same pericope of the sinful woman.

**The eight-resurrections scheme the *Catena aurea* attributes to *Ambrosius* is not in Ambrose’s Luke commentary**: a word search of the complete critical text for the scheme’s own terms returns V.86 and VI.64 only, and neither carries it; it may stand in another work of his, or be Ps.-Ambrose, or be a Glossa attribution. Three further attributions in the same catena at this chapter have no located text behind them — Theophylact’s widow-as-soul allegory, Titus of Bostra’s contrast with Elias and Eliseus, and the unnamed Chrysostom fragment on consolation. **One heading of the same catena disagrees with the work it lemmatises**: its *Cyrillus in Ioannem* at this chapter stands over a passage that is in Cyril’s Luke commentary, and Payne Smith’s own footnote at vol. 1, p. 135 records Aquinas and Cramer’s catena manuscript attributing the neighbouring fragment to Cyril as well.

> **Anthony of Padua preached this Gospel at his Sixteenth Sunday, not his Fifteenth**
>
> Anthony’s sermon on the widow of Naim is his *Dominica XVI post Pentecosten*, which opens *Evangelium in decima sexta dominica post Pentecosten: Ibat Iesus in civitatem quae vocatur Naim* and pairs the pericope with Eph. 3:13–21; his *Dominica XV* opens *Nemo potest duobus dominis servire*. A citation made by matching the ordinal would name the wrong sermon silently.

The same pericope is appointed at the Thursday of the fourth week of Lent in the tracked registry, where the Missal pairs it with 4 Kings 4:25–38, Eliseus and the Sunamitess’s son; Schuster and Guéranger both name that Lenten Thursday independently, and the Pustet 1862 does not print this Sunday’s Gospel at all but cross-references it there.

### Offertory <span class="nodecor">(*Off.*)</span>

The antiphon takes vv. 2, 3 and 4 of Ps. 39 and cuts the middle out of them: the whole of v. 3 from *et eduxit me de lacu miseriæ* to *direxit gressus meos* goes, and so does v. 4b. What lies immediately past the second cut is *Videbunt multi, et timebunt, et sperabunt in Domino* and, one verse on, *Beatus vir cujus est nomen Domini spes ejus*. Two verses further the psalm’s vv. 7–9 are what Hebrews puts in the mouth of Christ coming into the world, and there the Latin Bible disagrees with itself across the seam: the psalm reads *aures autem perfecisti mihi* where Heb. 10:5 quoting it reads *corpus autem aptasti mihi*, while Swete’s Greek Psalter reads *soma* with Hebrews and against the Latin psalm. The antiphon stops three verses short of that seam, at *Deo nostro*, so nothing in its own extent rests its place at the offering of the gifts on Hebrews.

**Three of the antiphon’s phrases are not the Clementine’s, and all three have Latin witnesses no later than the sixth century.** Against Swete, the second and third are the literal renderings and the Clementine’s are the freer: *deeseos* is a singular that *deprecationem meam* renders exactly where *preces meas* does not, and *hymnon* is *hymnum* and not *carmen*. The first, *respéxit me*, answers neither Swete’s *proseschen moi* nor any Greek variant collated here — but it stands in Cassiodorus’s lemma, so it has a witness even though it has no derivation.

> **Augustine, *Enarratio in Psalmum* 39 §§2–5 (PL 36, 433–450)** The dative is Augustine’s point, and his lemma is not the antiphon’s at either of its first two verbs, reading *Sustinens sustinui* for *Exspéctans exspectávi* and *attendit mihi* for *respéxit me*: *Ergo et cum malus esses, attendebat te, sed non attendebat tibi … Attendit mihi, inquit, id est, consolando attendit, ut mihi prodesset* (§2). The pit of misery — which the antiphon cuts — is *Profunditas iniquitatis, ex carnalibus concupiscentiis*, and the rock is Christ (§3). The new song is the song of the new man: *novus homo, dicat canticum novum: innovatus amet nova quibus renovatur … Dicimus ergo hymnum Deo nostro, et ipse hymnus liberat nos* (§4) — **his lemma reading *hymnum* with the antiphon and against the Clementine**. At §5 he answers the question of the psalm’s speaker outright: it is Christ, speaking now in the name of our Head and now of us who are his members. The exposition opens with a long anti-Donatist prologue at §1 that has nothing to do with the appointed verses.

> **Ambrose, *In Psalmum XXXIX enarratio* §§1–4 (PL 14, cols. 1109–1110)** **The doubled verb is the point**: the psalm announces the New Testament, and *non est perfecti exspectare, sed exspectasse; nemo enim nisi qui perseveraverit usque in finem, salvus erit* (§1) — the same Matt. 10:22 that Jerome and Augustine both reach for at the lesson’s *non deficiámus*. At §3, *immisisti in os meum canticum novum, quod est Novum Testamentum. Gaudentes iam canimus hymnum Deo nostro, quia novarum virtutum praecepta cognovimus; ut nostra omnia relinquamus, Christum sequentes, et nostros diligamus inimicos … Maledicentes ecce benedicimus.* His lemma carries the antiphon’s incipit and *hymnum*. The Latin was read on page images with no text layer and transcribed by eye.

> **Cassiodorus, *Expositio Psalmorum* on Ps. 39 (PL 70)** **Cassiodorus’s lemma is the antiphon’s text word for word** — *Exspectans exspectavi Dominum, et respexit me: et exaudivit deprecationem meam* — and he reads *hymnum* for the Clementine’s *carmen*. The speaker is the Church: *Ecclesia catholica, quae fuerat de totius mundi partibus congreganda, patientiae virtutem praedicat.* On the doubling, *considerandus est hic sermo geminatus, quia superflua non est tam decora repetitio*; on the verb the Clementine does not have, *Respexit: quia praevenit omne bonum nostrum*; on the noun, *Deprecatio quoque significat frequentissimam precem*; and on *hymnum Deo nostro*, *Hymnus autem Graecus sermo est, id est, laus carminum lege composita. Et quoniam hymni erant quos idolis suis etiam gentilitas personabat, addidit, Deo nostro.*

**Ps. 39 and Ps. 69 stand at three Sundays inside a five-Sunday span.** The Twelfth Sunday’s Introit sings Ps. 69:2–3, this Offertory Ps. 39:2–4 and the Sixteenth Sunday’s Offertory Ps. 39:14–15, one psalm supplying two consecutive Offertories. The doublet between the two psalms, and with it Cassiodorus’s division of the repeated words between speakers — Christ’s in his own person at Ps. 39 and his members’ at Ps. 69, which neither psalm’s own text marks — stand at their loci in the guide to the Twelfth Sunday, whose Introit sings that psalm, and were not opened again here: Cassiodorus was read for this Mass at Pss. 39, 85, 91 and 94. Within the tracked registry the antiphon also stands at the Tuesday of the fourth week of Lent, which Schuster states independently at printed p. 140.

### Secret <span class="nodecor">(*Sec.*)</span>

*Tua nos, Dómine, sacraménta custódiant: et contra diabólicos semper tueántur incúrsus.* Two verbs of defence, one object, and an adverb that has moved: both ancient Roman witnesses read *tueantur semper incursus* where 1962 reads *semper tueántur incúrsus*. The prayer’s object is the congregation and no longer the Church, one step of the narrowing the fourth unit of the thematic movement reads across the three orations.

**No exposition of this prayer’s text stands in any source opened for this formulary.** What the prayer asks to be defended from is named in the lesson it follows: Chrysostom, on Gal. 6:1, arraigns “the malice of the devil rather than the remissness of the soul” in the man overtaken in a fault, and that malice is the *diabólicos … incúrsus* of the Secret. He is commenting on Paul.

### Communion <span class="nodecor">(*Comm.*)</span>

The antiphon sings the exact clause at which the Bread of Life discourse turns from bread to flesh, and only its second half: the half not sung is *Si quis manducaverit ex hoc pane, vivet in æternum*, and the words that are sung are the ones that make the crowd start quarrelling in the next verse, *Litigabant ergo Judæi ad invicem, dicentes: Quomodo potest hic nobis carnem suam dare ad manducandum?* The element the excerpt carries in and cannot state is the discourse’s resurrection refrain: *et ego resuscitabo eum in novissimo die* three times, at vv. 40, 44 and 55 in the missal’s own Clementine numbering, and the same promise in other words at v. 39, *sed resuscitem illud in novissimo die*.

**Both of the antiphon’s departures are on the Latin side.** The Greek has a simple future *doso* and *kosmou*, so *dédero* and *sǽculi* answer nothing in it. Neither is an error — *dedero* is a future perfect and both *sæculum* and *mundus* are live Latin renderings of the Greek noun elsewhere in the Vulgate.

> **Augustine, *In Iohannis evangelium tractatus* 26 §§12–18 (PL 35, 1607–1616)** The tractate’s own printed heading is the antiphon’s verse in the Clementine’s wording, *Panis quem ego dabo, caro mea est pro mundi vita*. The eating that gives life is inward: *qui manducat intus, non foris; qui manducat in corde, non qui premit dente* (§12). Only the body of Christ lives by the Spirit of Christ: *De Spiritu Christi non vivit, nisi corpus Christi … O Sacramentum pietatis! o signum unitatis! o vinculum caritatis! Qui vult vivere, habet ubi vivat, habet unde vivat. Accedat, credat ut incorporetur, ut vivificetur* (§13). The *res* of the sacrament is *societatem corporis et membrorum suorum, quod est sancta Ecclesia*, and it is *omni homini ad vitam, nulli ad exitium* where the sacrament is *quibusdam ad vitam, quibusdam ad exitium* (§15); the species is made of many grains and many berries (§17). **His lemma is *dabo* and *pro mundi vita***, so this is exposition of the verse and not of the antiphon as sung.

> **Cyril of Alexandria, *Commentary on the Gospel according to S. John* IV.2 (Pusey, Library of the Fathers 43, Oxford 1874, vol. 1, pp. 409–412)** The chapter heading is the doctrine: *That the Holy Body of Christ is Life-giving*. The indwelling Word “transformed it into His Own proper good, that is life … rendered It life-giving, as Himself is by Nature,” with Jn. 17:19 read alongside and the heave-offering of Num. 15:18–21 as its figure. This is the same doctrine Cyril states at the Gospel’s bier, argued there from the touching and here from the Eucharist. The words are Pusey’s English of the Greek.

> **Chrysostom, *Homily* 46 *on the Gospel of John* (PG 59, 257–260), read in nineteenth-century English** “By bread He means here either His saving doctrines and the faith which is in Him, or His own Body; for both nerve the soul.” On the appointed clause: “observe how little by little He led them up to Himself. Here He says that Himself gives, not the Father.” And on the quarrelling that follows it, “when questioning concerning the how comes in, there comes in with it unbelief.”

> **Aquinas, *Super Evangelium S. Ioannis lectura* cap. 6 lect. 6** The flesh is *organum divinitatis suae*, and the instrument acts by the power of the agent: *cum instrumentum agat virtute agentis … ita ut Damascenus dicit et caro virtute verbi adiuncti vivificat: unde Christus tactu suo sanabat infirmos*. **That last clause reaches the Gospel of this same Mass**, where Christ touches the bier. The bread is the sacrament of the Church’s unity because made of many grains; on the verb, *dicit dabo, quia nondum institutum erat hoc sacramentum*; *Non dicit autem carnem meam significat sed caro mea est*; and on *pro mundi vita*, *vita quam confert … quantum in se est, totius mundi*. **His gloss on the tense turns on the simple future and cannot be transferred to *dédero***, nor his exposition of the universality of the effect to *sǽculi*.

> **Two numbering series meet at this antiphon**
>
> The missal cites the antiphon as Io. 6, 52, and the tracked Clementine and Douay both number it 52, so missal and Bible agree without adjustment. The Greek Fathers and the nineteenth-century English translations made from them number this clause 6:51 and reserve 6:52 for *Quomodo potest hic nobis carnem suam dare ad manducandum?* A citation of Cyril’s chapter by its modern label would therefore be wrong by one verse against the missal’s reference unless the series is named.

Within the tracked registry the antiphon also stands at the Thursday of the first week of Lent; Schuster states the same at printed p. 141 and adds that the Ninth Sunday’s antiphon comes from the same discourse, though his reference there is loose, the Ninth Sunday’s being *Qui manducat meam carnem* at Jn. 6:57.

### Postcommunion <span class="nodecor">(*Postcomm.*)</span>

*Mentes nostras et córpora possídeat, quǽsumus, Dómine, doni cæléstis operátio: ut non noster sensus in nobis, sed iúgiter eius prævéniat efféctus.* **The subject is held back to the end of its clause**, behind two objects and a vocative: it is *doni cæléstis operátio* that is asked to possess, and its *efféctus* — not *noster sensus* — that is asked to go before. The pointing carries the grammar: no comma after *nostras*, so *Mentes nostras et córpora* is a single object pair, and the colon after *operátio* divides the petition from the purpose clause.

### The three orations, and their liturgical history

No Father or Doctor comments on *Ecclésiam tuam, Dómine, miserátio continuáta*, *Tua nos, Dómine, sacraménta custódiant* or *Mentes nostras et córpora possídeat* in any tracked source, or in any patristic or medieval work opened for this formulary.

**Their history is liturgical, and it begins where the books begin.** None of the three occurs in the Veronense, in Férotin’s *Liber mozarabicus sacramentorum*, in Bannister’s *Missale Gothicum* or in Lowe’s *Bobbio Missal*. *Incursus* and *ecclesiam tuam* stand in both Veronense scans and in the Mozarabic and the Gothicum, *possideat* and *mentes nostras* in three of those four layers, and *continuata* in the Veronense scans alone; no control count stands for the Bobbio Missal, and an optical layer that renders the *e caudata* as a caret would hide any of the three prayers in any of the four books. No source opened for this formulary dates or attributes any of the three: their attested history begins at the Old Gelasian and the Hadrianum.

## Source-Grounded Synthesis Across the Propers

### C1. What the choir sings is not what the Bible on the facing page reads, at eight places in the sung texts, and the difference is older than 1962

Eight word-level departures separate the appointed antiphons and the Alleluia’s versicle from the Clementine — two at the Introit, one at the Alleluia, three at the Offertory and two at the Communion — and a ninth, in the Introit’s psalm verse, stands outside that count. The divergence table in the commentary prints each of the eight beside the Bible’s reading, and the three older Missals the commentary names there carry every one of them in their optical layers, so none of the eight is a 1962 act. Set against the witnesses collated for this Mass they fall into three groups, and each of the eight into one group only.

**Four of the eight are attested in Latin by the sixth century.** Cassiodorus’s lemma carries *ad me* at Ps. 85:1, and *respexit me*, *deprecationem meam* and *hymnum* at Ps. 39:2–4; Ambrose carries the Offertory’s incipit and *hymnum*; Augustine carries *deprecationem meam* and *hymnum*. Two of those same four, *deprecationem meam* and *hymnum*, are also the nearer renderings of Swete’s Greek, the Clementine’s *preces meas* and *carmen* the freer, as the Offertory subsection of the commentary shows word by word: at those two places the chant is the closer rendering and the Bible text the looser one. Beside the four, and not itself one of the eight, stands *Lætífica* at Ps. 85:4, where Cassiodorus reads with the missal and Augustine reads *Iucunda*.

**Two of the eight stand in no Bible witness and in no Latin or Greek lemma collated here**: *miserére mihi* at Ps. 85:3, where both Latin commentators read *mei*, and *super omnem terram* at Ps. 94:3, where every witness reads *super omnes deos*; the Introit and Alleluia subsections carry the collation. Nothing in Swete answers the Introit’s *ad me* either, which Cassiodorus’s Latin lemma nonetheless carries.

**The Communion’s two stand outside both groups.** *Dédero* and *sǽculi* answer nothing in the Greek, which has a simple future and *kosmou*, and none of the five English witnesses examined renders either reading distinctly. They are not variants a Greek witness can adjudicate but a future perfect and a second noun standing where the Bible text has neither.

The sixth-century lemmata put four of these readings in Latin before the Clementine, which is a date.

### C2. The lesson’s burden and the bier’s load are carried by one Latin verb, and Augustine and Bede put the same thing under it

The appointed Latin prints *portáte* at Gal. 6:2, *portábit* at 6:5 and *qui portábant* at Lk. 7:14, and the Epistle subsection of the commentary shows one Greek verb behind the three where the Greek nouns for the two burdens differ. What the two elements’ expositors put under that verb is the same thing. Augustine, at the burden no one can hand to another, names it conscience, and adds that our praisers do not lighten it and may add to it (*Exp. Gal.* §59), the bearing of one another’s burdens at 6:2 fulfilling, on his reading, the law of charity (§58); Bede, at the bier, makes the thing carried the falsely secure conscience of a despairing sinner (*In Luc.* II, PL 92, 417–420), the Gospel subsection of the commentary naming his bearers in his own words; Ambrose, at the same verse, names the bearers from the body rather than the conscience (*Exp. Luc.* V.90). **So the verb keeps its object across the two elements and reverses its office.** In the lesson the conscience is a load each man carries for himself and no admirer lightens, while charity carries the loads that can be shared; at Naim the conscience is what is carried, by the very things Augustine says add to its weight, and the carrying runs toward the tomb until Christ’s touch stops the bearers, *stetérunt*.

Two bounds hold the join where the witnesses put it. Each expositor reads his own passage, and none sets Gal. 6:5 beside Lk. 7:14: the identity is of the object each names, not a comparison either draws. And Jerome, Chrysostom and Aquinas name the unshareable burden otherwise — the tribunal, self-regard, the rendering of account — so the conscience on the bier answers Augustine’s reading of the lesson and not a reading the four expositors share.

### C3. Almost nothing this Mass sings is sung here alone

Against the tracked 1962 calendar registry — a repository derivative and not the printed book — most of this formulary’s chants, and its Gospel with them, are appointed somewhere else in the year as well, and the pattern is not random.

**Four of the chant texts are Lenten in their other life.** The Gradual, the Offertory and the Communion each stand at a Lenten weekday — a Saturday of the second week, a Tuesday of the fourth, a Thursday of the first — and Schuster states all three identities independently of the registry; Ps. 85:2, the Introit’s own *salvum fac*, is an Ember-Friday Gradual of Lent’s first week, and Ps. 91:2 returns as a Septuagesima Offertory. The loci stand at the four element subsections.

**Where the Gospel returns in Lent it is read against its own Old Testament figure.** At the Thursday of the fourth week of Lent the Missal sets this pericope beside Eliseus and the Sunamitess’s son, 4 Kings 4; the Gospel subsection of the commentary carries the witnesses to that day.

**The neighbouring Sundays share this Mass’s psalm stock.** The Sixteenth Sunday takes both the Introit’s psalm and the Offertory’s up where this Sunday leaves them — Ps. 85 at vv. 3 and 5 with v. 1 as its verse, Ps. 39 at vv. 14–15 — so this Mass sings the head of both psalms and the next their continuation; the Twelfth Sunday’s Introit sings Ps. 69, whose doublet with Ps. 39 stands in the guide to that Sunday at loci not opened here; and the Ninth Sunday’s Communion, Jn. 6:57, comes from the same discourse as this one’s. Shared psalm stock across neighbouring Sundays is what a chant repertory produces of itself.

**The Alleluia is the exception.** Within the 1962 temporal registry Ps. 94:3 is sung at this Mass and at no other, while the Fourteenth Sunday’s Alleluia takes Ps. 94:1 from the same psalm and skips v. 2 between them. The one element the rest of the year does not reuse is also the one whose sung predicate is not the Bible’s.

### C4. The word for the cross stands in none of the ten elements, and the expositors of the bier supply the cross, the life-giving flesh and the sin

**Ten elements, and the word stands in none of them.** The extent the missal appoints is cut on both sides of it: the lesson opens one verse after the letter’s *crucifixerunt* at Gal. 5:24 and closes four verses before its *in cruce* at 6:14, as the Epistle subsection of the commentary sets out. The Gospel gives a death undone at a town gate rather than a death died. The Communion sings *caro mea est pro sǽculi vita* and stops before the discourse says how the flesh is given.

**What the appointed wording withholds, the expositors of the one scene supply, each from the bier.** Ambrose reads the wood the dead man is carried on as the sign of the salvation the cross was to give back, in the sentence the Gospel subsection of the commentary prints (*Exp. Luc.* V.90), so the cross enters this Mass through the *lóculum* Christ touches and through no word the missal prints. Cyril reads the touch as instruction in the life-giving flesh, the doctrine the third unit of the thematic movement sets out from his sentence and Aquinas’s. Augustine reads the death as the middle of his three grades of sin (*Sermo* 98 §5), and Bede reads the restoration to the mother as the sinner’s return to the Church’s communion by priestly judgement (*In Luc.* II, PL 92, 417–420). So the cross reaches this Mass as the wood under the corpse; the flesh that touches the bier is, on Cyril’s and Aquinas’s showing, the flesh the Communion sings; and the death undone is read as sin. What the appointed wording itself puts where the word would stand is a visitation and three petitions — a crowd saying *Deus visitávit plebem suam*, and prayers asking that the Church be cleansed, that the congregation be guarded, and that mind and body be possessed. Ambrose alone names the cross; Augustine’s sermon stops at v. 15 and reads no crowd; and Cyril’s Luke reaches this page in an English of a Syriac version.

## The Propers: Notable and Quotable

Five afterlives of wording from the scriptural propers, each a use that moves the phrase into a register its writer did not occupy. Two elements supply all five: Gal. 6:2, 6:5, 6:7 and 6:9 have each become free-standing English proverbs; the Introit, the Gradual and the Communion have no such afterlife in the four corpora searched; and the Alleluia sings a reading the English Bibles do not carry, so no English corpus can be searched for it.

> **Epistle, Gal. 6:7 — the verse as the operative sentence of a money judgment**
>
> **Appointed text and locus.** Epistle, marginal no. 1584, printed p. 396: *Nolíte erráre: Deus non irridétur. Quæ enim semináverit homo, hæc et metet.*
>
> **Later text and locus.** *Benton v. Bakker (In re Heritage Village Church & Missionary Fellowship, Inc.)*, 92 B.R. 1000, **1022** (Bankr. D.S.C., 9 November 1988), Reynolds, Bankruptcy Judge. The final paragraph of the opinion, under the heading CONCLUSION: “In conforming with the religious overture of this case, this Court observes that James Bakker either overlooked or ignored parts of the Bible including I Timothy 6:10 … Now, however, we must apply Galatians 6:7 — ‘Be not deceived; God is not mocked: for whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap’. Let judgments be entered pursuant to this Memorandum Opinion.”
>
> **The turn.** In the lesson the verse is a warning inside a passage about supporting one’s catechist and doing good to the household of the faith. Here a federal bankruptcy court, having found that the president of a religious broadcasting corporation took excessive compensation from it, uses the verse as the pronouncement of civil liability: it does not illustrate the judgment, it delivers it, and the judge frames the borrowing as “conforming with the religious overture of this case.” The sentence a congregation hears read as exhortation becomes the state’s sanction against a preacher. The court quotes the Authorised Version exactly and names chapter and verse, so the dependence is explicit, and Bakker’s 1989 federal fraud conviction is a different case.

> **Epistle, Gal. 6:7–8 — the title and the mechanism of a 1917 War Office film**
>
> **Appointed text and locus.** Epistle, marginal no. 1584, printed p. 396: *Quæ enim semináverit homo, hæc et metet. Quóniam qui séminat in carne sua, de carne et metet corruptiónem.*
>
> **Later work and locus.** *Whatsoever A Man Soweth* (1917), silent film, 35mm, black and white, 2256 feet; director, producer and writer Joseph Best; sponsor, the War Office. Catalogued by the British Film Institute at Screenonline film id 1358391, with a curatorial analysis signed Bryony Dixon; corroborated in the Wellcome Collection catalogue, work id `n4b7rac9`, which lists it as the 1917 title in a British Film Institute collection of British sex-education films.
>
> **The turn.** The title is the King James wording of Gal. 6:7 verbatim, truncated where the proverb is usually truncated, and the BFI’s own curator states that the title is biblical and the film built as a sermon: “As the biblical title suggests, it is essentially a straight sermon, a form that its target audience would have found familiar both from church at home and during their military service.” **The film enacts the next appointed verse.** The synopsis records a Canadian soldier accosted by a prostitute outside the National Gallery, taken round hospital wards “occupied by victims of venereal disease at every stage, including rotting legs and hands,” a school for the blind where “more than half the children there contracted blindness as a result of hereditary venereal disease,” and the soldier’s brother infecting his wife and finding their child born blind. Gal. 6:8’s *de carne et metet corruptiónem* is staged as clinical corruption of the flesh, and the BFI’s own clip title for the temptation sequence is “Sowing wild oats.” Paul’s warning about self-deception in the moral life becomes a state hygiene instrument for keeping soldiers fit for duty, with the reaping specified as syphilis, congenital blindness and a ruined family. The sponsorship is the BFI record’s own.

> **Offertory, Ps. 39:2 — the incipit as the title of a soldier’s poem, and the choir taking it back**
>
> **Appointed text and locus.** Offertory, *Ant. ad Offertorium*, Ps. 39, 2, 3 *et* 4, marginal no. 1588, printed p. 397: *Exspéctans exspectávi Dóminum …* The uncorrected optical layers of the Pustet Ratisbon 1862, the Venice 1570 and the 1604 *typica* show *Expectans expectavi*, the 1570 with that book’s *u* for *v*.
>
> **Later texts and loci.** Charles Hamilton Sorley, “Expectans expectavi,” poem XXV in *Marlborough and Other Poems* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1916), pp. 65–66, dated at its foot “May 1915.” The poem opens “From morn to midnight, all day through, / I laugh and play as others do, / I sin and chatter, just the same / As others with a different name” and ends “With parted lips and outstretched hands / And listening ears Thy servant stands, / Call Thou early, call Thou late, / To Thy great service dedicate.” Sorley was killed at Loos on 13 October 1915, aged twenty, five months after the date under the poem, and the volume was published posthumously. Then Charles Wood, “Expectans Expectavi,” alternative title “This sanctuary of my soul,” for mixed chorus and organ, first published Boston: C. C. Birchard, plate no. 1147, 1919, with Sorley named as librettist.
>
> **The turn, twice.** Ps. 39:2 says the speaker waited and was answered; Sorley’s speaker is the one who does not wait — “From morn to midnight … I sin and chatter” — and the waiting is transferred to an inner shrine kept “Unlatched and lit” on the chance that God might “care / To enter or to tarry there,” with “Call Thou early, call Thou late” handing the initiative back. Sorley’s title, *Expectans expectavi*, sets the psalm’s confident perfect over a poem about not having been paying attention. The second turn is on top of the first: Wood’s 1919 anthem sets only the last two stanzas and is catalogued under their first line, so a poem whose point is a distracted young man is sung in choir as a straightforward act of devotion under a psalm title, with the confession cut. **Nothing in the poem or in its printing ties Sorley’s Latin to this Sunday’s Offertory rather than to the Psalter or the Vulgate at large**; the words are the same words, in the older spelling those three layers show, and they are the poem’s only Latin.

> **Offertory, Ps. 39:2–4 — the psalm closing a rock album, with the ending changed to a complaint**
>
> **Appointed text and locus.** Offertory, marginal no. 1588, printed p. 397. Douay–Rheims at Ps. 39:2–4: “With expectation I have waited for the Lord … and he put a new canticle into my mouth, a song to our God.”
>
> **Later work and loci.** U2, *War*, Island Records ILPS 9733, released 28 February 1983 in the United Kingdom; track 10 of 10 is “40,” 2:35. The album’s opening track is “Sunday Bloody Sunday.” Bono describes the adaptation in his own introduction to *Selections from the Book of Psalms* (Pocket Canons, 1999): “lost for words and forty minutes of recording time left before the end of our studio time, we were still looking for a song to close our third album, War. We wanted to put something explicitly spiritual on the record to balance the politics and the romance of it … We thought about the psalms … ‘Psalm 40’ … ‘40’ became the closing song at U2 shows and on hundreds of occasions, literally hundreds of thousands of people … have shouted back the refrain, pinched from ‘Psalm 6’: “How long” (to sing this song)”.”
>
> **The turn.** Ps. 39(40) is a thanksgiving: the waiting is over, the cry has been heard, the new song is already in the mouth. The song keeps the thanksgiving and appends a question from a different psalm — Ps. 6:4, *usquequo*, “how long,” a lament — so it ends not in the new song but in the question, and the crowd rather than the band is left singing it. On a record whose first track is “Sunday Bloody Sunday” the unanswered question lands on the Troubles. **The overlap with the appointed wording is the waiting-and-being-heard clause and the new-song clause and not the whole song**: the song follows the Authorised Version of Ps. 40:1–3, while the antiphon is the Roman chant’s Latin, which departs from the Vulgate at three places here, keeps only the opening clause of v. 3 and cuts the rest of it — the pit, the rock and the established goings, which the song keeps — and v. 4b with it. The statement about the refrain’s origin and the crowd’s response is the adapter’s own testimony.

> **Epistle, Gal. 6:2 and 6:5 — the lesson’s own internal tension argued out in wartime Parliament**
>
> **Appointed text and loci.** Epistle, marginal no. 1584, printed p. 396, containing both *Alter altérius ónera portáte, et sic adimplébitis legem Christi* (6:2) and *Unusquísque enim onus suum portábit* (6:5).
>
> **Later text and locus.** United Kingdom, House of Commons, Official Report, debate “National Scheme of Family Allowances,” HC Deb 23 June 1942, vol. 380, cc1853–944, at **c1895**. Mr Maurice Hely-Hutchinson, Member for Hastings, speaking against the Motion: “It is far simpler to base one’s whole attitude towards this as indeed towards all forms of social service on the words in the second verse of the 6th Chapter of St. Paul’s Epistle to the Galatians— Bear ye one another’s burdens than it is to struggle on to the fifth verse of the same chapter and there find the striking contradiction— For every man shall bear his own burden.” The interruption, at the same column: “Mr. George Griffiths (Hemsworth): That is not a contradiction but a confirmation.” And the reply: “It was the late Sir Arnold Wilson who called my attention to that apparent contradiction … The two verses reflect the use of the same English word ‘burden’ to represent two entirely different words in the original Greek … So the instruction appears to be that a man ought to carry his pack, and the rest of us ought to help him if it turns out to be so overwhelming a burden as to be a calamity.”
>
> **The turn.** The appointed lesson is treated as a self-contradicting statute to be construed. Paul sets the two sentences three verses apart in one paragraph about correcting a brother and testing one’s own work; in the Commons they become the two rival principles of the welfare state, in the debate whose subject-matter became the Family Allowances Act 1945. The reply is a lexical argument on the floor of the House, used to limit the state’s duty to what is “so overwhelming a burden as to be a calamity”; the retort from the Labour benches is the counter-reading. **The Greek argument in that speech is Hely-Hutchinson’s, and he attributes it to the late Sir Arnold Wilson**: his renderings “calamities” and “pack” are his own and are not established. Paul does write two different Greek nouns at 6:2 and 6:5, *baros* and *phortion*, which the Greek New Testament carries whatever was said in the House. The two phrases stand in twelve and in two spoken contributions across the whole Hansard corpus, several of them in the same class of debate, so this exchange is one attestation of a standing parliamentary habit.

## The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities

> Everything below is exploratory editorial and AI proposal. None of it is sourced historical intent, attributed teaching or documented reception, and none of it should be quoted as any of those. Each proposal joins at least two precisely named appointed elements and ends on the strongest thing that stands against it. **No connection here is claimed to be unknown, unprecedented, first or authored by the model**; where a proposal and a checked witness conflict, the witness wins. The precedent search behind each, its corpus and its bounds, is recorded in `research/scope.md` and summarised in the terminal appendix.

> **Editorial proposal — P1. Incapacity stated twice in one Mass, once of the Church and once of the person**
>
> **Anchors:** Collect <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span>, *et quia sine te non potest salva consístere*; Epistle <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>, Gal. 6:1 *consíderans teípsum, ne et tu tentéris* and Gal. 6:3 *Nam si quis exístimat se áliquid esse, cum nihil sit, ipse se sedúcit*
>
> **Mechanism:** The same denial made at two scales inside one formulary. The Church cannot stand safe of herself; the man who takes his own measure deceives himself. The Collect’s own grammar states the incapacity before its last petition, the semicolon after *consístere* being its hinge, as the Collect subsection of the commentary shows. The lesson does the same thing to the individual corrector, and Jerome, Aquinas and Augustine read it that way — Jerome noting that Paul warns of temptation and not of a fall, temptation lying in the tempter’s power, as the Epistle subsection of the commentary quotes him; Aquinas that nothing so breaks a man’s severity in correcting as the fear of his own fall; and Augustine requiring the corrector to answer God first that he acts from love.
>
> **Fruit:** A single doctrine of dependence preached at both scales in one hour: the ecclesiology of the Collect and the moral counsel of the lesson turn out to be the same sentence said twice, which is why the Mass can command correction of a brother without licensing severity.
>
> **What the element-by-element reading misses:** Read alone, the Collect is a conventional prayer for the Church and Gal. 6:1–3 is a rule of pastoral practice. Read together, the rule of practice is the Collect’s ecclesiology applied to one man, and the shared move is from an admission of nothing to an instruction about what to do next.
>
> **Strongest limit:** *Salva consístere* and *exístimat se áliquid esse* share no Latin root, so this is a doctrinal join throughout and not a word-play, and the shift from the ecclesial scale to the personal one is the proposal rather than an observation in the text. **Whether any checked expositor reads Gal. 6:3 ecclesially was not reached**, so the second half of the join has no reception behind it.

> **Editorial proposal — P2. The great King over all the earth, and the town he visits**
>
> **Anchors:** Alleluia <span class="nodecor">(*All.*)</span>, *et Rex magnus super omnem terram*, Ps. 94:3 as the chant sings it; Gospel <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>, Lk. 7:16 *Quia prophéta magnus surréxit in nobis: et quia Deus visitávit plebem suam*
>
> **Mechanism:** The chant’s substituted predicate is a claim of universal scope, and the Gospel that follows it directly puts God at one town’s gate. The chant’s *Rex magnus super omnem terram* names God king over the whole earth and the Gospel’s *prophéta magnus* a prophet risen among us, and the crowd’s second clause names the visit as God’s own. The substitution is not an invention of the chant’s: the predicate is the Latin Bible’s own phrase at Ps. 46:3, as the Alleluia subsection of the commentary shows.
>
> **Fruit:** The Mass’s widest predication of God and its narrowest scene of him stand within a few minutes of each other, so that the universality the Alleluia sings is what makes the visit at Naim more than a local wonder — and the Gospel’s own crowd, saying *Deus visitávit plebem suam*, is the one that draws the inference.
>
> **What the element-by-element reading misses:** Treated on its own the Alleluia is a verse of general praise, and no exposition located here rests on the predicate it sings. Set against the Gospel it turns out to be the element that states the scope of what the Gospel then narrates.
>
> **Strongest limit:** Severe and threefold. **No checked witness expounds the chant’s predicate at all**: Augustine and Cassiodorus both read *super omnes deos* and build their expositions on the very word the chant removes, so the proposal has no reception behind its key phrase. Nothing shows the chant took the phrase from Ps. 46:3 rather than from a psalter not opened here, no psalter of the older type having been collated at all. And the reading is inherited chant wording, standing in the uncorrected optical layers of the Pustet 1862, the Venice 1570 and the 1604 *typica*, so nothing about a 1962 compiler may be claimed from it.

> **Editorial proposal — P3. The ear the Mass asks God to bend, and the visitation the crowd reports**
>
> **Anchors:** Introit <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>, *Inclína, Dómine, aurem tuam ad me*, with the missal’s own *ad me*; Gospel <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>, Lk. 7:16 *et quia Deus visitávit plebem suam*
>
> **Mechanism:** The Mass opens by asking God to bend down toward one speaker and closes its Gospel with a crowd reporting that he has come. What makes it more than a theme is the added word: the tracked Clementine reads *Inclina, Domine, aurem tuam et exaudi me*, and the antiphon supplies a direction the Bible verse does not have, while the Gospel supplies the arrival. *Visitare* with God as subject stands in Luke four times only, at the loci the Gospel subsection of the commentary gives, and the crowd’s sentence at Naim is one of the four.
>
> **Fruit:** A shape for the whole Mass that is neither allegory nor coincidence: the first thing asked is a movement of God toward one voice, and the last thing narrated is a crowd of witnesses saying it happened. It gives the communicant a way of hearing the Introit as a petition the day’s own Gospel answers.
>
> **What the element-by-element reading misses:** The Introit’s *ad me* is otherwise only a textual curiosity, recorded in the divergence apparatus and dropped. Set against the Gospel’s *visitávit*, it is the element that makes the petition directional.
>
> **Strongest limit:** The addition is inherited chant wording and not a 1962 act, so nothing about a compiler may be claimed; *inclina aurem* is common psalter idiom and its recurrence proves nothing on its own; and **the appointed Gospel gives *Deus visitávit plebem suam* to the crowd and not to the Evangelist**, so a reading that makes it Luke’s own verdict is an interpretation and not the text. Cassiodorus’s lemma does carry *ad me*, so the addition is older than the Missal.

> **Editorial proposal — P4. What the cuts withhold, the Mass says elsewhere**
>
> **Anchors:** Offertory <span class="nodecor">(*Off.*)</span>, ending at *hymnum Deo nostro*; Introit <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>, *sperántem in te* and the half-verses it passes over; Secret <span class="nodecor">(*Sec.*)</span>, *Tua nos, Dómine, sacraménta custódiant*
>
> **Mechanism:** Two cuts in one Mass each withhold a word that another element of the same Mass carries: the hope the Offertory stops short of is sung already at the Introit, and the guarding the Introit passes over is asked at the Secret. The Offertory ends at *hymnum Deo nostro*, so Ps. 39:4b, *sperabunt in Domino*, is cut mid-verse and Ps. 39:5, *spes ejus*, is never reached, the Offertory subsection of the commentary giving the extent — while the Introit’s own antiphon already prays *salvum fac servum tuum … sperántem in te*. And the Introit’s centonisation passes over Ps. 85:2a *Custodi animam meam*, while the Secret asks *Tua nos, Dómine, sacraménta custódiant*: the verb the unsung half-verse used of the soul is the verb the Secret asks of the sacraments.
>
> **Fruit:** The Mass keeps saying, in prayers of its own, what its chants stop just short of singing. That is a way of hearing the orations as continuous with the chants rather than as a separate genre laid over them, and it gives the Secret — otherwise the least remarkable of the three prayers — a precise place in the formulary.
>
> **What the element-by-element reading misses:** A cut is normally noticed only as an absence. Here the absences are answered elsewhere in the same Mass, which no element-by-element reading can see, because the answer is never in the element where the cut is.
>
> **Strongest limit:** **The unsung matter is not appointed text, and a proposal that leans on it presses verses the assembly does not hear.** *Custodíre* is also among the commonest verbs in the Roman euchology, so the Introit-and-Secret leg rests on a coincidence of a very ordinary word.

> **Editorial proposal — P5. Let us walk, and Jesus was going**
>
> **Anchors:** Epistle <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>, Gal. 5:25 *Si spíritu vívimus, spíritu et ambulémus*, the lesson’s first imperative; Gospel <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>, *In illo témpore: Ibat Iesus in civitátem*, the pericope’s first finite verb as the missal prints it
>
> **Mechanism:** The appointed lesson’s first imperative and the appointed Gospel’s first finite verb are the same act, and the missal’s own arrangement puts them one after the other in the rite, with only a Gradual and an Alleluia between. The incipit is the missal’s own, standing where Luke’s connective stands, as the divergence table and the Gospel subsection of the commentary show, so that the first thing said of Christ in this Mass’s Gospel is that he was walking, immediately after the assembly has been told to walk.
>
> **Fruit:** A reading of the day’s two lessons as one instruction and its example, in the plainest possible form: the Epistle says walk, and the Gospel shows where the walking goes — to a gate, a bier and a widow. It also gives the Epistle’s opening imperative, which is otherwise a conclusion drawn from premises the Mass does not read, something in this Mass to attach to.
>
> **What the element-by-element reading misses:** Read separately, Gal. 5:25 is the tail of an argument about flesh and spirit and Lk. 7:11 is a scene-setting line. Read in the order the book prints them, they are an imperative and its enactment, and the enactment ends in mercy rather than in moral effort.
>
> **Strongest limit:** Obvious and severe. ***In illo témpore* is the standard Roman Gospel incipit formula throughout the book**, and *Ibat* is inherited from the Bible text’s own *deinceps ibat*, so the substitution moves the verb to the head of the pericope rather than creating it. A proposal that overstated this would be claiming a compiler’s wit where there is a formula. How many other 1962 Gospel pericopes have their incipit reshaped the same way is a whole-Missal count nobody has run, and the optical layers available would not support it.

### Evidence states and how claims are distinguished

Verified source text is wording checked against an identified primary edition: that is the Latin of all ten elements. A checked quotation is a published claim read at an exact locus in a named edition; that is every patristic, medieval and liturgical citation here, each carrying its locus at the point of use in *The Propers: Detailed Commentary* and, for the quotations the earlier pages carry, in *References*. Source-grounded synthesis is an editorial conclusion supported by checked evidence: the claims of *Source-Grounded Synthesis Across the Propers*. Editorial or AI proposal is labelled as such and confined to *The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities*. An unverified lead supports no published claim.

**Greek is given in transliteration throughout**, and the Greek witnesses are the 1904 Nestle text for the New Testament and Swete’s Septuagint for the psalms and 3 Kingdoms; those, and no other Greek edition, stand behind every Greek word printed. **No Greek patristic text was collated anywhere.** Cyril of Alexandria on Luke and on John, Chrysostom on Galatians and on John, and Gregory of Nyssa were all read in nineteenth-century English translation, and Cyril’s Luke commentary reaches English through an ancient Syriac version of a Greek original surviving elsewhere only in catena fragments. Where one of those witnesses makes a point about a Greek word, the point is reported as his argument and is not established here.

### Source scope, and what it does not reach

Everything checked in Latin was read in Latin: Ambrose, Augustine, Jerome, Bede, Hilary, Cassiodorus and Aquinas. The editions and their state vary and the variation is material. Ambrose on Luke was read as an optical layer of the critical edition, whose body text is sound and whose apparatus lines are mangled. **Hilary on Ps. 91 was read as an optical layer of a scan of the critical edition whose Latin recognition is poor**, whose running heads and apparatus are badly mangled and whose page-number sequence is unreliable; CCSL 61/61A was not consulted. Ambrose on Ps. 39 and Jerome on Galatians were read on page images with no text layer and transcribed by eye. ### Search limits and material negative results

Every negative below is the answer one corpus gave to one query, and a different corpus, a different phrasing or a non-English literature could overturn any of them.

- No Bible witness and no patristic or Greek lemma collated here reads *super omnem terram* at Ps. 94:3 or *miserere mihi* at Ps. 85:3. Both readings stand in the optical layers of the three older Missals read for the preexisting-material check, which are neither Bible text nor patristic lemma. Nothing in Swete answers the Introit’s added *ad me*, which Cassiodorus’s Latin lemma nonetheless carries.

- The Communion’s *dédero* and *sǽculi* answer nothing in the Greek, and no English witness examined — the tracked Douay, the Keating of 1806, the Cummiskey of 1861, the Lasance of 1945, Guéranger’s 1909 volume — renders either distinctly.

- *Naim* occurs once in the whole tracked Clementine, and *propheta magnus* only at Ecclus. 48:25 and Lk. 7:16.

- The eight-resurrections scheme the *Catena aurea* attributes to Ambrose is not in his Luke commentary, on a word search of the complete critical text; it may stand in another of his works, or be Ps.-Ambrose, or be a Glossa attribution.

- Gregory the Great has nothing on this pericope, on the evidence of the *Catena*’s lemma frequencies over Luke 7 and of the standard account of his forty homilies. PL 76 and CCSL 141 were not opened.

- No Father or Doctor comments on the three orations anywhere in this repository’s tracked sources or in the patristic and medieval works opened for this formulary; the patristic corpora at large were not searched, and a Father found quoting one of the three incipits corrects the result. None of the three occurs in the Veronense in two independent scans, in Férotin’s *Liber mozarabicus sacramentorum*, in Bannister’s *Missale Gothicum* or in Lowe’s *Bobbio Missal*. **Four bounds travel with that second negative**: it is a literal search over uncorrected optical text and cannot see a paraphrase or a recentonised descendant; three of the four books are diplomatic editions printing manuscript contractions; the *e caudata* failure demonstrated below would hide any of the three in any of the four books; and the corpus itself excludes every eighth-century Gelasian, every Ambrosian book, every Gregorian supplement beyond Wilson, every medieval monastic or diocesan sacramentary and every later Roman missal.

- No registered passage record exists anywhere in this repository’s source library at any locus of Luke 7, at any of the four appointed psalms, or at any verse of the appointed Epistle; no Graduale, Liber usualis, Cantatorium or Latin psalter other than the Clementine is registered at all, and the one Antiphonale registered is Hesbert’s *Antiphonale Missarum Sextuplex*, at catalogue level with no artifact, so that no chant book’s text is reachable here. No Psalterium Romanum, no Psalterium *iuxta Hebraeos* and no Old Latin psalter was collated for this formulary.

- **No qualifying cultural afterlife was located for the Introit, the Gradual, the Alleluia or the Communion**, across four corpora searched on 2026-09-05: the Library of Congress Chronicling America collection, the CourtListener opinion index, the UK Parliament Hansard contributions index and the Caselaw Access Project static corpus. The counts are recorded in `research/scope.md`. The Alleluia is closed to that sweep as a matter of text and not of effort, because no English corpus can be searched for a reading the English Bibles do not carry. **On this selection the gallery has no Gospel entry**: the one located non-preaching use of the appointed Gospel is contestable under the profile’s own rule, and the absence is left standing.

- Those four corpora are overwhelmingly American and English-language. No British, Irish, Canadian or Australian newspaper corpus, no non-English literature, no film or broadcast archive beyond two catalogue records, no advertising archive and no manuscript source was searched.

- The precedent search behind the five interpretive proposals ran inside this repository alone, over the tree at the commit `research/scope.md` records: the 669 proper-guide files that search swept across both providers and both calendars, the 628 article files it swept, both providers’ theology and history trees, the 1962 Ordinary and reference works beside this series, and the tracked 1962 calendar registry and commentary corpus index. Those are the sweep’s extent at that commit and not the collection’s present size. The open web, external sermon and homiletic databases, printed chant repertories and any universal literature search lie outside it, so *not located in the checked corpus* says only that this bounded corpus, fixed at that commit, does not hold the conjunction.

- No gallery entry rests on a search snippet: every legal candidate retained was read in the Caselaw Access Project’s continuous text of the printed reporter and the phrase confirmed there. **No page-image collation stands behind any gallery entry**, so none of the five is described as verified and the printed pointing of none of them is presented as settled.

**One method warning governs every literal search reported here.** A search over registered optical text layers produces false negatives, and the demonstration is on this formulary’s own prayers: a first pass over the Hadrianum layer returned nothing for *miseratio continuata*, *Mentes nostras et corpora* and *doni caelestis operatio*, and a second pass found all three, the layer rendering a footnote marker inside one word and a caret for the *ae*-ligature in another. Every negative here was run with loosened patterns and cross-checked against a second scan or a second pattern where one existed, and each remains correctable.

### Chronology

Every biblical date printed in this work comes from the Scripture chronology corpus and from nowhere else, carried in `research/chronology.toml` and projected mechanically into the Date column of the page-2 sheet. No date here was inferred, harmonised, or taken from a commentary or a chronological table. For all four psalm elements the composition answer is a single whole-Psalter boundary on a modern critical profile, which states in its own words that no individual psalm can be dated securely. For the three psalms whose titles name David the corpus also answers a traditional-attribution relation, marked disputed, which carries David’s reign in the usual chronology as a named reference point and not as a date of the psalm; that relation leads the Date cell, the explanatory row names its source, and the Gradual, whose title names no author, carries none. The Epistle carries five labels spanning nine years within one traditional profile, one preferred and four alternate, and all five are printed. The Gospel and the Communion each carry two disputed composition labels and one preferred narrated-event label, and the two kinds answer different questions — when the book was written and when the thing it tells of happened.

### Review state

The research behind this guide was carried out in one production and its audit record is `research/scope.md`, which holds the passage-by-passage reception matrix, the corpora and editions searched, the rejected and unresolved leads, the competing historical judgments, the gallery audit with its candidates not selected, and the audit of the interpretive proposals with their precedent classifications and search boundary. The bindings this publication makes to the repository’s reusable source library are recorded in `research/source-bindings.toml`. This is a hand-missal companion for study, not an official liturgical text, a critical edition or a substitute for the cited sources, and it has passed no specialist, clerical or ecclesiastical review.
