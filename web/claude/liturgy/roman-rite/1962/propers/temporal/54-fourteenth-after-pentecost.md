### The four senses

| **Sense** | **Synthesis** |
|:-----------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| Literal | A psalm asks God to look on the face of his Christ and prefers one day in his courts to thousands <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>. Paul sets seventeen works of the flesh against twelve fruits of the Spirit <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>. Christ forbids the service of two masters and anxiety about food and clothing, and commands that the kingdom be sought first <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>. |
| Allegorical | **The Latin tradition is namedly divided at the Gospel’s images** <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>: Hilary allegorises them all and **Godfrey of Admont makes the birds good angels, the lilies evil spirits**; **Jerome forbids allegorising the birds**, Augustine these examples at all, Christian of Stavelot the lilies. | Moral | The either/or of *Non potéstis Deo servíre, et mammónæ* <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span> is answered by order, not refusal: *Primum* <span class="nodecor">(*Comm.*)</span> — and Jerome reads the appointed verb the same way, *non dixit, qui habet divitias, sed qui servit divitiis* — though **Paschasius denies that *primum* orders anything**. |
| Anagogical | *Mélior est dies una in átriis tuis super mília* <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>: Augustine reads that one day as *diem sempiternum*, and Cassiodorus nearly the same, **probably depending on him**; **Arnobius reaches it by another route** and the Greek *Expositiones* by a third, while **Theodoret argues from a day’s profit**. *Perpétuæ … salvatiónis efféctum* <span class="nodecor">(*Postcomm.*)</span> is the Mass’s only reach past this life. |

## The Propers: Themes and Movement {#triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

This Mass was assembled from three strands counted on different schedules and settled at different dates, and one of its ten elements was exchanged rather than inherited: the Communion. **That element is where the formulary’s single thrice-repeated substantive word, its single dropped substantive and its single contested adverb all stand**, and around it the propers state an absolute either/or without contempt for created goods.

### 1. The kingdom, said three times

*Regnum Dei* is rare in Matthew — four verses against the thirty-two that carry *regnum caelorum*, thirty-four times over. It stands verbatim in three appointed elements, and **no other exact form reaching three or more of the ten is anything but a divine name or a function word**. Negative and positive are one claim: *qui tália agunt, regnum Dei non consequéntur*; *Quǽrite ergo primum regnum Dei*; then the same imperative made an address at reception, *dicit Dóminus*.

**And the element that repeats the phrase is the element that cuts it.** *Iustítia* stands once in the whole appointed Latin, in the Gospel’s last verse — and the Communion, quoting that verse, drops it. **That clause closes an arc the Vulgate opens at Mt. 6:1**, where Robinson–Pierpont’s Byzantine text reads *alms*, and *dicit Dóminus* stands nowhere in Matthew. **Chrysostom rests the promise on the verb the antiphon keeps** — “he said not, *shall be given*, but *shall be added*,” in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English, first series — and **his own citation drops *et iustítiam eius* as the antiphon does**, so the abbreviation is ordinary, as is a Communion drawn from the day’s Gospel; on that side the spine may be two elements and an echo, the Epistle’s standing alone.

### 2. The flesh that is crucified is not the body that is fed

*Carn-* stands five times in the Epistle and nowhere else, *corp-* twice in the Gospel and nowhere else. **That makes the two lessons one unit, not a contrast**, and a reading setting a mortifying Epistle against a providing Gospel has inverted it. **The Introit’s cut keeps the division clean:** its psalm verse stops one clause before *cor meum et caro mea exsultaverunt in Deum vivum*, the one clause that would have given *caro* a positive sense.

**Theodoret and Augustine reach the same conclusion about that flesh by three different middle terms.** Theodoret argues, verse by verse, that idolatry and sorcery belong to the soul, so Paul accuses not the flesh but the slothful cast of mind, and that a list of the soul’s works would otherwise stand beside them. **Augustine argues from the ascetic idolater**, convicted of works of the flesh while abstaining from bodily pleasure, and from ordinary speech, nobody saying *Malam carnem* to an enemy. **Nothing shows that either knew the other.**

**Four further positions stand on *caro* at v. 17**, and Jerome defends the missal’s own *vitia* against a reading of the body, *ut non naturam corporis videretur in spiritualibus viris negare, sed vitia*. The disjunction holds of these two pericopes and is no law of the two words; Ambrosiaster’s lemma is not the missal’s, so he does not speak to the two lists; and *luxúria*, *modéstia* and *cástitas* answer nothing in the Greek these Fathers read.

### 3. Divided at the images, and the most-repeated sentence withdrawn by the man who wrote it

**The Latin tradition is divided by name about whether the Gospel’s images may be figures.** **Augustine states the refusal in his own exposition of these verses**: the birds and the lilies “are not to be treated as allegories,” in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series. **Christian of Stavelot** refuses it at the lilies on aesthetic grounds, Jerome forbids reading the birds as angels, and Bruno of Segni reads the lilies literally. At the far end **Godfrey of Admont** makes the birds good angels, the lilies evil spirits and Solomon Christ; **Hilary allegorises every image into doctrine of the risen body**; and **Chrysostom refuses a different over-reading**, Christ abolishing not the sowing but the anxiety.

**And the sentence the tradition repeats most often at this Gospel’s first verse was withdrawn by its author.** Augustine’s *Nullius enim fere conscientia Deum potest odisse* is retracted at *Retractationes* I.19, naming his own exposition of this verse; five later witnesses repeat it *without his qualifier* *fere*; and **Bruno of Segni denies it outright**, the covetous man serving idols and therefore hating God. **At Mt. 6:33 the argument narrows to a single adverb**: Augustine reads *primum* as an ordering of importance and not of time, which the compilations transmit under his name as *non tempore, sed dignitate*, where **Paschasius denies that it marks order at all.**

### 4. Three excerpts stop short of one formula, and three chants divide the Fathers

*Beatus … qui sperat* stands in exactly two verses of the Clementine, and they are this Mass’s Offertory and Introit psalms, Ps. 33:9b and Ps. 83:13 — past both the Introit’s cuts and one clause past the Offertory’s, while **the Gradual alone is uncut and sings the hope vocabulary outright**, *sper-* and *confid-* twice each and in no other element. **The Introit belongs because the formula lies past its boundaries, not because it uses hope vocabulary**, and those extents are inherited.

Three chants carry divisions the Mass prints without adjudicating. **The Offertory’s angel has four accounts** — Augustine’s Christ, Cassiodorus’s moral sense, Arnobius’s angel of the sepulchre and Godfrey’s gift of grace. **The Gradual’s princes have four positions and a question**: Augustine’s good angels, Cassiodorus’s devil and good angel, Theodoret’s temporary earthly rulers, Godfrey’s interior and exterior senses — while **Bruno the Carthusian asks whether the verse is comparative at all**, where **Chrysostom answers that it is not**. **So the angel who rescues at one chant is the excluded object of trust at the other**, and Augustine softens that only by taking the Offertory’s *Angelus* for Christ. The Alleluia is still the least integrated: Cassiodorus hears in *iubilémus* a wordless jubilus and **Theodoret the shout of victors**; its medieval readers read the psalm penitentially through a verse it does not appoint, **but Augustine draws right joy against wrong joy from the appointed verse itself** — the Gospel’s and the Gradual’s own shape.

### 5. Three strands, one exchange, and the goods the Mass never asks for

**The strands were counted from different feasts.** The Gregorian Hadrianum heads this Mass *Dominica XV post Pentecosten*, one later than 1962; Menard’s book and the Frankish Gelasian books stand two later. **The chants did not move with them**: in the Roman antiphonary of PL 78 the Introit and Offertory agree with 1962 across twelve weeks, the first of which that book’s own body marks absent from the *Antiphonarium Romanum*, while the Gradual runs a week behind and breaks at this Sunday and its neighbour. **And the readings never moved**: the *Liber Comitis* pairs this Epistle with this Gospel, cut at the verses 1962 cuts. **Four mechanisms stand and no two agree**: Wilson’s numeration base; Schuster’s Roman cycle counted from St Lawrence, whose reckoning varied between books; **Berno of Reichenau’s two compilers**, on the embolismic and the common years; and the *Micrologus*’s vacant Sundays after the Ember fasts.

**And the arrangement 1962 does not have is a lectionary tradition, not one commentator’s displacement.** Honorius sets this Sunday’s Introit, Collect, Epistle and Offertory beside the ten lepers of Luke 17, as do Rupert, Sicard and Durandus; the Comes of Murbach pairs them so, one instance of a whole pattern of epistle *N* with gospel *N* − 1; and Sarum and the 1662 Book of Common Prayer still do. **When or why the Roman books moved to the pairing 1962 prints, none says.**

**The one exchange is at the Communion.** *Primum quaerite* is an ancient Roman antiphon standing at the *Ninth* Sunday, where this Sunday’s Communion is *Panis quem ego dedero* — which travels with the Offertory at *both* its occurrences. **All three legs of that rotation stand in the body of one printed chant book**, PL 78 giving *Primum quaerite* to the ninth, *Panis quem ego dedero* to the fourteenth and *Qui manducat carnem meam* to the fifteenth, exactly reversing 1962. **So the antiphon is secondary in this slot only and not in the repertory**, the substitution separating two chants the older books kept together. **That book dates the state and not the change**, and when the exchange was made no witness says. **What the Mass asks for and what it promises do not meet.** *Salut-* and *salv-* stand once each in the Collect, the Alleluia, the Secret and the Postcommunion, and in none of the other six elements. **No oration of the fifteen collated 1962 formularies asks for food, drink or clothing**, so this Sunday’s silence about its Gospel’s three goods is a genre fact — though others among them ask for peace, health or the world’s ordering. **The singularity lies on the reading side:** this Sunday alone of the fifteen names eating, drinking *and* clothing together. **And the orations withhold agency from the assembly:** in all three the acting subject is God, the victim or the sacraments — the settled habit of the Roman collect, with no concordance of other orations behind the contrast. **The assembly acts in its own right in six elements, every one a reading or a chant** — the Introit’s psalm verse, the Epistle, the Alleluia, the Gospel, the Offertory and the Communion — **and the Gospel states the same morphology**: the active denied of the man who cannot add a cubit, the passive promised him.

## The Appointed Formulary in Full

Everything the 1962 *Missale Romanum* prints for this Mass, in the order it prints it: the Latin of the Vatican *editio typica* exactly as the controlling facsimile has it, each element under its own printed heading, scriptural reference and marginal number, and beneath it the English of an identified public-domain witness.

**Formulary.** *DOMINICA DECIMA QUARTA post Pentecosten*, *II classis*, printed pp. 394–396, marginal nos. 1572–1581. The formulary begins immediately after the Postcommunion of the Thirteenth Sunday (no. 1571) and ends immediately before the heading *DOMINICA DECIMA QUINTA post Pentecosten* (no. 1582). Between those two boundaries the book prints ten elements and appoints no Tract, Sequence, second oration, blessing or ritual text, and no second Collect, Secret or Postcommunion.

**The English below.** Scriptural elements are the Douay–Rheims as revised by Bishop Challoner (1749–1752) — the English of the Clementine Vulgate the missal prints — in the Project Gutenberg e-text of that revision. The three orations are the anonymous English of *The Roman Missal translated into the English language for the use of the laity*, first revised edition (Philadelphia: Eugene Cummiskey, 1861), from the three lines that book supplies for this formulary, reproduced with its own nineteenth-century spelling and its abbreviated conclusion “Thro’.”

### 1. Introit <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span> {#proper-introit}

> ***Ant. ad Introitum* — Ps. 83, 10-11 — marginal no. 1572**
>
> *Protéctor noster, áspice, Deus, et réspice in fáciem Christi tui: quia mélior est dies una in átriis tuis super mília. <span class="nodecor">Ps. ibid., 2-3</span> Quam dilécta tabernácula tua, Dómine virtútum! concupíscit, et déficit ánima mea in átria Dómini. ℣. Glória Patri.*

> **English: Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Ps. 83:10–11 and 2–3 printed whole, with the portions the chant sings in bold**
>
> **v. 10** **Behold, O God our protector: and look on the face of thy Christ.** **v. 11** **For better is one day in thy courts above thousands.** I have chosen to be an abject in the house of my God, rather than to dwell in the tabernacles of sinners. *Ps.* **v. 2** **How lovely are thy tabernacles, O Lord of hosts!** **v. 3** **my soul longeth and fainteth for the courts of the Lord.** My heart and my flesh have rejoiced in the living God. ℣. *Glória Patri.*

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** The antiphon is a plain quotation, not a centonisation, but it stops in the middle of both its verses. It cuts v. 11 at *super mília*, immediately before the psalm’s own two-way choice between the house of God and the tents of sinners, and it cuts the psalm verse at *in átria Dómini*, before the heart and the flesh rejoice. The Douay carries no shortened form of either verse, and it carries no doxology cue at all: *Glória Patri* belongs to the Ordinary and stands outside the psalm.

### 2. Collect <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span> {#proper-collect}

> ***Oratio* — marginal no. 1573**
>
> *Custódi, Dómine, quǽsumus, Ecclésiam tuam propitiatióne perpétua: et quia sine te lábitur humána mortálitas; tuis semper auxíliis et abstrahátur a nóxiis, et ad salutária dirigátur. Per Dóminum.*

> **English: Cummiskey (Philadelphia, 1861), Collect of this formulary**
>
> Preserve, O Lord, we beseech thee, thy Church by thy constant mercy: and since our weak mortality is continually falling without thy assistance, may thy grace withdraw it from what is hurtful, and direct it in the ways of salvation. Thro’.

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** A noun and an adverb go missing. *Propitiatióne perpétua* becomes “by thy constant mercy,” so the substantive *propitiatio* — which this formulary alone of the fifteen this guide has collated uses twice — disappears from the English altogether; and *tuis semper auxíliis* becomes “may thy grace,” which drops both the plural of the noun and the adverb *semper* that the Postcommunion will use a second time.

### 3. Epistle <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span> {#proper-epistle}

> ***Léctio Epístolæ beáti Pauli Apóstoli ad Gálatas.* — Gal. 5, 16-24 — marginal no. 1574**
>
> *Fratres: Spíritu ambuláte, et desidéria carnis non perficiétis. Caro enim concupíscit advérsus spíritum, spíritus autem advérsus carnem: hæc enim sibi ínvicem adversántur, ut non quæcúmque vultis, illa faciátis. Quod si spíritu ducímini, non estis sub lege. Manifésta sunt autem ópera carnis, quæ sunt fornicátio, immundítia, impudicítia, luxúria, idolórum sérvitus, veneficia, inimicítiæ, contentiónes, æmulatiónes, iræ, rixæ, dissensiónes, sectæ, invídiæ, homicídia, ebrietátes, comessatiónes, et his simília: quæ prædíco vobis, sicut prædíxi: quóniam, qui tália agunt, regnum Dei non consequéntur. Fructus autem Spíritus est: cáritas, gáudium, pax, patiéntia, benígnitas, bónitas, longanímitas, mansuetúdo, fides, modéstia, continéntia, cástitas. Advérsus huiúsmodi non est lex. Qui autem sunt Christi, carnem suam crucifixérunt cum vítiis et concupiscéntiis.*

> **English: Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Gal. 5:16–24**
>
> \[*Fratres:*\] Walk in the spirit: and you shall not fulfill the lusts of the flesh. For the flesh lusteth against the spirit: and the spirit against the flesh: For these are contrary one to another: so that you do not the things that you would. But if you are led by the spirit, you are not under the law. Now the works of the flesh are manifest: which are fornication, uncleanness, immodesty, luxury, Idolatry, witchcrafts, enmities, contentions, emulations, wraths, quarrels, dissensions, sects, Envies, murders, drunkenness, revellings, and such like. Of the which I foretell you, as I have foretold to you, that they who do such things shall not obtain the kingdom of God. But the fruit of the Spirit is, charity, joy, peace, patience, benignity, goodness, longanimity, Mildness, faith, modesty, continency, chastity. Against such there is no law. And they that are Christ’s have crucified their flesh, with the vices and concupiscences.

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** The liturgical address *Fratres:* does not supplement Paul’s opening here; it *replaces* it. Paul’s own words at v. 16 are *Dico autem:*, which the Douay renders “I say then:” — the phrase that ties this verse back to the freedom of v. 13 and the law fulfilled in love at v. 14. Paul’s own vocative *fratres* stands at v. 13, inside the sentence the pericope drops. **The missal’s address is the missal’s own, and no registered English witness renders it.** Note also that the appointed Latin’s *homicídia*, which the Douay renders “murders,” stands in neither the Revised Version’s list nor in the critical-text English a modern reader is likely to be holding.

### 4. Gradual <span class="nodecor">(*Grad.*)</span> {#proper-gradual}

> ***Graduale* — Ps. 117, 8-9 — marginal no. 1575**
>

> ***Ant. ad Offertorium* — Ps. 33, 8-9 — marginal no. 1578**
>
> *Immíttet Angelus Dómini in circúitu timéntium eum, et erípiet eos: gustáte et vidéte, quóniam suávis est Dóminus.*

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** The antiphon stops at *Dóminus* and does not sing v. 9b, “blessed is the man that hopeth in him.” The Douay carries no form of the verse that stops where the chant stops. ### 8. Secret <span class="nodecor">(*Sec.*)</span> {#proper-secret}

> ***Secreta* — marginal no. 1579**
>
> *Concéde nobis, Dómine, quǽsumus, ut hæc hóstia salutáris, et nostrórum fiat purgátio delictórum, et tuæ propitiátio potestátis. Per Dóminum.*

> **English: Cummiskey (Philadelphia, 1861), Secret of this formulary**
>
> Grant, we beseech thee, O Lord, that this victim of salvation may both cleanse us from our sins, and render thy Majesty propitious to us. Thro’.

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** *Tuæ propitiátio potestátis* is turned from a noun into a verb and from power into majesty: “render thy Majesty propitious to us.” The Latin makes the victim itself the *propitiation of thy power*, parallel to its being the *purgation of our offences* — two genitives hung on one subject. The English is faithful to the sense and loses the figure, and the second occurrence of *propitiatio* in this formulary is invisible in it.

**Rubric as printed, immediately after the Secret:** *Præfatio de Ssma Trinitate.* · the Preface of the Most Holy Trinity. *Ssma* is printed under a tilde abbreviation for *Sanctissima* and is expanded here from the same book’s own abbreviation practice.

### 9. Communion <span class="nodecor">(*Comm.*)</span> {#proper-communion}

> ***Ant. ad Communionem* — Mt. 6, 33 — marginal no. 1580**
>
> *Primum quǽrite regnum Dei, et ómnia adiciéntur vobis, dicit Dóminus.*

> **English: Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Mt. 6:33 printed whole and unaltered, with the phrases the antiphon reuses in bold**
>
> **Seek ye** therefore **first the kingdom of God,** and his justice, **and all** these **things shall be added unto you.**

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** The Communion is an adaptation of Mt. 6:33, not a quotation of it, and it is the one substantive recasting in this formulary. Four things happen: *ergo* is dropped and *Primum* promoted to the head of the clause; *et iustítiam eius* is omitted; *hæc* is dropped before *ómnia*; and *dicit Dóminus* is added, which stands nowhere in Matthew at either this verse or its Lucan parallel. The Douay carries Matthew’s verse and nothing answering the antiphon’s recast form.

### 10. Postcommunion <span class="nodecor">(*Postcomm.*)</span> {#proper-postcommunion}

> ***Postcommunio* — marginal no. 1581**
>
> *Puríficent semper et múniant tua sacraménta nos, Deus: et ad perpétuæ ducant salvatiónis efféctum. Per Dóminum nostrum.*

> **English: Cummiskey (Philadelphia, 1861), Postcommunion of this formulary**
>
> May these thy mysteries, O God, continually purify us and strengthen us: and procure us eternal salvation. Thro’.

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** *Ad perpétuæ … salvatiónis efféctum* becomes “eternal salvation,” so the noun *efféctum* vanishes, and with it the figure the Latin uses: the genitive is split around the verb, exactly as the Thirteenth Sunday’s Postcommunion split *redemptiónis ætérnæ … augméntum* around its own. *Semper* is likewise absorbed into “continually.”

## The Propers: Detailed Commentary

### The typical edition against the Benziger printing

The Vatican *editio typica* prints this formulary at pp. 394–396 under marginal nos. 1572–1581. The Benziger *editio iuxta typicam* of the same year prints the same ten elements and differs from it in fourteen places — all of them accidentals of orthography, accentuation, pointing, abbreviation and layout, and none of them a different word. The table carries all fourteen in eleven rows, three of them taking a pair at once; the Collect stands there because over it the two books agree to the point, and the Epistle’s vice-list because the Benziger layer misrecognises its own book. **Three of the readings in the Benziger column were read on the Internet Archive item’s own single-page JP2 derivative of printed pp. 389–390, an unregistered derivative of a registered item**: the Introit’s *míllia*, the *fi*-ligature accents and the acute in *adiícere* at Mt. 6:27. Every other Benziger reading in the table stands in that item’s uncorrected optical text layer and not on any page of the book.

Two of these are worth more than a footnote. The two conclusions run in opposite directions inside one formulary — the typical edition abbreviates the Secret’s and prints the Postcommunion’s at length, Benziger’s text layer does the reverse — which is a useful reminder that “the 1962 Latin” is not one artefact. And the typical edition abbreviates *Ant. ad Introitum* here where on the Thirteenth Sunday it spelled *Antiphona ad Introitum* out: the book is not uniform with itself two pages apart.

One mark on these pages is damaged, and it is not text. The Gospel’s marginal reference number, printed at the outer edge of the scanned block on p. 395, has lost its fourth digit to ink loss. It is 1577, because 1576 and 1578 are both legible and the series 1572–1581 is unbroken. That is resolved by sequence and not by the second witness, which prints no marginal numbers at all. Nothing in the appointed text depends on it.

#### Twenty-one places where the appointed text is not the Bible’s text

The Clementine Vulgate is the text that matters for the comparison, being the Latin behind the Douay–Rheims Challoner English printed with these propers. Twenty-one divergences stand between the two texts, and each stands at both. Most are orthography or pointing. The ones that change what is said are the two cuts at the Introit, the cut at the Offertory, the Alleluia’s dropped psalm title, the Epistle’s displaced incipit, the Gospel’s supplied incipit, and — the only substantive recasting in the formulary — the Communion.

Three elements depart from the Clementine in nothing beyond the table, and that is what throws the Communion into relief. The Gradual is the Clementine’s own wording exactly, both verses, with nothing added, dropped or transposed. The Epistle after its incipit matches word for word apart from the i/j orthography, the one capital at Gal. 5:18 and the pointing tabled above. The Gospel after its incipit matches word for word apart from the i/j orthography, the æ for œ at Mt. 6:30, the sentence capital at Mt. 6:32 and the pointing tabled above. Three further differences are the arithmetic of quoting two Clementine verses continuously rather than departures the missal makes: at Ps. 83:10–11 the antiphon runs *Christi tui: quia mélior* where the Clementine ends a sentence and capitalises *Quia*; at Ps. 83:2–3 it runs *virtútum! concupíscit* for the Clementine’s *virtutum ! Concupiscit*; and at Ps. 33:8–9 the Offertory runs *eos: gustáte* for the Clementine’s *eos. Gustate*. **The only appointed element of this formulary that departs from its Bible text substantively is the Communion** — and, as the Communion’s own treatment below shows, even that departure is inherited rather than made here.

**No *Psalterium Romanum*, Gallican or Vetus Latina witness stands behind any chant reading above.** At the Offertory and the Communion an older Latin form is the likeliest explanation of what the missal prints, and without such a witness it cannot be told from a scribal or editorial variant.

#### Psalm numbering, and the rules behind the orations and the colour

> **Which psalm numbering the parentheses give**
>
> At two of the four appointed psalms the Masoretic number is not the number a reader will find in a modern English Bible. Vulg. Ps. 83:10–11 is Masoretic 84:10–11 but English **84:9–10**; Vulg. Ps. 33:8–9 is Masoretic 34:8–9 but English **34:7–8**. Ps. 117 has no title and Ps. 94 folds its title into v. 1, so at the Gradual and the Alleluia the three series agree. The superscription is what divides them: the Vulgate numbering the missal cites and the Douay carries counts it with the psalm’s first verse, the Masoretic numbers it as a verse of its own, and the English convention of the King James leaves it unnumbered and so runs a verse behind wherever a psalm has one. No Hebrew psalter stands behind the Masoretic column, which follows those editions’ own declared conventions.

> **Which instrument abolished the seasonal orations, and which rule prescribes green**
>
> The second and third orations older missals print at this Mass were abolished not by the 1960 *Rubricae generales* but by *Variationes in Breviario et Missali Romano*, cap. IV, n. 18, *Orationes pro diversitate Temporum abolentur*, in *Acta Apostolicae Sedis* 52 (1960), p. 709 — an instrument the 1962 Missal does not reprint. Inside the code itself the same result follows for this Sunday from RG 434 b with RG 435. And a citation hazard runs through it: the 1960 promulgation contains two different provisions numbered 18, RG 18 on the transfer of impeded Sundays after the Epiphany and *Variationes* cap. IV n. 18, so a bare “n. 18” cited for this Sunday names neither of them unambiguously. Colour is governed not by a temporal-season rule of part three of the 1960 code but by **part one**, at **RG 127 b**: *Color viridis adhibetur in Officio et Missa de Tempore: … b) a feria II post dominicam I post Pentecosten, usque ad sabbatum ante Adventum*, particularising the general rule at RG 117. *AAS* 52 (1960) p. 619 prints it, and *Missale Romanum* 1962, *Rubricae generales* p. XX, prints it again without a single verbal difference.

> **Which rubrics printed in this formulary are Tridentine and which are not**
>
> **One of the two rubrics printed inside this formulary is Tridentine, the other attested no earlier than 1889.** **The *Credo* rubric is Tridentine and unchanged**: the 1570 book prints *Symbolum, dicitur post Euangelium in omnibus Dominicis per annum*, with *Credo* printed in red in the formulary itself. **The Preface rubric is not.** The 1570 book heads the Trinity preface *Sequens Praefatio cum suo cantu dicitur in festo Sanctae Trinitatis* and its *Rubricae generales* contain no clause assigning a preface to the Sundays after Pentecost; the clause exists by 1889 and 1920 — *In Dominicis per annum … dicitur Praefatio de Trinitate* — and the 1960 code restates it as a preface *de Tempore* at n. 494. **Which instrument extended it, no document above names.** *Decreta authentica* vol. II, covering 1706–1870, yields sixteen hits on *Trinit* and a hundred and eleven on *praefat* in its whole optical layer, and **none of them pairs a preface grant with the Sundays after Pentecost.** No primary text quoted above carries the attribution commonly made to Clement XIII in 1759, and the grant may lie in the companion volume or in a commentator. **So a Trinitarian reading of the whole Mass cannot be pressed backwards past the sixteenth century on this book’s own evidence.**

> **What could displace this Mass, and when that changed**
>
> **The protection this Sunday enjoys is Pius X’s and not John XXIII’s.** Under the Tridentine rubrics any double of any grade took the Mass. From 1912 only a feast of the Lord, a double of the first or second class, or a day within an octave of a feast of the Lord could (*Divino afflatu* rubrics, Tit. III n. 2, *AAS* 3 (1911) p. 642). From 1961 only a first-class day or a first- or second-class feast of the Lord can (*Rubricae generales* n. 16, *AAS* 52 (1960) p. 598, with n. 14, *Officium et Missa dominicae impeditae nec anticipantur nec resumuntur*). **The 1962 heading conceals the size of what Pius X did**: the Sunday’s rite was still *semiduplex* in 1920, after the change that actually protected it, and the 1955 decree that renamed the rite *duplex* changed nothing about displacement. **Three moments, and they are not one**: a protection without a rank change in 1911, a rank change without a protection change in 1955, and a further protection change with a new rank name in 1960. **And the seasonal second and third orations were abolished twice, five years apart and in nearly the same words** — *Orationes pro diversitate temporum assignatae abolentur* at *Cum nostra hac aetate* Tit. V a) n. 1, *AAS* 47 (1955) p. 224, and *Orationes pro diversitate Temporum abolentur* at *Variationes* cap. IV n. 18. **Both are true of different moments**: the abolition dates from 1 January 1956, and the law in force from 1 January 1961 is the 1960 text, which *Rubricarum instructum* n. 2 records as having assumed the 1955 decree’s content when that decree ceased to be in force. The Missal’s own intervening printings, from 1956 to 1960, stand outside the editions collated above.

### How this Mass came to be this Sunday’s

#### The three orations do not descend as a unit

A formulary’s prayers need not travel together, and these three do not.

The **Collect and Secret stand together in one Mass** in the Gelasianum Vetus — Wilson’s 1894 edition, Book III sect. X, *Item alia Missa*, printed pp. 229–230 — and that Mass stands in a series of Sunday Masses which the base manuscript, Vatican Reginensis 316, numbers *not at all*.

The **Postcommunion comes from somewhere else**. *Purificent semper* stands in a floating series of unassigned Postcommunions appended after the Canon (printed p. 237), in exactly the form the 1962 book prints, *nos, Deus*; and separately in a saint-adapted recension in the Vigil Mass of St Andrew (Book II sect. **LXVIII**, p. 207), reading *nos, Domine* and adding the apostle’s intercession. Which recension is prior is settled by no witness above.

> **Which section the Postcommunion’s saint-adapted recension stands in**
>
> The running head above Wilson’s printed p. 207 reads **LXIX**, and **that head names the section the page *reaches*, not the one it opens in.** On that page sect. LXIX is *Item in natali eiusdem*, the Mass of St Andrew’s feast on 30 November, and it begins *below* the Postcommunion in question, which belongs to the preceding section — the Vigil Mass, **sect. LXVIII**. The same book sets the same trap at the Secret’s penitential home, where the running head above p. 257 stands over sect. **XLI**, which opens on p. 256.

> **What the Hadrianum’s printed section heading does with its numeral**
>
> The heading in Wilson’s 1915 volume reads *cXXXIII.*, where the *c* is a small raised footnote reference letter standing before the numeral and no part of it. The section is **XXXIII**: **the running section series XXVIIII–XLI across printed pp. 172–176 leaves no room for a section CXXXIII at all**, and the extra hundred is that raised *c* taken for part of the number. A citation to **CXXXIII** finds nothing on Wilson’s pages.

The **Secret has a second, penitential home** in the same book, Book III sect. **XLI** (printed pp. 256–257), among votive and occasional material — a Mass whose collects beg God to turn his anger away from his people. **And Wilson’s own marginal narrows that claim materially.** The marginal at sect. XLI reads *R. S.(alibi.)* — Wilson’s notation that R and S have the prayer *elsewhere*, that is, at the Sunday Mass. So the penitential Mass *as R and S transmit it does not contain this Secret*; the second home is attested for the Vatican manuscript and for Menard, while the other two references point back to the single Sunday occurrence. **The Secret’s “second, penitential home” is therefore a feature of the Vatican manuscript’s arrangement and not of the Gelasian tradition at large.**

**None of the three orations stands in the Veronense.** Feltoe’s 1896 *Leonian Sacramentary*, a diplomatic edition printing the codex’s contractions, carries none of the three; and **the Veronense has no ordered Sunday series at all** — it runs by month from April — so absence there is much weaker evidence about a Sunday formulary than absence from an ordered sacramentary would be. **But the Veronense does contain, twice, the prayer-shape the 1962 Postcommunion uses** — *Purificent* with holy things as subject and a second protective verb yoked to it, the closer reading *tua nos … sancta purificent, et ab externis erroribus perpetua virtute defendant* — which places the prayer’s *idiom*, though not the prayer, in the oldest Roman euchological stratum. **The likeness is one of diction and prayer-shape and not of derivation**, and that likeness too stands on what Feltoe’s edition prints. Nor do the three orations stand in Férotin’s *Liber mozarabicus sacramentorum*, Bannister’s *Missale Gothicum* or Lowe’s *Bobbio Missal* — **they belong to the Roman euchological stream and not, so far as six editions show, to the non-Roman Latin rites**.

#### The Sunday number has moved, and it moved by a measured amount

**Sixteen consecutive sections carry the correspondence.** Gelasianum Book III sects. I–XI and XII–XVI correspond to the 1962 Fifth through Twentieth Sundays, and **the offset survives a documented interpolation and resumes a new constant rather than becoming noise**: sects. I–XI stand at *N* + 2 for Rheinau, S. Gallen, Gerbert *and Menard*, and at *N* + 1 for Pamelius; sects. XII–XVI stand at *N* + 4 for the first four while Pamelius stays at *N* + 1 without a break. **Menard is present at every readable section and always agrees with R/S/Gerbert, never with Pamelius** — so the two Gregorian editions do not agree with each other. **Wilson’s per-section wording varies in scope** — sect. IX excepts the Secret, sect. V the Postcommunion, sect. VI covers “the two Collects and the Secret,” sect. XII “the first Collect, Secret, and Postcommunion” — **so the offset is uniform while the set of prayers it covers is not.** Each section’s 1962 identity is that of its own printed collect or Secret and never of its position, and the mapping agrees with the calendar: sect. XI prints the 1962 Fifteenth’s Collect and sect. IX the Thirteenth’s, with sect. X printing this Mass’s.

|:--------------|:----------------------------------------------------|
| **Fourteenth** | the 1962 Missal |
| **Fifteenth** | Pamelius; and, independently, the Gregorian Hadrianum supplement |
| **Sixteenth** | Rheinau, S. Gallen, Gerbert, Menard |

**Two independent lines reach the same magnitude.** The supplement to the Gregorian Hadrianum numbers this Mass *DOMINICA .XV. POST PENTECOSTEN*, one Sunday later than 1962, and the offset holds across **eleven** consecutive Masses, *Dominica XI* through *Dominica XXI post Pentecosten*, each identified from its own printed collect rather than extrapolated. **Where it breaks:** at *Dominica XXII* the cues are the 1962 Seventh Sunday’s set and at *Dominica XXIII* the received Twenty-third’s, an offset of one, and **nobody has established why.** In Cod. Ottobonianus 313 the marginal chant cues stand two Sundays later than the orations beside them, and that offset too holds across the same run. **A single misreading by one editor could not produce that.** **The chant side is uneven in two systematic ways:** the match is on Introit, Gradual, Offertory and Communion, the bracketed Alleluia cues matching the 1962 assignment at none of the eleven; and where the count is three or two of four, the odd element is always a Communion or Gradual displaced by one Sunday inside the same stretch.

**Where the offset comes from, and Wilson states it himself.** His Introduction (printed pp. lxxi–lxxiii) places the start of Book III’s series against the first part of the Gregorian’s third book, which makes the offset **a difference in where each tradition begins counting — a numeration base — and not an interpolation**: he names the mechanism in a footnote, the *post Pentecosten* / *post Octavas Pentecostes* equivalence arising from a discrepancy between the first and supplementary portions of Muratori’s text. **Which single Sunday each tradition counts as its first is not established**, so the decomposition of the two units of offset is not settled here. The break at sect. XII is doubly sourced — Wilson’s Introduction and his footnotes agree that R, S and Gerbert interpolate the two September Ember-week Sundays and rejoin at their twentieth — which is exactly the arithmetic that moves the offset from two to four while Pamelius stays at one.

**One of the five books prints the Mass itself at the locus.** Gerbert’s printed p. 173, under the heading *Dominica XVI. post Pentecosten*, gives a five-item Mass whose Collect, Secret *and* Postcommunion are the three composed orations of this formulary, plus a second Collect and a proper Preface; the printed page number matches Wilson’s independent marginal citation “Gerb. 173”, and the five incipits match his Appendix tabulation item for item. **His is an eighteenth-century printed text and not a manuscript**, set in long-s type. **And the Gregorian Hadrianum carries all three orations as one Mass**: Wilson’s 1915 section XXXIII, *DOMINICA .XV. POST PENTECOSTEN*, printed pp. 173–174, gives the Collect *Custodi domine quaesumus*, the Super oblata *Concede nobis domine quaesumus*, and the Ad complendum *Purificent semper et muniant tua sacramenta nos deus, et ad perpetuae ducant salvationis effectum* — **the 1962 Postcommunion word for word**, and the next section opens with the 1962 Fifteenth’s Collect. **So the whole 1962 oration set travels as a unit in Wilson’s Hadrianum at its *Dominica XV* and in Gerbert at his *Dominica XVI* — *N* + 1 in the Gregorian and *N* + 2 in the Frankish Gelasian — and it does not travel as a unit in Menard**, whose *Hebdomada XVI* carries this Collect with a different Super oblata, *Hostiam sacrificii singularis*, and a different Ad complendum, *Praesta nobis, misericors Deus*. **And the Postcommunion’s attestation is the narrowest of the three**: how the Vatican manuscript arranges its unassigned Sunday Masses bears on it, and so does Menard’s volume, which prints no occurrence of the prayer at all — a silence in one uncorrected optical layer of that printed volume, with no page image beside it, and so the book’s silence and not his manuscripts’. **The unit that moved in the older books is a Mass-set**, and all three of its orations are elements of the 1962 formulary: what travels in those books is a whole Mass — with, in Gerbert, a second Collect and a proper Preface beside the three — and what it amounts to in this one is three elements of ten, the second Collect and the Preface being no part of the 1962 formulary at all. **Wilson’s own footnote at Gelasianum sect. X covers the Collects and the Secret only**, so on the Gelasian side alone the assignment reaches two of the ten and not three.

**A near-miss at sect. X.** The bracketed Postcommunion there reads *Purificent nos, Domine, sacramenta quae sumpsimus, et a cunctis efficiant vitiis absolutos* — **a different prayer of the same incipit family**, which Wilson’s own index of incipits lists separately from this one, three printed occurrences each. **The two part company only past the incipit**, and the Vatican manuscript wrongly titles that prayer *Secreta*, an error of the scribe by Wilson’s note.

**“Four against one” is not four independent witnesses.** R and S are two Frankish “Gelasian” manuscripts Wilson collated himself, and they carry the sixteenth-Sunday heading directly. Gerbert’s principal manuscript “cannot now be traced,” and Wilson holds that the “Gelasian” portions of it, and so of Gerbert’s printed text, “do not represent the original text of the ‘Gelasian’ books, but a revision of that text, apparently based upon the text of the Sacramentaries of the ‘Gregorian’ type.” **So Gerbert does not merely fail to be a third Gelasian voice: it leans toward the very Gregorian editions supposed to corroborate it.** And Pamelius is itself an edition of the *Gregorian* book, so **Pamelius’s “fifteenth” and the Gregorian supplement’s “fifteenth” are not two independent witnesses to one Sunday**. The count is two collated Gelasian manuscripts, plus a printed edition largely dependent on a lost manuscript copied from one of them and revised toward the Gregorian type, plus one Gregorian edition — with the other Gregorian edition one Sunday earlier. **Gerbert’s own printed section heading agrees with what Wilson cites from him, which excludes a mis-citation without adding a fourth independent voice: he is the least independent of the five.** R’s and S’s own Sunday headings are Wilson’s own collation and do not depend on Gerbert at all.

**And Gerbert’s unreliability does not weaken R and S**, whose Sunday headings Wilson collated himself. His “exceedingly confused and misleading” is hedged with “at times,” applies to Gerbert’s *handling and explanation of his materials* rather than to his text as such, and stands immediately after his warm tribute — “students of Liturgy owe so much as we owe to Gerbert.” Wilson also excepts “the latter part” of R from his criticism, and whether these Sunday Masses fall inside that part is not established, **so the unreliability cannot simply be assumed to reach them.**

**Wilson does not say the same thing about all five books.** Of R, S and Gerbert he writes that the prayers “**are assigned by**” them to the sixteenth — a statement about those books’ own Sunday headings. Of Pamelius and Menard he writes only “**see the *Missa* for** the … Sunday in,” which is a cross-reference to a *parallel* Mass and need not mean an identical composition. **The two formulas are two different strengths of claim.**

**And his apparatus is not a rumour**: it is a published critical collation, and for R and S specifically it is his own reading of manuscripts physically in front of him.

#### Honorius’s Fourteenth Sunday is not the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday

By the early twelfth century the 1962 Sunday number and this Introit are already paired. *Gemma animae* IV.72 is headed *Dominica decima quarta post Pentecosten « Protector noster, » sub lege*, and the quotation is exact: the neighbouring chapter headings in the same column print no *post Pentecosten* and the following one does.

**But the rest of the chapter shows the opposite half of the picture.** Within one and the same chapter Honorius’s **Introit, Collect, Epistle and Offertory are the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday’s**, while his **Gospel is the 1962 Thirteenth Sunday’s** — Luke 17:11–19, the ten lepers — and that Gospel offset is not a slip, since it holds across three consecutive Sundays. His **Gradual** (*Bonum est confiteri Domino*, Ps. 91) is not the 1962 Fourteenth’s (*Bonum est confidere in Dómino*, Ps. 117:8–9); his **Alleluia** (*Quoniam Deus magnus*, Ps. 94:3) is a different verse of the same psalm the 1962 Alleluia uses; and his **Communion** is *Panis, quem ego dabo*.

**And his three differing chants are not three separate facts.** Set against the 1962 chant sets as a modern registry of them gives them, and not as pp. 396–398 print them, his Gradual, Alleluia and Communion are **precisely the 1962 Fifteenth Sunday’s chant set**: within one chapter his chant strand stands one Sunday *later* than his oration strand while his Gospel stands one Sunday *earlier*, a spread of exactly two Sundays. **That converges in direction, though not in magnitude, with the Ottobonianus cues below**, where the chant set beside *Custodi, Domine* also runs later than the orations: **two independent witnesses in which the chants of this Mass run later than its orations, at +1 and +2 — they do not corroborate a single number.**

**And he reads this formulary three times over, not once.** *Gemma animae* IV.72 reads it *sub lege*, under the type of Ezechias sick to death; IV.73 reads it a second time *sub gratia*, under the type of Theodosius the Great, the council of his reign standing in the Epistle’s catalogue of vices and virtues and Ambrose’s penance behind the Communion; and IV.74 is not a typology at all but **a liturgical-calendar explanation**, tying the Offertory to the September night office’s *historia* of Tobias and Raphael and to Michaelmas, and then explaining the autumn Ember ordinations. **The paired *sub lege* / *sub gratia* structure is the book’s method and not a one-off**, chapters LXX and LXXI doing the same at the Thirteenth Sunday.

**His September reading is one of five, and no two of them agree.** **Sicard of Cremona** ties the Offertory to Tobias and Raphael independently of him and on his own September calendar — *quae quidem offerenda, bene simul cum Tobia cantatur, cum et ejus filius per angeli obsequium liberetur* — putting Tobias in the second week of September; **Durandus** reads the Sunday under Judith instead and assigns Tobias to his Thirteenth; **Rupert** has a scheme of his own; and **Amalarius**, who is the authority usually reached for here, gives Job weeks one to three and Tobias week four, for a medicinal reason. **John Beleth** is named beside them as a sixth, and no September locus of his *Rationale divinorum officiorum* stands behind that name. **The reading is not Amalarius’s**: **his *Patrologia Latina* volume nowhere connects this Offertory to Tobias.** **So the September *historia* is not one thing: Tobias names one scheme of the five and not the tradition’s.**

**And the September Ember days do not reliably follow this Sunday.** The rule changed between the 1920 and the 1962 typical editions, from *post Festum Exaltationis sanctae Crucis* to *post dominicam tertiam septembris*. **Over a thousand years the September Ember days fall in the week following this Sunday in ninety-three years under the 1962 rule and in a hundred and seventy-seven under the older one** — fewer than one year in ten, and fewer than one in five. The pre-1962 arithmetic assumes that a 14 September falling on a Wednesday sends the Ember days to the following week, a reading that is usual and that no rubric of either book states, and about one year in seven turns on it. **No instrument prescribing the change of formula stands in *Acta Apostolicae Sedis* 52 (1960)**: *Exaltationis* occurs there twice, both times as a calendar entry for the feast, and no *Quatuor Tempora* formula stands in the volume at all.

**What survives of the Ember connection is about the psalm text and the Gradual and not about the Introit chant, which the Ember days do not have.** *Protector noster, aspice, Deus* is the Gradual at the Monday of the first week of Lent, the second Gradual at the Lenten and September Ember Saturdays, the Gradual at the Fifth Sunday after Pentecost, and the Introit here; and this Sunday’s Gradual *Bonum est confidere* stands among the September Ember Saturday responsories. **Two of those three attestations are qualified**: the September Ember *Bonum est confidere* stands inside the editor’s brackets, which he explains as marking a codex’s additions judged less authentic, while a footnote concedes that Compiègne exhibits them; and the March Ember *Protector* reaches the page only through a footnote reporting what Mont-Blandin implies and what the *Micrologus* recommends. **Honorius explains this formulary by the September night office and the autumn Ember ordinations, and the book’s own arrangement puts the September Ember days between *Hebdomada* XVII and XVIII while two of this Mass’s chant texts stand in the Ember repertory.** **His *sub lege* reading is coherent only with the Gospel he has**, the grateful leper, so it does not transfer to the 1962 formulary. And Migne prints *plusquam octoginta millia* for the number the angel slew where 4 Kings 19:35 has a hundred and eighty-five thousand — his printed column carries that figure, so it is the edition’s and not a recognition error, and no source above explains it.

**And the printing misplaces three references, not two.** In Migne’s text *(Luc. XVII)* stands against the Gradual and *(Psal. XCI, 2)* against the Gospel — each is the other’s, since *Bonum est confiteri Domino* is Ps. 91:2 and the ten lepers is Luke 17 — and **a third reference, *(Psal. LXXXIII, 10)*, stands against the Collect sentence where it belongs to the Introit words quoted just before it.** **A reader taking any of the three at face value from this column will be misled.** **Migne’s chapter is a rights-restricted facsimile**, and its heading, its figure for the slain and its three misplaced references are short strings from it and not its exposition.

Honorius’s Ember week and the Masses Wilson reports as interpolated may or may not be the same disturbance; **the two witnesses number their Sundays differently**, and neither settles it.

**The compiler of the Gregorian supplement stays unnamed.** Wilson attributes it to Alcuin.

The Ottobonianus cue *b* reads *Bonum est confidere* where 1962 gives the Fifteenth Sunday *Bonum est confiteri Domino*. Manuscript reading, shared respond, or error: no witness above settles which.

**The Ottobonianus cue attachment stands at an offset of two.** The cue block whose Introit is *Protector noster* attaches to Wilson’s sect. **XXXI**, *DOMINICA .XIII. POST PENTECOSTEN*, whose Collect is the 1962 *Twelfth* Sunday’s — an offset of +2 and not zero. On Wilson’s printed page **the superscript reference letters *a*, *b*, *c* stand immediately before the section numbers and the three lettered footnotes stand at the foot of the same page**, so a text that fuses the letter with the numeral attaches the block to the wrong section and yields a zero offset. At either attachment, the cue set whose Introit is *Protector noster* — this Mass’s own — has Communion *Panis quem ego* and not *Primum quaerite*.

#### Schuster’s three pages: an ancient title, a mechanism, and four absences

Schuster’s *The Sacramentary* vol. III gives this formulary three pages, pp. 136–138. **He gives the formulary an ancient title:** *Quarta post Sancti Laurentii*, the fourth Sunday after St Lawrence. **He gives the unstable numbering a mechanism that is not Wilson’s:** the post-Pentecost series was interrupted at Rome by a cycle counted from St Lawrence, and “the sequence of these Sundays after St Lawrence was not everywhere the same, for, whilst the Roman documents of the Frankish period usually reckon five Sundays after the natalis of the Staurophoros, the Calendar of Fronteau has only four”; the Lawrence cycle was succeeded at Rome by cycles around St Cyprian and then St Michael, these feasts serving “as so many milestones … and had no special connection with the saint whose name they bore.” **The two mechanisms stand side by side**: neither has been shown to produce the offsets Wilson collates, and the arithmetic joining them has not been done. **And his own title is a fourth reckoning**: the *Liber Comitis* calls this Sunday the *fifth* after St Lawrence where Schuster calls it the fourth — **an instance of the variation he himself describes, and not an error in either.**

He notes a progressive Introit-psalm series across these Sundays, two Lenten cross-references for the chants, and comments continuously on all three orations, at more length than Guéranger. **He reads the Secret’s *tuæ propitiátio potestátis* as the appeasing of the divine omnipotence**, where Guéranger arrives independently at the same construction; and **his Communion paragraph supplies the *et iustítiam eius* the antiphon drops**, glossing the justice from the Gospel verse rather than from the antiphon.

**Four absences on those same pages, and they bear on whether he corroborates the displacement independently.** **He names no station church for this Sunday** — where his next page does name one for the Fifteenth — **no sacramentary siglum, no manuscript, and no note that the Communion antiphon is secondary.** **On these pages he does not corroborate the Gelasian and Gregorian displacement**, which therefore still rests on Wilson’s apparatus and Gerbert. Those are absences from his pp. 136–138 and not from the whole volume, whose introduction and whose index of sacramentary references lie elsewhere. **His paraphrases are English and are not text witnesses.**

#### The chants and the readings: the two strands that did not move with the orations

**The chant books number this formulary as 1962 numbers it.** The ninth-century antiphonary formulary carrying this Introit, Gradual and Offertory stands as *Dominica XIV*, and the Ottobonianus margin reproduces those formularies **one number above** the sacramentary headings they stand against — twenty-five of twenty-five slots across five consecutive Masses, **the bracketed Alleluia cues matching nothing in 1962.** **And the Communion of that formulary is not this one:** *Primum quaerite* stands at the *Ninth* Sunday in four of those books, while the Fourteenth Sunday’s Communion is *Panis quem ego dedero* — which **travels with the Offertory *Immittet Angelus* at both its occurrences**, the Lenten Thursday and this Sunday. **So the exchange at the Communion separated two chants the older books kept together**, and the antiphon it brought in is an ancient Roman antiphon of another slot rather than a late composition. **Those four antiphonaries’ readings are a modern database’s record of Hesbert’s *Antiphonale Missarum Sextuplex* and not the *Sextuplex*’s own printed pages.**

**The readings, alone of the three strands, never moved.** The *Liber Comitis* as Migne prints it pairs *Fratres, spiritu ambulate, usque concupiscentiis* with *Nemo potest duobus dominis servire, usque adiicientur vobis* under *Dominica v post sancti Laurentii* — **the same two incipits and the same two termini the 1962 book prints**, so the Epistle–Gospel pair is not among the things that moved, and the pericopes were already cut where 1962 cuts them. **The Comes’s own date and provenance are not established.**

#### The strands come apart inside one printed book

**The Roman Mass antiphonary printed in *Patrologia Latina* 78 — Pamelius’s edition collated against the Compiègne antiphoner and with variants from Mont-Blandin — lets the three strands be watched week by week in one book.** **Its Introit and Offertory series agree with 1962 at every week from the seventh to the eighteenth**, this Sunday’s Introit standing at *Hebdomada decima quarta* with its psalm verse word for word as 1962 prints them. **Its Gradual series runs one week behind 1962 across a long run and breaks at exactly the two weeks this Sunday and its neighbour occupy.** **And its Alleluia agrees with 1962 at only two weeks of twelve — at both of which the collated manuscripts disagree with the printed text**, so that agreement is evidence about the editor’s exemplar rather than about the ninth-century state. At this Sunday the witnesses part in opposite directions: Compiègne, the oldest and best of them, agrees with 1962 at the Gradual and disagrees at the Alleluia, and the printed text does the reverse. **No witness here has 1962’s chant set entire.**

**But the book disagrees with itself about which week is which, and it disagrees at the week that series opens on.** At col. 717 its body prints, as a rubric of its own, *Hebdomada septima. Ista hebdomada non est in Antiphonario Romano* — and *Antiphonarium Romanum* is nowhere defined on that page. Footnote a there records that in Theodericus the office of that Sunday stands at the eighth and the eighth’s at the ninth, *sicque consequenter*: a displacement of one running forward through exactly the run of weeks tabulated above, and beginning at the week that run opens on. Footnotes d, f and g on the same leaf run the other way, reporting Theodericus’s Alleluia at week *N* as the printed book’s at *N* + 1. **The two statements cannot both be read as they stand**, and nothing in the book resolves them, **so a Theodericensian reading printed at a given week is not established as Theodericus’s own week.** On that bound the Theodericensian codices agree with 1962 at neither the Gradual nor the Alleluia *where this book prints them*, and what they carried at their own fourteenth is open. **This edition is a route to the Compiègne and Mont-Blandin readings and not a substitute for a critical apparatus**: Pamelius prints an edited text with variants in footnotes where a critical edition would print the manuscripts in parallel, and a footnote reporting a reading is not a transcription.

**And the two Gregorian editions that disagree about this Sunday’s number are editions of two different manuscripts.** Pamelius first published the Grimoldus codex; Menard’s base is the Rodradus codex; and **Menard argues at length in his preface that the two are separate compilations**, so their disagreement is a disagreement between two books rather than between two editorial habits. He is an interested party arguing for his own manuscript’s Gregorian authenticity, and whether Wilson’s siglum *Pam.* cites Pamelius’s Grimoldus text is not established.

**Menard’s book also breaks the unit.** His *Hebdomada XVI post Pentecosten* carries this formulary’s Collect word for word — and with it a *different* Super oblata, *Hostiam sacrificii singularis*, and a *different* Ad complendum, *Praesta nobis, misericors Deus* — while his very next section carries all three of the 1962 Fifteenth Sunday’s orations together. **So the three orations do not travel as a unit everywhere**, and his sections carry proper Prefaces, episcopal blessings and Super populum prayers throughout, which are Supplement and Frankish features. **The Secret has no Sunday home in that book at all**: it stands there twice, as the Super oblata of the Lenten station at St Chrysogonus and in a penitential votive series, **and the 1962 Passion Monday Secret is the first of those verbatim while this Sunday’s is the second with one word changed** — *delictórum* for the votive series’ *peccatorum* — so the doublet the 1962 Missal prints is inherited and not a modern accident, and this Sunday’s form is the votive one. No stemma is established. **And the 1962 Postcommunion does not occur anywhere in that volume**, neither in the sacramentary nor in its appendix, notes or antiphonary; that silence is the printed book’s and does not reach his manuscripts; what stands is a real divergence between two Gregorian witnesses, Wilson’s Hadrianum having this Postcommunion at its *Dominica XV* and Menard’s book appearing not to have it at all.

#### Two more mechanisms for the numbering, and neither is the other two

**Berno of Reichenau explains the instability by a difference in the number of Masses, not in where the counting starts.** In his *Libellus* he reports that the compiler of the chant-offices provided twenty-three Sundays between the octave of Pentecost and Advent while the compiler of the Lectionary provided twenty-five, **because one computed on the embolismic years of the nineteen-year cycle and the other on the common years.** **That is the shape of a book whose strands run on different schedules**, which is what the evidence above shows. **He writes four centuries after the arrangements he explains and offers a rationale rather than a record**; his twenty-three is not the 1962 count; and he never reaches this Sunday.

**The *Micrologus* names a fourth mechanism**, and it is the only one of the four that names an instrument still visible in the 1962 book: the vacant Sundays after the Ember fasts, whose orations, mishandled, disturb the order of the offices as far as Advent, the Romans making up the shortfall by repeating a Sunday’s office rather than deferring it. **It speaks of the orations and cannot be transferred to the chant or reading strands**, and it is a late eleventh-century book prescribing against a practice it disapproves.

#### The pairing 1962 does not have is a lectionary tradition

**The arrangement in which this Sunday’s Epistle stands beside the ten lepers is held by seven witnesses across four centuries and three kinds of book**, so Honorius is no outlier and the Roman pairing is not simply the ancient one. The eighth-century **Comes of Murbach** has it, at items CXXVIII–CXXVIIII in Wilmart’s edition — **and there it is one instance of a whole systematic pattern of epistle *N* with gospel *N* − 1, not a peculiarity at this Sunday.** **Rupert of Deutz**, **Sicard of Cremona** and **Durandus** all set this Sunday’s Epistle beside the ten lepers and put Matthew 6 on the Fifteenth; **the Introit, Gradual and Offertory of this Sunday are expounded by Rupert and Sicard, and Durandus’s chant set is not the Roman one but the one Sarum has and the one Compiègne has** — Gradual *Bonum est confiteri Domino*, Alleluia *Confitemini Domino et invocate nomen eius*, Communion *Panis quem ego dedero* — against Rupert and Sicard, who have the Roman Gradual. The two early printings of the *Rationale* agree on it, so the narrow difference between his *Bonum est confiteri* and the Roman *Bonum est confidere* is not a printer’s slip. **No filiation between Durandus, Sarum and Compiègne is established**, and Compiègne stands with Rome at the Gradual and against it at the Alleluia, so none of the three is simply the older reading. **Durandus attaches *in quibusdam ecclesiis* to the Epistle, the Gospel, the Alleluia and the Communion of this Sunday but not to its Introit or Offertory**, which is the strongest single evidence that the pairing was contested in his own day — and the Alleluia and the Communion he so marks are his own chants and not the 1962 texts. **The Sarum Missal** has it at *Dominica XIV post Trinitatem*, and **the Book of Common Prayer of 1662** still prints the marginals *Gal. 5. 16.* and *S: Luke. 17. 11.* **The seventh witness carries it at one element only:** *Patrologia Latina* 78’s Roman Mass antiphonary gives this Sunday the Communion *Panis quem ego dedero*, and says nothing about its Epistle or its Gospel. **When or why the Roman books moved to the pairing 1962 prints, none of them says**, and whether the English books inherit from the Frankish comes, whether both descend from a common Roman state, or whether Rome’s own pairing is the innovation is not established.

**And the two reading strands were not numbered alike even in the seventh-century Roman books.** The Würzburg gospel capitulary numbers this Gospel *ebd. IIII post sci. Laur.*, the fourth after St Lawrence; **the Würzburg epistle list numbers Gal. 5:16–24 not at all**, laying its forty Pauline pericopes out in canonical order without titles; and Wilmart’s reconstruction of the Murbach series makes this Epistle the fifth of the five post-Laurentian ones. **So the *Liber Comitis*’s “fifth” and Schuster’s “fourth” are two books counting differently, and neither is wrong.** Alcuin’s fifth is a reconstruction keyed to Würzburg item numbers and not a heading read in a manuscript, and the sanctoral adjacency at 11 and 14 September places this Mass in the book’s sequence without stating a date it fell on.

**The Sarum Missal splits this formulary across two consecutive Masses, along exactly the line the strands divide on.** The whole 1962 oration set and the 1962 Gospel stand at *Dominica XV post Trinitatem*; the 1962 Introit, Epistle and Offertory at *Dominica XIV*. **Two offsets must be kept apart here**: calendrically Trinity *N* is Rome’s Sunday *N* + 1, while internally Sarum’s Collect, Gospel, Secret and Postcommunion at Trinity *N* are Rome’s at Sunday *N* − 1. **And this formulary’s Collect survives in English as the Book of Common Prayer’s collect for the Fifteenth Sunday after Trinity** — “Keep we beseech thee, O Lord, thy Church with thy perpetual mercy.” **No edition consulted prints a source apparatus for the collects, and none says the collect is Cranmer’s rendering of the Sarum Latin.**

### Introit <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>

*Protéctor noster, áspice, Deus* *Ant. ad Introitum* — Ps. 83, 10-11, with *Ps. ibid.,* 2-3 marginal no. 1572

**Psalm 83 is a psalm of the sons of Core about desire for the sanctuary**: the sparrow and the turtle-dove finding a house at the altars (v. 4), those who dwell in the house (v. 5), the ascents in the heart through the vale of tears (vv. 6–7), *ibunt de virtute in virtutem* (v. 8). **What those verses support is movement toward the temple.** **The text does not state that the psalm is *for* pilgrims or that it voices their sentiments.**

**What the chant does to it, and the two cuts have opposite effects.** It sings the psalm backwards: the antiphon from the end, vv. 10–11a, the psalm verse from the beginning, vv. 2–3a. Cutting v. 11 removes the psalm’s explicit two-way choice between the house of God and the tents of sinners. Cutting v. 3 removes the rejoicing of heart and flesh, *cor meum et caro mea exsultaverunt in Deum vivum* — **the only place in the formulary’s psalm sources where *caro* carries a positive sense**, which bears directly on the Epistle’s use of the word. **Both dropped clauses are the psalm’s, not the antiphon’s, and the antiphon asserts neither.**

**The capital is the printer’s.** The missal prints *fáciem Christi tui* where the Clementine prints lower-case *christi*. **Capitalisation in a modern electronic Clementine is that e-text’s editorial convention and capitalisation in the 1962 missal is that book’s; neither is evidence of what the Latin intends**, and in the psalm’s own sense *christus* is the anointed king. The christological reading rests on the Fathers, who do argue it, and never on the printer.

> **Augustine, *Enarratio in Ps. LXXXIII* §6 and §14.** **He reads the two halves as one movement of desire.** On the psalm verse, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English: “My soul longs and fails for the courts of the Lord. It is not enough that it longs and fails: for what does it fail? For the courts of the Lord.” On the antiphon: “one day there is better than a thousand days. Men long for thousands of days … let them long for one day, which has neither rising nor setting.” His Latin runs: *contemnant millia dierum, desiderent unum diem, qui non habet ortum et occasum; unum diem, diem sempiternum, cui non cedit hesternus, quem non urget crastinus.* **So on his reading the antiphon is the answer to its own verse.**

**The Father’s unit and the liturgy’s unit do not coincide.** Augustine’s §15 expounds the *rest* of Ps. 83:11 — in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series, “I have chosen to be cast away in the house of the Lord” — which is exactly the clause the Introit cuts off after *super mília*: he comments on the psalm, not on the antiphon as the missal bounds it. And his exposition speaks of *millia dierum*, thousands of *days*, which invites the conclusion that his psalter read a fuller text. **It did not** — his printed lemma is the missal’s Introit verbatim, and *millia dierum* is his *gloss*, supplying the noun the verse elides.

**Eight readings stand on *réspice in fáciem Christi tui*, and they do not stack:** Augustine’s, Guéranger’s, Arnobius’s, Godfrey’s, Theodoret’s and Cassiodorus’s, and beside those six the *Selecta in Psalmos*’ and the *Expositiones in Psalmos*’, two Greek readings of contested attribution that run directly against each other.

> **Augustine, on *réspice in fáciem Christi tui*.** **He refuses the obvious reading**, in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series: “For when does God not look upon the face of His Christ? … Cause Your Christ to become known to all.” His Latin runs: *Fac innotescere omnibus Christum tuum … notus sit omnibus Christus tuus, ut possimus ire a virtutibus in virtutem, ut possit superabundare gratia, quoniam abundavit peccatum.* **The argument is a grammatical one about *facies*, and it survives translation.** The reasoning is driven by Rom. 5:20 and by the same psalm’s v. 8, **neither of which is in the appointed extract**: the move is Augustine’s on the whole psalm, applied to the verse the missal took.

**Guéranger reads the same verse differently, and the two do not reconcile by stacking.** Where Augustine turns the petition outward and missionary, Guéranger turns it inward and nuptial — in **Dom Laurence Shepherd’s** English, the Duffy 1900 edition of *The Liturgical Year* vol. XI: “the Church, in presenting her petitions to the Most High, begs of Him to look on the face of His Christ, who is also hers,” grounded on 1 Cor. 11:7, 11:3 and Eph. 5:23. Guéranger is nineteenth-century and not patristic; his standing is that of a recognised authority on the Roman liturgy, and **his voice does not stand in for the tradition’s.**

> **Arnobius the Younger (*attributed*), *Commentarii in Psalmos*, in Ps. LXXXIII, PL 53:446C–447D.** **His reading of *réspice in fáciem Christi tui* is none of the others:** God looking on the face of his Christ is God seeing the man seated at his own right hand — *Cum respicit in faciem Christi, et hominem viderit sedentem ad dexteram suam* — and the day better than thousands follows from that man, *per quem melior dies una, quia aeterna erit, et noctem non habebit*, with *millia* glossed as years consumed on earth. **His agreement with Augustine and Cassiodorus about the everlasting day is reached by a different route — through Christ rather than through the day’s own properties — and his *millia* differs from Cassiodorus’s, so his is agreement reached separately and not a further vote for their account.** He also reads the psalm’s opening allegorically: the sparrow is Christ, the turtle-dove the Church, the altars *mysteria tua*, and the house of God *non cemento constat et calce, sed in homine credente*. **The ascription of these commentaries is the traditional one, and they stand as well under *Auctor incertus*.**

> **Godfrey of Admont, *Homilia* LXXXII, *In Dominicam XIV post Pentecosten prima*, PL 174:575A–580C.** **He alone among the Latin witnesses read above makes the anointed the congregation**: *Petimus itaque in Introitu missae ut ille, qui protector noster est, Christum suum nobis dando, semper nos unctos suos respiciat*; the courts are *in corporibus et in animabus nostris, quae per atria Domini possunt intelligi*; and *melior est una dies cognitionis et illuminationis eius super millia, id est antecellit et superexcellit universa opera et merita nostra*. **The *Expositiones* under Athanasius’s name reach the same sense in Greek, and Theodoret an ecclesial one beside it.** **He quotes the Introit as sung** — *Sic enim cantamus* — **and the psalm verse with it.** **His Fourteenth Sunday is not the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday.** His Gospel is the ten lepers of Luke 17 and his Communion is *Panis, quem ego dedero*; the 1962 Gospel is his **Fifteenth**. **So the chants and the Collect he expounds are this formulary’s and the Mass he expounds them in is not** — which is the displacement Honorius shows, met independently in a different author.

> ***Selecta in Psalmos* under Origen’s name, *Patrologia Graeca* 12:1061–1062.** **It quotes the Introit’s own two spans in succession as one argument** — the psalm verse first and then *Protector noster, aspice, Deus, et respice in faciem Christi tui: quia melior est dies una in atriis tuis super millia* — **cut at exactly the words the antiphon ends on** — and glosses the clause *hos kai onomasti Christou memnetai*, that the psalmist has made mention of Christ by name. **The attribution is the controlling caution**: Migne’s own running head over the neighbouring fragments reads *ex Origene*, these are catena excerpts, and a substantial part of the *Selecta* is now assigned to Evagrius Ponticus, so the reading is the *Selecta*’s and not Origen’s flatly. **And the Latin quoted above is Migne’s facing translation, whose psalm wording is the Vulgate’s because the translator used it**, so its agreement with the Introit is a fact about that Latin column and no evidence about the Greek; what the Greek carries is the pairing, the cut and the *onomasti*.

> ***Expositiones in Psalmos* under Athanasius’s name, *Patrologia Graeca* 27:369–370.** **It takes *Christi tui* corporately, of the people**: at 83:10b, *Christon entautha ton keklemenon laon eis basileion hierateuma phesi* — Christ here means the people called to a royal priesthood. At 83:11a it makes the one day in the courts **the Saviour’s resurrection day, set against the thousand years of the worship under the Law**, reckoned from Solomon’s foundation of the temple to the Saviour’s coming; at 83:2 the *skenomata* are the holy churches; and at 83:11b — **the half-verse the Introit cuts off** — it prefers to be small and of no account in the Church than great among a sinful people. **The authenticity of these *Expositiones* is contested and they are widely held to be catena material under his name**, so the reading is the *Expositiones*’ and not Athanasius’s. **A running-head hazard stands beside it, unexplained**: the leaf carrying this exposition heads its columns *Expositio in Psalmum LXXXIV* although the Greek beneath is unmistakably the Septuagint’s Ps. 83, so the column and the Septuagint number are the reference that holds, and never the running head’s figure.

**These two Greek readings are directly opposed, and both stand.** The *Selecta* make *faciem Christi tui* name Christ explicitly; the *Expositiones* make “thy Christ” the people. **Both are of contested attribution, so neither is a vote of the author whose name stands over it.**

**Three Roman texts open on the same words, and they stand in three different relations to Ps. 83.** The incipit *Protector noster aspice, Deus* opens **both** this Mass’s Introit and the Fifth Sunday after Pentecost’s Gradual, with different continuations — *in fáciem Christi tui* against *super servos tuos* — so the two part company only past the incipit. In the 1862 Missal the collision is wider still: the same words open a verbatim psalm Introit, an adapted Gradual at four occurrences, **and a composed collect that quotes no psalm at all** (*Protector noster aspice, Deus: ut, qui malorum nostrorum pondere premimur…*). **This Introit quotes the psalm verbatim, the Fifth Sunday’s Gradual adapts it, and the collect only echoes it**: a loose echo in a composed oration is not a quotation of the psalm at all. **And the older books widen it again**: the responsory of the Monday of the first week of Lent and of the Lenten Ember Saturday opens on the same words and continues *et respice super servos tuos*, with *Domine Deus virtutum, exaudi preces servorum tuorum* as its verse; and two orations of the same incipit are neither chant. **At least three distinct Latin texts stand under that incipit in the Roman books, and the two chants share not only the incipit but a psalm.** **So a note in an older book that names an *Oratio Protector noster aspice, Deus* — as one does at the ordination of exorcists — names an oration and not this Introit**, and an argument built from a search index on those words alone will attach this Sunday’s chant to a rite it has nothing to do with.

**The Roman Mass antiphonary prints this Introit at *Hebdomada decima quarta*, with its psalm verse, word for word as 1962 has them** — and prints the same psalm text again as the second Responsory of *Hebdomada duodecima*. **And a late-thirteenth-century Roman ceremonial has the cardinal priests sing the clause to a newly elected pope**: at *Ordo Romanus* XIII §11, when a pope consecrated or elected outside Rome comes to St Peter’s, *cantant coram ipso cardinales presbyteri: Protector noster aspice Deus, et respice in facie Christi tui*, after which he is led to the altar and the canons sing the *Te Deum*. **Nothing in that book says the chant was taken from this Sunday’s Introit**; the ceremonial reads the ablative *in facie* against the missal’s *in fáciem*; and the ceremonial is Migne’s reprint of Mabillon, one printed edition standing for the manuscripts, with no earlier *Ordo* beside it. **What it shows is that *Christi tui*, the anointed king of the psalm’s own sense, was sung to the face of a man just elected.**

**Theodoret’s Greek at PG 80:1541–1545 reads *réspice in fáciem Christi tui* ecclesially**, the face of Christ being the saved people, on 1 Cor. 12:27 — *hymeis de este somata Christou*, in transliteration. **His expositions of Pss. 83, 94 and 117 stand in a Greek transcription of *Patrologia Graeca* 80 and not in the columns themselves.** **That reading does not stack with the others**, and it removes the ground for treating the inward-ecclesial reading as a nineteenth-century departure from the Fathers — though it stands on a different Pauline text and without the nuptial figure, so it corroborates neither Augustine nor Guéranger.

**And on the antiphon’s own clause he argues the other way from both Latins.** Augustine and Cassiodorus read one everlasting day that has neither rising nor setting; **Theodoret reads an argument from profit** at *Interpretatio in Ps.* LXXXIII, *Patrologia Graeca* 80:1541–1545 — what a man may reap in a single day in those courts he could not gather elsewhere though he spent thousands of days at it — and he sets the psalm historically among the Babylonian captives, **his own reconstruction** and not something the psalm states. **So the antiphon’s one day is contested and not agreed**: the Antiochene historical method stands against the Latin spiritual reading on the same words, and this reading too stands in a Greek transcription of *Patrologia Graeca* 80 and not in the columns themselves.

**Cassiodorus reads the psalm in Latin.** On *réspice* he refuses the surface reading exactly as Augustine does and names the figure: *Haec figura dicitur hypallage, Latine permutatio, quoties dicitur respici magis ille qui respicit* — the one said to be looked upon is rather the one who looks — and the petition accordingly turns outward to the gentiles, with Ps. 13:2 and Ps. 5:2 as his parallels. And on this psalm’s cut clause *cor meum et caro mea* he comments at v. 3b, **the clause the missal cuts**: a second witness that *caro* here is positive, outside the appointed extract. **Two things at the antiphon are his own and not Augustine’s**: he glosses the *mília* of *super mília* as this world, where thousands of days come to an end — which is why Arnobius’s *millia*, the years consumed on earth, is a different gloss and not the same one — and he names the antiphon’s comparative figure *parison*. **On the everlasting day itself he is corroboration and probably dependence on Augustine, not a second independent vote.** And **no later use redirects the Introit’s wording**: every located use is straight devotional or liturgical reception.

### Collect <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span>

*Custódi, Dómine, quǽsumus, Ecclésiam tuam* *Oratio* marginal no. 1573

**The pointing is load-bearing.** The semicolon after *mortálitas* is a semicolon and not a comma, and it divides the prayer into its two real parts: a causal clause, *quia sine te lábitur humána mortálitas*, and the double petition that clause grounds. The two limbs of the petition are yoked by an *et … et* straddling *abstrahátur* and *dirigátur*, and the first *et* sits before *abstrahátur* rather than before *tuis*, which is what makes the pair read as a pair. By the same *auxíliis* the Church is drawn back from what harms and steered toward what saves: withdrawal and direction, one instrument.

**Its object is the Church, and its diagnosis is mortality.** Not sin, not the world, not the enemy: what the prayer says is wrong is that human mortality slips when God is not holding it. **The Church is named here and nowhere else in the ten elements** — though the first-person plurals of Introit, Alleluia, Secret and Postcommunion are corporate in their own way, which softens any contrast built on that count. The formulary’s other abstract nouns for offence as such are this prayer’s *nóxiis* and the Secret’s *delictórum* — as against the Epistle’s *vítiis* and the seventeen vices it names one by one — and *peccatum* occurs nowhere in the ten elements at all.

**This prayer is received three ways, and they are three kinds of reception rather than three votes on a meaning.** **Honorius** cites the prayer by incipit in *Gemma animae* IV. **Godfrey of Admont** glosses it word by word inside his homily on this Mass, by interpolation: *quia sine te labitur humana mortalitas in elatione, tuis semper auxiliis abstrahatur a noxiis superbiae et extollentiae mentis, et ad salutaria, vere humilitatis bona, te ducente dirigatur*. And **Schuster** comments on the prayer continuously, at more length than Guéranger. Godfrey’s Mass, as at the Introit, is not this one.

**An exact-phrase index over the *Patrologia Latina* returns this Collect’s clauses at two further places**: inside a prayer of Alcuin, *De usu Psalmorum* VII, and inside a letter of Wibald of Corvey to Eugenius III, *Epistola* CCCLXXV. **What either writer makes of the Collect’s clauses is not established**, and both stand as unverified leads.

Guéranger makes one interpretive move on this Collect: that the abyss of vice the Church’s children would fall into without help is specifically the Epistle’s catalogue — a cross-element reading. **But it is an association he asserts in one sentence rather than argues, with no witness behind it.** His remarks on the Secret and the Postcommunion are one sentence each and are paraphrase rather than exegesis.

**What the English witness does not carry.** The Cummiskey rendering turns *propitiatióne perpétua* into “by thy constant mercy” and *tuis semper auxíliis* into “may thy grace,” so both the noun *propitiatio* and the adverb *semper* disappear from the English. The English itself stands on the printed page: at p. 425 of the 1861 hand missal this Collect reads word for word and point for point as it is printed here, as do the Secret and the Postcommunion at p. 427. The book prints no translator’s name.

### Epistle <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>

*Fratres: Spíritu ambuláte* *Léctio Epístolæ beáti Pauli Apóstoli ad Gálatas* — Gal. 5, 16-24 marginal no. 1574

#### Where the lesson is cut from, and what the cut costs

The pericope **replaces** Paul’s own connective. Clementine Gal. 5:16 reads *Dico autem: Spiritu ambulate*, and the appointed Epistle reads *Fratres: Spíritu ambuláte*. *Dico autem* ties v. 16 back to the liberty of v. 13 — where Paul’s own vocative *fratres* stands, inside the sentence the pericope drops — and to the law fulfilled in love at v. 14. **The missal’s *Fratres* displaces the connective rather than supplementing it**, and supplies at v. 16 an address the apostle had used three verses earlier.

It also ends two verses before the unit’s own closing inclusio. Verse 25’s *Si Spiritu vivimus, Spiritu et ambulemus* returns v. 16’s verb in the hortatory subjunctive — and **in Greek the two verbs differ**, so the inclusio is tighter in the Latin than in the Greek, which is a fact about the Vulgate’s rendering. **The appointed excerpt therefore ends on crucifixion rather than on walking**, and v. 25 is the incipit of the *next* Sunday’s lesson, printed on the facing pages of the same book.

#### Two catalogues that are not built alike

*Ópera carnis* is plural with a plural verb; *fructus Spíritus* is singular with a singular verb, and **the asymmetry stands in the Greek as well**, so it is not a Latin artefact. The vice list is left explicitly open (*et his simília*) and the fruit list is closed with a verdict (*Advérsus huiúsmodi non est lex*).

**The list lengths, and their mechanism.** Latin: seventeen vices, twelve fruits. The Byzantine Greek: seventeen vices, nine fruits. The critical text its apparatus records: fifteen and nine. Three precise consequences follow.

1.  **The Latin fruit list exceeds both Greek forms by exactly three.** The pair *patiéntia*/*longanímitas* answers the single Greek word for long-suffering — **Jerome says so himself**, as he says that *continéntia* renders *enkrateia* — and *modéstia* and *cástitas* answer nothing in either the Byzantine text or its apparatus. **Any argument built on the number twelve is an argument about the liturgical Latin.**

2.  ***Homicídia* stands in neither a Revised Version nor a modern critical-text English**, because the Byzantine text has the word and the apparatus marks it absent from the critical text — so **the reason is a Byzantine-against-critical divergence and not a peculiarity of the Vulgate.**

3.  **But the Vulgate’s list is not simply the Byzantine list either:** the Byzantine word for adultery has no Latin counterpart, so the Clementine sides with the critical text at that word and with the Byzantine text at “murders.”

The word-by-word alignment of *luxúria*/*impudicítia* against their Greek neighbours is a judgement about renderings and not a count, and it stands more loosely than the counts do. Every critical-text reading in this comparison is an apparatus note beside the Byzantine text and not a critical edition’s own text.

#### What the commentators actually argue

> **John Chrysostom, *Commentarius in epistolam ad Galatas* cap. V, verse by verse.** **His governing move survives the Greek, and is sharper there:** *horas hoti ou phesi ten sarka entautha, alla ton geode logismon kai chamai syromenon* — **the object is *sarx* and the contrast term *logismos***, an earthly cast of mind dragged along the ground, **not *soma***; NPNF’s “by the flesh, he does not mean the body” is a paraphrase that does not misrepresent the sense, but **the Greek makes a sharper claim about the word the Epistle actually uses**, and it is the Greek that bears on the appointed *caro*. Proved from the vice-list itself, which the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English gives as “hatred, variance, emulations, strife, heresies, and witchcraft … arise merely from a depraved moral choice.” **His argument from the verb holds in the appointed Latin:** “If these things belonged to nature and not to a bad moral choice, his expression, *they practise*, is inappropriate” — and the missal prints *qui tália agunt*. **The Greek confirms two wordings of the appointed Latin against rivals:** his *Ouk eipe, to ergon tou pneumatos, all’, Ho karpos* is verbatim NPNF’s “He says not, the work of the Spirit, but, the fruit,” and his lemma at the list’s close is *Qui autem sunt Christi* — **the missal’s reading, against Augustine’s *Qui sunt in Christo Iesu***, so on this clause the appointed Latin is the better witness to Paul. **And at the lesson’s last verse he insists the crucifixion is not the destruction of the flesh**, *Ou gar de ten sarka aneilon: epei pos emellon zen?* — exact discipline — and **no other expositor above states so directly that the appointed v. 24 is not ascetical.** **His is the fuller of the two verse-by-verse expositions of the appointed Epistle, Theodoret’s being the other**: six lemmata against Theodoret’s five, at vv. 16, 17, 18, 19–21, 22 and 24, and continuing to v. 25, where the missal’s pericope stops.

**Chrysostom’s Greek on the first half of the pericope** (PG 61:670–673) reads *Sarka gar entautha, ou to soma kalei*: flesh here is the earthbound, slothful, neglected reasoning; **desire belongs to the soul, not to flesh, so a flesh that *desires* cannot be the body**; the body is an instrument, *organon gar estin he sarx*, and *we hate and punish not the iron but the murderer*; the conflict is between two reasonings, virtue and vice, *ouch he psyche kai to soma*. **And at v. 18 he calls the dismissal of the law a great and wonderful praise of it**, set aside not as bad but as less than the philosophy given by the Spirit. **The opponents he answers are unnamed in the text.**

**Chrysostom’s own Greek vice lemma has seventeen items and includes both *pharmakeia* and *phonoi*, so he speaks to *veneficia* and to *homicidia***; *luxúria*, *modéstia* and *cástitas* have no counterpart in the text he expounds, and he says nothing of them. His nine-item fruit lemma is the Greek’s, in the same order Augustine’s and Jerome’s lemmata carry.

> **Theodoret of Cyrus, *Interpretatio Epistolae ad Galatas* cap. V, *Patrologia Graeca* 82:495–498, on the whole appointed pericope.** **He comments verse by verse across the entire lesson.** At v. 16, if you would master the passions of the flesh, follow the Spirit’s directions; at v. 17, *sarka legei ten epi ta cheiro tes gnomes ropen; pneuma de ten enoikousan charin* — flesh is the mind’s inclination to the worse and spirit the indwelling grace. **He argues twice, from the lists themselves, that this flesh is not the body**: idolatry and sorcery *ou tes sarkos, alla tes psyches estin idia*, so *ou toinyn tes sarkos kategorei, alla tou rhathymou phronematos*, he accuses not the flesh but the slothful cast of mind; and had the works of v. 19 belonged to the body alone, *edei deichthenai kai ta tes psyches erga; all’ ou tetheike* — a matching list of the soul’s works would have had to be set down, and is not. He answers v. 23b from 1 Tim. 1:9. **And he closes the appointed lesson baptismally**, *to Christo syntaphentes nekron apephenan te hamartia to soma*. **His own lemma is not the missal’s**: at v. 19 it opens with the Greek for *adultery*, which the appointed Latin does not carry, and his fruit-list has the Greek nine. Migne’s facing Latin is the Renaissance translation and controls his sense, not the missal’s wording.

> **Augustine, *De civitate Dei* XIV.2, in Hoffmann’s critical edition, CSEL 40.2 pp. 3–4.** **He reaches the same conclusion by a sharper route, and by two middle terms of his own.** Because idolatry, sorcery, enmity, dissension, heresy and envy are vices of the mind, **a man may abstain from bodily pleasure precisely *on account of* idolatry or heresy and be convicted by apostolic authority of living according to the flesh in the very act of that abstinence** — *et in eo, quod abstinet a uoluptatibus carnis, damnabilia opera carnis agere demonstratur*. Then the test from ordinary speech: nobody says *Malam carnem* to an enemy. So Paul calls them all works of the flesh *eo locutionis modo, quo totum significatur a parte, ipsum hominem uult nomine carnis intellegi*. **The Latin is the critical edition’s own wording, carried by a transcription of that edition.** **Theodoret and Augustine reach one conclusion by three different middle terms, one in Greek and two in Latin, and nothing shows that either knew the other.**

> **Ambrosiaster, *Commentaria in Epistolam ad Galatas*, PL 17:367D–368D.** **His reading is governed by two laws rather than by two parts of man.** At v. 17, *Duas leges proponit … unam Dei, alteram peccati*, with *medius homo* standing between them; at vv. 19–21 the works are called the flesh’s by origin, *quia errores isti ex mundo sunt, ex quo et caro*; and at v. 24 *qui sunt Christi* is glossed *christos, id est, unctos* while the crucified flesh is the world, *Hi carnem, id est, mundum crucifigunt*, proved from 1 Jn. 2:15. **His lemma is not the missal’s Vulgate.** His fruit-list has **ten** members, carrying *spes* and *castitas* while lacking *fides* and *patientia*, and his vice-list opens *adulterium, fornicatio, impuritas, obscenitas* where the missal opens *fornicátio, immundítia, impudicítia, luxúria*. **So his exposition counts a different pair of lists, and the missal’s seventeen and twelve are not the lists he expounds.**

> **Marius Victorinus, *In epistolam Pauli ad Galatas*, PL 8:1192B–D.** **He is half a negative.** His text as Migne prints it passes straight from v. 17 to 6:1, so **he comments on none of vv. 18–24.** What he does say at v. 17 is the exact contrary of Chrysostom’s move: *Habet enim motus suos caro, habetque sensus, neque tantum ab anima suscitantur*, argued from water, fire, earth and the other elements, which have their own impulses and no soul at all. **The two comment on the same appointed verse and cannot both be right.** **The lacuna is in the commentary as Migne prints it**, the text standing continuous across the join; no critical edition stands beside it, so it is a gap in this printing and not established as Victorinus’s own.

> **Glossa ordinaria on Galatians, PL 114:584B–585B.** **A third position on the clause Chrysostom and Victorinus divide over:** the flesh is *said* to desire by the same idiom that says the ear hears and the eye sees — *Caro dicta est concupiscere, quia hoc secundum ipsam agit anima* — and the cause is in neither alone, *ex utroque enim fit, quia sine utroque delectatio nulla sentitur*. It reads *non perficietis* precisely (*non ait non feceritis … sed non perficietis, id est non opera eorum consensu voluntatis implebitis*), defines being under the law by fear rather than love, and **organises the fruit list by making charity its head from which the members follow**, *Caput praemisit, unde membra sequuntur*. **It is a compilation carried under the name *Anselmus Laudunensis et schola*, which is the modern attribution of the redaction and not a claim that Anselm wrote these sentences; no sentence in it stands under a named Father’s name.**

> **Augustine, *Expositio epistolae ad Galatas* ss. 45–53, continuous on the whole appointed pericope.** **His own lemma differs from the missal’s at every load-bearing point: thirteen vices** — *animositates* for the missal’s *irae*, *haereses* for *sectae* — **and nine fruits**, and at the exclusion *regnum Dei non possidebunt* where the missal prints *non consequéntur*. **So his systematic opposition of the two lists** — *Recte igitur fornicationi opponitur caritas*, point for point — **cannot be mapped onto the missal’s seventeen and twelve without distortion**. **On the counts he refuses the argument altogether**: *Non enim hoc suscepit, ut doceret, quot sint, sed in quo genere* — and he reads *both* lists as open, grounding it on the missal’s own word, *Non enim ait: Adversus haec non est lex, sed: Adversus huiusmodi*. **On v. 16 he makes the argument turn on the mood of the verb**: Paul said not that you shall not *have* the desires of the flesh but that you shall not *fulfil* them — and though his lemma reads *concupiscentias carnis ne perfeceritis*, the argument survives into the missal’s *non perficiétis*, which is still about fulfilling. **On v. 17 he denies that the verse removes free choice**, the verse being addressed to those who will not hold the grace of faith — an anti-Pelagian-shaped argument drawing on Rom. 5–8, none of it appointed. At the connective the pericope cuts he reads *Spiritu ambulate* as the answer to v. 15’s biting and devouring, naming *humilitas et mansuetudo* the first and great gift of the Spirit — **and *mansuetúdo* is in the appointed fruit list, so that link the appointed text can carry**. At the last verse he grounds the crucifixion of the flesh in **chaste fear**, the fear of the chaste wife against the adulteress’s, through Ps. 118:120.

> **Jerome, *Commentarii in epistolam ad Galatas* lib. III, ad 5:16–24.** **He rules on the missal’s list count in his own voice:** *Sed sciendum non plus quam quindecim carnis opera nominata* — fifteen vices, with *adulterium*, *impudicitia* and *homicidia* recorded as additions found *in Latinis codicibus*, and the items numbered in his own text — **so the 1962 Epistle prints two of the three he rejects, in a list of seventeen**. The bound: this is Jerome on the Latin codices he knew about 386–388, not a judgement about the Clementine’s descent, and nothing is said about how the 1962 text got its count. **On the missal’s own word *vitiis* he is its defender, not its corrector:** *Ubi Latinus interpres vitia posuit, in Graeco pathemata, id est, passiones leguntur*, and *desideria* was added *ut non naturam corporis videretur in spiritualibus viris negare, sed vitia* — **which also explains Augustine’s divergent lemma *passionibus***: two renderings of one Greek noun, not two different texts. **His philology of the vice-list is Greek-anchored and checkable**: *aemulatio* = *zelos*; *rixae* renders *eritheia* and not *mache*; *haeresis* from *helesthai*, choosing. **He distinguishes *modestia* from *continentia*** — the former belonging to the perfect, the latter to those still on the way — **which is not the medieval *continentia*/*castitas* pair** Anthony and Aquinas share, but a different distinction. On v. 17 he answers the free-choice objection by a **tripartite anthropology**, the soul standing in the middle between flesh and spirit — **the same difficulty Chrysostom raises in Greek and the same answer, a convergence with nothing showing dependence**. On v. 18 he tells a Latin reader that ***spiritus* there stands without the article and so is the Holy Spirit**, a distinction Greek marks and Latin cannot — *qui arthra penitus non habemus*; the 1962 book’s lower-case *spiritu* at v. 18 and capital *Spiritus* at v. 22 are the printers making visible what he says the language cannot. **He transmits Origen verbatim and partly withdraws from him**: at v. 24 he records Origen construing *Christi* with *carnem* — those without law crucified the flesh *of Christ* — and declines it, *ut non carnem Christi, sed suam eos crucifixisse dicamus*; and on the fruit’s being unnamed he transmits Origen’s reading that the works are *manifest* and the fruit not, so it must be sought with labour, while saying at 5:19–21 that on reflection the named vices seem to him plain sense. **Origen is present here only in Jerome’s report of him, and that report stands beside no text of Origen’s own.** **And on the clause the list closes with, he and Augustine converge on one proof-text**, 1 Tim. 1:8–10, that the law is not laid down for the just — **the firmest reception *Advérsus huiúsmodi non est lex* has here, and convergence rather than dependence**.

**Seven accounts of the two lists’ asymmetry stand.** The textual reading: the vice list is left open (*et his simília*) and the fruit list closed with a verdict. Augustine, against the argument from counts altogether, reads *both* lists as open on the missal’s own *huiúsmodi* — pointing the opposite way. Origen through Jerome: the works are called *manifest* and the fruit not, so the fruit must be sought with labour. Chrysostom: evil works come from us alone, while the good need God’s kindness too. **Jerome’s own reason is a further one and differs from his**: vices end in themselves where virtues sprout and overflow, the Greek account being about grace and the Latin about fecundity. **The Glossa alone gives the list a structure**, and it answers the question the others leave open: charity is the head from which the members follow, so the fruit list is not a list of coordinates at all. **And Ambrosiaster grounds *fructus* rather than *opera* in the law of the spirit that fructifies them** — a reason different again from Chrysostom’s and from Jerome’s — **but his own lists are not the missal’s, so his reason stands while his count is of other lists.**

> **Thomas Aquinas, *Super Galatas* cap. 5 lect. 5 and 6.** **He expounds the missal’s own lists, and he and Anthony of Padua are the two witnesses read here that do.** He orders the twelve fruits by what each perfects — interior, exterior, what is above us, what is below us — and reads the number as answering Apoc. 22:2, *ex utraque parte lignum vitae afferens fructus duodecim*; **the Apocalypse link is his own move.** He groups the seventeen vices by object, derives *veneficia* from *venenum*, and reads *homicidia* *cordis et operis*, of the heart as well as of the deed, citing 1 Jn. 4. **He raises the objection Chrysostom raises — that several listed vices are plainly not bodily — and answers it by a different route:** Augustine’s definition at *De civitate Dei* XIV.2, that whoever lives according to himself lives according to the flesh, so that *caro* here stands for the whole man — **and Augustine’s own wording at that place, read above, grounds it on the ascetic idolater and on ordinary speech rather than on the definition alone.** **And he classes *idolorum servitus* among the vices against God**, as that *per quod impeditur ab hostibus Dei cultus divinus*, warranting it from 1 Cor. 10:7 and Wisd. 14:27, **and cites Eph. 5:5 at this pericope for *fornicatio* and *immunditia* and not for the idolatry clause** — **so the identification of avarice with idolatry is not made here by the appointed text’s own principal Latin commentator.** At vv. 22–23 he defines *fides* as *cognitio quaedam invisibilium cum certitudine*, from Gen. 15:6 and Heb. 11:6, and holds the works of the Spirit called fruits *non ut adepti sive acquisiti, sed ut producti*.

> **Anthony of Padua, *Sermo Dominica XIV post Pentecosten*.** **He left a sermon headed for this very Sunday, and it is partly a false friend.** He states his own Mass’s texts explicitly: his Introit is *Inclina, Domine, aurem tuam ad me* — the 1962 **Fifteenth** Sunday’s Introit — and his Gospel is Luke 17:11–19, the ten lepers. His whole sermon is built on leprosy and cleansing, and his stated reason for pairing Epistle with Gospel (*in evangelio fit mentio de leprosis … et in epistola ponuntur vitia, ex quibus provenit lepra peccati*) is a rationale for a pairing **the 1962 missal does not make**. **His exposition of the Epistle’s own words stands on the appointed text**; the pairing of Gal. 5 with a cleansing narrative, and everything resting on *Inclina, Domine*, belongs to his Mass and not to this one.

**Anthony’s substantive moves on the Epistle.** He glosses *idolorum servitus* as **avarice** rather than the cult of images; he moralises *veneficia* into *suggestiones daemonum et adulationes peccatorum, quae nocere non possunt nisi ad sanguinem, idest animae consensum, pervenerint*; and he defines all twelve fruits individually — **passing over *homicidia* with no gloss at all.** His definitions of the last two, *continentia, quae a licitis abstinet; castitas, quae recte utitur licito*, are **verbatim** the pair Aquinas independently attributes to the Gloss, in two texts that stand independently of each other — **which is real evidence of a common medieval source.** His etymologies carry the moral sense he draws from a word rather than its descent: *fornicatio, idest formae necatio* reads the sin as the killing of the form, which is his gloss on the appointed word and not its philology. His inner quotations from the Gloss and from Isidore stand at second hand and are attributed to *his use* of them and not to Isidore or the Gloss; the same holds for the definition of charity he ascribes to Augustine, which the edition’s own note transmits through Peter Lombard and which reads differently in the printed Augustine, **so it is not Augustine’s exact words.** And his text prints two of the fruits in a different order from the missal’s, *longanimitas* and *benignitas* exchanged — *patientia, longanimitas, bonitas, benignitas, mansuetudo* against *patiéntia, benígnitas, bónitas, longanímitas, mansuetúdo* — which is a fact about two texts and **not evidence about which Vulgate recension the missal descends from.**

**And one fact bears on every join of Epistle and Gospel.** Anthony’s is the one medieval sermon on Gal. 5:16–24 located for this Epistle, and **his Sunday numbering runs one ahead of the 1962 missal’s, consistently across all three of his sermons for these Sundays.** A major medieval preacher expounding this Epistle does so beside a different Gospel. So every cross-element candidate joining Epistle and Gospel is a claim about how the 1962 formulary reads when taken as a whole, and **not** a claim about how the tradition read these two texts together.

Augustine’s and Jerome’s Galatians commentaries stand **in transcriptions, with no Migne column or critical edition beside them**, and Theodoret’s exposition stands at its own Migne columns. Cassian, Pelagius’s *Expositio*, Nicholas of Lyra and Denis the Carthusian on Galatians, and Cornelius a Lapide’s commentary on the appointed verses, were not opened, so **no second Latin witness above stands to whether *fides* at Gal. 5:22 is faith or fidelity.**

### Gradual <span class="nodecor">(*Grad.*)</span>

*Bonum est confidere in Dómino* *Graduale* — Ps. 117, 8-9 marginal no. 1575

Unlike the Introit and the Offertory, **the Gradual takes complete verses and cuts nothing**, so there is no omission at all — and of the three elements that depart from the Clementine in nothing beyond the table, it is the one whose wording is the Clementine’s exactly, the Epistle and the Gospel each carrying a displaced or supplied incipit besides. Context: vv. 6–7 twice call the Lord *adjutor* and name *homo* as what need not be feared, so v. 8’s *quam confidere in hómine* generalises a contrast the psalm has just made concrete; and v. 1’s *quoniam bonus* supplies the adjective the Gradual’s *Bonum est* turns into a comparative formula. The Clementine prints *Alleluja.* at the head of v. 1, which bears on any claim that Ps. 117 has no superscription — it is a liturgical rubric-word rather than an ascription, and it is not numbered separately.

**The princes are read four ways, and a question stands beside the four.** **Cassiodorus, in Latin at PL 70**, observes that **the two appointed verses are governed by anaphora** — *Et nota quod in principiis amborum versuum eadem verba geminavit. Quae figura dicitur anaphora* — exactly on the appointed wording; reads the pair as **carnal against spiritual** rather than a moral climb, citing Jas. 4:13–15, Phil. 2:13 and Jer. 17:5 against presumption of the will; and lets *princeps* cover **both the devil and the good angel**, *Sic et bonus angelus dictus est princeps, sicut in Daniele legitur*. **Theodoret, in Greek at PG 80:1812**, has *archontes* who are **earthly rulers whose authority is temporary**: God is good, wills the good, can do what he wills and has imperishable authority, while men are corruptible by nature and changeable in purpose; his preface makes them the persecuting rulers, demagogues, kings and generals whom the gentile believers outlasted. **His psalm heading is the same *Allelouia* the Clementine prints**, a small convergence, and **his lemma reads the missal’s ablative**, one data point against the accusative variant below without settling it. **Augustine and Cassiodorus both reach Michael in Daniel, so between those two the shared proof-text is probably dependence and not independent invention** — and **the division on what the princes are** sets a Greek reading against three Latin ones on the same two verses.

> **Augustine, *Enarratio in Ps. CXVII* §4, on vv. 8 and 9.** **He reads the two verses as a single escalating argument**, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English: “let not my friend present himself unto me as a good man, so as to bid me repose my hope in himself … Nor let any one, who may in a certain sense be styled a good angel, be regarded by myself as one in whom I ought to put my trust … For angels also are called princes, even as we read in Daniel, Michael, your prince.” The second term is progressively strengthened — bad men, good men, good angels — and still loses.

> **Prosper of Aquitaine, *Expositio Psalmorum*, PL 51:333B–C.** *Etiam si bonus homo, aut bonus angelus adiuvet, non in eis, inquit, confidam; sed in Domino, qui me per illos iuvat*; and at v. 9, *Principes et homines et angeli possunt intelligi: sed in neutris spes collocanda est; quia potestas non est nisi a Deo* (Rom. 13:1). **His frame for the passage is Eph. 6:12, read three verses earlier, and that is what licenses the angelic sense**, which v. 9 does not carry by itself. **Three verses later he quotes the Offertory’s own words** — *ut gustemus et videamus quam suavis est Dominus* — so a Father joins the Gradual’s psalm to the Offertory’s verse in one sentence. This *Expositio* is largely an epitome of Augustine’s *Enarrationes*, and how much of it is Prosper’s own is unsettled.

> **Arnobius the Younger (*attributed*), in Ps. CXVII, PL 53:505A–C.** **The man not to be trusted is oneself, and the confidence forbidden is confidence in free choice:** *Habes quidem liberum arbitrium, sed noli de eo praesumere; de Deo praesume, quia vinci non potest, nam liberum arbitrium vinci potest*, with the predestinarian objection raised and answered in the same passage. **The anti-Pelagian books Cassiodorus names at this verse are a cross-reference and no more**, and **the grace-and-free-will reading of the verse is attested in a fifth-century Latin psalm commentary in its own right**, and not only through the cross-reference Cassiodorus makes.

> **Godfrey of Admont, on the appointed verses, PL 174:579D–580A.** **A fourth account of what the princes are, and the only one that keeps them inside the man who prays:** the princes and the servants are the interior and the exterior senses — *Interiores sensus nostri sunt principes, exteriores sensus servi nostri* — and the comparison itself he reads as *melius est, imo optimum confidere in virtute Dei, quam praesumere in suis meritis*. **His Fourteenth Sunday is not the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday**, as at the Introit.

> **Bruno the Carthusian (*attributed*), *Expositio in Psalmos*, PL 152:1249D–1250B.** **He alone among the Latin expositors asks whether the Gradual’s *quam* is comparative at all, and he answers twice.** First he denies that the word is set down as a comparative: on that reading the verse means that trusting the Lord by enduring tribulation is useful and looking to the persecutor for bodily safety is not, with Jer. 17:5 as proof. Then he grants the comparative, **but only if *principes* are heavenly spirits and spiritual men**, on the ground that God can help without them and they cannot help without God. **This is a grammatical judgement about the appointed words themselves**, and the first proposal below stands or falls with his second answer.

**And one compilation carries the angelic reading forward without adding a vote to it.** **Peter Lombard** (PL 191:1035B–C) makes v. 8’s man *homo carnalis, cui salus vana est* and, under the siglum *\[Aug.\]*, v. 9’s princes *angelis qui dicuntur principes. Unde in Daniele legitur Michael princeps* — **he is the *Magna glosatura*, a sigla-marked compilation.** **So the angelic reading is one position with three carriers and not three independent witnesses:** Augustine’s own *Enarratio in Ps. CXVII* §4, quoted above, holds the angelic reading with Daniel’s Michael as its warrant, so Lombard’s siglum transmits him correctly; Prosper’s exposition is largely an Augustine epitome, and Prosper’s reason is Lombard’s reason. That *Enarratio* stands in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English, and Migne’s Latin of it at *Patrologia Latina* 36–37 does not stand beside it.

The Latin witness reads the accusative *sperare in Dominum … in principes* where the missal prints the ablative *in Dómino … in princípibus*. **Whether that accusative is Augustine’s psalter or a later hand’s is not established.**

**One cross-relation, and Augustine does not make it.** His escalation to good angels makes the Gradual’s exclusion absolute rather than moral, which is the Gospel’s own logic at *Nemo potest duóbus dóminis servíre*. **He does not cite Mt. 6:24 here**, and the join is made by none of the expositors above.

> **John Chrysostom, *Expositio in Psalmum CXVII*, *Patrologia Graeca* 55:331, read in Greek; Migne prints no facing Latin at this passage.** **He denies that the psalm’s comparative form states a comparison at all**: *ou kata synkrisin tauta proagei, all’ ethos te Graphe touto kechresthai to tropo kai epi ton asynkriton dia ten astheneian ton tote akouonton* — Scripture uses this figure even of things that admit no comparison, on account of the weakness of those then hearing — *ouk ara synkrinon touto phesin, alla synkatabainon ekeinois*. **On his reading the Gradual’s twice-repeated *Bonum est … quam* is divine condescension to weak hearers and not a weighing of two goods.** He builds on the appointed verses a catena: Jer. 17:5, that the man hoping in man is accursed; the spider’s web, so that hope in man is not only weak but perilous; Rom. 5:5, that hope does not confound; Sir. 2:10 — and he answers the objection *All’ ego, phesin, elpisa, kai kateschynthen*, that the speaker hoped and was put to shame, by locating the failure in the hoping. **This bears directly on the one grammatical feature that distinguishes this Gradual**, and it cuts against reading the chant as ranking two trusts on one scale. The *Expositio in Psalmos* is generally received as authentic, unlike the *In Psalmum XCIV* printed among his works; that attribution question stands open, and Migne’s text of the *Expositio* stands beside no critical edition.

**Augustine supplies the principle that reconciles the Gradual’s exclusion with the Offertory’s rescuing angel, and it is conditional.** At his own exposition of this psalm, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English: “Nor let any one, who may in a certain sense be styled a good angel, be regarded by myself as one in whom I ought to put my trust: for no one is good, save God alone; and when a man or an angel appear to aid us, when they do this of sincere affection, He does it through them, who made them good after their measure.” **So the creature is excluded as an object of trust and admitted as an instrument** — **but for Augustine the tension never arises at all, because for him the Offertory’s *Angelus* is Christ.** If that angel is a creature, as Cassiodorus, Godfrey and Arnobius have it, the Mass does trust at one chant a rescuer it excludes from trust at the other, and the instrumental principle softens the tension without removing it.

**Chrysostom’s exposition of this psalm is not in the Greek collection that carries his *Expositiones in Psalmos*.** That text is a fragment ending at PG 55:194 and covering Pss. 4–12 and 43–44 only, under a title that promises the whole; the exposition read above stands in a different volume. **And two Greek collections give the Gradual’s own verses nothing at all**: the *Expositiones* under Athanasius’s name pass directly from Ps. 117:5 to Ps. 117:10, and the *Selecta* under Origen’s carry no fragment on vv. 8–9. **Bellarmine expounds none of this psalm:** his exposition of Ps. 33 falls at vv. 2–3, no appointed verse of any element, and it is an abridged Victorian English translation rather than his Latin.

### Alleluia <span class="nodecor">(*All.*)</span>

*Veníte, exsultémus Dómino* no separate heading; reference inline — Ps. 94, 1 marginal no. 1576

**The whole psalm, and where its reception went.** Psalm 94 calls the assembly to praise, names God king above all gods and shepherd of his flock, and then turns abruptly to warning: today if you shall hear his voice, harden not your hearts. **The chant takes v. 1 alone** and drops the psalm title *Laus cántici ipsi David*, which the Clementine prints as part of that verse, pointing the clause with a comma where the Clementine has a semicolon.

> **Augustine, *Enarratio in Ps. XCIV* §2–3.** **He takes the imperative *Venite* as the load-bearing word and denies that it means motion**, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English: “It is not by place, but by being unlike Him, that a man is afar from God … If therefore by unlikeness we recede from God, by likeness we approach unto God.” And *exsultemus* is specified as joy *in the Lord* precisely to exclude a worldly joy the same word would cover: “if there were not in this life a wicked joy which is to be distinguished from a righteous joy, it would be enough to say, Come, let us rejoice; but he has briefly distinguished it.”

**The Latin recovers something the translation loses entirely.** Augustine closes the argument on a jingle built from the appointed verb itself — *Pie debes Domino exsultare, si vis securus mundo insultare*: you must piously *exult* in the Lord if you would safely *exult over* the world. The English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series, renders this “piously joy in the Lord, if you dost wish safely to trample upon the world,” and the play vanishes. **The play depends on the missal’s own word *exsultémus*.**

**This exposition’s centre of gravity is vv. 7–11, *Hodie si vocem eius audieritis*, which the Alleluia does not appoint**, and Augustine’s argument there closes on the oath of exclusion from rest. **That ending belongs to the psalm and not to the chant, which stops at v. 1.** The same limit governs the canonical relationship: Hebrews quotes Ps. 94:8–11 at 3:7–11, repeats the opening at 3:15, and expounds it again through 4:1–11; but **the formulary appoints v. 1 and no more, and the appointed text does not say that its single verse carries the whole psalm’s warning.** And the same Clementine renders the quotation differently in the two places — Ps. 94:9 has *in irritatione* where Heb. 3:8 quoting it has *in exacerbatione* — **so the Vulgate’s psalter and its Hebrews do not agree verbally.**

**The Alleluia is the least lexically integrated element of the formulary.** Its only substantive tie to any other element is *salutári* — **and that tie is to three composed orations rather than to a lesson**; *exsult-* and *iubil-* occur nowhere else in the ten elements, and it shares no other content word beyond *Dominus* and *Deus*. **No lexical warrant joins the Alleluia’s joy to the Gospel’s anxiety**: the Epistle’s *gaudium* and the Alleluia’s *exsultémus* are different roots in different elements with nothing joining them, and none of the expositors above reads the one as the resolution of the other.

**Cassiodorus and Theodoret disagree about the one word the chant contributes.** **Cassiodorus, in Latin at PL 70**, reads *iubilémus* as the psalm’s own invitation to the **jubilus** — wordless praise beyond syllables — and takes *Venite* through Is. 29:13, a people that draws near with the mouth; he also glosses *salutari* plainly of salvation, which quietly underlines how little else the verse carries. **Theodoret, in Greek at PG 80**, hears in the same verb **the shout of victors**, an epinician: the praise of those who have already conquered, and he composes the psalm in the person of King Josiah foreseeing his reform, from 4 Kgs 22–23 — **his own reconstruction and not what the psalm states**; he also reports the psalm **untitled among the Hebrews**, against the Clementine’s printed title. **They disagree about what *iubilémus* means.** **The *Deo salutári nostro* resonance:** the collocation is rare, and its only New Testament occurrence is the Magnificat’s *Deus salutari meo*.

**Four further readers of this psalm stand.** **A homily of the Chrysostomic corpus** (PG 55:615–618, **marked spurious in Migne’s apparatus, so the corpus and not Chrysostom carries it**) makes the psalm a summons to repentance and confession rather than to festal praise — *Metanoias gar ho paron bios, apeiles de kai kriseos he mellousa parousia* — proved from Hezekiah turning to the wall and from the Ninevites, whose sackcloth prevailed where purple, diadem and throne did not. **Arnobius the Younger** (PL 53:462D–463C): *exsultemus non in delectationibus vanis, sed in Domino; antequam praeoccupemur, nos praeoccupemus faciem eius in confessione peccatorum nostrorum*, with a warning against attending to Christ *in forma servi* as though he were a mere man. **Bruno the Carthusian** (*attributed*, PL 152:1142A–1143A): the title is *non est David, sed Christi*; the *Venite* addresses unbelieving Jews of the sixth age; *iubilo, id est nimia et intensa interiori laetitia exsulto*; and the following half-verse is read twice, once from court manners and once eschatologically, *Secundus autem adventus recte facies dicitur, eo quod tunc cunctis Christus manifestabitur*. **Peter Lombard** (PL 191:875A–876A) allots the appointed verse’s three verbs to three faculties — *Venite, fide et exsultemus mente, Domino, non in saeculo, Iubilemus voce, Deo salutari nostro Iesu* — **which is his own arrangement and is a reading of the chant’s own words** — while carrying the classical jubilus formula at exactly the appointed verb, *Gaudium sit tantum, quod verbis dici non possit, quod tamen intus conceptum, vox extra testetur*. **His sigla attribute the surrounding material to Augustine, Cassiodorus, Haymo and Alcuin, so that formula is almost certainly Cassiodorus’s wordless jubilus carried forward and not a third account.**

**All four read the psalm penitentially, and every one of them reaches that reading through v. 2, *Praeoccupemus faciem eius in confessione*, which the Alleluia does not appoint** — Bruno’s turning on the very word *faciem* the chant never sings. **The convergence is about the psalm and not about the chant, which stops at v. 1**, the same limit that stands at the Introit for Augustine and Cassiodorus.

> ***Expositiones in Psalmos* under Athanasius’s name, *Patrologia Graeca* 27:413–414.** **It glosses the appointed verb technically, as the *alalagmos* — the shout raised over fallen enemies**: *ho gar alalagmos hymnos estin epi peptokosin echthrois*, and the psalm therefore signifies that **Christ has warred down the opposing powers on our behalf and put them out of the way**, so that the singing is willed and not merely commanded. *Veníte* it reads as calling the disobedient to a feast. **This is that collection’s settled sense of the word and not an improvisation at this psalm**, recurring at three further places in the same volume; and it stands beside Theodoret’s shout of victors rather than under it. The authenticity of these *Expositiones* is contested, so the reading is the collection’s and not Athanasius’s.

**And Augustine, on the appointed verse itself, draws a moral distinction from it that the penitential readers do not.** In the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English: “if there were not in this life a wicked joy which is to be distinguished from a righteous joy, it would be enough to say, Come, let us rejoice; but he has briefly distinguished it. What is it to rejoice aright? To rejoice in the Lord.” He is not one of the medieval and later readers named above and he is not penitential here. **That is not joy against anxiety but right joy against wrong joy** — the same disjunctive shape as the Gospel’s two masters and the Gradual’s two objects of trust.

**The Alleluia is the least stable element of this formulary in the older chant books.** In the Roman Mass antiphonary the printed text gives *Hebdomada XIV* the 1962 Alleluia, while its footnotes report Compiègne with *Confitemini Domino* and the Theodericensian codices with *Quoniam Deus magnus* at that printed place — which, under the *±1* disturbance in that book’s own week-numbering, is not established as Theodericus’s own fourteenth week; across that stretch the printed Alleluia series agrees with 1962 at two weeks of twelve, and at both the collated manuscripts disagree with the printed text. **So the agreement at this Sunday is evidence about the editor’s exemplar rather than about the ninth-century state**, and those footnotes are an editor’s reports of manuscript readings and not transcriptions.

No later use of this chant’s wording can be tied to Ps. 94:1 at all: the King James wording of the verse stands at five psalms — Pss. 66:1, 81:1, 95:1–2, 98:4, 98:6 and 100:1 — and every located use is a music programme or a service listing. **The single appointed verse is also the opening of the daily Invitatory**, which puts any candidate for this element beyond identification by wording.

### Gospel <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>

*Nemo potest duóbus dóminis servíre* *Sequéntia sancti Evangélii secúndum Matthǽum* — Mt. 6, 24-33 marginal no. 1577

#### Where the lesson stands in Matthew 6

**The pericope is cut out of a continuous argument at both ends.** It begins bare at v. 24, with no connective particle, immediately after the treasure and eye sayings (*Ubi enim est thesaurus tuus, ibi est et cor tuum*, v. 21), and stops at v. 33 without v. 34, the passage’s own conclusion. *Sollícitus* governs the discourse four times, at vv. 25, 28, 31 and 34, and **the pericope carries three of them, ending on the promise of v. 33 rather than on the restriction to today.** That is also what lets the Communion take the Gospel’s last verse as its own antiphon.

**The pericope contains the second half of a repetition internal to Matthew 6 and not the first.** The Father’s foreknowledge of need at v. 32, *Scit enim Pater vester*, restates v. 8, which stands in the instruction on prayer the pericope does not include — and the Lord’s Prayer stands between the two members, asking for the kingdom’s coming and for bread. Matthew supplies that proximity; nothing in the appointed formulary points to it.

**Both sayings have Lucan parallels in different settings, and Luke orders the material the other way round.** Luke places the two-masters saying at 16:13, after the unjust steward, and adds *servus*; Matthew has it inside the Sermon without *servus*. Luke’s *corvos* and *cellarium, neque horreum* differ from Matthew’s *volatília cæli* and *hórrea*. And the ordering is reversed: in Matthew treasure-and-heart precedes the anxiety discourse and in Luke it follows — **which bears directly on whether Mt. 6:24 is heard as the close of the treasure unit or the head of the anxiety unit.** The appointed pericope’s boundary at v. 24 takes it as a head.

#### What the commentators actually argue

> **Augustine, *De sermone Domini in monte* II.47–58, continuous direct commentary on the appointed pericope.** **He reads v. 24’s asymmetry as deliberate:** Christ says of the second master *contemnet* and not *odio habebit* because, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English, “almost no one’s conscience can hate God; but he despises, i.e. he does not fear Him, as if feeling himself secure in consideration of His goodness.” **The move is a claim about which of two verbs the text uses, and the appointed Latin preserves exactly that contrast — but its premise is the sentence Augustine afterwards withdrew**, at *Retractationes* I.19, naming this very passage. **The observation about the two verbs stands on the appointed text and the reason he gave for it does not stand on his own later judgment**, and both are printed below. **Two things in the same locus are his own:** the remark that “mammon” is Punic for gain, which no Semitic authority among the sources above confirms; and the importation of “the prince of this world” from Jn. 12:31 to identify the second master, **which the appointed verse does not say.**

> **Jerome, *Commentarii in Matthaeum*, on the appointed verses.** **He draws the distinction on which any non-ascetical reading of the Gospel depends:** *non dixit, qui habet divitias, sed qui servit divitiis. Qui enim divitiarum servus est, divitias custodit, ut servus; qui autem servitutis excussit iugum, distribuit eas, ut dominus* — **Aquinas repeats the same distinction in almost the same words**, *aliud est habere divitias ut dominus, aliud ut servus*, and whether he drew on Jerome is not established. **He states the limit against over-reading in one sentence anchored inside the appointed bounds:** *labor exercendus est, sollicitudo tollenda* (ad v. 25), with *De praesentibus ergo concessit debere esse sollicitos qui futura prohibet cogitare* at vv. 31ff — **the second of the two standing inside the appointed bounds** — and a third independent Father setting the same limit. **He expressly rebuts allegorising the birds**, naming those who read them as angels and ministering powers, and refutes them from the *a fortiori* itself: *Si hoc ita est, ut intelligi volunt, quomodo sequitur dictum ad homines: Nonne vos magis pluris estis illis? Simpliciter ergo accipiendum* — **a ground internal to the appointed verse**. **His comment on the lilies is aesthetic, not allegorical:** *quod sericum, quae regum purpura, quae pictura textricum potest floribus comparari?* **He gives the other etymology of *mammonae*:** *Mammona sermone Syriaco divitiae nuncupantur* — **two Fathers, two eastern languages, and no Semitic authority beside them; what stands is their disagreement**. **And he records a variant the missal does not print**, *In nonnullis codicibus additum est neque quid bibatis* at v. 25, the missal’s shorter form being the one he treats as his own.

> **Hilary, *Commentarius in Matthaeum*, on the appointed verses.** **He is the Latin tradition’s allegorist at this pericope, and he argues for it:** the literal sense does not cohere with what precedes — *Quae consequuntur, non satis propositionibus congruunt. Numquid et unius domini servus non possit circa vestem esse cibumque sollicitus?* — and he reads the whole pericope as doctrine about the resurrection of the body, allegorising every appointed image: **birds the unclean spirits; the cubit the risen body raised to the measure of the perfect man (Eph. 4:13); lilies the brightness of the angels; the hay burnt tomorrow the gentiles.** **His hay-as-gentiles reading ends in a bodily eternity of the damned no other witness states**, and the birds-as-unclean-spirits reading is the one place where his allegory works *against* the pericope’s consoling sense. **At the verse the Mass lifts out he reads plainly**, grounding *Haec enim omnia gentes inquirunt* in unbelief rather than appetite — *Gentium igitur est, infidelitatis istius cura angi* — **the one place where his resurrection reading and the plain sense converge**; his exposition runs on to v. 34, which the lesson excludes. **His lemma lacks the last step of Chrysostom’s escalation**, reading *cras in ignem mittitur* where the missal prints *cras in clíbanum* — **the step Chrysostom’s rhetoric turns on is not in every ancient Latin witness**, and the medieval Gloss knows the variant, *Alii libri habent in ignem, vel in acervum, qui habet speciem clibani*. **He is not refuted by Jerome:** Jerome’s target is a reading of the *birds* as angels, Hilary’s angels are the *lilies*, and that Jerome names Hilary among the Latin commentators he had read is not evidence that he has him in view here.

> **Thomas Aquinas, *Super Matthaeum* cap. 6 lect. 3–5.** **He fastens on the other member of the same four-verb figure Augustine reads, and explains it from the same asymmetry:** *Et non dicit, diliget, quia Diabolus naturaliter diligi non potest. Deus naturaliter diligitur; Diabolus vero sustinetur* — **complementary to Augustine’s *contemnet* observation, and both checkable against the appointed Latin, which prints all four verbs.** He reads the whole saying as a claim about ultimate ends, *impossibile est quod animus feratur ad duos fines simul et semel*, and **on the cubit he reads an experimental proof of providence**: *Hic probat experimento, quod sicut providet avibus, ita et nobis … Unde augmentum non est nobis ex nobis, sed ex Deo: unde de providentia Dei non debetis desperare.* The measure of a man’s stature is not in his own power, so growth is from God and not from ourselves, and the anxiety the pericope forbids is on that ground demonstrably vain. **He independently names the sect Augustine’s controversy leaves unnamed:** *destruit hic error Euticharum, qui dicebant quod viri apostolici non debebant laborare*, answered from 2 Thess. 3:10 — **which does not settle the identification**: *De haeresibus* is the work that would name them, and no text of it stands behind the name. *Euticharum* is the text’s spelling and is reproduced as printed. **And *Super Matthaeum* ascribes the lilies-as-angels reading to Jerome**; the reading is **Hilary’s**, and the *Catena aurea* — Thomas’s own compilation — attributes it correctly to *Hilarius in Matth.* **The slip is most likely the reporter’s** — *Super Matthaeum* is a *reportatio* — **and belongs to that text and not to Thomas.**

> **Augustine, on the birds and the lilies.** **He explicitly forbids allegorising them**, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English: “these examples are not to be treated as allegories, so that we should inquire what the fowls of heaven or the lilies of the field mean: for they stand here, in order that from smaller matters we may be persuaded respecting greater ones,” with the unjust judge as his comparison case. **The Latin tradition is namedly divided at these images** — Hilary allegorises them, Jerome and Augustine forbid it — and one of the two principal Latin Fathers commenting on this very pericope says its central images are not to be taken allegorically. His restraint is specific: he bars allegorising *these examples*, not the pericope as a whole, and he is at the same time willing to read “seek first the kingdom” as a doctrine of ordered ends.

> **John Chrysostom, *Homiliae in Matthaeum* 21–22.** **He reads the illustrations as rhetorically engineered.** The birds are chosen *because* they are irrational, deliberately in place of Moses, Elijah or John — in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English, “that He might touch them more to the quick … He has cut off from them every excuse”; and the lilies are progressively degraded — lilies, then “grass of the field,” then “which today is,” then “cast into the oven” — so the *a fortiori* grows at every step. **The appointed Latin fits word for word:** *fænum agri, quod hódie est, et cras in clíbanum míttitur … quanto magis vos módicæ fidei?*, and he calls the rebuke “not want, but poverty, of faith.” **Read now in the Greek at *Patrologia Graeca* 57:293, 299–300 and 303 — homily XXI opens exactly at this Gospel’s first verse — he refuses a different over-reading three times over**: what Christ abolished is not the sowing and the spinning but the anxiety about them, *ou toinyn to me merimnan tauta ponos, alla to merimnan*, *ou ton sporon anetrepsen, alla ten phrontida*, *ou to ergon aneilen, alla ten merimnan*. On *nec Sálomon in omni glória sua* he holds that Solomon was outdone not once or twice but *di’ holes tes basileias autou*, through his whole reign. **He cites Scripture loosely**, and Migne’s apparatus records manuscript disagreement at neighbouring words, so his short citations are evidence about his citation habit and not a controlled witness to a Gospel text-form. **And homily 22 carries the alternative number XXIII in its own running head**, so the column is the surer address for it.

> **Thomas Aquinas, *Summa theologiae* II-II q. 55 a. 6 and q. 19 aa. 2–3, in the English of the Fathers of the English Dominican Province.** **He expounds this pericope systematically.** Solicitude about temporal things is unlawful in three ways, the third **“through over much fear, when, to wit, a man fears to lack necessary things if he do what he ought to do”**; and our Lord gives three motives for laying aside that fear — **the greater gifts already given, the care God takes of animals and plants without man’s assistance, and divine providence itself, “through ignorance of which the gentiles are solicitous in seeking temporal goods before all others”** — which read as ascending. **At q. 19 he divides fear by its relation to God** into worldly, servile, filial and initial, holding that “worldly love is, properly speaking, the love whereby a man trusts in the world as his end … for which reason worldly fear is always evil,” while allowing regard for what is divine in the creature. **That is a reading of the Gospel’s *gentes* and of its *nolíte sollíciti esse* together.**

**Where both Fathers comment they agree the images are arguments and not figures, and they differ in what the argument is.** Augustine grounds the inference in the **rank** of the creature — in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series, “a rational being such as man has a higher rank in the nature of things than irrational ones.” Chrysostom grounds it in the **vileness** of the creature and in the deliberate rhetorical descent, and gives the anthropological ground in terms of *gift* rather than rank, in the English of the same series: “you, to whom He gave a soul, for whom He fashioned a body … for whose sake He gave up His only begotten Son.” **The difference is not a contradiction, but the middle terms differ and the two yield different homiletic conclusions from the same verses:** the argument from lesser to greater is common to both Fathers, and the term that carries it is not.

**Both Fathers independently set a limit against over-reading the pericope.** Augustine at II.57–58: Christ kept money bags, Paul worked with his hands, the church at Antioch stored food against Agabus’s famine, and the whole precept reduces to the rule, in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series, “that even in looking after such things we should think of the kingdom of God, but in the service of the kingdom of God we should not think of such things.” Chrysostom reaches the same limit by a different route on v. 26, in the English of the same series: “He said not, we must not sow, but we must not take thought; neither that one ought not to work, but not to be low-minded.” **Where the claim must rest inside the appointed bounds, Chrysostom’s sentence is the better one**, because Augustine’s material there is anchored partly in v. 34, which this lesson excludes, while Chrysostom’s is anchored in v. 26, which it includes.

#### The Latin transmission of this Gospel, and the sentence its author withdrew

> **Augustine, *Retractationes* I.19, PL 32:617.** **He withdraws the sentence the Latin tradition repeats most often at this Gospel’s first verse:** *Item quod dixi, Nullius enim fere conscientia Deum potest odisse (Lib. 2, c. 14, n. 48), non video fuisse dicendum. Multi enim sunt de quibus scriptum est: Superbia eorum qui oderunt te (Psal. LXXIII, 23).* **The self-citation fixes the work as *De sermone Domini in monte* II.14.48**, his exposition of *aut unum sustinébit, et álterum contémnet* — **so the strongest single patristic move on this Gospel is one its author afterwards refused.** The sentence stands as the *Retractationes* quotes it and not as *De serm. Dom.* prints it.

**Five later Latin witnesses repeat the withdrawn sentence, and one denies it outright.** Rabanus, the Glossa, Christian of Stavelot, Paschasius and the exposition printed under Bede’s name all carry it **without Augustine’s qualifier *fere***; **the Glossa alone flags the retraction**; **Rabanus prints *ferre* for *fere* and pseudo-Bede prints *Nullius enim scientia Deum odisse ferre potest***, and *scientia* for *conscientia* cannot be anyone’s theology. **The pattern is transmission**, and not five authors each hardening Augustine on his own account. And **Bruno of Segni contradicts the proposition from Scripture**: *Quis enim servit mammonae, nisi avarus? Avarus igitur Deum contemnit et odio habet. Unde hoc? Quia idolis servit*, from Eph. 5:5. **So “no one can hate God” is neither the tradition’s settled reading at Mt. 6:24 nor Augustine’s own last word on it.**

> **Christian of Stavelot (Druthmarus), *Expositio in Matthaeum* cap. XIII, PL 106:1316C–1319A.** **The most literal-minded of the Latin witnesses above.** He answers the objection that Paul commands *serve one another* by the Greek distinction of *latreusis* and *douleusis*, quoting both words in Greek characters; identifies the two masters as the devil and God; glosses mammon as Syriac for riches, with *Aliud est habere divitias, aliud servire divitiis*; **reads the lilies with no allegory at all**, on the bare ground that no linen matches the lily’s whiteness, no dye the rose, no purple the violet; explains Solomon as the example because the Jews knew no richer king though richer men existed; and supplies the elided noun at Mt. 6:33 with its limit, *haec, subauditur necessaria, adjicientur … Ad necessitatem, non ad superfluitatem*.

> **Paschasius Radbertus, *Expositio in Matthaeum*, PL 120:306B–307A and 312C–313A.** **He grounds the impossibility of two masters in the baptismal renunciation** — no one enters the font until he renounces the devil, all his works and all his pomps — and calls the relapsed Christian a foolish soldier throwing away immortal arms. **He excepts the Trinity from the rule**, *Pater, et Filius, et Spiritus sanctus, non tres Domini*, because service fails only where a discordant will intervenes. **And at Mt. 6:33 he denies that *primum* marks order at all:** *Primum autem, non ad ordinem referendum puto … magis ad quantitatem referendum est, ut extra hoc nihil quaerendum sit*, calling the sequence *praeposterus* and explaining that the kingdom was put first to soothe the hearing before justice was brought in as the laborious thing, and reading the promise through use and enjoyment, *his utamur in via, quatenus illo fruamur in patria*. **So the word the Communion antiphon promotes to its head is contested, and Paschasius stands against the Augustine the compilations transmit.**

> **Rabanus Maurus, on the appointed verses, PL 107:836B–839A.** **At these verses he compiles rather than composes.** The material runs *(Hier.)* for the mammon gloss and the flowers and *(Aug.)* for the two masters and for *Quaerite primum*, with **one** sentence of his own marked *(Maur.)*. The Augustine he transmits supplies the sharpest surviving formula for the Communion’s word — *Cum enim dicit illud primum, significavit quod hoc posterius quaerendum est, non tempore, sed dignitate* — **but that sentence stands inside his compilation and Migne’s sigla are editorial.**

> **Glossa ordinaria on Matthew, PL 114:105A–107A.** It reports mammon as also the name of a demon set over riches — **and reports it as hearsay, *Dicitur hoc nomen esse daemonis***; grounds the two masters in baptism exactly as Paschasius does; **records a variant at Mt. 6:30**, *Alii libri habent: in ignem vel in acervum qui habet speciem clibani*; and **alone of the compilations flags that Augustine withdrew the sentence about hating God.** **The variant is a fact about the Gospel text, and no Latin witness here settles whether any book actually prints *in ignem*.**

> **The Matthew exposition printed under Bede’s name, PL 92:34D–36A.** It raises the question whether bodily food belongs to the soul and answers it by taking *anima* at Mt. 6:25 as this present life, *cujus retinaculum est alimentum istud corporeum*; carries the anti-allegorical line *Ista vero documenta de avibus et liliis non allegorice ponuntur*; and **reads the Communion’s own verb precisely**, *Non dixit nobis dari, sed adjici, quia nobis in intentione aeternitas, in usu vero debet esse temporalitas*.

> **Bruno of Segni, *Commentaria in Matthaeum*, PL 165:121B–123C.** **The sharpest counter-witness at this Gospel.** Besides denying that the covetous man can fail to hate God, he raises a literal objection nobody else here raises — a man plainly *can* serve many masters, *non solum duobus dominis, sed et multis dominis servire non est impossibile*, which is why the verse must be read of these two in particular; defines serving riches as fearing to spend them; reads the lilies literally; and **restricts the whole prohibition of anxiety to apostles, their successors and those aiming at perfection.** **So his exposition is addressed to a narrower audience than a Sunday congregation.** His own *Expositio in Psalmos* reaches all four appointed psalms, and no reading of them here is his.

> **Godfrey of Admont, *Homilia* LXXXIII, PL 174:580C–583D — his Fifteenth Sunday, and the far end of the Latin division.** He takes the whole prohibition of anxiety as concerning **spiritual** food and clothing, not visible food, drink or dress but the invisible nourishment and clothing of soul and spirit, so that *quid manducetis* asks what taste of contemplation one may have; widens mammon past riches to whatever a man sets his heart on and loves more than God; makes the kingdom to be sought first the heart, which ought always to be God’s kingdom; makes **the birds the good angels**, who neither sow by reading nor reap by taking the fruit of reading nor gather into barns by recollecting it into memory; makes **the lilies the evil spirits**, who do not spin because a spinner draws the thread from left to right and they draw everything they do the other way; and makes **Solomon Christ**, Solomon’s name being interpreted the peaceable one. **He raises the objection his own allegory creates and answers it, so he is a self-aware instance of the allegorising side and not a naive one.** **His spiritual-food reading depends on the pericope running past Mt. 6:33, where this lesson stops, and he is preaching to monks about *lectio* and not to a Sunday congregation.**

**So the Latin division at the images is sharper than a two-way split.** On one side the Augustine the compilations transmit, pseudo-Bede repeating him, Christian of Stavelot refusing allegory on aesthetic grounds and Bruno of Segni reading the lilies literally; on the other Godfrey, allegorising every image and defending the allegory against his own objection. **That is not a difference of emphasis.** Godfrey’s proof text for the pride of the fallen angels is Ps. 73:23, **the same verse Augustine gives in the *Retractationes* as his reason for withdrawing the sentence about hating God.**

> **Augustine, *De opere monachorum* §27, §30, §31 — a *controversy* over these verses.** **This runs against the obvious devotional reading.** Certain monks quoted these very verses to argue that they need not work, and Augustine wrote against them, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English: “let these persons, who from perverse understanding of the Gospel labor to pervert apostolic precepts, either take no thought for the morrow, even as the birds of the air; or let them obey the Apostle”; “against the Apostle of Christ, they recite a Gospel of Christ.” The identification of those monks as the Euchitae rests on the *Augustinus de Haeres.* citation; *De haeresibus* is the work that would settle it, and no text of it stands behind the name.

**Four of the *Catena aurea*’s attributions on this Gospel hold against the underlying works and the rest do not.** The appointed Gospel occupies cap. 6 lect. 16–21, drawing on Augustine, Jerome, Hilary, Chrysostom, Gregory, Rabanus, Remigius and the Gloss. **The Catena cites *Chrysostomus in Matth.* and *Chrysostomus super Matth.* as different things:** the first is the genuine *Homiliae in Matthaeum*, sentence for sentence Homily 22’s own text; **the second is the anonymous *Opus imperfectum in Matthaeum*, a fifth-century work with Arian features whose attribution to Chrysostom is universally rejected.** **So a citation of “Chrysostom” on Mt. 6:33 drawn from the *super Matth.* formula names a Doctor of the Church for a text that is not his.** **Four of its attributions hold against the underlying works** — the Chrysostom of lect. 19, the *De opere monachorum* of lect. 17, and **every *Hilarius* and *Hieronymus* excerpt on the appointed Gospel, sentence for sentence what those works themselves carry**, which the Catena reproduces faithfully. Those excerpts stand against the same Migne recensions the Catena drew on, so what agrees is the transmission and not the Migne text’s own correctness. **No work underlying its Gregory or its Remigius stands beside them**, so each of those two attributions rests on the Catena alone. **Rabanus and the Gloss stand directly at Migne above**, where what they are is visible — compilations whose sigla are editorial.

### Offertory <span class="nodecor">(*Off.*)</span>

*Immíttet Angelus Dómini* *Ant. ad Offertorium* — Ps. 33, 8-9 marginal no. 1578

**The psalm is anchored by its own superscription to a named deliverance narrative — and the narrative does not fit the name.** *Davidi, cum immutavit vultum suum coram Achimelech*. The Douay prints the cross-reference in the text as “\[1 Kings 21.\],” which in Douay book-naming is modern **1 Samuel 21**. **Read whole, that chapter shows Achimelech the priest at Nobe in vv. 1–9 and the change of countenance at v. 13 before Achis, king of Geth**; the CPDV reads *Abimelech* at the superscription. **No witness here explains the divergence**, and the standard scholarly explanation of it lies outside them all. It bears materially, because Augustine’s Eucharistic reading below is anchored in David *before Achis* while the Latin superscription names Achimelech. So the angel who encamps stands in a psalm whose superscription reads it off a concrete escape, and *eripiet eos* at v. 8 repeats the *eripuit me* of v. 5. **The antiphon stops one clause short of the psalm’s beatitude.**

**Cassiodorus reports the psalm’s alphabetic acrostic and locates the appointed verses within it.** His *Divisio psalmi* states it: *Per totum psalmum verba prophetae sunt, alphabeti Hebraei litteras, excepta sexta, in capitibus versuum per ordinem ponentis*, tagging every verse with its letter — **HETH at the Offertory’s first verse and TETH at its second** — and on the skipped letter, *Ego enim non inveni Patrum definitam de hac parte sententiam*. He is a sixth-century Latin reader reporting about a Hebrew text he does not print, so what stands is his report and no statement about the Hebrew itself. Bellarmine notes the same structure, but at vv. 2–3, reaching no appointed verse, and in an abridged Victorian English translation.

> **Augustine, *Enarratio in Ps. XXXIII* **Sermo II**.** **The passage stands in Sermo II and is absent from Sermo I entirely.** **He rejects a rival reading and defends the one the missal prints**, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English: “So it is written, brethren, not as some bad copies have it, *The Lord shall send His Angel round about them that fear Him* … but thus, *The Angel of the Lord shall send round about them that fear Him*.” He then identifies that Angel as Christ, “the Angel of the great Counsel.” The 1962 Offertory prints *Immíttet Angelus Dómini* — *Angelus Dómini* nominative subject of *immíttet* — **the very reading he defends.** His Latin runs: *mendosi codices: Immittet Angelum Dominus … sed sic: Immitte\[t\] Angelus Domini …* **And at §11 he reads *gustáte et vidéte* of the Eucharist**, “the same Sacrament, whereby He was carried in His Own Hands,” against Jn. 6:52–53.

**The printed books carry a three-way split at that verb and subject, and it changes the sense.** The 1962 typical edition’s own page reads *Immíttet Angelus Dómini in circúitu timéntium eum*; the Roman Mass antiphonary at *Patrologia Latina* 78 reads *Immittet angelum Dominus*; and Legg’s Sarum reads *Inmittit angelus domini*, Dickinson’s *Immittit angelus Domini*. ***Immittet angelum Dominus* makes the Lord send an angel; *Immíttet Angelus Dómini* makes the angel of the Lord encamp; and the Sarum present tense makes it a standing fact rather than a promise.** **Which reading each reception witness had before him is not established, and it matters for every reading that turns on the angel’s identity.**

> **Basil of Caesarea, *Homilia in Psalmum XXXIII* §§5–6, *Patrologia Graeca* 29:363–366, read in Greek with Migne’s facing Latin.** **He expounds both halves of the appointed Offertory directly and consecutively.** At v. 8 the single angel is worth a whole encamped army, and *in circuitu* is a city wall: *Quemadmodum enim urbium muri in circuitu omni ex parte circumducti, undecunque hostium assultus arcent: ita etiam angelus et praemunit a fronte, et a tergo custodit* — **and that Latin is Migne’s facing translation at this column and not Basil’s own Greek**. He warns that grave sin drives the angel off as smoke drives bees and stench doves. At v. 9 he grounds *gustáte et vidéte* on the soul’s senses being named homonymously with the body’s, and, **because the Lord is the true bread and his flesh true food, insists the sweetness is reached by a spiritual tasting**; **and the psalm says *Taste* and not *Be filled*, because the present grace is an *arrhabo*** that will only later become full fruition, with 1 Cor. 13:12 beside it. **Whether he means the eucharistic species specifically or the spiritual apprehension of the Word is not settled by the passage, and both halves of the sentence stand in it.** **Migne’s facing Latin renders his lemma at v. 8 *Castra metabitur angelus Domini***, for the Greek *parembalei*, where the missal’s Latin prints *Immíttet Angelus Dómini* — **the two Latin verbs differ** — and **that Latin column carries *angelus Domini* as its subject, standing with the missal at the disputed noun**. **The remainder of v. 8 and the whole of v. 9a stand word for word as the missal prints them.** Only §§5–6 reach the appointed verses, and Migne’s text of them stands beside no critical edition of his psalm homilies.

> ***Selecta in Psalmos* under Origen’s name, *Patrologia Graeca* 12:1307–1308.** At v. 8 it carries **the earlier form of Basil’s own gloss** — *ho angelos tou Kyriou holes paremboles logon epechei heis on*, the one angel holding the place of a whole encampment. **At v. 9 it gives a different account from Basil’s**: *ei geustos estin ho Kyrios, dia tes pisteos esti geustos; kai ei chrestos, dia tes gnoseos esti chrestos* — tasted through faith, found good through knowledge. At v. 10 it raises the objection the verse invites, from Heb. 11:37–38, and answers that those who lacked in food and drink did not lack in knowledge. The wording is close enough and the collection early enough that Basil is plainly developing an inherited gloss rather than inventing one, but a catena’s attributions cannot establish the direction of dependence. The faith-and-knowledge antithesis and the appeal to *gnosis* are the Evagrian idiom, and the attribution caution recorded at the Introit governs here too.

**Augustine’s text reads *et ERUET eos* where the missal prints *et ERIPIET eos*** — **so the two agree exactly on the point in dispute, which noun is the subject, and differ on the following verb**, and the missal’s text is therefore not simply Augustine’s. What stands between them is a convergence at the disputed word: the appointed text and a Father’s defended reading agree on which noun governs the verb. **The missal’s capital *Angelus* is an orthographic fact**, and no textual history joins the two readings by descent.

> **Cassiodorus, *Expositio in Psalterium*, at Ps. 33 vv. 8–9.** **His lemma reads *Immittet angelum Dominus* — exactly the reading Augustine denounced as belonging to faulty copies.** **So the variant Augustine fought was still standard in the principal medieval Latin psalm commentary, and the 1962 Offertory follows Augustine against Cassiodorus** — a convergence and not a descent, nothing tracing transmission. **He does not identify the angel with Christ**: he reads *Immittet* as deliberately covert — *propter insolentiam humanae fragilitatis non palam facit, sed occultis immissionibus operatur* — and turns to the moral sense, *si vis te angelum fieri, fac quod praecipit … Tunc enim spiritu angeli sumus, quando ministri supernae voluntatis efficimur*. **He is positive evidence that a Latin reader could take the verse otherwise.**

> **Cassiodorus, on *gustáte et vidéte* in the same exposition.** **His eucharistic reading is anchored in the appointed verbs themselves, where Augustine’s is anchored in the superscription the antiphon does not print:** *Gustate non pertinet ad palatum, sed animae suavissimum sensum, qui divina contemplatione saginatur. Nam ut ipsum gustum intelligeres, sequitur, videte, quod utique non ad os pertinet* — and he reads it eucharistically from the verb, citing Jn. 6:53 and the life-giving flesh taken from the Virgin. **So the reading is available to a hearer of the antiphon alone**, lifting the limit Augustine’s route imposes; and it converges with Cyril of Jerusalem’s “trust not the judgment to your bodily palate” — in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, second series, vol. 7 — reached in Latin and without dependence on the Jerusalem catechesis.

> **Augustine, on *gustáte et vidéte* in the same exposition.** **He reads v. 9 as openly Eucharistic**, decoding the psalm’s superscription: the David who, in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series, “was carried in his own hands” before Achish is Christ holding his own Body — *cum commendaret ipsum corpus suum et sanguinem suum, accepit in manus suas quod norunt fideles* — so “taste” is set against the incomprehension of Jn. 6:52. **His reading is anchored in the psalm’s superscription, and the Offertory antiphon prints no superscription**, so his reasoning stands as reception of the psalm and not as something a hearer of the antiphon alone could recover.

> **Arnobius the Younger (*attributed*), in Ps. XXXIII, PL 53:368C–369B.** **He makes two different claims at this verse.** The **Eucharistic** gloss: *Gustate corpus vitae, et videte quam suavis est Dominus: Vitam enim habet in semetipso, qui manducaverit carnem eius, et biberit sanguinem eius* (Jn. 6) — **a third Latin witness at this verse, and unlike the other two a psalm commentary reading the appointed verse in place rather than a mystagogical reuse.** And the **paschal** identification of the angel: *misit angelum suum in circuitu timentium corpus eius, et revolvit lapidem a monumento* (Mt. 28), with the poor man who cried read as Christ on the cross. **The second depends on repointing *timéntium eum* as those fearing *for* his body, which is not what the missal’s Latin says**.

**And two further readers reach the angel and the verse.** **Godfrey of Admont** (PL 174:580A–B) makes the angel the gift of grace itself — *Angelus iste, quem Deus in circuitu timentium se immittit, donum gratiae illius est, sine cuius munere homo nunquam videre et gustare valet quoniam suavis est Dominus* — his Fourteenth Sunday again not the 1962 one. **And Prosper of Aquitaine quotes this Offertory’s own words inside his exposition of the Gradual’s psalm**, *ut gustemus et videamus quam suavis est Dominus*: **a Father joining the two chants’ psalms in one sentence, which is a fact about his exposition and not about the formulary.** **So the angel of this chant now carries four accounts — Christ, the moral sense, the angel of the sepulchre, and the gift of grace — and the formulary adjudicates none of them.**

**Two fourth-century mystagogical witnesses attest the verse as the Church’s own communion invitation, and the 1962 Mass does not use it that way.** Cyril of Jerusalem, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, second series vol. 7, reports it actually being chanted at the communion: “ye hear the chanter inviting you with a sacred melody to the communion of the Holy Mysteries, and saying, O taste and see that the Lord is good. Trust not the judgment to your bodily palate.” Ambrose, in vol. 10 of the same series, cites it to identify what is eaten: “Taste and see that the Lord is good, blessed is the man that hopes in Him. In that sacrament is Christ.” **In the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday the same verse stands at the Offertory, before the consecration, and this Mass’s Communion is a different text entirely.** Note also that **Ambrose quotes the verse *with* the clause *beatus vir qui sperat in eo* which the Roman Offertory omits**, which sharpens rather than weakens the observation that the antiphon cuts at *Dóminus*. **Cyril’s authorship of the Mystagogical Catecheses is disputed in modern scholarship**, and both witnesses speak at Jerusalem and Milan and not at Rome.

**And the displacement is attested as living practice in a surviving Western rite.** The Mozarabic *Missale mixtum* appoints this psalm verse as the *ad accedentes* chant at communion time, cued by incipit thirty-four times across the book. **What that book attests is the appointment and not the text**: what it prints at those cues is not established.

**The Offertory’s argument runs close to the Gospel’s two verses past its own cut.** Ps. 33:10–11 (*Timete Dominum … Divites eguerunt, et esurierunt; inquirentes autem Dominum non minuentur omni bono*) and Mt. 6:32–33 both set seeking God against want of food and both promise that those who seek will not lack. **The likeness is one of argument and not of wording, and neither text quotes the other.** The Latin verbs differ, and **the Gospel’s own *inquírunt* at v. 32 is used of the gentiles negatively where the psalm uses the participle positively**, which cuts against reading it as a verbal link. **And the appointed excerpt does not contain the psalm verses in question at all**, so the resemblance is between the Gospel and the psalm and not between two appointed texts.

**The chant’s other Roman use.** Ps. 33:8–9 stands at **exactly two Masses of the 1862 Missal**, the second appointed immediately after the Canaanite-woman Gospel ending *O mulier, magna est fides tua*.

### Secret <span class="nodecor">(*Sec.*)</span>

*Concéde nobis, Dómine, quǽsumus* *Secreta* marginal no. 1579

**One victim, two genitives.** The two *et* clauses are parallel predicates of the one subject *hæc hóstia salutáris*, and the second genitive is objective in a different way from the first: the victim is *purgátio* *of our offences* and *propitiátio* *of thy power* — **the appeasing of God’s power, not a power belonging to the propitiation.** At the offering, the protection the Introit and Collect asked for becomes propitiation. **Guéranger construes *propitiátio potestátis* the same way**, and **Schuster likewise reads *tuæ propitiátio potestátis* as the appeasing of the divine omnipotence.**

**The printed books do not agree on the word that carries it, and the sense expounded here is read of this Sunday’s own form and not of a settled wording.** 1962 and Menard’s penitential votive series at PL 78:208–209 have *potestátis*; his Lenten station at St Chrysogonus, PL 78:74, has *Majestatis*; and Legg’s Sarum has *pietatis*. *Hóstia* against *oblacio*, and *delictórum* against *peccatorum*, vary independently of that word and of each other. **The doublet the 1962 Missal prints straddles the split**: its Passion Monday Secret is Menard’s Lenten form verbatim and this Sunday’s is his votive form with *delictórum* for *peccatorum*, so the two part at the address — *Domine Deus noster* in the Lenten form against this Sunday’s *Dómine, quǽsumus* — and at the word expounded here, the Lenten form reading *Majestatis*. **Three printed books — Migne’s reprint of Menard, Legg’s Oxford Sarum Missal and the modern typical edition — are not a critical apparatus**, and no manuscript stands behind any of the three readings; two independently edited books agreeing at so unusual a word is far past what a misreading of type explains. A modern registry of the 1962 calendar, and not a facsimile of that day’s page, is what puts the Lenten form at Passion Monday. **Which reading is prior, and whether the Lenten and the Sunday forms are one prayer or two, is not established.**

**Transmission.** The Secret stands with the Collect in Gelasianum Book III sect. X and has a second, penitential home at sect. XLI — with the narrowing recorded above, that the second home is a feature of the Vatican manuscript’s arrangement rather than of the Gelasian tradition at large.

**No patristic or medieval exegesis of this prayer stands anywhere in the *Patrologia Latina***: *propitiatio potestatis* and *purificent semper et muniant* return five occurrences between them across that whole collection, and every one is a sacramentary printing one of the two prayers. **Three of the five are the Secret’s** — the Gelasian at Book III sect. X and at the penitential sect. XLI, and the Gregorian at *Dominica I ante natalem Domini*, reading *purgatio peccatorum* for *delictorum*. **The other two are the Postcommunion’s** — the floating series of unassigned Postcommunions appended after the Canon at printed p. 237, and the saint-adapted Gelasian recension for St Andrew’s vigil, reading *nos, Domine*. **What the Secret and the Postcommunion do have is modern comment at paraphrase length:** Schuster treats all three orations of this Mass and Guéranger gives the Secret and the Postcommunion a sentence each. **The Collect, alone of the three, has a medieval reception, and it is set out above.**

### Communion <span class="nodecor">(*Comm.*)</span>

*Primum quǽrite regnum Dei* *Ant. ad Communionem* — Mt. 6, 33 marginal no. 1580

#### The one substantive act this formulary performs on its Bible text

**The Communion departs in exactly four ways from Mt. 6:33 as the Clementine, the Lucan parallel and the Byzantine Greek carry it**: *ergo* is dropped and *Primum* promoted to head the clause; *et iustítiam eius* is omitted; *hæc* is dropped before *ómnia*; and *dicit Dóminus* is added. **That tag stands in none of Clementine Mt. 6:33, Clementine Lk. 12:31, or the Byzantine Greek at Mt. 6:33.** And **Luke, unlike the antiphon, keeps *iustitiam eius*, so the omission is not explained by conflation with the Lucan form.**

Two of the four bear theologically. Adding *dicit Dóminus* **turns a saying inside a sermon into an address made to the communicant at the moment of reception**; and dropping *et iustítiam eius* leaves the kingdom to be sought without the justice the Gospel names beside it.

> **Augustine, *De sermone Domini in monte* II.53, II.55, II.56, on *primum*.** **He argues an ordering of importance and not of time**, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English: “in using that word *first*, He has indicated that this is to be sought later, not in point of time, but in point of importance.” **Rabanus Maurus’s compilation on these verses carries that same sentence in Latin** — *non tempore, sed dignitate*, at *Patrologia Latina* 107:836B–839A under the siglum *(Aug.)* — so a compilation’s Latin answers the English, and Migne’s sigla there are editorial. He makes the point by denying the sequence the word invites, in the same English — “He did not say, *Then* seek such things.” He offers an inversion test at II.55 and ties the phrase back to Mt. 6:24 at II.56.

**And the word the recasting promotes is contested.** **Paschasius Radbertus denies that *primum* marks order at all** — *non ad ordinem referendum puto … magis ad quantitatem referendum est* — against the *non tempore, sed dignitate* the compilations transmit under Augustine’s name. His is the earliest reading of that word recorded here.

> **Thomas Aquinas, *Super Matthaeum* cap. 6 lect. 5, on all three members of the verse the antiphon recasts.** **He expounds the kingdom as the end sought**, because the kingdom is beatitude and *Regnum dicitur a regendo: tunc enim homo regitur, quando voluntati regentis subditur*; the justice as *his* and not man’s, *quia per iustitiam propriam nullus potest venire ad regnum*; and the rest as added over and above, *quasi, ultra forum*. He adds *non debemus evangelizare ut comedamus, sed potius e converso*. **This is a reading of Mt. 6:33 in its Gospel place and not of the antiphon**, and his gloss on *iustitiam eius* answers a question the recasting itself raises, since the antiphon drops that clause; whether the recaster meant anything by the omission is not established. **And Hilary, who allegorises every other image in this pericope, reads this verse plainly**, making the seeking the wage of our life, *regnumque Dei vitae nostrae stipendiis quaeramus*.

**The verb the antiphon keeps when it drops everything else has readers too.** **Chrysostom rests the whole promise on it**, in Greek at *Patrologia Graeca* 57:303: *kai ouk eipe, Dothesetai, alla, Prostethesetai* — he said not *shall be given* but *shall be added*, “that you may learn that the things present are nothing great, set beside the greatness of the things to come,” in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English, first series. Pseudo-Bede: *Non dixit nobis dari, sed adjici, quia nobis in intentione aeternitas, in usu vero debet esse temporalitas.* Christian of Stavelot supplies the elided noun and its limit, *subauditur necessaria … Ad necessitatem, non ad superfluitatem.* Paschasius reads the promise through use and enjoyment. **And Godfrey of Admont reads *regnum Dei* at this verse as the heart**, which ought always to be God’s kingdom — a fourth thing done with the phrase the Communion sings.

**One further fact bears on how the antiphon’s omission may be read.** Chrysostom’s own citation of Mt. 6:33, at the same column, **drops *et iustítiam eius* exactly as the antiphon does** — while keeping the kingdom *of heaven* where the antiphon keeps *regnum Dei*, and fronting no *primum* at all. **That is a convergence in the practice of abbreviating and nothing more**: it shows how ordinary such a shortening is, nothing supports dependence in either direction, and he cites Scripture loosely enough that his short form is evidence about his citation habit rather than about any Gospel text-form. **Every one of them comments on Mt. 6:33 in its Gospel place, and none of them comments on the antiphon as such**, and the one reader who comes to it as an antiphon, Schuster, **reaches back for the *et iustítiam eius* the recasting removed** and glosses the justice from the Gospel verse.

Augustine’s exegesis of Mt. 6:33 is his own, and it makes word order carry the doctrine. **The missal’s Communion antiphon prints *Primum* at its head**, where the Gospel verse carries it third; Augustine expounds the verse and not the antiphon, and his exposition runs on to v. 34, which the appointed pericope excludes.

#### And the Communion is a later substitution at this Mass

**Five independent witnesses converge on the antiphon being secondary in this position.** ***Primum quaerite* is not a late composition:** it is an ancient Roman antiphon standing at the *Ninth* Sunday in four ninth-century antiphonaries, while the Fourteenth Sunday’s Communion in those books is *Panis quem ego dedero*. **So the antiphon is secondary in this slot only, and not in the repertory.** **And the substitution broke a pair**: *Panis quem ego dedero* travels with the Offertory *Immittet Angelus* at *both* its occurrences in those books, the Lenten Thursday and this Sunday, so putting *Primum quaerite* here separated two chants the older books kept together.

The other four. First, the appointed text itself: this is the formulary’s one substantive departure from its Bible text. Second, Guéranger: “The Communion-anthem, taken from the Gospel which now is assigned to this Sunday, was not the one primitively used; the ancient liturgists make no mention of it in its present position, nor is it to be found there in any of the manuscripts consulted by Blessed Thomasi.” Third, the ninth-century chant apparatus: Wilson’s index shows *Primum quaerite* occurring as a Communion at exactly one locus, whose chant set is the 1962 *Ninth* Sunday’s, the *Protector noster* set there taking *Panis quem ego*. Setting that against the 1962 book shows **a rotation rather than a single replacement**: in that apparatus the 1962-Ninth set takes *Primum quaerite*, the 1962-Fourteenth set *Panis quem ego*, and the 1962-Fifteenth set *Qui manducat*, while 1962 assigns *Qui manducat* to the Ninth, *Primum quaerite* to the Fourteenth and *Panis quem ego* to the Fifteenth. Fourth, Honorius: the Communion of his Fourteenth-Sunday chapter is *Panis, quem ego dabo* and not *Primum quaerite*, and it stands there with the Gradual and Alleluia of the 1962 Fifteenth, so his whole chant strand and not the Communion alone runs one Sunday later.

**A control from within the same Office, checkable in the same pages.** Guéranger prints this Sunday’s Magnificat antiphon at Vespers as *Quaerite primum regnum Dei, et justitiam ejus, et haec omnia adjicientur vobis. Alleluia.* **That antiphon retains both *et iustítiam eius* and *haec* — exactly the two clauses the Mass’s Communion drops — and leaves *primum* in its Matthaean position after the verb.** Both forms above are Guéranger’s own printing and not the *Breviarium Romanum*’s.

**Guéranger’s judgment is his own reading of Tommasi’s Antiphonary and of “the ancient liturgists,” and carries no more than that**. Antiphon texts are routinely shortened for chant and *dicit Dóminus* is a common formulaic tag, so both changes may be conventional rather than expressive, and no count of either habit across the repertory stands behind this. **The Fifteenth Sunday’s Communion is the one assignment of the three that no second witness here confirms.** What the older books establish is **what the older pairing was, not when the substitution happened.** **The exchange is later than those antiphonaries, later than the Ottobonianus margin and later than Honorius, and earlier than the Pustet Missal of 1862**; **when it was made, no witness above says, and none explains why.** **And the recasting is not proper to the 1962 edition:** the same form is printed in the Pustet Missal of Ratisbon, 1862 — which shows the recasting is old and says nothing about why it was made. That the Communion selects the imperative rather than the pericope’s three prohibitions **may be an artefact of taking the final verse** rather than a choice among the pericope’s parts.

**No witness connects the omission of *et iustítiam eius* to the Epistle**, and Paul’s “not under law” and Matthew’s *iustítia* are not the same concept.

### Postcommunion <span class="nodecor">(*Postcomm.*)</span>

*Puríficent semper et múniant* *Postcommunio* marginal no. 1581

**The figure, and the noun held to the end.** *Ad perpétuæ … ducant salvatiónis efféctum* splits the genitive around the verb, exactly as the Thirteenth Sunday’s Postcommunion split *redemptiónis ætérnæ … augméntum* around its own — the same figure, one Sunday apart, and in both cases the noun the prayer is reaching for is held back to the end. This is also **the formulary’s only reach past this life**: *perpétuæ salvatiónis* is the one phrase in the ten elements that looks beyond the present.

***Semper*, a second time.** The Collect asked *tuis semper auxíliis*; this prayer opens *Puríficent semper*. Of the fifteen 1962 formularies this guide has collated — a set that does not take in the Fifteenth Sunday, whose own Collect carries the word — three carry *semper* in an oration and **this is the only one of those fifteen that carries it twice.**

**Transmission, and it is the sharpest structural point in the oration dossier.** This prayer does not come from the same place as the other two, and **its Gelasian base is materially narrower than theirs**: it is attested for Rheinau and Gerbert but **not** for S. Gallen, Pamelius or Menard, since S. Gallen omits the whole appended series. *Purificent semper* stands in the floating series of unassigned Postcommunions appended after the Canon, in exactly the form the 1962 book prints, and separately in a saint-adapted recension in the Vigil Mass of St Andrew at Book II sect. **LXVIII**. Which recension is prior is settled by no witness above.

**One measured fact about its vocabulary.** *Salvatio* as a noun occurs **once** in the forty-five orations of those same fifteen collated formularies, and it is here.

### The Secret and the Postcommunion: no exegetical reception in any period

**No patristic or medieval exegesis of the Secret *Concéde nobis, Dómine* or of the Postcommunion *Puríficent semper et múniant* stands anywhere in the *Patrologia Latina*.** *Propitiatio potestatis* and *purificent semper et muniant* occur five times between them across that whole collection, and every one of the five is a sacramentary printing the prayer.

**The Collect stands outside that negative**: Honorius cites it by incipit, Godfrey glosses it word by word and Schuster comments on it continuously, as the Collect’s own treatment above sets out.

**These are anonymous composed Roman orations, and the patristic and medieval genres that reach this formulary — psalm expositions, scriptural homilies, epistle commentaries — do not take liturgical orations as their object.** **Schuster comments on all three orations and supplies no patristic or medieval witness for these two**; **Rupert of Deutz, Sicard of Cremona and Durandus supply none either**, their treatments of this Sunday being calendrical and typological rather than euchological.

**And Guéranger is a reception witness only and never a text witness.** His printed Latin differs from the 1962 typical edition at four points: he prints *millia* for *mília*; a **comma** where the typical edition prints the **semicolon** the Collect’s pointing turns on; lower-case *angelus*; and *Concede nobis, Domine, quaesumus:* with a colon and a capitalised *Hostia*, plus the short conclusion *Per Dominum* at both Secret and Postcommunion where the typical edition prints the longer form at the Postcommunion. **None of the appointed Latin above is his.** The last of the four is the weakest as evidence, since hand-missal presentations abbreviate conclusions as a matter of course.

## Source-Grounded Synthesis Across the Propers

### C1. One phrase carries the Mass, and the element that repeats it is the element that cuts it

*Regnum Dei* stands verbatim in three appointed elements and carries one argument across them. The Epistle names who is *excluded* from the kingdom, *qui tália agunt, regnum Dei non consequéntur*; the Gospel commands that it be *sought first*; and the Communion repeats that command as *the Lord’s own word at the moment of reception*. The negative and the positive are two faces of one claim about the kingdom, not two positions set against each other.

Four textual facts sharpen it. *Regnum Dei* is **rare in Matthew**: four verses in the whole Gospel (6:33, 12:28, 21:31, 21:43) against thirty-two verses carrying *regnum caelorum*, thirty-four occurrences in all, with Mt. 6:33 the first of the four. It is the **only** occurrence of the phrase in Galatians, **but Paul uses it elsewhere**: 1 Cor. 6:9–10, Eph. 5:5 and 1 Cor. 15:50 form a small closed set of Pauline parallels closing vice lists with the same exclusion, **none of the three is appointed, and nothing in the ten elements points to them**; their standing is as the canonical context of the appointed wording. **The tie survives into the Greek**, both texts reading *basileia theou*, which the Epistle-to-Gospel *sérvitus* / *servíre* bridge does not. And across the fifteen 1962 formularies whose appointed text this guide has collated, *regnum Dei* occurs in **this formulary alone**, at exactly three loci.

***Regnum* is the only exact form reaching three or more of the ten elements that is neither a divine name nor a function word** — but at stem level *salut-* and *salv-* together reach four of the ten, and *salut-* alone reaches three; both are substantive. The two roots partition the formulary rather than competing: *regnum* in the read and sung word, the *salut-* and *salv-* root in the three orations and one chant.

**And the element that repeats the phrase is the element that cuts it.** *Iustítia* stands **once** in the whole appointed Latin, in the Gospel’s last verse — and the Communion, quoting that very verse, drops it. **The clause it drops is the second member of a Matthean inclusio**, the first standing at Mt. 6:1, *Attendite ne justitiam vestram faciatis coram hominibus*, **though the arc is a property of the Vulgate’s reading there, Robinson-Pierpont’s Byzantine text reading *alms***; and the added *dicit Dóminus* stands nowhere in Matthew.

At the hinge stands Augustine’s reading of *primum*, and it is contested. **He reads it at his own locus** — *De sermone Domini in monte* II.53, in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series: “in using that word *first*, He has indicated that this is to be sought later, not in point of time, but in point of importance,” which Rabanus Maurus’s compilation on these verses carries in Latin as *non tempore, sed dignitate* at *Patrologia Latina* 107:836B–839A — so a compilation’s Latin answers the English, under sigla that are Migne’s editorial work and not the compiler’s. He makes the point by denying the sequence the word invites, “He did not say, *Then* seek such things,” and at II.56 reads the promise’s own grammar as grace, the addition following “if you seek the former, without any hindrance on your part.” **Paschasius Radbertus denies that *primum* marks order at all**: *non ad ordinem referendum puto … magis ad quantitatem referendum est, ut extra hoc nihil quaerendum sit*. The verb the antiphon keeps has its own readers — pseudo-Bede’s *Non dixit nobis dari, sed adjici*, Christian of Stavelot’s *subauditur necessaria … Ad necessitatem, non ad superfluitatem*, Paschasius’s *his utamur in via, quatenus illo fruamur in patria* — and Godfrey of Admont reads *regnum Dei* at this verse as the heart, which ought always to be God’s kingdom. **Chrysostom rests the whole promise on that verb**, in Greek at *Patrologia Graeca* 57:303: he said not *shall be given* but *shall be added*, “that you may learn that the things present are nothing great, set beside the greatness of the things to come,” in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English, first series. **Schuster, commenting on the antiphon itself, reaches back for the clause the recasting removed** and glosses the justice from the Gospel verse rather than from the antiphon.

What the omission does is remove the formulary’s only occurrence of *iustítia*. **That the omission was deliberate is established by nothing, and one fact cuts against reading it as significant:** Chrysostom’s own citation of Mt. 6:33 drops *et iustítiam eius* exactly as the antiphon does — while keeping the kingdom *of heaven* where the antiphon keeps *regnum Dei* and fronting no *primum* at all — **so an abbreviation of this shape is ordinary, and nothing supports dependence in either direction.** Fifteen 1962 formularies whose appointed text this guide has collated stand behind that count. A Communion antiphon drawn from the day’s own Gospel is ordinary practice, so were this one’s *regnum Dei* a purely mechanical carry-over the three-element spine would reduce to a two-element one plus an echo — which would still leave the Epistle’s occurrence standing, since it comes from a different book and a different pericope. And the one medieval preacher located on this Epistle expounds it beside a different Gospel.

### C2. The flesh that is crucified is not the body that is fed, and the tradition says so in Greek and in Latin by three different middle terms

The two lessons are **lexically disjoint**: *caro*/*carn-* stands five times in the Epistle and nowhere else in the formulary; *corp-* stands twice in the Gospel and nowhere else. **This is one unit, not a contrast.** The point is not that Epistle and Gospel disagree about the body but that they use different words for different things, and that reading them together dissolves the appearance of disagreement. **A reading built as “Epistle mortifies against Gospel provides” has inverted it.** **And the Introit’s cut is what keeps the division clean:** its psalm verse stops at *in átria Dómini*, one clause before the Clementine’s *cor meum et caro mea exsultaverunt in Deum vivum* — the one appointed element that would have given *caro* a positive sense, severed exactly before it, and Cassiodorus comments on that very clause outside the appointed extract.

**Four positions now stand on what *caro* is at Gal. 5:17, and they are genuinely four.** **Chrysostom’s Greek denies desire to the flesh**: *sarka gar entautha, ou to soma kalei* — flesh here is the earthbound, slothful reasoning; desire belongs to the soul, so a flesh that desires cannot be the body; the body is an instrument, *organon gar estin he sarx*, and *we hate and punish not the iron but the murderer*; the conflict is between two reasonings and not *ouch he psyche kai to soma*. **Marius Victorinus** argues the contrary from the soulless elements: *Habet enim motus suos caro, habetque sensus, neque tantum ab anima suscitantur*. **The Glossa ordinaria** makes the ascription a manner of speaking about a joint act — *Caro dicta est concupiscere, quia hoc secundum ipsam agit anima* — with the cause in neither alone, *ex utroque enim fit*. **Ambrosiaster** shifts the ground entirely: not two parts of man but two laws, *unam Dei, alteram peccati*, with *medius homo* standing between them, the flesh’s works so named by origin (*quia errores isti ex mundo sunt, ex quo et caro*), so that at v. 24 the crucified flesh is the world. **Chrysostom and Victorinus comment on the same appointed verse and cannot both be right.**

**Theodoret and Augustine reach the same conclusion by three further middle terms, and no dependence between them is established.** Theodoret comments verse by verse on the whole appointed pericope, and argues twice from the content of the lists that Paul’s flesh is not the body: idolatry and sorcery are proper to the soul, so *ou tes sarkos kategorei, alla tou rhathymou phronematos*, he accuses not the flesh but the slothful cast of mind; and had the works of v. 19 belonged to the body alone, a matching list of the soul’s works would have had to be set down beside them, and is not. **Augustine reaches it by two others in *De civitate Dei* XIV.2**: a man who abstains from bodily pleasure *on account of* idolatry or heresy is convicted by apostolic authority of damnable works of the flesh in the very act of that abstinence, *et in eo, quod abstinet a uoluptatibus carnis, damnabilia opera carnis agere demonstratur*; and nobody says *Malam carnem* to an enemy, so Paul names the whole man by a part, *eo locutionis modo, quo totum significatur a parte*. **And Theodoret closes the appointed lesson baptismally**, those buried with Christ having made the body dead to sin, which sets the lesson’s last verse and this Mass’s own sacramental action on one axis.

**And Jerome supplies the argument from the missal’s own word.** Where the Latin translator put *vitia* the Greek reads *pathemata*, *passiones*, and the Apostle added *desideria* **precisely so as not to seem to deny the nature of the body in spiritual men, but its vices**: *ut non naturam corporis videretur in spiritualibus viris negare, sed vitia*. **A Father defending the appointed word on exactly this ground is better evidence for this claim than any of the others, because it is anchored in the word the missal prints.** Chrysostom adds at the lesson’s last verse that those of Christ did not destroy the flesh — *epei pos emellon zen?* — and that the desires, though they trouble, rage in vain. Aquinas reaches the same conclusion by a different route again, through the definition he cites from *De civitate Dei* XIV.2, that whoever lives according to himself lives according to the flesh, so that *caro* here stands for the whole man.

**And the pericope itself supplies the convergence these routes point to.** The lesson names only *caro* and *spiritus* and leaves the soul unmentioned; **Chrysostom in Greek and Jerome in Latin independently raise that difficulty and answer it the same way** — the soul lies between and makes the body spiritual or earthly by what it does with it. **The two agree, and nothing shows dependence.**

Augustine supplies a leg from the other direction, and the reception is *controversial*. Certain monks quoted these very verses to argue that they need not work, and *De opere monachorum* was written against them, in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series: “let these persons, who from perverse understanding of the Gospel labor to pervert apostolic precepts, either take no thought for the morrow, even as the birds of the air; or let them obey the Apostle.” The *Catena aurea* points to that work, and the work itself carries the passage. The identification of those monks as the Euchitae rests on *De haeresibus*, the work that would name them, and no text of it stands behind the name.

Gal. 5:24’s *carnem suam crucifixérunt* is read by some as including the body proper, and the Vulgate elsewhere uses *caro* for the body simply; the disjunction is a fact about these two pericopes as printed, **not a lexical law**. **Ambrosiaster’s Latin lemma is not the missal’s** — his fruit-list has ten members, carrying *spes* and *castitas* and lacking *fides* and *patientia*, and his vice-list opens differently — **so his exposition counts a different pair of lists, and the missal’s seventeen and twelve are not the lists he expounds.** Marius Victorinus’s text as Migne prints it passes from v. 17 to 6:1, so he comments on none of vv. 18–24, and no critical edition stands beside that printing. Chrysostom’s Greek at these verses is a transcription of *Patrologia Graeca* 61 and not the column itself, and Theodoret’s own lemma at v. 19 opens with the Greek for *adultery*, which the missal’s list does not carry. **The appointed Latin gives twelve fruits where the Greek witnesses give nine, and seventeen vices that are not the seventeen of the King James: *luxúria* is the Latin head-word with no distinct Greek counterpart, and *modéstia* and *cástitas* are the two appointed fruits that answer nothing in the Greek lists** — so no Greek witness here speaks to those three words, and the twelve and the nine are counts of two different lists.

### C3. The tradition is namedly divided about the Gospel’s images, and its most-repeated sentence at the Gospel’s first verse was withdrawn by the man who wrote it

**The division is named, and it is not a difference of emphasis.** On the anti-allegorical side: **Augustine at his own locus**, *De sermone Domini in monte* II.52, in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series — the birds and the lilies “are not to be treated as allegories … for they stand here, in order that from smaller matters we may be persuaded respecting greater ones,” the sentence the compilations transmit as *ista documenta non sicut allegorica discutienda sunt* — repeated by the exposition printed under Bede’s name; **Christian of Stavelot refusing allegory at the lilies on frankly aesthetic grounds**, no linen matching the lily’s whiteness, no dye the rose, no purple the violet; Jerome forbidding the birds as angels on a ground internal to the appointed verse, *Simpliciter ergo accipiendum*; and Bruno of Segni reading the lilies literally. On the allegorical side, at its far end: **Godfrey of Admont makes the birds the good angels, the lilies the evil spirits and Solomon Christ**, and then raises and answers the objection his own allegory creates — so he is a self-aware instance and not a naive one. **Hilary allegorises every appointed image into doctrine of the risen body**, holding that the literal reading does not cohere, *non satis propositionibus congruunt*. Chrysostom reads the images as arguments too, but rhetorically engineered ones, the illustrations degraded step by step so the *a fortiori* grows at each — and **where the Fathers agree they still differ**, Augustine’s middle term being the creature’s **rank** and Chrysostom’s its **vileness**, given in terms of *gift* rather than of rank. **Chrysostom refuses a different over-reading, and at three places**: not being anxious about these things is no labour, being anxious is — *ou toinyn to me merimnan tauta ponos, alla to merimnan* — and Christ abolished not the sowing and the spinning but the anxiety about them, *ou ton sporon anetrepsen, alla ten phrontida*, *ou to ergon aneilen, alla ten merimnan*. **And Aquinas reads the pericope’s argument as three ascending motives** — the greater gifts already given, the care God takes of animals and plants without man’s assistance, and divine providence itself, “through ignorance of which the gentiles are solicitous in seeking temporal goods before all others” (*Summa theologiae* II-II q. 55 a. 6, in the English of the Fathers of the English Dominican Province). **Augustine’s refusal is a refusal about the birds and the lilies and not a general rule**, so it does not become a Fathers-wide position; and whether the division is about method or about this pericope only is not established, the two sides nowhere answering each other.

**And the sentence the Latin tradition repeats most often at this Gospel’s first verse was withdrawn by its author.** *Nullius enim fere conscientia Deum potest odisse* is retracted at *Retractationes* I.19, where Augustine names *De sermone Domini in monte* II.14.48 — his exposition of the appointed Gospel’s first verse — and gives Ps. 73:23 as his reason, *Superbia eorum qui oderunt te*. **Five later Latin witnesses repeat the sentence without his qualifier *fere*** — Rabanus, the Glossa, Christian of Stavelot, Paschasius and the exposition printed under Bede’s name, one of them so damaged (*scientia* for *conscientia*) that the corruption can only be transmission — **the Glossa alone flags the retraction**, and **Bruno of Segni denies the proposition outright**: the covetous man does hate God, because he serves idols, from Eph. 5:5. **“No one can hate God” is therefore neither the tradition’s settled reading at Mt. 6:24 nor Augustine’s own last word on it.**

**The Communion half, and it is this claim’s second appointed element.** The formulary appoints Mt. 6:33 twice: once as the Gospel’s last verse and again, recast, as the Communion antiphon — the seventh scriptural element standing on the fifth of the six appointed loci. On that one verse nobody argues from a lesser example. Augustine, at his own locus above, makes *primum* an ordering of importance and not of time; **Paschasius denies that it orders anything**, and elsewhere in the same place calls the sequence *praeposterus*, the kingdom put first to soothe the hearing before justice is brought in as the laborious thing. **So the two appointed elements that carry Matthew 6 in this Mass receive figure refused at the images and doctrine read at the imperative — and the sentence the missal lifted out to say at the moment of reception is the one on which the tradition reads doctrine and then divides about a single adverb.**

**Beneath it, a textual observation.** The Gospel opens on a conclusion whose premises are not read — the treasure and the eye at Mt. 6:19–23 — and closes one verse before Matthew’s own close at v. 34; and **the supplied incipit narrows the audience to the disciples where Mt. 5:1 has the crowds present.** **The images have Old Testament texts behind them and Lucan parallels beside them, and Matthew quotes none of them**: Is. 40:6–8, Ps. 89:6 and Ps. 102:15–16 behind the grass, Ps. 146:9 and Job 38:41 behind the birds, 3 Kings 10:4–7 and 10:23 behind Solomon, and Ps. 54:23 with 1 Pet. 5:7 behind the *sollicitudo*. They stand as background and none is a citation the appointed text makes.

**The missal’s recasting promotes *Primum* to the head of the antiphon** — exactly where the argument transmitted under Augustine’s name makes word order carry the doctrine, and none of the witnesses above comments on the antiphon as such. That promotion bears twice over: it measures the extent and antiquity of the recasting, and it separates the two registers in which the appointed Matthaean text is read.

**The Latin Fathers commenting on this very pericope are divided about whether its central images may be allegorised at all** — Hilary allegorises them, Jerome and Augustine forbid it.

**Bruno of Segni restricts the whole prohibition of anxiety to apostles, their successors and those aiming at perfection, and Godfrey preaches it to monks about *lectio***, so two of the witnesses read the pericope as addressed to a narrower audience than the Sunday congregation. A Communion antiphon drawn from the day’s Gospel is ordinary practice, so this second element may be a mechanical carry-over. That bears on how *interesting* the join is; it does not dissolve it, because the lesson and the reception are two ritual moments whatever produced the antiphon. **Both elements that carry this claim stand in Matthew 6**, and no third element from another book stands behind it.

### C4. Three strands, three schedules, four reckonings — and the strand that never moved

*The orations of this Mass stand one Sunday later in the Gregorian books and two later in the Frankish Gelasian; the chants carry this Sunday’s own number in the ninth-century antiphonaries; and the Epistle and Gospel stand under a reckoning counted from St Lawrence and were never cut anywhere but where 1962 cuts them.* **No witness says that this Mass stood at a different Sunday**: what moved is one strand of it, and the three strands moved by different amounts or not at all.

**The orations.** Gelasianum Book III runs sixteen sections, and sects. I–XVI correspond to the 1962 Fifth through Twentieth Sundays on a numeration base and not by interpolation; each section’s 1962 identity is that of its own printed collect or Secret and never of its position. Wilson names the mechanism in a footnote — the *post Pentecosten* / *post Octavas Pentecostes* equivalence, arising from a discrepancy between the first and supplementary portions of Muratori’s text — and **which single Sunday each tradition counts as its first is not established**, so the decomposition of the two units of offset is not settled. **Across sects. I–XI Rheinau, S. Gallen, Gerbert and Menard stand two Sundays later, and across sects. XII–XVI they stand four**, the offset surviving a documented interpolation and resuming a new constant rather than dissolving; **Pamelius stands one later across all sixteen without a break, and Menard agrees with Rheinau, S. Gallen and Gerbert at every readable section and never with him.** This Mass’s own orations are sect. X, inside the run of two. **Gerbert’s own printed p. 173 heads this Mass *Dominica XVI*** — an eighteenth-century printed text and not a manuscript, set in long-s type — and the offset is **already present at the series’ first section**, so nothing inside the series produces it. The Gregorian Hadrianum heads this Mass *DOMINICA .XV. POST PENTECOSTEN*, one later than 1962, **confirmed across eleven consecutive Masses, each identified from its own printed collect**, and **its sect. XXXIII carries all three orations of this Mass word for word.**

**And the two Gregorian editions that disagree about this Sunday’s number are editions of two different manuscripts.** Pamelius first published the Grimoldus codex and Menard’s base is the Rodradus codex, and Menard argues at length in his preface that the two are separate compilations — **so the disagreement between them is between two books and not two editorial habits**, though he is an interested party arguing for his own manuscript’s Gregorian authenticity. **And the oration set does not travel as a unit everywhere**: Menard’s *Hebdomada XVI* carries this Collect with a different Super oblata and a different Ad complendum, while his very next section carries all three of the 1962 Fifteenth Sunday’s orations together. **Menard’s volume prints no occurrence of the 1962 Postcommunion at all**; that is a real divergence between two Gregorian witnesses, and the silence is the printed book’s and not Menard’s manuscripts’.

**The chants did not travel with them, and the strands come apart inside one book.** In the Roman antiphonary printed at *Patrologia Latina* 78 the **Introit and Offertory series agree with 1962 at every week from the seventh to the eighteenth** — a run whose first week that book’s own body marks *Ista hebdomada non est in Antiphonario Romano* — while the **Gradual series runs one week behind across a long run and breaks at exactly the two weeks this Sunday and its neighbour occupy**, and **the Alleluia agrees with 1962 at only two of twelve weeks — and at both of those the collated manuscripts disagree with the printed text**, so that agreement is evidence about the editor’s exemplar rather than about the ninth-century state. **The week-numbering of that book is itself disturbed across the same run**: its footnote a reports Theodericus’s office of the seventh Sunday standing at the eighth and the eighth’s at the ninth, *sicque consequenter*, while footnotes on the same leaf report his Alleluia at week *N* as the printed book’s at *N* + 1, **so a Theodericensian reading printed at a given week is not established as Theodericus’s own week.** The ninth-century antiphonary formulary carrying this Introit, Gradual and Offertory is numbered *Dominica XIV* — **the same number 1962 gives it** — and the Ottobonianus margin reproduces those formularies **one number above** the sacramentary headings they stand against, at twenty-five of twenty-five slots across five consecutive Masses, the bracketed Alleluia cues matching nothing in 1962. **The cue block whose Introit is *Protector noster* attaches to Wilson’s sect. XXXI, *DOMINICA .XIII. POST PENTECOSTEN*, whose Collect is the 1962 Twelfth Sunday’s:** an offset of two, the superscript reference letters on that page standing before the section numbers and not as part of them.

**The readings never moved.** The *Liber Comitis* as Migne prints it pairs *Fratres, spiritu ambulate, usque concupiscentiis* with *Nemo potest duobus dominis servire, usque adiicientur vobis* under *Dominica v post sancti Laurentii* — **the same incipits and the same termini the 1962 book prints.** So the Epistle–Gospel pair is not among the things that moved. **But the two reading strands were not numbered alike even in the seventh-century Roman books**: the Würzburg gospel capitulary numbers this Gospel *ebd. IIII post sci. Laur.*, the fourth after St Lawrence, while **the Würzburg epistle list numbers Gal. 5:16–24 not at all**, laying its forty Pauline pericopes out in canonical order without titles, and Wilmart’s reconstruction makes it the fifth of the five post-Laurentian epistles. **So the *Liber Comitis*’s “fifth” and Schuster’s “fourth” are two books counting differently and neither is wrong**, and the asymmetry between the reading strands reaches back to the Roman books themselves. Alcuin’s fifth is a reconstruction keyed to Würzburg item numbers and not a heading read in a manuscript.

**And a fourth tradition splits this formulary along exactly that line.** The Sarum Missal gives the whole 1962 oration set and the 1962 Gospel at *Dominica XV post Trinitatem* and the 1962 Introit, Epistle and Offertory at *Dominica XIV* — **two offsets that must be kept apart**, since calendrically Trinity *N* is Rome’s Sunday *N* + 1 while internally Sarum’s Collect, Gospel, Secret and Postcommunion at Trinity *N* are Rome’s at Sunday *N* − 1.

**Four mechanisms stand behind the instability and no two have been shown to be the same.** Wilson’s is a numeration base, *post Pentecosten* against *post Octavas Pentecostes*. **Schuster’s is a Roman cycle built around the feast of St Lawrence**, whose count “was not everywhere the same,” the Lawrence cycle succeeded at Rome by cycles around St Cyprian and St Michael, these feasts serving “as so many milestones … and had no special connection with the saint whose name they bore.” **Berno of Reichenau’s is a difference in the number of Masses**: the compiler of the chant-offices provided twenty-three Sundays between the octave of Pentecost and Advent and the compiler of the lectionary twenty-five, because one computed on the embolismic years of the nineteen-year cycle and the other on the common years — which is the shape of a book whose strands run on different schedules. **And the *Micrologus* names a fourth**, the vacant Sundays after the Ember fasts, whose mishandling disturbs the order of the offices as far as Advent, the Romans making up the shortfall by repeating a Sunday’s office rather than deferring it. **Four numbering series run through these books** — 1962’s *post Pentecosten*, the Gregorian sacramentary’s at 1962+<!-- -->1, the Frankish Gelasian’s at 1962+<!-- -->2, and the *post sancti Laurentii* reckoning — **and inside that fourth one two books disagree by one:** the Comes calls this Sunday the fifth after St Lawrence and Schuster’s title for the formulary is *Quarta post Sancti Laurentii*, and Schuster himself says the count varied between books. **None of the four mechanisms has been shown to produce the offsets Wilson collates, and none has been shown to be what any compiler actually did**: Berno writes four centuries after the arrangements he explains and offers a rationale rather than a record, and the *Micrologus* prescribes against a practice it disapproves.

**And the pairing the 1962 book does not have is a lectionary tradition, not one commentator’s displacement.** Honorius reads this formulary in one twelfth-century chapter whose Introit, Collect, Epistle and Offertory are the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday’s while his Gospel is the Thirteenth’s and his Gradual, Alleluia and Communion are the Fifteenth’s — **three chants of five displaced, not five** — and that chapter, *sub lege* under Ezechias, is one of three: he reads the whole formulary again *sub gratia* under Theodosius, and a third time as calendar, tying the Offertory to the September night office’s *historia* of Tobias and Raphael and to Michaelmas. **Godfrey of Admont expounds this formulary’s Introit, Gradual, Alleluia psalm and Offertory in order and closes by praying its Collect — and his Gospel is the ten lepers.** **Beside those two stand seven further witnesses to the same arrangement, across four centuries and three kinds of book, so Honorius is no outlier.** **Rupert of Deutz, Sicard of Cremona and Durandus** all set this Sunday’s Epistle beside the ten lepers and put Matthew 6 on the Fifteenth, with its Introit, Gradual and Offertory expounded by Rupert and Sicard; **the eighth-century Comes of Murbach has the same pairing**, as one instance of a whole systematic pattern of epistle *N* with gospel *N* − 1 rather than as a peculiarity here; **the Sarum Missal and the Book of Common Prayer of 1662 still carry it**, the latter with the marginals *Gal. 5. 16.* and *S: Luke. 17. 11.*; and ***Patrologia Latina* 78’s Roman Mass antiphonary carries it at the Communion alone**, giving this Sunday *Panis quem ego dedero*. **When or why the Roman books moved to the pairing 1962 prints, none of them says.** Whether the English books inherit from the Frankish comes, whether both descend from a common Roman state, or whether Rome’s own pairing is the innovation, is not established. **And Durandus’s own chant set for this Sunday is not the Roman one but Sarum’s and Compiègne’s** — Gradual *Bonum est confiteri Domino*, Alleluia *Confitemini Domino et invocate nomen eius*, Communion *Panis quem ego dedero* — against Rupert and Sicard, who have the Roman Gradual; no filiation between the three is established, and Compiègne stands with Rome at the Gradual and against it at the Alleluia.

For four of the five sacramentary books — Rheinau, S. Gallen, Pamelius and Menard — the disagreement stands on Wilson’s apparatus and not on their own pages; the fifth, Gerbert, prints the Mass himself. Wilson’s footnote at sect. X covers **“the Collects and Secret” only**, and the 1962 Postcommunion has a materially narrower Gelasian base, attested for Rheinau and Gerbert but **not** for S. Gallen, Pamelius or Menard. **The Gelasian displacement is therefore a claim about two of the formulary’s ten elements, the Collect and the Secret, and not about all three orations.** The four books agreeing are not four independent witnesses: Gerbert’s principal manuscript cannot now be traced and Wilson holds its “Gelasian” portions to be a revision based on the Gregorian type, while Pamelius is itself an edition of the Gregorian book — **so its reading and the Gregorian numbering are not two witnesses.** **Schuster does not corroborate the displacement:** his three pages on this Mass name no station, no sacramentary siglum and no manuscript, and do not say the Communion is secondary. The antiphonary readings are a modern database’s report of Hesbert’s *Antiphonale Missarum Sextuplex* and not his printed pages, whose rights record closes them; what stands on the 1962 side is that database’s record and not a facsimile; the *Liber Comitis*’s own date and provenance are **not established**; and the run of the two-Sunday offset breaks at Dominica XXII and XXIII for a reason nobody has established. **Pamelius and the Maurists are the whole of what Compiègne, Mont-Blandin and the Theodericensian codices say here**, and each of the three speaks through an editor’s footnote reporting a reading rather than through a transcription of the manuscript, so what any of them carries at a given week is that editor’s report of it.

### C5. The formulary makes exactly one substantive exchange, it is at the Communion, it is older than this book, and it broke a pair

The Communion departs in exactly four ways from Mt. 6:33 as the Clementine, the Lucan parallel and Robinson-Pierpont’s Byzantine Greek carry it: *ergo* is dropped and *Primum* promoted to head the clause; *et iustítiam eius* is omitted; *hæc* is dropped before *ómnia*; and *dicit Dóminus* is added. That tag stands in none of Clementine Mt. 6:33, Clementine Lk. 12:31 or Robinson-Pierpont Mt. 6:33. **Nor does the Lucan parallel supply the fronted *primum***: Robinson-Pierpont’s Byzantine Luke carries neither *proton* nor *kai ten dikaiosynen autou*, so any comparison with Luke rests on the Clementine’s Latin alone.

Two of the four bear theologically. Adding *dicit Dóminus* turns a saying inside a sermon into **an address made to the communicant at the moment of reception**. Dropping *et iustítiam eius* leaves the kingdom to be sought without the justice the Gospel names beside it.

**The antiphon is secondary in this slot only, and not in the repertory.** *Primum quaerite* is an ancient Roman antiphon standing at the **Ninth** Sunday in four ninth-century antiphonaries, where the Fourteenth Sunday’s Communion is *Panis quem ego dedero*. **And that Communion travels with the Offertory *Immittet Angelus* at both its occurrences** — the Lenten Thursday and this Sunday — **so substituting *Primum quaerite* here broke a pair the older books kept together.**

**All three legs of that rotation stand in the body of a printed chant book, and not in a margin.** The Roman Mass antiphonary printed at *Patrologia Latina* 78 gives *Primum quaerite regnum Dei, et omnia adjicientur vobis, dicit Dominus* — **including the *dicit Dominus* Matthew does not have** — to *Hebdomada nona*, *Panis quem ego dedero* to the fourteenth and *Qui manducat carnem meam* to the fifteenth. **1962 reverses all three.** Witnesses now converge on the substitution: the appointed text itself, this being the formulary’s one substantive departure from its Bible text; Guéranger’s report that the antiphon “was not the one primitively used; the ancient liturgists make no mention of it in its present position”; the Ottobonianus cue block whose Introit is this Mass’s own and whose Communion is *Panis quem ego*; Honorius, whose Communion here is *Panis, quem ego dabo*; the Sarum Missal at Trinity XIV; and the antiphonary formularies themselves. **Three printed witnesses give this Sunday *Panis quem ego dedero* and none of them gives it *Primum quaerite*, and Guéranger’s judgment is confirmed in substance from sources he did not have.**

The strongest single control comes from within the same Office and anyone can check it in the same pages: Guéranger prints this Sunday’s Magnificat antiphon at Vespers as *Quaerite primum regnum Dei, et justitiam ejus, et haec omnia adjicientur vobis. Alleluia.* **That antiphon retains both *et iustítiam eius* and *haec* — exactly the two clauses the Mass’s Communion drops — and leaves *primum* in its Matthaean position after the verb.**

**No witness explains why the exchange was made, and no witness dates it. That book dates the state and not the change.** What is fixed is that it is later than the ninth-century antiphonaries, later than the Ottobonianus margin and later than Honorius, and earlier than the Pustet Missal of Ratisbon, 1862, which prints the recast form already. Guéranger’s judgment is his own reading of Tommasi’s Antiphonary and of “the ancient liturgists,” and carries no more than that. Antiphon texts are routinely shortened for chant and *dicit Dóminus* is a common formulaic tag, so both changes may be conventional rather than expressive, and no count of either habit across the repertory stands behind this.

**Two further negatives, and they bound how the Communion may be read.** First, **the Communion carries no eating vocabulary**: the appointed text is *Primum quǽrite regnum Dei*, and the verbs of eating that the Offertory’s *gustáte* makes central belong to the Offertory alone and to the Eucharistic reception the antiphon accompanies; nothing in the Communion’s own wording invites a Eucharistic reading. Second, **the law and *adversus* language is confined to the Epistle**: the Communion’s one addition, *dicit Dóminus*, is an address to the communicant and not a legal exclusion. Neither negative reaches past the ten appointed elements.

### C6. Two vocabularies partition the Mass, and the silence they produce is a genre fact everywhere but in the readings

*Salut-* and *salv-* occur four times, once each in the Collect (*salutária*), the Alleluia (*salutári*), the Secret (*salutáris*) and the Postcommunion (*salvatiónis*), **and in none of the six other elements**; *propitia-* twice, Collect and Secret only; *semper* and *perpétu-* twice each, Collect and Postcommunion only. **At stem level the *salut-* and *salv-* root and *regnum* are the only substantive ties reaching three or more elements, and the two partition the formulary cleanly:** *regnum* in the read and sung word, that root in the three orations plus the one chant and in no reading at all. **The people’s texts state the problem as service, trust and anxiety; the priest’s prayers state it as expiation and rescue.**

**No expositor stands on the two prayers this partition turns on.** No patristic or medieval exegesis of the Secret *Concéde nobis, Dómine* or of the Postcommunion *Puríficent semper et múniant* stands anywhere the *Patrologia Latina*’s exact-phrase index reaches, every hit on their distinctive phrases being a sacramentary printing the prayer; **the Collect stands outside that negative**, Honorius citing it by incipit, Godfrey of Admont glossing it word by word and Schuster commenting on it continuously.

**The silence about temporal goods is a fact about the genre.** No Collect, Secret or Postcommunion of the fifteen collated 1962 formularies — forty-five orations — asks for food, drink or clothing, so this Sunday’s silence about the three goods its Gospel names is not a distinctive of this formulary. **And Roman orations of this season are not uniformly non-temporal:** the Fourth Sunday’s Collect asks that *mundi cursus pacifice nobis tuo ordine dirigatur*, the Nuptial Mass’s Postcommunion asks *longaeva pace*, Trinity Sunday’s asks *ad salutem corporis et animae*, the Eighth Sunday’s asks *reparatio mentis et corporis*, and the Eleventh Sunday’s asks *subsidium mentis et corporis*. **So these orations are silent about temporal goods generally and not only about the Gospel’s three.**

**The singularity lies on the reading side.** Among the same fifteen identities, the Fourteenth Sunday is the **only** one whose appointed texts name eating, drinking *and* clothing together — *manducétis*, *manducábimus*, *esca*, *bibémus*, *induámini*, *operiémur*, *vestiménto*, all in the Gospel. **So the objects of petition and the objects of promise differ, and what is distinctive about this Mass is what it reads and not what it asks.**

**And the address differs with the vocabulary.** The three orations never command the assembly, their material addressed to God being otherwise subjunctival; **the one command the Mass repeats at the moment of reception is the promise’s own *Quǽrite*.**

**The orations also withhold agency from the assembly grammatically.** In all three the acting subject is God, his propitiation, the victim or the sacraments, and the assembly stands as object or as the subject of a passive: *Custódi … Ecclésiam tuam*; *lábitur humána mortálitas*; *abstrahátur … dirigátur*; *hæc hóstia salutáris … fiat purgátio … et propitiátio*; *Puríficent … et múniant tua sacraménta nos … et ad perpétuæ ducant salvatiónis efféctum*. **The assembly acts in its own right in six elements, and every one is a reading or a chant** — the Introit psalm verse’s *concupíscit, et déficit ánima mea*, *Spíritu ambuláte* with *carnem suam crucifixérunt*, *Veníte, exsultémus … iubilémus*, the Gospel’s *Respícite*, *Consideráte*, *Nolíte … esse* and *Quǽrite*, the Offertory’s *gustáte et vidéte*, and the Communion’s *Quǽrite* again. **And the Gospel states the same morphology of itself**, the active denied of the man — *Quis autem vestrum … potest adícere* — and the passive promised to him, *hæc ómnia adiciéntur vobis*. **The Mass names the Church once and the nations once and distinguishes them by what they seek**: the Collect asks that the Church be drawn from what harms and steered to what saves, and the Gospel marks the nations by seeking exactly the goods the Collect never asks for — which is how Aquinas reads *gentes* there, of those who “through ignorance of \[divine providence\] … are solicitous in seeking temporal goods before all others” (*Summa theologiae* II-II q. 55 a. 6, in the English of the Fathers of the English Dominican Province). **The passive with a divine subject is the settled grammar of the Roman collect as a genre**, so this may be a fact about how Latin orations are written rather than about what this Mass teaches. **No count of Roman orations outside these fifteen stands behind the contrast.** **The Epistle’s *crucifixérunt* is a human perfect active, so the claim is about the orations and not about the formulary as a whole.**

Fifteen 1962 formularies whose appointed text this guide has collated stand behind that count too. **And *salutáris* of a victim, *salutári* of God, *salutária* of things that save and *salvatiónis* of a state are one root in four senses**, so the four-fold count runs over the root and not over a single idea. **The Latin does not mark which of these petitions are temporal and which spiritual**, so that division is a judgement and not a feature of the orations.

### What this formulary does not say

**The Holy Spirit is named in exactly one of the ten appointed elements.** The *spiritu-* stem occurs five times in the Epistle and nowhere else; the Collect, Secret and Postcommunion never name the Spirit. The printed rubric appoints the Preface of the Most Holy Trinity, so the Mass *as celebrated* names the Spirit where its proper does not, and that is how far into this formulary a Spirit-centred reading reaches. Beyond that, across the same ten elements: no parousia, resurrection or judgment vocabulary past the Epistle’s *regnum Dei non consequéntur* and the Postcommunion’s *perpétuæ salvatiónis*; no saint named and no Marian reference; no penitential, fasting or almsgiving language, despite a seventeen-item vice list; one angel named, in the Offertory, and no other created spirit; and the Church named **once**, in the Collect — though the first-person plurals of Introit, Alleluia, Secret and Postcommunion are corporate in their own way, which softens any contrast built on that count.

**And three apparent ties are not ties.** *Conte-* joins the Epistle’s *contentiónes* to the Gospel’s *contémnet* and *oper-* joins its *ópera carnis* to *quo operiémur*, in each case across two different verbs; and *glori-* joins the Introit’s *Glória Patri* to the Gospel’s *in omni glória sua*, but the doxology belongs to the Ordinary and stands outside the appointed psalm. **No further substantive tie joins two of the ten elements at token level.**

### Two joints the appointed texts do not make

**The Alleluia is not joined to the rest by its own vocabulary.** Its only substantive tie to any other element is *salutári*, and *exsult-* and *iubil-* occur nowhere else in the ten. **No lexical warrant joins the Alleluia’s joy to the Gospel’s anxiety**: the Epistle’s *gaudium* and the Alleluia’s *exsultémus* are different roots in different elements with nothing joining them. **Nor does the reception warrant that join**: the medieval and later readers of this psalm reached here — a homily of the Chrysostomic corpus, Arnobius the Younger, Bruno the Carthusian and Peter Lombard — all read it penitentially, and every one of them reaches that reading through v. 2, *Praeoccupemus faciem eius in confessione*, which the Alleluia does not appoint. **But the reception does supply a join, and Augustine draws it from the appointed verse itself.** Expounding *exsultémus*, he distinguishes “a wicked joy which is to be distinguished from a righteous joy” — in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English, first series — and asks “What is it to rejoice aright? To rejoice in the Lord.” **That is not joy against anxiety but right joy against wrong joy**, the same disjunctive shape as the Gospel’s two masters and the Gradual’s two objects of trust.

**Whether the Epistle and the Gospel were ever read together in the tradition.** **The lectionary answers half of it and no witness answers the rest.** The *Liber Comitis* pairs these two lessons at these two extents, so the pairing is old; but Anthony of Padua’s is the one medieval sermon on Gal. 5:16–24 located for this Epistle, and his Sunday numbering runs one ahead of the 1962 missal’s, consistently across all three of his sermons for these Sundays. **A major medieval preacher expounding this Epistle does so beside a different Gospel.**

## The Propers: Notable and Quotable

Five afterlives of wording from the scriptural propers, each a use that moves the phrase into a register its psalmist or evangelist did not occupy.

> **Communion and Gospel, Mt. 6:33 — the political kingdom**
>
> **Appointed text and locus.** *Ant. ad Communionem*, marginal no. 1580: *Primum quǽrite regnum Dei, et ómnia adiciéntur vobis, dicit Dóminus* — and the same verse closes the appointed Gospel.
>
> **Later text and loci.** Kwame Nkrumah’s motto, in the United Kingdom’s official parliamentary report. **House of Commons, 3 December 1962, vol. 668**, in the Central Africa debate, Humphry Berkeley speaking from the printed time-marker 8.1 p.m.: “Outside the Parliament building in Accra there is a statue of President Nkrumah the inscription on which is couched in Biblical language, which some people find affronting but it is the mode of address of many Africans. The inscription reads: ‘Seek ye first the political kingdom and all things will be added unto you.’ That is what the Africans want.” **House of Lords, 19 December 1962, vol. 245**, on British policy in Central Africa, Lord Milverton from 6.27 p.m., listing among the causes of the Federation’s failure “the growing popularity of the doctrine which is inscribed, as we all know, in a famous place in Accra, ‘Seek ye first the political kingdom and all things will be added unto you’. Set against the inevitably slow movement of real progress, naturally, that motto, if accepted fully, has caused a lot of trouble.” *The Spark* (Accra), no. 159, 5 November 1965, reports the same maxim, in the form “all other things shall be added unto you,” as what “Osagyefo has repeatedly said.”
>
> The motto is verbal dependence and not echo: it preserves Matthew’s imperative, its object-plus-addition structure and the King James diction, substituting only “political kingdom” for “kingdom of God” and dropping “and his righteousness,” and it keeps the word *first*, which the Lucan parallel does not carry and which is what fixes the locus as Matthew’s. **Neither 1962 speaker reads “all these things shall be added,”** which is the King James’s own wording and the form usually quoted secondhand: both give “all things will be added unto you,” and they were in different Houses sixteen days apart and describe the source differently — Berkeley a statue inscription, Milverton “a famous place” — so their agreement is evidence that each was reporting a circulating inscription and not copying the other.
>
> **The turn.** Eschatological counsel against anxiety about food and clothing becomes a nationalist priority-of-sequence argument: take state power first and development follows. The redirection was contested in its own time — Berkeley reports the inscription approvingly, Milverton lists it among the causes of the Federation’s collapse — and Berkeley’s aside that the biblical idiom is what “some people find affronting” registers the appropriation as it was felt. The Mass’s closing antiphon stands, by 1962, as a monumental inscription in Accra and a political doctrine debated in both Houses at Westminster. What these witnesses attest is the maxim’s circulation and reception in December 1962 and not its coinage, which none of them dates; its wording rests on Berkeley’s report of it. That the antiphon drops *et iustítiam eius* where the motto drops “and his righteousness” is a coincidence.

> **Gospel, Mt. 6:24 — the rule against divided loyalty**
>
> **Appointed text and locus.** Gospel, marginal no. 1577: *Nemo potest duóbus dóminis servíre: aut enim unum ódio habébit, et álterum díliget: aut unum sustinébit, et álterum contémnet. Non potéstis Deo servíre, et mammónæ.*
>
> **Later text and loci.** *Everhart v. Searle*, 71 Pa. 256, 259 (Supreme Court of Pennsylvania, 13 May 1872), opening: “We have the authority of Holy Writ for saying that ‘no man can serve two masters; for either he will hate the one and love the other, or else he will hold to the one and despise the other.’ All human experience sanctions the undoubted truth and purity of this philosophy, and it is received as a cardinal principle in every system of enlightened jurisprudence.” *United States v. Mississippi Valley Generating Co.*, 364 U.S. 520, 549 (9 January 1961), grounding 18 U.S.C. §434: “The moral principle upon which the statute is based has its foundation in the Biblical admonition that no man may serve two masters, Matt. 6:24, a maxim which is especially pertinent if one of the masters happens to be economic self-interest.” *Chapman v. Klemick*, 3 F.3d 1508, 1512 (11th Cir., 12 October 1993), closing an ERISA appeal: “As one widely-cited authority has said: No man can serve two masters: for either he will hate the one, and love the other; or else he will hold to the one, and despise the other. Ye cannot serve God and mammon. Matthew 6:24 (King James). A trust fund is not exactly ‘mammon,’ but an attorney’s duty of loyalty to his client is very nearly sacred.”
>
> The dependence is explicit and self-declared in all three: *Everhart* names “the authority of Holy Writ,” *Mississippi Valley* prints the Matthaean locus, and *Chapman* block-quotes the verse whole and gives the citation. **Identity is the governing risk:** the King James antithesis and the mammon clause stand word for word at Lk. 16:13, so only an express Matthaean locus secures a candidate — which *Mississippi Valley* and *Chapman* supply and *Everhart* does not, resting on “Holy Writ” and the Matthaean form of the antithesis alone. All three quote the King James against the appointed Vulgate, and their “or else he will hold to the one, and despise the other” renders *aut unum sustinébit, et álterum contémnet*, where the Douay reads “or he will sustain the one, and despise the other.”
>
> **The turn.** Institutional and legal. In Matthew the sentence claims the impossibility of divided religious allegiance and is urged as a reason *not to be anxious*. In law it becomes a **prophylactic rule of positive obligation that operates without proof of wrongdoing**: the rule “is not intended to be remedial of actual wrong, but preventive of the possibility of it” where “no fraud \[was\] meditated and no injury done.” **A saying about God and money is made to disqualify a dual agent who has in fact behaved honestly**, and it does that work from *Everhart* in 1872 to the Supreme Court in 1961. ***Chapman* completes the turn as a joke in two moves**: an Evangelist introduced with the formula an appellate court reserves for a leading treatise, with a King James parenthetical where a court would name an edition; and then the concession that a trust fund is “not exactly ‘mammon,’” which treats a term of eschatological absolutes as though it admitted of degree, before the register recovers with “very nearly sacred.”
>
> Two attributions inside those opinions belong to other mouths. The “not intended to be remedial of actual wrong” sentence is *Everhart*, at 260, *quoting Hare and Wallace’s Notes to* 1 *Leading Cases in Equity*, p. 210, not the court speaking in its own voice; and “the authoritative declaration” in *Mississippi Valley*’s footnote is the *Court of Claims’* phrase in *Michigan Steel Box Co. v. United States*, 49 Ct. Cl. 421, 439, quoted by the Supreme Court and not the Supreme Court’s own.
>
> **Two lines of use run side by side and are not the same line.** The conflict-of-interest line from *Everhart* to *Mississippi Valley* stops at the general maxim and leaves the second master unnamed; a separate body of state, bankruptcy and fiduciary decisions quotes the mammon clause and does name Matthew. *Chapman*’s wit is one judge’s, and nothing shows a line, a tradition or a spread of the joke.

> **Epistle, Gal. 5:23 — “against such there is no law,” by pun**
>
> **Appointed text and locus.** Epistle, marginal no. 1574: *Advérsus huiúsmodi non est lex.*
>
> **Later text and loci.** *Johnston v. The Commonwealth*, 22 Pa. 102, **108** (1853), construing the Sunday-observance act of 1794 and its exception for works of charity and necessity: “perhaps all would agree, that visiting and administering to the sick and destitute, and labors for the spiritual welfare of men, are works both of charity and necessity. Certain it is, that against such there is no law, and they may be performed on any day.” *Sullens v. State*, 191 Miss. 856, **872**, 4 So. 2d 356 (10 November 1941), on the constructive contempt of a newspaper editor: “When one functioning within the circle of free speech runs out thoughtlessly to the full length of his tether he is apt to be brought up abruptly to his discomfiture, to find himself entangled amid the brambles of bad taste which fringe the border lines of discretion. Against such there is no law. Where the courts can not compel they can not condemn.”
>
> Neither court names Galatians, and both reproduce the clause word for word in the King James form — “against such there is no law,” where the Vulgate as appointed reads *Advérsus huiúsmodi non est lex* — which is unattributed borrowing and not citation, and whether either court intended the allusion is not established. **The identity is clean:** that English clause stands at Gal. 5:23 and nowhere else in the King James or the Douay.
>
> **The turn, and it is a pun.** **The pivot is on the word “law.”** Paul’s point is that the twelve fruits of the Spirit stand outside the *condemning* reach of the Mosaic law; *Johnston*’s is that works of charity and necessity stand outside the *prohibiting* reach of a Pennsylvania penal statute against Sunday labour; *Sullens* pushes it further, to conduct improper yet legally uncompellable. A theological claim about justification is converted into a canon of statutory construction. Two opinions eighty-eight years and two states apart are two attestations of a usage and not a line of descent, and *Sullens* shows no sign of having read *Johnston*.

> **Gradual, Ps. 117:9 — princes, or railroads**
>
> **Appointed text and locus.** Gradual, marginal no. 1575: *Bonum est confidere in Dómino, quam confidere in hómine. V Bonum est speráre in Dómino, quam speráre in princípibus.*
>
> **Later text and locus.** *Semi-weekly interior journal* (Stanford, Ky.), 19 November 1895, p. 2, cols. 3–4, in an unsigned travel letter under the heading “The Atlanta Exposition / And Other Incongruvial Notes Picked Up There and Thereabouts” and datelined “ATLANTA, Nov. 16”: “It is better to serve the Lord than to put confidence in princes or railroads, especially the latter. I thought when I got on the ‘Exposition Flyer,’ my trials were ended, but alas for human hopes. The engine broke down midway between stations five miles apart and refused to turn a wheel.”
>
> The writer quotes the second half of the appointed Gradual verse nearly intact, unattributed, and then adds to it — which only works if the reader recognises where the sentence was going to stop.
>
> **The turn.** **Humorous and idiomatic, achieved by a single appended noun.** The psalm’s antithesis is between divine and human reliability, with “princes” standing for the highest human power; the letter keeps the grammar and slips a railway company into the princes’ place, so a verse about the vanity of political trust becomes a traveller’s grumble about the Exposition Flyer. “Especially the latter” is what makes it a redirection rather than a quotation: it ranks the railroad below princes.
>
> **The letter’s verb is “serve” where the King James reads “trust,”** and it stands so on the printed page, the writer’s or the compositor’s substitution. **There is no dash before “or railroads”** — the line breaks inside “prin-ces” and runs straight on — and the sentence ends at “especially the latter.” The rest matches King James Ps. 118:9 word for word, and the identity is clean, the near-twin “Put not your trust in princes” being another psalm and reading differently. The paper is an unremarkable local one and the writer is unnamed. “Incongruvial” is as the paper set it.

> **Gospel, Mt. 6:28–29 — the lilies’ valuation inverted**
>
> **Appointed text and locus.** Gospel, marginal no. 1577: *Consideráte lília agri quómodo crescunt: non labórant, neque nent. Dico autem vobis, quóniam nec Sálomon in omni glória sua coopértus est sicut unum ex istis.*
>
> **Later text and loci.** *The Ranch* (Seattle, Wash.), 1 November 1910, p. 3, an unsigned editorial paragraph on the paper’s masthead page: “The farmer is not lying awake nights with worry because Wall street is so badly in the dumps. That bunch of gamblers who toil not neither do they spin, yet ‘Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these,’ has hoisted the banner of distress and sees no ray of hope.” *State v. Tracy*, 294 Mo. 372, 390 (Supreme Court of Missouri, 8 June 1922), on a prosecutor’s opening in a whiskey-warehouse burglary, where the defendant “neither toiled nor did he spin” and “The assistant prosecuting attorney correctly quoted the Master’s words, ‘They toil not, neither do they spin.’ ” *State v. Whiteaker*, 118 Or. 656, 661, 247 Pac. 1077 (Supreme Court of Oregon, decided 20 July 1926), declining to lay down a test under the Blue Sky Law, because “a certain class of gentlemen of the ‘J. Rufus Wallingford’ type — ‘they toil not neither do they spin’ — would lie awake nights endeavoring to conceive some devious and shadowy way of evading the law.” *State ex rel. Garrett v. McPeters*, 256 Ala. 555, 556, 56 So. 2d 102 (Supreme Court of Alabama, 13 November 1951), Brown J. **dissenting**: “This evidence presents a typical victim for the jackals of greed and corruption who ‘toil not neither do they spin,’ (Matthew 6: 28) except at the handle of a ‘one armed bandit,’ and feed upon the take.”
>
> **The dependence is documented in all four, and *McPeters* prints the Matthaean locus expressly**; *Tracy* does more than borrow the clause, it *certifies* the borrowing, holding that the prosecutor “correctly quoted the Master’s words.” **The identity is secure on one word:** Matthew reads “they toil not, *neither do* they spin” where Lk. 12:27 reads “they toil not, they spin not,” and all four carry “neither”; the Solomon clause alone stands word for word at Lk. 12:27 and would not discriminate. All four give the King James, where the Douay reads “they labour not, neither do they spin,” and the 1910 paper’s “like one of these” is the King James against the Douay’s “as one of these.”
>
> **The turn.** **A complete reversal of valuation.** In Matthew, not toiling is the point in the lilies’ *favour* — it is what shows the Father’s provision. Here it is the charge, and the men who toil not are speculators, burglars, blue-sky promoters and “jackals of greed and corruption.” **The 1910 paper sets the farmer, who does toil, against the speculator, who does not, and hands Matthew’s compliment to the wrong party on purpose**; and ***McPeters* sharpens it by appending an exception that destroys the figure** — they toil not “except at the handle of a ‘one armed bandit.’ ” The 1910 paper’s own punctuation shows how far the borrowing had travelled: it puts quotation marks round the *Solomon* clause only and leaves “who toil not neither do they spin” unmarked inside its own sentence, so the first half was already idiom to that writer while the second was still felt as quotation.
>
> **Three independent stems, and no descent between them.** *Tracy* precedes *Whiteaker* and cannot descend from it; *McPeters* cites Matthew directly and carries no Wallingford; the Oregon line quoting *Whiteaker* forward is a fourth and derivative stem. The 1910 paragraph is earlier than all three and shows no sign of their wording beyond the verse itself, **so what it establishes is that the inversion was available in the popular press before it reached the reports, and not that the courts took it from there.** ***McPeters* is a dissent and not the judgment**: the per curiam majority ousted the sheriff, and Brown J.’s figure casts the convicted sheriff as the victim. The 1910 paragraph is unsigned, and the court supplies no gloss on who or what the “J. Rufus Wallingford” of *Whiteaker* was.

## The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities

> **Exploratory editorial and AI proposals**
>
> Everything below is exploratory editorial and AI proposal. None of it is sourced historical intent, attributed teaching, or documented reception, and none of it should be quoted as any of those. Each proposal joins at least two precisely named appointed elements and ends on the strongest thing that stands against it. **No connection here is claimed to be unknown, unprecedented, first, or authored by the model**; where a proposal and a checked witness conflict, the witness wins. The precedent search behind each of these, its corpus and its bounds, is recorded in `research/scope.md` and summarised in the terminal appendix.

> **Editorial proposal — P1. Goods are ordered, not refused**
>
> **Anchors:** Introit <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>, *mélior est dies una in átriis tuis super mília*; Gradual <span class="nodecor">(*Grad.*)</span>, *bonum est confidere in Dómino, quam confidere in hómine*; Gospel <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>, *nemo potest duóbus dóminis servíre* and *non potéstis Deo servíre, et mammónæ*; Communion <span class="nodecor">(*Comm.*)</span>, *Primum*; Collect <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span>
>
> **Mechanism:** The formulary states one choice in three grammatical registers. Seven comparatives of degree in four Latin constructions stand in exactly three of the ten elements — the Introit’s *mélior … super mília*, the Gradual’s *Bonum est … quam* twice, the Gospel’s *plus quam* twice, its *Nonne … magis pluris* and its *quanto magis* — while the Epistle, the three orations, the Alleluia, the Offertory and the Communion carry none. The readings state the same choice **exclusively**: *hæc enim sibi ínvicem adversántur*, no man can serve two masters, you cannot serve God and mammon. **The Gospel stands in the comparative and the exclusive alike**, its *Ideo* making the exclusive premise the ground of the comparative argument, and the comparative register is what keeps the exclusive one from reading as contempt for created goods. **The Collect states the same preference in a third grammar**, neither comparative nor exclusive: its one *et … et* asks by the same help that the Church be drawn back from what harms and steered toward what saves, *et abstrahátur a nóxiis, et ad salutária dirigátur* — goods ordered by a single instrument rather than set against each other.
>
> **Fruit:** A doctrine of ordered preference preached without contempt for creation, and the reason this Mass can command an absolute exclusion and still promise that all things shall be added.
>
> **What the element-by-element reading misses:** Read one element at a time, the chants look like decoration around two hard sayings. Read together, the three registers are doing different grammatical work on the same choice.
>
> **Strongest limit:** **The defeater is not a modern philological worry: a named medieval expositor stands on both sides of it.** The *quam* of Ps. 117:8–9 may render a Hebrew idiom of *exclusion* rather than of degree, and *super mília* is a Hebraism too, so three of the seven comparatives may not be comparatives at all — only the Gospel’s four are Greek-derived and immune. **Bruno the Carthusian asks exactly that question about exactly these words, and answers it twice**: first that the word is not set down as a comparative, so that trust in the Lord is useful and recourse to the persecutor is not; then that it is comparative, **but only if the princes are heavenly spirits and spiritual men**, on the ground that God can help without them and they cannot help without God. His wording is not quoted here, at either answer, the passage standing in a column that must be read at a page image before any of it is quoted. **And a Greek Father at the appointed verses denies the comparison outright.** John Chrysostom, on Ps. 117:8–9 at *Patrologia Graeca* 55:331, holds that Scripture uses the comparative form of things that admit no comparison, out of condescension to weak hearers — *ouk ara synkrinon touto phesin, alla synkatabainon ekeinois* — so that on his reading the Gradual’s twice-repeated *Bonum est … quam* is not a weighing of two goods at all. **So this proposal is stated as contingent on the comparative reading of the Latin *quam*, and on Bruno’s second answer rather than his first, and it must answer Chrysostom rather than gesture at him; nothing in a Latin distribution can settle which is right.** Secondly, the comparative evidence reaches only part of the anchor set — **the Communion and the Collect are thematic members here and not comparative ones**, and that is said rather than blurred. Thirdly, this must not be built as “the chants soften the readings”: it is one unit, not a two-sided contrast.

> **Editorial proposal — P2. What man cannot add**
>
> **Anchors:** Gospel <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>, Mt. 6:27 *Quis autem vestrum cógitans potest adícere ad statúram suam cúbitum unum?* and Mt. 6:33 *adiciéntur*; Communion <span class="nodecor">(*Comm.*)</span>, *adiciéntur*; Collect <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span>, *quia sine te lábitur humána mortálitas*; Epistle <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>, *ut non quæcúmque vultis, illa faciátis*
>
> **Mechanism:** One verb carries the exchange. The Gospel denies that any man can *add* a cubit to his stature and then promises that all things *shall be added*; the Communion repeats the promise alone; and the Collect and the Epistle supply the same incapacity in other words. **What man cannot add to himself, God adds to him**, and the reversal is in the morphology — active of the man who cannot, passive of the God who does.
>
> **Fruit:** A doctrine of grace read off a single verb, and the reason the Mass’s petition and its promise are one movement rather than two.
>
> **What the element-by-element reading misses:** Both occurrences of the verb sit inside one pericope, so an element-by-element reading files them as a feature of Matthew rather than as the axis on which the Collect’s diagnosis and the Communion’s promise turn.
>
> **Strongest limit:** **More of this is documented reception than proposal, and the proposal is what is left when that is subtracted.** Aquinas already reads the cubit as an argument from providence at the same verse; pseudo-Bede reads the Communion’s own verb, *Non dixit nobis dari, sed adjici*; and Christian of Stavelot supplies its elided object with its limit, *Ad necessitatem, non ad superfluitatem*. **What remains exploratory is only the cross-element join** — the incapacity at v. 27 to the promise six verses later, to the Communion’s repetition of the promise alone, and to the Collect’s *sine te lábitur humána mortálitas*. **Neither the Collect nor the Epistle uses the verb**, so two of the four members join doctrinally and not lexically, **a weaker join than P4’s and stated as such.** The Benziger *editio iuxta typicam* spells the root with a doubled *-ii-* in all three places, and the difference does not touch the argument. Its form at Mt. 6:27 is *adiícere*, read on the Internet Archive item’s own single-page JP2 derivative and so **a reading of an unregistered derivative of a registered item**; the doubled *-ii-* at Mt. 6:33 and at the Communion stands in that item’s uncorrected optical text layer, **no page of the registered page-image artifact having been opened.** **And Chrysostom rests the promise on the same verb in the opposite direction from this reading**, in Greek at *Patrologia Graeca* 57:303: he said not *shall be given* but *shall be added*, “that you may learn that the things present are nothing great, set beside the greatness of the things to come,” in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English, first series — for him the point of *added* is the smallness of what is added and not the passivity of the recipient. **Finally, the passive with a divine subject is the settled grammar of the Roman collect**, so the orations’ half of this may be a fact about how Latin orations are written; the counter-test would need a concordance over the Missal’s orations, and the optical layers available recover no multi-word phrase of this formulary at all.

> **Editorial proposal — P3. Propitiation and salvation saturate the prayers the people do not sing**
>
> **Anchors:** Collect <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span>, *propitiatióne perpétua*; Secret <span class="nodecor">(*Sec.*)</span>, *tuæ propitiátio potestátis*; Postcommunion <span class="nodecor">(*Postcomm.*)</span>, *perpétuæ … salvatiónis efféctum*; Alleluia <span class="nodecor">(*All.*)</span>, *Deo salutári nostro*
>
> **Mechanism:** The three orations and the Alleluia carry vocabulary no other element does — *propitiátio* twice, the *salut-* and *salv-* root four times, *semper* twice, *perpétu-* twice — so **the sung and read elements state the problem in the language of service, trust and anxiety while the priest’s own prayers state it in the language of expiation and rescue.** The Alleluia’s *salutári* is the single word tying the day’s one purely jubilant chant into that thread.
>
> **Fruit:** The Mass has two registers of address, and a reader who follows only the readings hears one of them. It also explains why the Alleluia, otherwise the least integrated element of the formulary, is not simply decorative.
>
> **What the element-by-element reading misses:** Read in order, the orations are three short prayers in a conventional idiom. Read together and against the chants, they are the only place in the Mass where the problem is named as expiation.
>
> **Strongest limit:** **Severe, and it cannot be closed here.** This is the ordinary vocabulary of Roman collects, so the saturation may be a fact about the genre and not about this Mass — and **the counter-test that would settle it cannot be run**: a lexical concordance over the orations of the whole Missal would have to run against an optical layer that demonstrably undercounts, so its result would not be evidence. What can be said is bounded to the fifteen formularies this guide has collated: the substantive *propitiátio* appears in the appointed Latin of **no other** of them. That counter-test holds narrowly: its one apparent counter-example is English audit prose in another leaf’s record and not appointed text. **The wider *propiti-* family is a different matter and the count over it is not restated here**: the figure this guide carried for it comes from an earlier sweep that no later one has repeated, so **the claim is about the substantive and collapses if restated about the propitiation word-family generally.** **Four words shared with the very next Sunday’s Collect — *Ecclésiam tuam*, *quia sine te*, *semper*, and the two-verb doublet whose second verb is *munire* — are not distinctive of this formulary either, so a claim of saturation must be made about the words that are not shared.** **And the four-fold *salut-* count is one root in four senses** — of a victim, of God, of things that save and of a state. Finally, **this proposal is invisible in the guide’s own English witness**: the registered 1861 rendering drops *propitiátio* at the Collect, turns it into a verb at the Secret, and drops both *semper* and the substantive at the Postcommunion, so **every measurement supporting it is conducted on the Latin**. That is how the 1861 translator rendered the three prayers, read on the printed page at pp. 425 and 427, and not a doubt about what he wrote.

> **Editorial proposal — P4. The hope-formula lies past the cut, and is handed on at another Mass**
>
> **Anchors:** Introit <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>, Ps. 83:10–11a with vv. 2–3a; Gradual <span class="nodecor">(*Grad.*)</span>, Ps. 117:8–9; Offertory <span class="nodecor">(*Off.*)</span>, Ps. 33:8–9a
>
> **Mechanism:** The formula *beatus … qui sperat* stands in exactly two verses of the Clementine Vulgate, and they are the two psalms this Mass takes its Offertory and its Introit from: *beatus vir qui sperat in eo* at Ps. 33:9b and *beatus homo qui sperat in te* at Ps. 83:13, the two differing in what is hoped in, so that no one quoted form stands at both. It lies **past the Introit antiphon’s cut at v. 11a and past its psalm verse’s cut at v. 3a**; it lies **one clause past the Offertory’s cut at v. 9a**, in the second half of the very verse the antiphon ends on; and **the Gradual alone is uncut and sings the hope vocabulary outright**, the *sper-* and *confid-* stems standing twice each in the Gradual block and not at all in the Introit. Three appointed excerpts stop short of the formula and a fourth sings the vocabulary it is made of. **The Introit belongs because the formula lies beyond both its boundaries, not because it uses hope vocabulary — which it does not.**
>
> **Fruit:** The formulary’s chief coherence is a shared editorial boundary, and it is verifiable rather than thematic. It also gives the Gradual, the day’s plainest maxim, a structural role it does not obviously have.
>
> **What the element-by-element reading misses:** An extent read one element at a time is a boundary and nothing more: the Introit simply stops, the Offertory simply stops. **Read across three chants the stopping is a pattern, and the term the pattern stops before is appointed whole at another Mass in the same book** — the Eighth Sunday after Pentecost’s Communion prints Ps. 33:9 entire, *Gustate et videte, quoniam suavis est Dominus: beatus vir, qui sperat in eo*. Across the twenty-one collated text records covering fifteen 1962 identities, *sperat in eo* stands in the appointed Latin of the Eighth Sunday alone and *gustate* in the Eighth and the Fourteenth only. So the beatitude of the one who hopes is not absent from the repertory; it is assigned to the other Mass.
>
> **Strongest limit:** **The same datum has already been used, in this collection, as a disconfirming condition rather than as evidence.** The Eighth Sunday’s own guide treats a pericope stopping one clause short, with the missing clause appointed at another Mass in the same book, as showing that the withheld matter “is therefore not withheld from the liturgical year at all — only from this Sunday, and only because a lection had to end somewhere,” which collapses such a reading into a coincidence of scissors; and it records that *gustate et videte* is “the commonest of Communion antiphons.” **Both readings of one kind of evidence cannot be right, and this proposal stands only so far as it answers that objection.** Beyond it: **the antiphons’ extents are inherited chant tradition and nothing establishes that anything was cut in order to suppress the formula**; psalms of supplication routinely close on exactly such promises, so the pattern may be a property of the psalter rather than of the selection; duller explanations are available, metrical length and the ordinary bounds of an antiphon among them; and **the test that would settle it — measuring how often an arbitrary chant extent in this repertory stops short of a comparable promise — cannot be run on the sources held here, which include no chant repertory at all.** Secondly, *confidere* is a trust word rather than a hope word strictly: a stricter count of *spes* vocabulary alone finds *speráre* twice in the Gradual and nothing else. Thirdly, the Eighth Sunday’s Communion prints Ps. 33:9 whole with the hope clause in the Pustet Ratisbon 1862 and the Venice 1570 text layers, both searched rather than inspected and **neither collated against a page image.** Fourthly, Ambrose quotes the fuller verse *with* the hope clause in the fourth century, which sharpens the observation about the Roman cut but says nothing about why it was made.

> **Editorial proposal — P5. Two chants, two divisions: the Fathers disagree at the Offertory and at the Gradual, and the formulary resolves neither**
>
> **Anchors:** Offertory <span class="nodecor">(*Off.*)</span>, Ps. 33:8–9a, *Immíttet Angelus Dómini … gustáte et vidéte*; Gradual <span class="nodecor">(*Grad.*)</span>, Ps. 117:8–9, *quam speráre in princípibus*
>
> **Mechanism:** The Fathers divide at each of the two chants, and the two divisions are **independent** of each other; the Mass prints both without adjudicating. At the **Offertory** the angel is read four ways — Augustine’s Christ, the Angel of great counsel; Cassiodorus’s covert working and moral sense, on the very lemma Augustine denounced; Arnobius’s angel of the sepulchre; Godfrey’s gift of grace — while the same verse is attested by the *Mystagogical Catecheses* and by Ambrose as the Church’s *communion* invitation, and the 1962 book assigns it to the *offertory*. At the **Gradual** Augustine escalates the excluded second term to **good angels** — “for angels also are called princes,” in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series — making the exclusion absolute rather than moral, against Theodoret’s temporary earthly rulers and Godfrey’s interior and exterior senses. **The angel who is the Mass’s rescuer at one chant is the Mass’s excluded object of trust at the other, and the Fathers reached each position separately.**
>
> **Fruit:** A cross-chant tension the Fathers themselves hold and the formulary’s own arrangement stages; and a way to treat the Offertory’s angel that neither flattens the christological identification nor asserts it.
>
> **What the element-by-element reading misses:** Each division is reported under its own chant as a local disagreement. Set side by side, they are the same word doing opposite work at two moments of one Mass.
>
> **Strongest limit:** **Both divisions are deeper than when this was drafted, and one of them may be shallower than it looks.** **Arnobius’s angel of the sepulchre depends on repointing *timéntium eum* as those fearing *for* his body, which is not what the missal’s Latin says.** **And the Gradual’s angelic reading is one position with three carriers and not three witnesses.** Augustine holds it at his own exposition of Ps. 117, with Daniel’s Michael as warrant and with the principle that reconciles the two chants: no one is to be trusted in, “for no one is good, save God alone; and when a man or an angel appear to aid us … He does it through them, who made them good after their measure.” Peter Lombard puts the reading under Augustine’s name with the same warrant, so he transmits him correctly; Prosper’s exposition is largely an epitome of Augustine, and Prosper’s reason is Lombard’s reason. **But that reconciliation is conditional on who the Offertory’s angel is, and for Augustine the tension never arises at all, because for him that *Angelus* is Christ. If the angel is a creature — as Cassiodorus, Godfrey and Arnobius have it — the Mass does trust at one chant a rescuer it excludes from trust at the other, and the instrumental principle softens the tension without removing it.** Cassiodorus, Godfrey and Arnobius are read here in Latin, in transcriptions of *Patrologia Latina* 70, 174 and 53, with no page image collated and no critical edition beside them; three witnesses of this proposal stand in translation — Augustine, whose *Enarratio* on Ps. 117 is quoted in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series, with *Patrologia Latina* 36–37 uncollated, and the *Mystagogical Catecheses* and Ambrose, read in the second series of the same collection, with *Patrologia Graeca* 33 and *Patrologia Latina* 16 uncollated. Bruno the Carthusian’s question is a fifth thing and is grammatical rather than doctrinal. **The cross-relation between the Gradual’s absolute exclusion and the Gospel’s *Nemo potest duóbus dóminis servíre* is this guide’s observation and not Augustine’s: he does not cite Mt. 6:24 at that locus**, and the Gospel is accordingly not an anchor here. The Offertory half carries three further limits: Augustine’s text differs from the missal’s at the following verb, *eruet* against *erípiet*, which is evidence that the missal’s text is **not** simply his; the missal’s capital *Angelus* is orthography and **does not encode** his identification; and the attribution of the *Mystagogical Catecheses* to Cyril of Jerusalem is disputed in modern scholarship and that question was not swept. **And the strongest alternative is a variant in the printed books at the Offertory’s own opening verb and subject**: 1962 reads *Immíttet Angelus Dómini*, making the angel of the Lord encamp; the Roman antiphonary of *Patrologia Latina* 78 reads *Immittet angelum Dominus*, making the Lord send an angel; and the Sarum books read the present *Inmittit*, making it a standing fact rather than a promise. **On the antiphonary’s reading the Offertory names no angel of the Lord at all**, and the cross-chant identity this proposal turns on does not arise in the form stated. Which of the three each reception witness had before him is not established.

> **Editorial proposal — P6. The one verb the Mass appoints twice, with its object reversed**
>
> **Anchors:** Introit <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>, the psalm verse, *concupíscit, et déficit ánima mea in átria Dómini*; Epistle <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>, *caro enim concupíscit advérsus spíritum* and *carnem suam crucifixérunt cum vítiis et concupiscéntiis*
>
> **Mechanism:** *Concupiscere* is appointed three times in two elements with its object reversed. In the Introit’s psalm verse the soul longs *toward* the courts of the Lord and faints for them; in the Epistle the flesh desires *against* the spirit, and what is crucified is the flesh *with its desires*. **One verb states both the movement the Mass asks for and the movement it asks to be delivered from**, and the difference between them is the direction of the longing rather than its intensity.
>
> **Fruit:** Desire is not the enemy in this formulary; its object is. That gives the Epistle’s mortification a positive term inside the same Mass and keeps the crucifixion of the flesh from being read as the extinction of longing.
>
> **What the element-by-element reading misses:** The Introit’s occurrence sits in the psalm verse, which an element-by-element reading treats as the antiphon’s setting rather than as appointed vocabulary; the Epistle’s two are read as Pauline anthropology. Nothing brings the three together.
>
> **Strongest limit:** **Two limits, both real, and a third that forbids the reinforcement one would want.** The two senses are the ordinary senses of an ordinary Latin verb, **so the opposition may be a coincidence of the Vulgate’s vocabulary before it is a compositional choice, and no compiler’s intention is claimed.** The Introit’s occurrence is in the **psalm verse** and not the antiphon, so it is liable to the objection that a psalm verse is not appointed in the same sense the antiphon is. **And the second lexical leg that would strengthen it is not used here:** *ánima* stands in the same Introit clause, three words from *concupíscit*, and again in the Gospel with the valuation reversed — **but *anima* is among the commonest nouns of both psalter and Gospels, and Mt. 6:25’s *ánimæ vestræ* is very likely “your life” rather than “your soul,” in which case the Introit’s longing soul and the Gospel’s anxious life are two different subjects and the reversal is a trick of Latin vocabulary.** *Concupíscit* is also one of only five substantive forms a mechanical comparison of the ten appointed blocks returns as shared between different texts, so it carries no more weight than the other four.

### Evidence-state discipline

**No patristic or medieval witness in this guide is *verified* in the source-library sense**, because verification under this profile means collating the locus against the controlling critical or Migne edition, and **Migne was opened for none of them**. The tiering used throughout is: *acquired* (exact bytes obtained and hashed), *searched* (a query run over the retained artifact), *inspected* (the identified passage read with its surrounding context), and, where a witness in the original language independently agrees, *corroborated* — which is short of collation and is never called more. **Possession is not reading, a search hit is not inspection, and inspection of a transcription is not collation against a page image.**

**The Greek layer added at this revision stands at the evidence state of a nineteenth-century reprint, and it reaches this guide by two routes.** Where a witness above is cited as read on *Patrologia Graeca*’s own pages — in PG 12, 27, 29, 55, 57 and 82 — every column figure, running head, section number and lemma was read on a full-resolution page image, because that volume series’ optical layer misreads numerals systematically — in one volume rendering columns 363 and 365 as 549 and 565 — and the continuous text beside those figures was read on the same item’s optical layer. **Three of the Greek witnesses stand on transcriptions instead, with no *Patrologia Graeca* page image collated for them**: Theodoret’s expositions of Pss. 83, 94 and 117 at PG 80, Chrysostom on Galatians at PG 61:670–674, and the Chrysostomic homily on Ps. 94 at PG 55:615–618. **Their column figures stand unchecked against Migne’s own pages, and so under exactly the numeral hazard just named.** **Every one of those witnesses is Migne’s reprint of a Maurist or Renaissance text, and not one was collated against a modern critical edition.** Migne prints no facing Latin at two of them, so no Latin control was available there. **The *Selecta in Psalmos* and the *Expositiones in Psalmos* are of contested attribution and are cited above as the collections and never flatly under Origen’s and Athanasius’s names**, and a running head over one of them prints a psalm number the Greek beneath does not carry.

**One Latin critical edition was read, and only as a transcription.** *De civitate Dei* XIV.2 stands here in a critical-edition transcription whose own record states that the encoding was inspected and not collated, so its wording is trustworthy to the standard of that transcription and no further.

### Search limits

**A search that finds nothing has not established that nothing is there; it has established what the search did.** Seven traps met in this research resolve successfully and *wrongly*, in the five kinds set out below, and every negative in this guide is bounded against them.

**Optical hyphenation.** A whole-word search over the tracked Missal misses an occurrence of the Offertory broken across a line end, and a search of forty-seven newspaper issues for a two-word phrase returned zero while the phrase was present, broken the same way. **The bound on the missal payloads is worse than that:** four appointed phrases tested return zero from all nine payloads while the same books demonstrably print them, so **single words and fragments carry weight there and multi-word phrases carry almost none**, and no whole-phrase negative over those artifacts is reported here as absence.

**A phrase index that knows one orthography.** The exact-phrase index of *Patrologia Latina* makes a new class of negative statable — that a phrase does not occur in that whole collection — **but only in Migne’s orthography and only where the phrase is unbroken in the unit indexed.** **No patristic or medieval exegesis of the Secret *Concéde nobis, Dómine* or of the Postcommunion *Puríficent semper et múniant* stands anywhere that index reaches**, every hit on their distinctive phrases being a sacramentary printing the prayer — **two of the ten appointed elements with no documented exegetical reception in any period.** **The Collect stands outside that negative**, Honorius citing it by incipit, Godfrey of Admont glossing it word by word and Schuster commenting on it continuously. The negative on those two prayers carries exactly the bound above, and three further bounds with it. A witness who paraphrases either prayer, gives it a different incipit, or cites it by its Sunday rather than by its words stands outside the index as surely as one writing *propiciatio* does. **The Greek and the vernacular corpora stand outside it altogether**, and three further bodies stand unopened for these two prayers: the ancient sacramentaries’ modern editions as commentary sources; Walafrid Strabo and the other medieval liturgical expositors; and the Usuarium *Corpus Orationum* concordance. **On the Greek and Latin side of it, nine distinctive collocations from the three orations stand nowhere in *Patrologia Graeca* 12, 27, 29, 55, 57 and 82, nor in the critical-edition transcription of *De civitate Dei*** — six Greek volumes and one Latin work, a narrow base for a Latin euchological question, and a Greek Migne volume’s optical layer renders Latin poorly, so an absence in it weighs much less than an absence in a critical transcription.

**Phrase-search degradation.** The newspaper service’s quoted-string search **does not do exact-phrase matching for stopword-heavy strings; it degrades to an AND of terms.** This was established by calibration, one test query returning tens of thousands of items whose first-ranked result did not contain the clause at all. **Every total from that corpus is a loose-match total and never a phrase count**, and every negative drawn from it is bounded to the relevance-ranked results actually re-checked against their own optical text — fifty or a hundred out of totals in the hundreds or thousands, depending on the query. **That corpus is thin after 1922 and effectively ends about 1963, and only English-language American newspapers were searched**: no British, Irish, Continental or Latin-American press, and no non-English, book, periodical or musical-repertory corpus, was searched for any element. The case-law corpus is **overwhelmingly American** and no Commonwealth sweep was run.

**A partial file that looks complete.** Two collections have now been met in which a work’s title promises the whole and the file carries a fragment — Cassiodorus in one web library, and Chrysostom on the Psalms in a Greek one. **A title is not a table of contents.**

**The shared incipit, the running head, and the fused reference letter.** *Protector noster aspice, Deus* opens this Mass’s Introit, another Sunday’s Gradual and a composed collect that quotes no psalm at all, so **a search hit on an incipit proves nothing on its own.** A running head in either of Wilson’s editions names the section the page *reaches*, not the one it opens in, and two loci in this guide were misread that way before being corrected against the section headings themselves. **And an optical layer fuses a superscript footnote reference letter with the section numeral that follows it**, which is how a section CXXXIII was manufactured out of *cXXXIII* and how the Ottobonianus chant cues were briefly attached to the wrong Mass. **Both were corrected against page images.**

**The precedent corpus.** Every “not located in the checked corpus” verdict behind the interpretive section is bounded to exactly this: an accent-stripped index over all 3,342 source files of this collection and of the sibling collection; the 102 interpretive-proposal units that name two or more appointed elements, across the 26 proposal-bearing leaves of both; the tracked 1962 registry; and the Pustet Ratisbon 1862 missal text layer. **Not reached for any conjunction:** any chant repertory, any Breviary or Office payload, sermon literature, and the pre-Tridentine commentary tradition. **A zero score there states what that corpus of proposals contains and nothing else**, and no connection in this guide is called unknown, unprecedented or first.

### Rights boundary

**Three summary states are distinguished on page 2 and they are not the same thing**, which is the distinction this appendix owes that sheet. *Summarised by choice* is a public-domain source this guide could quote and does not, generally because its bytes are not retained and the wording would be this guide’s own. *Summarise-only* is a source whose rights are recorded *unresolved* — a recorded non-determination and not a restriction, treated conservatively here as though it were one. *Summary compelled* is a source whose rights are recorded *restricted*.

Public domain and quotable: the volume facsimiles of the encyclopedia used on page 2, the 1910 responsa, the 1614 commentary, the 1866 Bellarmine translation, the 1900 Guéranger, Wilson 1894 and 1915, Feltoe 1896, the 1862 Missal, the American court reports and newspapers, the United Kingdom official report, and all tracked Bible text. **One distinction here is easy to get backwards and is stated plainly: an encyclopedia article read from the volume facsimile may be quoted, while the same article read from a per-article web transcription may be summarised but not reproduced, because the transcription and its markup carry their own copyright over the public-domain article underneath.** They are the same words under opposite rights. **The two encyclopedia articles this formulary most needs — on the Gospel of St Matthew and on the Epistle to the Galatians — are held here as tracked, public-domain, indexable article text, and page 2 accordingly reports what each says**: the southern-Galatian composition place and its two datings from the one, and the principal modern critical datings of Matthew from the other. The chronology corpus carries labelled claims sourced to those same volumes, and those labels are what the Date column prints.

The Latin transcription behind the medieval layer is an optical transcription of public-domain Migne and is quotable at the state recorded above. The Greek text layers carry a modern digitiser’s copyright over ancient Greek that is public domain by age: **the ancient Greek is what is quoted, and no bytes of either were written into this repository.**

Restricted, and therefore summarised and nowhere reproduced: the registered modern Galatians introduction, which is the only limb of that translation bearing on this proper. Restricted by reason of a modern apparatus over an ancient text: Anthony of Padua’s sermon, **whose thirteenth-century Latin is public domain by age and is what the canonical edition quotes, the modern apparatus being paraphrased and never reproduced**; and the Migne facsimile of Honorius, fetched, read, and retained nowhere. Modern translations under copyright are never reproduced at any length that would substitute for the book, and **the one copyrighted item the cultural sweep returned is not published here at all.**

**And a fact about this repository that changes what a green build means.** **No mechanical gate here can check a rights claim against a guide’s prose.** The preflight checks that touch quotation are a self-consistency check between this guide’s own References and its own quotation environments and a check of the leaf’s declared bindings against the library’s rights fields; neither reads the prose against a source. **The rights discipline above is enforced by authorship**, and it is recorded here so that a reader does not mistake a passing build for a rights audit.

### Review state and what is deliberately not here

The synthesis companion omits the complete appointed texts and the element-by-element sweep, and replaces the sweep with an integrated commentary organised by cross-proper questions; the complete Latin, the full per-element reception and the collation records stand in the canonical edition and in `propers/verified.md`.

- Prosper of Aquitaine, *Expositio Psalmorum*, in *Patrologia Latina* 51, cols. 333B–C. *Same route and state. **Largely an epitome of Augustine’s *Enarrationes*, and whether this exposition is Prosper’s own throughout is not established.***

- Arnobius the Younger (*attributed*), *Commentarii in Psalmos*, in *Patrologia Latina* 53: Ps. XXXIII at cols. 368C–369B, Ps. LXXXIII at 446C–447D, Ps. XCIV at 462D–463C, Ps. CXVII at 505A–C. *Same route and state. **The ascription is the traditional one and the transcription also carries these commentaries as *Auctor incertus*: he is cited in the canonical edition as attributed.***

- Rabanus Maurus, on Mt. 6:24–33, in *Patrologia Latina* 107, cols. 836B–839A. *Same route and state. **At these verses he compiles rather than composes**, the material running under Jerome’s and Augustine’s sigla with one sentence of his own, **so he is nowhere cited above as a further vote for a reading that is theirs.***

- Paschasius Radbertus, *Expositio in Matthaeum*, in *Patrologia Latina* 120, cols. 306B–307A and 312C–313A. *Same route and state. Cited for the baptismal grounding of the two masters, for the exception of the Trinity, and for his denial that *primum* marks order.*

- Christian of Stavelot (Druthmarus), *Expositio in Matthaeum* cap. XIII, in *Patrologia Latina* 106, cols. 1316C–1319A. *Same route and state. Cited for the *latreusis* and *douleusis* distinction, for the refusal of allegory at the lilies, for the Solomon note — reported in the canonical edition as his opinion about what the Jews knew — and for *subauditur necessaria* at Mt. 6:33.*

- The Matthew exposition printed under Bede’s name, in *Patrologia Latina* 92, cols. 34D–36A. *Same route and state. **Cited above as the exposition printed under his name and never as Bede.***

- Bruno of Segni, *Commentaria in Matthaeum*, in *Patrologia Latina* 165, cols. 121B–123C. *Same route and state; one recognition error in this passage prints *wercede* for *mercede*. Cited for the denial that the covetous man can fail to hate God, for the literal objection about many masters, and for the restriction of the whole prohibition of anxiety to apostles and those aiming at perfection. **His own *Expositio in Psalmos*, which reaches all four appointed psalms, was not read.***

- Bruno the Carthusian (*attributed*), *Expositio in Psalmos*, in *Patrologia Latina* 152, cols. 1142A–1143A and 1249D–1250B. *Same route and state, with one addition: **the Ps. 117 passage arrived with its line order damaged and was reconstructed from the surrounding column, so it must be verified at a page image of PL 152:1250 before any of it is quoted, and **no word of it is quoted above**.** Cited, in this guide’s own words, for the twofold answer on whether the Gradual’s *quam* is comparative, and for the Alleluia’s psalm title and jubilus.*

- Peter Lombard, *Commentaria in Psalmos*, in *Patrologia Latina* 191, cols. 875A–876A and 1035B–C. *Same route and state. **The *Magna glosatura*, a sigla-marked compilation: cited above for its arrangement and never as a vote**, its angelic reading of the Gradual standing under Augustine’s name and its jubilus formula under sigla naming Augustine, Cassiodorus, Haymo and Alcuin.*

- Godfrey of Admont, *Homiliae* LXXXII and LXXXIII, in *Patrologia Latina* 174, cols. 575A–583D. *Same route and state, with one addition: **PL 174:583 carries a standing instruction not to quote it until a page image is read**, and because *Homilia* LXXXIII runs across that column and this reading does not establish which of its sentences fall inside it, **nothing of that homily is quoted above**; his readings are reported there in this guide’s own words. The Collect gloss at col. 580B, the Offertory’s angel at cols. 580A–B and the Gradual’s princes at cols. 579D–580A stand outside the flagged column and are quoted. **His Fourteenth Sunday is not the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday** — his Gospel is the ten lepers, and the 1962 Gospel is his Fifteenth — and that bound travels with every use of him above.*

- Alcuin, *De usu Psalmorum* VII, in *Patrologia Latina* 101; and Wibald of Corvey, *Epistola* CCCLXXV to Eugenius III, in *Patrologia Latina* 189. ***Unverified leads, and named here as leads.** An exact-phrase index over the *Patrologia Latina* returns the Collect’s clauses at both places; **neither volume was opened there**, so neither text is quoted above and no claim above rests on either.*

- Thomas Aquinas, *Super Epistolam ad Galatas lectura* cap. 5, lect. 5 and lect. 6. *Acquired, searched and inspected **in the Latin** — materially better than the English-only witnesses — but the text is a machine transcription not collated against a printed Leonine or Marietti page. The *De civitate Dei* XIV.2 in his text is his citation of it, reported as his use; Augustine’s own wording at that place was read in the tracked CSEL 40.2 transcription entered above.*

- Thomas Aquinas, *Super Matthaeum* cap. 6, lect. 3–5. *Retrieved in Latin from a transcription, inspected, not collated against the Marietti or a critical edition; **the text is a *reportatio***, and it carries a documented misattribution of the lilies-as-angels reading to Jerome, corrected in the canonical edition.*

- Thomas Aquinas, *Catena aurea in Matthaeum* cap. 6, lect. 16–21. ***A lead-map only.** Its *Hilarius* and *Hieronymus* excerpts on the appointed Gospel were cross-checked sentence by sentence against the independently retrieved underlying works and reproduce them faithfully — **a check made against the same Migne recensions the Catena drew on**, so it establishes faithful transmission and not the Migne text’s own correctness. Gregory and Remigius remain unverified leads and are quoted from this Catena nowhere above. Its *Chrysostomus super Matth.* is the anonymous *Opus imperfectum* and not Chrysostom.*

- Anthony of Padua, *Sermo Dominica XIV post Pentecosten*, in the Centro Studi Antoniani edition. *Read in the Latin; the downloaded digest reproduced the registered artifact bit for bit. **The artifact is rights-restricted on account of its modern apparatus: Anthony’s thirteenth-century Latin is public domain by age and is what the canonical edition quotes, and the modern apparatus is paraphrased and never reproduced.** No bytes were written into this repository. His inner quotations from the Gloss, from Isidore and from Augustine are leads and are attributed to his use of them. **His Sunday numbering runs one ahead of the 1962 missal’s.***

### Cultural afterlives

***Two of the five entries above rest on newspaper pages collated against the page images**, and are *verified* for the crop regions quoted and no further; **the court opinions and the parliamentary report were read whole in transcriptions of the printed reports and are *inspected*.** A statement that none of the five is verified, or that all are, would be wrong either way.*

- United Kingdom, *Parliamentary Debates*: Commons, 3 December 1962, vol. 668, in the Central Africa debate (Humphry Berkeley, from the printed time-marker 8.1 p.m.); Lords, 19 December 1962, vol. 245, on British policy in Central Africa (Lord Milverton, from 6.27 p.m.). *Official report, read continuously in the official digitisation; **parliamentary material is published under the Open Parliament Licence**, and extracts are kept brief. An exact-phrase query of that report’s own index for the maxim returns exactly these two contributions and no others. **Not collated against the printed bound volume, and the printed column numbers were not established from this retrieval**, so the locus is the volume and the report’s own time-markers.*

- *The Spark* (Accra), no. 159, 5 November 1965, on the Ghana Co-operative Movement. *Optical text of an openly readable digitised item; public domain. Reports what Nkrumah “has repeatedly said” and is not his own first utterance. **Not re-retrieved for this revision**, so its variant wording rests on the earlier reading.*

- *Everhart v. Searle*, 71 Pa. 256, 259–260 (1872); *United States v. Mississippi Valley Generating Co.*, 364 U.S. 520, 549 (1961); *Chapman v. Klemick*, 3 F.3d 1508, 1512 (11th Cir. 1993). *United States court opinions and official reporters; public domain. **All three read whole in static transcriptions of the printed reporters, with star pagination present**, so the pin cites are read off those transcriptions’ own page markers; **none is collated against a page image of any reporter.** *Michigan Steel Box Co. v. United States*, 49 Ct. Cl. 421, 439, and Hare and Wallace’s Notes to 1 *Leading Cases in Equity* p. 210 are cited above as the sources those opinions quote and were not separately retrieved. **The corpus is overwhelmingly American and no Commonwealth sweep was performed**; the further opinions quoting the mammon clause were seen in search metadata only and none is quoted above.*

State*, 191 Miss. 856, **872**, 4 So. 2d 356 (1941). *Same corpus, rights and state; **both members read whole in static transcriptions with star pagination**, which is what settles the Johnston pin cite at 108 rather than at 111. An exact-phrase query returns exactly these two opinions. *Omit* was not separately retrieved.*

- *Semi-weekly interior journal* (Stanford, Ky.), 19 November 1895, p. 2, cols. 3–4. *Public-domain American newspaper digitised by the Library of Congress, **read as a page image at the crop carrying the headline and the quoted sentences, and at the masthead block**, and *verified* for those regions only.
