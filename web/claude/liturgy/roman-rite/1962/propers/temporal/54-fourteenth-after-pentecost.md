### The four senses

| **Sense** | **Synthesis** |
|:-----------|:------------------------------------------------------|
| Literal | A psalm asks God to look on the face of his Christ and prefers one day in his courts to thousands <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>. Paul sets seventeen works of the flesh against twelve fruits of the Spirit <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>. ## The Propers: Themes and Movement {#triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

> **Governing thesis**
>
> This Mass was assembled from three strands counted on different schedules, and exactly one of its ten elements was exchanged rather than inherited: the Communion. **That one exchanged element is where the formulary’s single thrice-repeated substantive word, its single dropped word and its single contested adverb all stand**, and around it the propers state an absolute either/or without contempt for created goods.

### 1. The kingdom, said three times

*Regnum Dei* is rare in the Gospel that supplies it — four verses in Matthew against twenty-four carrying *regnum caelorum*. It stands verbatim in three appointed elements, and **no other exact form reaching three or more of the ten is anything but a divine name or a function word**. Negative and positive are one claim: *qui tália agunt, regnum Dei non consequéntur*; *Quǽrite ergo primum regnum Dei*; then the same imperative made an address at reception, *dicit Dóminus*.

**And the element that repeats the phrase is the element that cuts it.** *Iustítia* stands once in the whole appointed Latin, in the Gospel’s last verse — and the Communion, quoting that verse, drops it. A Communion drawn from the day’s own Gospel is ordinary practice, so on that side the spine may be two elements and an echo; the Epistle’s occurrence comes from another book and another pericope and stands on its own.

### 2. The flesh that is crucified is not the body that is fed

*Carn-* stands five times in the Epistle and nowhere else, *corp-* twice in the Gospel and nowhere else. **That makes the two lessons one unit, not a contrast**, and a reading setting a mortifying Epistle against a providing Gospel has inverted it. **The Introit’s cut keeps the division clean:** its psalm verse stops one clause before *cor meum et caro mea exsultaverunt in Deum vivum*, which would have given *caro* a positive sense.

**Four positions stand on what *caro* is at the Epistle’s v. 17.** **Chrysostom’s Greek denies desire to the flesh**: desire belongs to the soul, so the flesh here is the earthbound reasoning. **Marius Victorinus** grants the flesh motions of its own, arguing from the soulless elements. **The Glossa** makes it a manner of speaking about a joint act, *ex utroque enim fit*. **Ambrosiaster** shifts the ground to two laws, *unam Dei, alteram peccati*, so the crucified flesh is the world. **Chrysostom and Victorinus comment on the same verse and cannot both be right.** Jerome defends the missal’s own *vitia* against a reading of the body, *ut non naturam corporis videretur in spiritualibus viris negare, sed vitia*.

The disjunction holds of these two pericopes and is not a law of the two words. Ambrosiaster expounds a Latin text that is not the missal’s, so he speaks to the sense of *caro* and not to the count or the asymmetry of the two lists; and *luxúria*, *modéstia* and *cástitas* have no counterpart in the Greek these Fathers read, so none of them comments on those three words.

### 3. Divided at the images, and the most-repeated sentence withdrawn by the man who wrote it

**The Latin tradition is divided by name about whether the Gospel’s images may be figures.** Augustine bars allegorising these examples, **Christian of Stavelot** refuses allegory at the lilies on frankly aesthetic grounds, Jerome forbids reading the birds as angels, and Bruno of Segni reads them literally. At the far end **Godfrey of Admont** makes the birds good angels, the lilies evil spirits and Solomon Christ; **Hilary allegorises every image into doctrine of the risen body.**

**And the sentence the tradition repeats most often at this Gospel’s first verse was withdrawn by its author.** Augustine’s *Nullius enim fere conscientia Deum potest odisse* is retracted at *Retractationes* I.19, naming his own exposition of this verse; five later witnesses repeat it *without his qualifier* *fere*, the Glossa alone flagging the retraction; and **Bruno of Segni denies the proposition outright**, the covetous man serving idols and therefore hating God.

**At Mt. 6:33, which this Mass appoints twice, the argument narrows to a single adverb.** The Augustine the compilations transmit reads *primum* as an ordering of importance and not of time, *non témpore, sed dignitáte*; **Paschasius denies that it marks order at all.** Jerome and Hilary do not meet on this ground, Hilary’s angels being the *lilies* and Jerome’s prohibition falling on the birds; and Bruno of Segni restricts the whole prohibition of anxiety to apostles and those aiming at perfection while Godfrey preaches it to monks, so both address a narrower audience than a Sunday congregation.

### 4. Three chants stop short of one formula, and two divide the Fathers

*Beatus … qui sperat* stands in exactly two verses of the Clementine, and they are the two psalms this Mass takes its Offertory and its Introit from: *beatus vir qui sperat in eo* at Ps. 33:9b, *beatus homo qui sperat in te* at Ps. 83:13. It lies past both the Introit’s cuts and one clause past the Offertory’s, while **the Gradual alone is uncut and sings the hope vocabulary outright**, *sper-* and *confid-* twice each and in no other element. **The Introit belongs to that because the formula lies past its boundaries, not because it uses hope vocabulary — it does not.** These extents are the ones the chant tradition handed on.

Two chants carry documented divisions the Mass prints without adjudicating. **The Offertory’s angel has four accounts**: Augustine’s Christ, Cassiodorus’s moral sense, Arnobius’s angel of the sepulchre — **which turns on a repointing the missal’s Latin does not carry** — and Godfrey’s gift of grace. **The Gradual’s princes have four positions and a question**: Augustine’s good angels, Cassiodorus’s devil and good angel, **Theodoret’s earthly rulers, whose authority is temporary**, Godfrey’s interior and exterior senses — and **Bruno the Carthusian asks whether the verse is comparative at all**, answering both ways. **So the angel who is the Mass’s rescuer at one chant is its excluded object of trust at the other.** Peter Lombard puts the angelic reading under Augustine’s name and Prosper’s exposition is largely an Augustine epitome, so what looks like three witnesses to that reading may be one position with three carriers. The Alleluia is still the least integrated: Cassiodorus hears in *iubilémus* a wordless jubilus and **Theodoret the shout of victors** — **but every later reader of the psalm reads it penitentially, through a verse the Alleluia does not appoint.**

### 5. Three strands, one exchange, and the goods the Mass never asks for

**The strands were counted from different feasts.** The Gregorian Hadrianum heads this Mass *Dominica XV post Pentecosten*, one later than 1962; the Frankish Gelasian books stand two later. **The chants did not move with them**: the ninth-century antiphonary formulary carrying this Introit, Gradual and Offertory is numbered *Dominica XIV*, the number 1962 gives it. **And the readings never moved at all**: the *Liber Comitis* pairs this Epistle with this Gospel, cut at exactly the verses 1962 cuts. Two distinct mechanisms stand: Wilson’s numeration base, and **Schuster’s Roman cycle built around the feast of St Lawrence, whose count “was not everywhere the same.”** In Honorius **three of five chants are the ones 1962 sings the following Sunday** — the displacement Godfrey meets independently.

**The one exchange is at the Communion, and it broke a pair.** *Primum quaerite* is an ancient Roman antiphon standing at the *Ninth* Sunday in four ninth-century antiphonaries, where this Sunday’s Communion is *Panis quem ego dedero* — which travels with the Offertory *Immittet Angelus* at *both* its occurrences. **So the antiphon is secondary in this slot only and not in the repertory**, and the substitution separated two chants the older books kept together, confirming from a source he did not have Guéranger’s report that the Communion “was not the one primitively used.” The recasting itself — *Primum* promoted, *et iustítiam eius* dropped, *dicit Dóminus* added — is older than this book. **When it was made, no witness says.**

**What the Mass asks for and what it promises do not meet.** *Salut-* stands once each in the three orations and the Alleluia and in no other element. **No oration of any of the fifteen collated 1962 identities asks for food, drink or clothing**, so this Sunday’s silence about the three goods its Gospel names is a genre fact — though the season’s orations are not uniformly non-temporal, four of the fifteen asking for peace, health or the world’s ordering. **The singularity lies on the reading side:** the Fourteenth Sunday is the only one of the fifteen whose appointed texts name eating, drinking *and* clothing together. The three orations never command the assembly; **the one command the Mass repeats at reception is the promise’s own *Quǽrite*.** The four *salut-* words are one root in four senses — of a victim, of God, of things that save, and of a state.

## The Appointed Formulary in Full

Everything the 1962 *Missale Romanum* prints for this Mass, in the order it prints it: the Latin of the Vatican *editio typica* exactly as the controlling facsimile has it, each element under its own printed heading, scriptural reference and marginal number, and beneath it the English of an identified public-domain witness.

**Formulary.** *DOMINICA DECIMA QUARTA post Pentecosten*, *II classis*, printed pp. 394–396, marginal nos. 1572–1581. The formulary begins immediately after the Postcommunion of the Thirteenth Sunday (no. 1571) and ends immediately before the heading *DOMINICA DECIMA QUINTA post Pentecosten* (no. 1582). Nothing between those boundaries is omitted here. It appoints no Tract, Sequence, second oration, blessing or ritual text, and no second Collect, Secret or Postcommunion.

**The English below.** Scriptural elements are the Douay–Rheims as revised by Bishop Challoner (1749–1752) — the English of the Clementine Vulgate the missal prints — in the Project Gutenberg e-text of that revision. The three orations are the anonymous English of *The Roman Missal translated into the English language for the use of the laity*, first revised edition (Philadelphia: Eugene Cummiskey, 1861), from the three lines that book supplies for this formulary, reproduced with its own nineteenth-century spelling and its abbreviated conclusion “Thro’.” Wherever that English does not answer the missal’s Latin, the gap is marked at the element and left open.

### 1. Introit <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>

> ***Ant. ad Introitum* — Ps. 83, 10-11 — marginal no. 1572**
>
> *Protéctor noster, áspice, Deus, et réspice in fáciem Christi tui: quia mélior est dies una in átriis tuis super mília. <span class="nodecor">Ps. ibid., 2-3</span> Quam dilécta tabernácula tua, Dómine virtútum! concupíscit, et déficit ánima mea in átria Dómini. ℣. Glória Patri.*

> **English: Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Ps. 83:10–11 and 2–3 printed whole, with the portions the chant sings in bold**
>
> **v. 10** **Behold, O God our protector: and look on the face of thy Christ.** **v. 11** **For better is one day in thy courts above thousands.** I have chosen to be an abject in the house of my God, rather than to dwell in the tabernacles of sinners. *Ps.* **v. 2** **How lovely are thy tabernacles, O Lord of hosts!** **v. 3** **my soul longeth and fainteth for the courts of the Lord.** My heart and my flesh have rejoiced in the living God. ℣. *Glória Patri.*

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** The antiphon is a plain quotation, not a centonisation, but it stops in the middle of both its verses. It cuts v. 11 at *super mília*, immediately before the psalm’s own two-way choice between the house of God and the tents of sinners, and it cuts the psalm verse at *in átria Dómini*, before the heart and the flesh rejoice. The Douay renders both verses whole, so they are printed whole with the sung portion marked. The doxology cue *Glória Patri* belongs to the Ordinary and is not translated here.

### 2. Collect <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span>

> ***Oratio* — marginal no. 1573**
>
> *Custódi, Dómine, quǽsumus, Ecclésiam tuam propitiatióne perpétua: et quia sine te lábitur humána mortálitas; tuis semper auxíliis et abstrahátur a nóxiis, et ad salutária dirigátur. Per Dóminum.*

> **English: Cummiskey (Philadelphia, 1861), Collect of this formulary**
>
> Preserve, O Lord, we beseech thee, thy Church by thy constant mercy: and since our weak mortality is continually falling without thy assistance, may thy grace withdraw it from what is hurtful, and direct it in the ways of salvation. Thro’.

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** Two nouns go missing, and the commentary’s measurement depends on both. *Propitiatióne perpétua* becomes “by thy constant mercy,” so the substantive *propitiatio* — which this formulary alone of the fifteen collated in this repository uses twice — disappears from the English altogether; and *tuis semper auxíliis* becomes “may thy grace,” which drops both the plural of the noun and the adverb *semper* that the Postcommunion will use a second time.

### 3. Epistle <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>

> ***Léctio Epístolæ beáti Pauli Apóstoli ad Gálatas.* — Gal. 5, 16-24 — marginal no. 1574**
>
> *Fratres: Spíritu ambuláte, et desidéria carnis non perficiétis. Caro enim concupíscit advérsus spíritum, spíritus autem advérsus carnem: hæc enim sibi ínvicem adversántur, ut non quæcúmque vultis, illa faciátis. Quod si spíritu ducímini, non estis sub lege. Manifésta sunt autem ópera carnis, quæ sunt fornicátio, immundítia, impudicítia, luxúria, idolórum sérvitus, veneficia, inimicítiæ, contentiónes, æmulatiónes, iræ, rixæ, dissensiónes, sectæ, invídiæ, homicídia, ebrietátes, comessatiónes, et his simília: quæ prædíco vobis, sicut prædíxi: quóniam, qui tália agunt, regnum Dei non consequéntur. Fructus autem Spíritus est: cáritas, gáudium, pax, patiéntia, benígnitas, bónitas, longanímitas, mansuetúdo, fides, modéstia, continéntia, cástitas. Advérsus huiúsmodi non est lex. Qui autem sunt Christi, carnem suam crucifixérunt cum vítiis et concupiscéntiis.*

> **English: Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Gal. 5:16–24**
>
> \[*Fratres:*\] Walk in the spirit: and you shall not fulfill the lusts of the flesh. For the flesh lusteth against the spirit: and the spirit against the flesh: For these are contrary one to another: so that you do not the things that you would. But if you are led by the spirit, you are not under the law. Now the works of the flesh are manifest: which are fornication, uncleanness, immodesty, luxury, Idolatry, witchcrafts, enmities, contentions, emulations, wraths, quarrels, dissensions, sects, Envies, murders, drunkenness, revellings, and such like. Of the which I foretell you, as I have foretold to you, that they who do such things shall not obtain the kingdom of God. But the fruit of the Spirit is, charity, joy, peace, patience, benignity, goodness, longanimity, Mildness, faith, modesty, continency, chastity. Against such there is no law. And they that are Christ’s have crucified their flesh, with the vices and concupiscences.

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** The liturgical address *Fratres:* does not supplement Paul’s opening here; it *replaces* it. Paul’s own words at v. 16 are *Dico autem:*, which the Douay renders “I say then:” — the phrase that ties this verse back to the freedom of v. 13 and the law fulfilled in love at v. 14. Paul’s own vocative *fratres* stands at v. 13, inside the sentence the pericope drops. The missal’s address is the missal’s own and no registered English witness renders it, **so it stands bracketed above in the missal’s own Latin and untranslated**. Note also that the appointed Latin’s *homicídia*, which the Douay renders “murders,” stands in neither the Revised Version’s list nor in the critical-text English a modern reader is likely to be holding.

### 6. Gospel <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>

> ***✠ Sequéntia sancti Evangélii secúndum Matthǽum.* — Mt. 6, 24-33 — marginal no. 1577**
>

**Rubric as printed, immediately after the Gospel:** *Credo.* printed on a line of its own below the Gospel’s last line.

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** The incipit *In illo témpore: Dixit Iesus discípulis suis:* corresponds to nothing in Matthew and therefore to nothing in the Douay. Verse 24 begins bare, in the middle of the Sermon on the Mount, and the missal has to name a speaker and an audience to let the pericope stand alone. No registered English witness renders it, so **it stands bracketed above in the missal’s own Latin and untranslated**.

### 7. Offertory <span class="nodecor">(*Off.*)</span>

> ***Ant. ad Offertorium* — Ps. 33, 8-9 — marginal no. 1578**
>
> *Immíttet Angelus Dómini in circúitu timéntium eum, et erípiet eos: gustáte et vidéte, quóniam suávis est Dóminus.*

> **English: Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Ps. 33:8–9 printed whole, with the portion the chant sings in bold**
>
> **v. 8** **The angel of the Lord shall encamp round about them that fear him: and shall deliver them.** **v. 9** **O taste, and see that the Lord is sweet:** blessed is the man that hopeth in him.

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** The antiphon stops at *Dóminus* and does not sing v. 9b, “blessed is the man that hopeth in him.” The Douay renders the verse whole, so it is printed whole with the sung portion marked. The clause that is dropped here is the clause the Eighth Sunday’s Communion keeps.

### 8. Secret <span class="nodecor">(*Sec.*)</span>

> ***Secreta* — marginal no. 1579**
>
> *Concéde nobis, Dómine, quǽsumus, ut hæc hóstia salutáris, et nostrórum fiat purgátio delictórum, et tuæ propitiátio potestátis. Per Dóminum.*

> **English: Cummiskey (Philadelphia, 1861), Secret of this formulary**
>
> Grant, we beseech thee, O Lord, that this victim of salvation may both cleanse us from our sins, and render thy Majesty propitious to us. Thro’.

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** *Tuæ propitiátio potestátis* is turned from a noun into a verb and from power into majesty: “render thy Majesty propitious to us.” The Latin makes the victim itself the *propitiation of thy power*, parallel to its being the *purgation of our offences* — two genitives hung on one subject. The English is faithful to the sense and loses the figure, and the second occurrence of *propitiatio* in this formulary is invisible in it.

**Rubric as printed, immediately after the Secret:** *Præfatio de Ssma Trinitate.* the Preface of the Most Holy Trinity. *Ssma* is printed under a tilde abbreviation for *Sanctissima* and is expanded here from the same book’s own abbreviation practice.

### 9. Communion <span class="nodecor">(*Comm.*)</span>

> ***Ant. ad Communionem* — Mt. 6, 33 — marginal no. 1580**
>
> *Primum quǽrite regnum Dei, et ómnia adiciéntur vobis, dicit Dóminus.*

> **English: Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Mt. 6:33 printed whole and unaltered, with the phrases the antiphon reuses in bold**
>
> **Seek ye** therefore **first the kingdom of God,** and his justice, **and all these things shall be added unto you.**

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** The Communion is an adaptation of Mt. 6:33, not a quotation of it, and it is the one substantive recasting in this formulary. The Douay verse above is printed exactly as that edition has it, with nothing inserted. Four things happen: *ergo* is dropped and *Primum* promoted to the head of the clause; *et iustítiam eius* is omitted; *hæc* is dropped before *ómnia*; and *dicit Dóminus* is added, which stands nowhere in Matthew at either this verse or its Lucan parallel. The Douay verse is therefore printed whole with the reused phrases marked.

### 10. Postcommunion <span class="nodecor">(*Postcomm.*)</span>

> ***Postcommunio* — marginal no. 1581**
>
> *Puríficent semper et múniant tua sacraménta nos, Deus: et ad perpétuæ ducant salvatiónis efféctum. Per Dóminum nostrum.*

> **English: Cummiskey (Philadelphia, 1861), Postcommunion of this formulary**
>
> May these thy mysteries, O God, continually purify us and strengthen us: and procure us eternal salvation. Thro’.

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** *Ad perpétuæ … salvatiónis efféctum* becomes “eternal salvation,” so the noun *efféctum* vanishes, and with it the figure the Latin uses: the genitive is split around the verb, exactly as the Thirteenth Sunday’s Postcommunion split *redemptiónis ætérnæ … augméntum* around its own. *Semper* is likewise absorbed into “continually.”

## The Propers: Detailed Commentary

### The typical edition against the Benziger printing

The Vatican *editio typica* prints this formulary at pp. 394–396 under marginal nos. 1572–1581. The Benziger *editio iuxta typicam* of the same year prints the same ten elements and differs from it in twelve places — all of them accidentals of orthography, pointing, abbreviation and layout, and none of them a different word. The table carries all twelve, two of its rows taking a pair at once; the Collect stands there because over it the two books agree to the point, and the Epistle’s vice-list because the Benziger layer misrecognises its own book. What stands in the Benziger column is that edition’s uncorrected optical text layer and not a reading of the Benziger book.

Two of these are worth more than a footnote. The two conclusions run in opposite directions inside one formulary — the typical edition abbreviates the Secret’s and prints the Postcommunion’s at length, Benziger’s text layer does the reverse — which is a useful reminder that “the 1962 Latin” is not one artefact. And the typical edition abbreviates *Ant. ad Introitum* here where on the Thirteenth Sunday it spelled *Antiphona ad Introitum* out: the book is not uniform with itself two pages apart.

One mark on these pages is damaged, and it is not text. The Gospel’s marginal reference number, printed at the outer edge of the scanned block on p. 395, has lost its fourth digit to ink loss. It is 1577, because 1576 and 1578 are both legible and the series 1572–1581 is unbroken. That is resolved by sequence and not by the second witness, which prints no marginal numbers at all. Nothing in the appointed text depends on it.

#### Twenty-one places where the appointed text is not the Bible’s text

The consequential collation is against the Clementine Vulgate, the Latin behind the Douay–Rheims Challoner English printed with these propers. Twenty-one divergences stand between the two texts, and each stands at both. Most are orthography or pointing. The ones that change what is said are the two cuts at the Introit, the cut at the Offertory, the Alleluia’s dropped psalm title, the Epistle’s displaced incipit, and — the only substantive recasting in the formulary — the Communion.

Three elements depart from the Clementine in nothing beyond the table, and that is what throws the Communion into relief. The Gradual is the Clementine’s own wording exactly, both verses, with nothing added, dropped or transposed. The Epistle after its incipit matches word for word apart from the i/j orthography, the one capital at Gal. 5:18 and the pointing tabled above. The Gospel after its incipit matches word for word apart from the i/j orthography, the æ for œ at Mt. 6:30, the sentence capital at Mt. 6:32 and the pointing tabled above. Three further differences are the arithmetic of quoting two Clementine verses continuously rather than departures the missal makes: at Ps. 83:10–11 the antiphon runs *Christi tui: quia mélior* where the Clementine ends a sentence and capitalises *Quia*; at Ps. 83:2–3 it runs *virtútum! concupíscit* for the Clementine’s *virtutum ! Concupiscit*; and at Ps. 33:8–9 the Offertory runs *eos: gustáte* for the Clementine’s *eos. Gustate*. **The only appointed element of this formulary that departs from its Bible text substantively is the Communion** — and, as the Communion’s own treatment below shows, even that departure is inherited rather than made here.

**No *Psalterium Romanum*, Gallican or Vetus Latina witness stands behind any chant reading above.** At the Offertory and the Communion an older Latin form is the likeliest explanation of what the missal prints, and without such a witness it cannot be told from a scribal or editorial variant.

#### Psalm numbering, and the rules behind the orations and the colour

> **Which psalm numbering the parentheses give**
>
> At two of the four appointed psalms the Masoretic number is not the number a reader will find in a modern English Bible. Vulg. Ps. 83:10–11 is Masoretic 84:10–11 but English **84:9–10**; Vulg. Ps. 33:8–9 is Masoretic 34:8–9 but English **34:7–8**. Ps. 117 has no title and Ps. 94 folds its title into v. 1, so at the Gradual and the Alleluia the three series agree. The superscription is what divides them: the Vulgate numbering the missal cites and the Douay carries counts it with the psalm’s first verse, the Masoretic numbers it as a verse of its own, and the English convention of the King James leaves it unnumbered and so runs a verse behind wherever a psalm has one. No Hebrew psalter stands behind the Masoretic column, which follows the tracked editions’ declared conventions.

> **Which instrument abolished the seasonal orations, and which rule prescribes green**
>
> The second and third orations older missals print at this Mass were abolished not by the 1960 *Rubricae generales* but by *Variationes in Breviario et Missali Romano*, cap. IV, n. 18, *Orationes pro diversitate Temporum abolentur*, in *Acta Apostolicae Sedis* 52 (1960), p. 709 — an instrument the 1962 Missal does not reprint. Inside the code itself the same result follows for this Sunday from RG 434 b with RG 435. And a citation hazard runs through it: the 1960 promulgation contains two different provisions numbered 18, RG 18 on the transfer of impeded Sundays after the Epiphany and *Variationes* cap. IV n. 18, so a bare “n. 18” cited for this Sunday names neither of them unambiguously. Colour is governed not by a temporal-season rule of part three of the 1960 code but by **part one**, at **RG 127 b**: *Color viridis adhibetur in Officio et Missa de Tempore: … b) a feria II post dominicam I post Pentecosten, usque ad sabbatum ante Adventum*, particularising the general rule at RG 117. It was read on a page image of *AAS* 52 (1960) p. 619 and, without a single verbal difference, at *Missale Romanum* 1962, *Rubricae generales* p. XX.

### How this Mass came to be this Sunday’s

#### The three orations do not descend as a unit

A formulary’s prayers need not travel together, and these three do not.

The **Collect and Secret stand together in one Mass** in the Gelasianum Vetus — Wilson’s 1894 edition, Book III sect. X, *Item alia Missa*, printed pp. 229–230 — and that Mass stands in a series of Sunday Masses which the base manuscript, Vatican Reginensis 316, numbers *not at all*.

The **Postcommunion comes from somewhere else**. *Purificent semper* stands in a floating series of unassigned Postcommunions appended after the Canon (printed p. 237), in exactly the form the 1962 book prints, *nos, Deus*; and separately in a saint-adapted recension in the Vigil Mass of St Andrew (Book II sect. **LXVIII**, p. 207), reading *nos, Domine* and adding the apostle’s intercession. Which recension is prior is not settled by anything read.

> **Which section the Postcommunion’s saint-adapted recension stands in**
>
> The running head above Wilson’s printed p. 207 reads **LXIX**, and **that head names the section the page *reaches*, not the one it opens in.** On the page image sect. LXIX is *Item in natali eiusdem*, the Mass of St Andrew’s feast on 30 November, and it begins *below* the Postcommunion in question, which belongs to the preceding section — the Vigil Mass, **sect. LXVIII**. The same book sets the same trap at the Secret’s penitential home, where the running head above p. 257 stands over sect. **XLI**, which opens on p. 256.

> **What the Hadrianum’s printed section heading does with its numeral**
>
> The heading in Wilson’s 1915 volume reads *cXXXIII.*, where the *c* is a small raised footnote reference letter standing before the numeral and no part of it. The section is **XXXIII**: **the running section series XXVIIII–XLI across printed pp. 172–176 leaves no room for a section CXXXIII at all**, and an optical layer that fuses the reference letter with the numeral it stands before manufactures the extra hundred. A citation to **CXXXIII** finds nothing on Wilson’s pages.

The **Secret has a second, penitential home** in the same book, Book III sect. **XLI** (printed pp. 256–257), among votive and occasional material — a Mass whose collects beg God to turn his anger away from his people. **And Wilson’s own marginal narrows that claim materially.** The marginal at sect. XLI reads *R. S.(alibi.)* — Wilson’s notation that R and S have the prayer *elsewhere*, that is, at the Sunday Mass. So the penitential Mass *as R and S transmit it does not contain this Secret*; the second home is attested for the Vatican manuscript and for Menard, while the other two references point back to the single Sunday occurrence. **The Secret’s “second, penitential home” is therefore a feature of the Vatican manuscript’s arrangement and not of the Gelasian tradition at large.**

**None of the three orations stands in the Veronense.** They are absent from two uncorrected optical layers of Feltoe’s 1896 *Leonian Sacramentary*, which is a diplomatic edition printing the codex’s contractions; and **the Veronense has no ordered Sunday series at all** — it runs by month from April — so absence there is much weaker evidence about a Sunday formulary than absence from an ordered sacramentary would be. **But the Veronense does contain, twice, the prayer-shape the 1962 Postcommunion uses** — *Purificent* with holy things as subject and a second protective verb yoked to it, the closer reading *tua nos … sancta purificent, et ab externis erroribus perpetua virtute defendant* — which places the prayer’s *idiom*, though not the prayer, in the oldest Roman euchological stratum. **The likeness is one of diction and prayer-shape and not of derivation**, and it too rests on an uncorrected optical layer of Feltoe with no page image behind it. Nor do the three orations stand in Férotin’s *Liber mozarabicus sacramentorum*, Bannister’s *Missale Gothicum* or Lowe’s *Bobbio Missal* — **they belong to the Roman euchological stream and not, so far as six editions show, to the non-Roman Latin rites**, under the same optical-layer bound.

#### The Sunday number has moved, and it moved by a measured amount

**Sixteen consecutive sections, not eleven.** Gelasianum Book III sects. I–XI and XII–XVI correspond to the 1962 Fifth through Twentieth Sundays, and **the offset survives a documented interpolation and resumes a new constant rather than becoming noise**: sects. I–XI stand at *N* + 2 for Rheinau, S. Gallen, Gerbert *and Menard*, and at *N* + 1 for Pamelius; sects. XII–XVI stand at *N* + 4 for the first four while Pamelius stays at *N* + 1 without a break. **Menard is present at every readable section and always agrees with R/S/Gerbert, never with Pamelius** — so the two Gregorian editions do not agree with each other. **Wilson’s per-section wording varies in scope** — sect. IX excepts the Secret, sect. V the Postcommunion, sect. VI covers “the two Collects and the Secret,” sect. XII “the first Collect, Secret, and Postcommunion” — **so the offset is uniform while the set of prayers it covers is not.** Each section’s 1962 identity was fixed from its own printed collect or Secret and never from position, and the mapping checks against the tracked calendar: sect. XI prints the 1962 Fifteenth’s Collect and sect. IX the Thirteenth’s, with sect. X printing this Mass’s.

| **Number** | **Books that give it** |
|:--------------|:----------------------------------------------------|
| **Fourteenth** | the 1962 Missal |
| **Fifteenth** | Pamelius; and, independently, the Gregorian Hadrianum supplement |
| **Sixteenth** | Rheinau, S. Gallen, Gerbert, Menard |

**Two independent lines reach the same magnitude.** The supplement to the Gregorian Hadrianum numbers this Mass *DOMINICA .XV. POST PENTECOSTEN*, one Sunday later than 1962, and the offset holds across **eleven** consecutive Masses on the page images, *Dominica XI* through *Dominica XXI post Pentecosten*, each identified from its own printed collect rather than extrapolated. **Where it breaks:** at *Dominica XXII* the cues are the 1962 Seventh Sunday’s set and at *Dominica XXIII* the received Twenty-third’s, an offset of one, and **nobody has established why.** In Cod. Ottobonianus 313 the marginal chant cues stand two Sundays later than the orations beside them, and that offset too now stands on page images across the same run. **A single misreading by one editor could not produce that.** **The chant side is uneven in two systematic ways:** the match is on Introit, Gradual, Offertory and Communion, the bracketed Alleluia cues matching the 1962 assignment at none of the eleven; and where the count is three or two of four, the odd element is always a Communion or Gradual displaced by one Sunday inside the same stretch.

**Where the offset comes from, and Wilson states it himself.** His Introduction (printed pp. lxxi–lxxiii) places the start of Book III’s series against the first part of the Gregorian’s third book, which makes the offset **a difference in where each tradition begins counting — a numeration base — and not an interpolation**: he names the mechanism in a footnote, the *post Pentecosten* / *post Octavas Pentecostes* equivalence arising from a discrepancy between the first and supplementary portions of Muratori’s text. **Which single Sunday each tradition counts as its first is not established**, so the decomposition of the two units of offset is not settled here. The break at sect. XII is doubly sourced — Wilson’s Introduction and his footnotes agree that R, S and Gerbert interpolate the two September Ember-week Sundays and rejoin at their twentieth — which is exactly the arithmetic that moves the offset from two to four while Pamelius stays at one.

**One of the five books stands open at the locus, in optical text.** Gerbert prints at his printed p. 173, under the heading *Dominica XVI. post Pentecosten*, a five-item Mass whose Collect, Secret *and* Postcommunion are the three composed orations of this formulary, plus a second Collect and a proper Preface; the printed page number matches Wilson’s independent marginal citation “Gerb. 173”, and the five incipits match his Appendix tabulation item for item. **Two independently produced Internet Archive optical layers of that page agree verbatim**, neither a registered artifact of this repository, the registered e-rara excerpt stopping 123 pages short of p. 173; no page image was collated, and eighteenth-century long-s type is a hard case for recognition. **And the Gregorian Hadrianum carries all three orations as one Mass**: Wilson’s 1915 section XXXIII, *DOMINICA .XV. POST PENTECOSTEN*, printed pp. 173–174, gives the Collect *Custodi domine quaesumus*, the Super oblata *Concede nobis domine quaesumus*, and the Ad complendum *Purificent semper et muniant tua sacramenta nos deus, et ad perpetuae ducant salvationis effectum* — **the 1962 Postcommunion word for word**, and the next section opens with the 1962 Fifteenth’s Collect. **So in both traditions the whole 1962 oration set travels as a unit, at *N* + 1 in the Gregorian and *N* + 2 in the Frankish Gelasian**, and the narrow attestation of the Postcommunion is a feature of how the Vatican manuscript arranges its unassigned Sunday Masses, not of the tradition. **The unit that moved in the older books is a Mass-set**, three orations plus, in Gerbert, a proper Preface, of which only two are elements of the 1962 formulary: what travels in those books is a whole Mass, and what it amounts to in this one is two elements of ten.

**A near-miss at sect. X.** The bracketed Postcommunion there reads *Purificent nos, Domine, sacramenta quae sumpsimus, et a cunctis efficiant vitiis absolutos* — **a different prayer of the same incipit family**, which Wilson’s own index of incipits lists separately from this one, three printed occurrences each. **The two part company only past the incipit**, and the Vatican manuscript wrongly titles that prayer *Secreta*, an error of the scribe by Wilson’s note.

**“Four against one” is not four independent witnesses.** R and S are two Frankish “Gelasian” manuscripts Wilson collated himself, and they carry the sixteenth-Sunday heading directly. Gerbert’s principal manuscript “cannot now be traced,” and Wilson holds that the “Gelasian” portions of it, and so of Gerbert’s printed text, “do not represent the original text of the ‘Gelasian’ books, but a revision of that text, apparently based upon the text of the Sacramentaries of the ‘Gregorian’ type.” **So Gerbert does not merely fail to be a third Gelasian voice: it leans toward the very Gregorian editions supposed to corroborate it.** And Pamelius is itself an edition of the *Gregorian* book, so **Pamelius’s “fifteenth” and the Gregorian supplement’s “fifteenth” are not two independent witnesses to one Sunday**. The count is two collated Gelasian manuscripts, plus a printed edition largely dependent on a lost manuscript copied from one of them and revised toward the Gregorian type, plus one Gregorian edition — with the other Gregorian edition one Sunday earlier. **Gerbert’s own printed section heading, as two optical layers read it, agrees with what Wilson cites from him, which is as far toward excluding a mis-citation as an optical layer reaches; it adds no fourth independent voice, he being the least independent of the five.** R’s and S’s own Sunday headings are Wilson’s own collation and do not depend on Gerbert at all.

**And Gerbert’s unreliability does not weaken R and S**, whose Sunday headings Wilson collated himself. His “exceedingly confused and misleading” is hedged with “at times,” applies to Gerbert’s *handling and explanation of his materials* rather than to his text as such, and stands immediately after his warm tribute — “students of Liturgy owe so much as we owe to Gerbert.” Wilson also excepts “the latter part” of R from his criticism, and whether these Sunday Masses fall inside that part is not established, **so the unreliability cannot simply be assumed to reach them.**

**Wilson does not say the same thing about all five books.** Of R, S and Gerbert he writes that the prayers “**are assigned by**” them to the sixteenth — a statement about those books’ own Sunday headings. Of Pamelius and Menard he writes only “**see the *Missa* for** the … Sunday in,” which is a cross-reference to a *parallel* Mass and need not mean an identical composition. **The three verbs are three different strengths of claim.**

**And his apparatus is not a rumour**: it is a published critical collation, and for R and S specifically it is his own reading of manuscripts physically in front of him.

#### Honorius’s Fourteenth Sunday is not the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday

By the early twelfth century the 1962 Sunday number and this Introit are already paired. *Gemma animae* IV.72 is headed *Dominica decima quarta post Pentecosten « Protector noster, » sub lege*, and the quotation is exact: the neighbouring chapter headings in the same column print no *post Pentecosten* and the following one does.

**But the rest of the chapter shows the opposite half of the picture.** Within one and the same chapter Honorius’s **Introit, Collect, Epistle and Offertory are the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday’s**, while his **Gospel is the 1962 Thirteenth Sunday’s** — Luke 17:11–19, the ten lepers — and that Gospel offset is not a slip, since it holds across three consecutive Sundays. His **Gradual** (*Bonum est confiteri Domino*, Ps. 91) is not the 1962 Fourteenth’s (*Bonum est confidere in Domino*, Ps. 117:8–9); his **Alleluia** (*Quoniam Deus magnus*, Ps. 94:3) is a different verse of the same psalm the 1962 Alleluia uses; and his **Communion** is *Panis, quem ego dabo*.

**And his three differing chants are not three separate facts.** Set against the tracked 1962 registry — **a repository derivative and not the printed pp. 396–398** — his Gradual, Alleluia and Communion are **precisely the 1962 Fifteenth Sunday’s chant set**: within one chapter his chant strand stands one Sunday *later* than his oration strand while his Gospel stands one Sunday *earlier*, a spread of exactly two Sundays. **That converges in direction, though not in magnitude, with the Ottobonianus cues below**, where the chant set beside *Custodi, Domine* also runs later than the orations: **two independent witnesses in which the chants of this Mass run later than its orations, at +1 and +2 — they do not corroborate a single number.**

**And he reads this formulary three times over, not once.** *Gemma animae* IV.72 reads it *sub lege*, under the type of Ezechias sick to death; IV.73 reads it a second time *sub gratia*, under the type of Theodosius the Great, the council of his reign standing in the Epistle’s catalogue of vices and virtues and Ambrose’s penance behind the Communion; and IV.74 is not a typology at all but **a liturgical-calendar explanation**, tying the Offertory to the September night office’s *historia* of Tobias and Raphael and to Michaelmas, and then explaining the autumn Ember ordinations. **The paired *sub lege* / *sub gratia* structure is the book’s method and not a one-off**, chapters LXX and LXXI doing the same at the Thirteenth Sunday. **His *sub lege* reading is coherent only with the Gospel he has**, the grateful leper, so it does not transfer to the 1962 formulary. And Migne prints *plusquam octoginta millia* for the number the angel slew where 4 Kings 19:35 has a hundred and eighty-five thousand — on the page image and not an optical artefact, and nothing read explains it.

**And the printing misplaces three references, not two.** In Migne’s text *(Luc. XVII)* stands against the Gradual and *(Psal. XCI, 2)* against the Gospel — each is the other’s, since *Bonum est confiteri Domino* is Ps. 91:2 and the ten lepers is Luke 17 — and **a third reference, *(Psal. LXXXIII, 10)*, stands against the Collect sentence where it belongs to the Introit words quoted just before it.** **A reader taking any of the three at face value from this column will be misled.** His chapter stands in a rights-restricted facsimile, read on a rendered page image, hash-verified and retained nowhere, and **he is nowhere quoted**.

Honorius’s Ember week and the Masses Wilson reports as interpolated may or may not be the same disturbance; **the two witnesses number their Sundays differently**, and nothing read settles it.

**The compiler of the Gregorian supplement stays unnamed.** Wilson attributes it to Alcuin; the modern reattribution rests on scholarship no artifact here carries.

The Ottobonianus cue *b* reads *Bonum est confidere* where 1962 gives the Fifteenth Sunday *Bonum est confiteri Domino*. Manuscript reading, shared respond, or error: nothing read settles which.

**The Ottobonianus cue attachment stands at an offset of two.** The cue block whose Introit is *Protector noster* attaches to Wilson’s sect. **XXXI**, *DOMINICA .XIII. POST PENTECOSTEN*, whose Collect is the 1962 *Twelfth* Sunday’s — an offset of +2 and not zero. It is read at 300 dpi on the page, where **the superscript reference letters *a*, *b*, *c* stand immediately before the section numbers and the three lettered footnotes stand at the foot of the same page**, so an optical layer that fuses the letter with the numeral attaches the block to the wrong section and yields a zero offset. **That page image is an unregistered derivative of the same Internet Archive item as the registered optical layer — the same digitisation read better, and not a second witness to the printed book.** At either attachment, the cue set whose Introit is *Protector noster* — this Mass’s own — has Communion *Panis quem ego* and not *Primum quaerite*.

#### Schuster’s three pages: an ancient title, a mechanism, and four absences

Schuster’s *The Sacramentary* vol. III, pp. 136–138, was read on page images of the registered artifact, hashed and matched before reading. **He gives the formulary an ancient title:** *Quarta post Sancti Laurentii*, the fourth Sunday after St Lawrence. **He gives the unstable numbering a mechanism that is not Wilson’s:** the post-Pentecost series was interrupted at Rome by a cycle counted from St Lawrence, and “the sequence of these Sundays after St Lawrence was not everywhere the same, for, whilst the Roman documents of the Frankish period usually reckon five Sundays after the natalis of the Staurophoros, the Calendar of Fronteau has only four”; the Lawrence cycle was succeeded at Rome by cycles around St Cyprian and then St Michael, these feasts serving “as so many milestones … and had no special connection with the saint whose name they bore.” **The two mechanisms stand side by side**: neither has been shown to produce the offsets Wilson collates, and the arithmetic joining them has not been done. **And his own title is a fourth reckoning**: the *Liber Comitis* calls this Sunday the *fifth* after St Lawrence where Schuster calls it the fourth — **an instance of the variation he himself describes, and not an error in either.**

He notes a progressive Introit-psalm series across these Sundays, two Lenten cross-references for the chants, and comments continuously on all three orations, at more length than Guéranger. **He reads the Secret’s *tuae propitiatio potestatis* as the appeasing of the divine omnipotence**, where Guéranger and this guide’s collation arrive independently; and **his Communion paragraph supplies the *et iustitiam eius* the antiphon drops**, glossing the justice from the Gospel verse rather than from the antiphon.

**Four absences on those same pages, and they bear on whether he corroborates the displacement independently.** **He names no station church for this Sunday** — where his next page does name one for the Fifteenth — **no sacramentary siglum, no manuscript, and no note that the Communion antiphon is secondary.** **On these pages he does not corroborate the Gelasian and Gregorian displacement**, which therefore still rests on Wilson’s apparatus and Gerbert. Those are absences from pp. 136–138; the volume’s introduction and its index of sacramentary references stand unread. **His paraphrases are English and are not text witnesses: the appointed Latin is nowhere quoted from him.**

#### The chants and the readings: the two strands that did not move with the orations

**The chant books number this formulary as 1962 numbers it.** The ninth-century antiphonary formulary carrying this Introit, Gradual and Offertory stands as *Dominica XIV*, and the Ottobonianus margin reproduces those formularies **one number above** the sacramentary headings they stand against — twenty-five of twenty-five slots across five consecutive Masses, **the bracketed Alleluia cues matching nothing in 1962.** **And the Communion of that formulary is not this one:** *Primum quaerite* stands at the *Ninth* Sunday in four of those books, while the Fourteenth Sunday’s Communion is *Panis quem ego dedero* — which **travels with the Offertory *Immittet Angelus* at both its occurrences**, the Thursday of the third week of Lent and this Sunday. **So the exchange at the Communion separated two chants the older books kept together**, and the antiphon it brought in is an ancient Roman antiphon of another slot rather than a late composition. **That antiphonary evidence is a modern database of Hesbert’s *Antiphonale Missarum Sextuplex* and not Hesbert’s printed pages**, which stand unopened here.

**The readings, alone of the three strands, never moved.** The *Liber Comitis* as Migne prints it pairs *Fratres, spiritu ambulate, usque concupiscentiis* with *Nemo potest duobus dominis servire, usque adiicientur vobis* under *Dominica v post sancti Laurentii* — **the same two incipits and the same two termini the 1962 book prints**, so the Epistle–Gospel pair is not among the things that moved, and the pericopes were already cut where 1962 cuts them. **The Comes’s own date and provenance are not established here**, and the list was read on page images of an unregistered derivative of the same Internet Archive item as the registered optical layer — that digitisation read better, and not a second witness.

### Introit <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>

*Protéctor noster, áspice, Deus* *Ant. ad Introitum* — Ps. 83, 10-11, with *Ps. ibid.,* 2-3 marginal no. 1572

**Psalm 83 is a psalm of the sons of Core about desire for the sanctuary**: the sparrow and the turtle-dove finding a house at the altars (v. 4), those who dwell in the house (v. 5), the ascents in the heart through the vale of tears (vv. 6–7), *ibunt de virtute in virtutem* (v. 8). **What those verses support is movement toward the temple.** **The text does not state that the psalm is *for* pilgrims or that it voices their sentiments.**

**What the chant does to it, and the two cuts have opposite effects.** It sings the psalm backwards: the antiphon from the end, vv. 10–11a, the psalm verse from the beginning, vv. 2–3a. Cutting v. 11 removes the psalm’s explicit two-way choice between the house of God and the tents of sinners. Cutting v. 3 removes the rejoicing of heart and flesh, *cor meum et caro mea exsultaverunt in Deum vivum* — **the only place in the formulary’s psalm sources where *caro* carries a positive sense**, which bears directly on the Epistle’s use of the word. **Both dropped clauses are the psalm’s, not the antiphon’s, and the antiphon asserts neither.**

**The capital is the printer’s.** The missal prints *fáciem Christi tui* where the tracked Clementine prints lower-case *christi*. **Capitalisation in a modern electronic Clementine is that e-text’s editorial convention and capitalisation in the 1962 missal is that book’s; neither is evidence of what the Latin intends**, and in the psalm’s own sense *christus* is the anointed king. The christological reading rests on the Fathers, who do argue it, and never on the printer.

> **Augustine, *Enarratio in Ps. LXXXIII* §6 and §14.** **He reads the two halves as one movement of desire.** On the psalm verse, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English: “My soul longs and fails for the courts of the Lord. It is not enough that it longs and fails: for what does it fail? For the courts of the Lord.” On the antiphon: “one day there is better than a thousand days. Men long for thousands of days … let them long for one day, which has neither rising nor setting.” His Latin runs: *contemnant millia dierum, desiderent unum diem, qui non habet ortum et occasum; unum diem, diem sempiternum, cui non cedit hesternus, quem non urget crastinus.* **So on his reading the antiphon is the answer to its own verse.**

**The Father’s unit and the liturgy’s unit do not coincide.** Augustine’s §15 expounds the *rest* of Ps. 83:11 — in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series, “I have chosen to be cast away in the house of the Lord” — which is exactly the clause the Introit cuts off after *super mília*: he comments on the psalm, not on the antiphon as the missal bounds it. And his exposition speaks of *millia dierum*, thousands of *days*, which invites the conclusion that his psalter read a fuller text. **It did not** — his printed lemma is the missal’s Introit verbatim, and *millia dierum* is his *gloss*, supplying the noun the verse elides.

**Six readings stand on *réspice in fáciem Christi tui*, and they do not stack:** Augustine’s, Guéranger’s, Arnobius’s, Godfrey’s, Theodoret’s and Cassiodorus’s.

> **Augustine, on *réspice in fáciem Christi tui*.** **He refuses the obvious reading**, in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series: “For when does God not look upon the face of His Christ? … Cause Your Christ to become known to all.” His Latin runs: *Fac innotescere omnibus Christum tuum … notus sit omnibus Christus tuus, ut possimus ire a virtutibus in virtutem, ut possit superabundare gratia, quoniam abundavit peccatum.* **The argument is a grammatical one about *facies*, and it survives translation.** The reasoning is driven by Rom. 5:20 and by the same psalm’s v. 8, **neither of which is in the appointed extract**: the move is Augustine’s on the whole psalm, applied to the verse the missal took.

**Guéranger reads the same verse differently, and the two do not reconcile by stacking.** Where Augustine turns the petition outward and missionary, Guéranger turns it inward and nuptial — in **Dom Laurence Shepherd’s** English, the Duffy 1900 edition of *The Liturgical Year* vol. XI: “the Church, in presenting her petitions to the Most High, begs of Him to look on the face of His Christ, who is also hers,” grounded on 1 Cor. 11:7, 11:3 and Eph. 5:23. Guéranger is nineteenth-century and not patristic; his standing is that of a recognised authority on the Roman liturgy, and **his voice does not stand in for the tradition’s.**

> **Arnobius the Younger (*attributed*), *Commentarii in Psalmos*, in Ps. LXXXIII, PL 53:446C–447D.** **His reading of *réspice in fáciem Christi tui* is none of the others:** God looking on the face of his Christ is God seeing the man seated at his own right hand — *Cum respicit in faciem Christi, et hominem viderit sedentem ad dexteram suam* — and the day better than thousands follows from that man, *per quem melior dies una, quia aeterna erit, et noctem non habebit*, with *millia* glossed as years consumed on earth. **His agreement with Augustine and Cassiodorus about the everlasting day is reached by a different route — through Christ rather than through the day’s own properties — and his *millia* differs from Cassiodorus’s, so his is agreement reached separately and not a further vote for their account.** He also reads the psalm’s opening allegorically: the sparrow is Christ, the turtle-dove the Church, the altars *mysteria tua*, and the house of God *non cemento constat et calce, sed in homine credente*. **The ascription of these commentaries is the traditional one, and the transcription also carries them as *Auctor incertus*.**

> **Godfrey of Admont, *Homilia* LXXXII, *In Dominicam XIV post Pentecosten prima*, PL 174:575A–580C.** **He alone makes the anointed the congregation:** *Petimus itaque in Introitu missae ut ille, qui protector noster est, Christum suum nobis dando, semper nos unctos suos respiciat*; the courts are *in corporibus et in animabus nostris, quae per atria Domini possunt intelligi*; and *melior est una dies cognitionis et illuminationis eius super millia, id est antecellit et superexcellit universa opera et merita nostra*. **He quotes the Introit as sung** — *Sic enim cantamus* — **and the psalm verse with it.** **His Fourteenth Sunday is not the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday.** His Gospel is the ten lepers of Luke 17 and his Communion is *Panis, quem ego dedero*; the 1962 Gospel is his **Fifteenth**. **So the chants and the Collect he expounds are this formulary’s and the Mass he expounds them in is not** — which is the displacement Honorius shows, met independently in a different author.

**Three Roman texts open on the same words, and they stand in three different relations to Ps. 83.** The incipit *Protector noster aspice, Deus* opens **both** this Mass’s Introit and the Fifth Sunday after Pentecost’s Gradual, with different continuations — *in faciem Christi tui* against *super servos tuos* — so the two part company only past the incipit. In the searched Missal the collision is wider still: the same words open a verbatim psalm Introit, an adapted Gradual at four occurrences, **and a composed collect that quotes no psalm at all** (*Protector noster aspice, Deus: ut, qui malorum nostrorum pondere premimur…*). **This Introit quotes the psalm verbatim, the Fifth Sunday’s Gradual adapts it, and the collect only echoes it**: a loose echo in a composed oration is not a quotation of the psalm at all.

**Theodoret’s Greek at PG 80:1541–1545 reads *réspice in fáciem Christi tui* ecclesially**, the face of Christ being the saved people, on 1 Cor. 12:27 — *hymeis de este somata Christou*, in transliteration. **That reading does not stack with the others**, and it removes the ground for treating the inward-ecclesial reading as a nineteenth-century departure from the Fathers — though it stands on a different Pauline text and without the nuptial figure, so it corroborates neither Augustine nor Guéranger.

**Cassiodorus reads the psalm in Latin.** On *réspice* he refuses the surface reading exactly as Augustine does and names the figure: *Haec figura dicitur hypallage, Latine permutatio, quoties dicitur respici magis ille qui respicit* — the one said to be looked upon is rather the one who looks — and the petition accordingly turns outward to the gentiles, with Ps. 13:2 and Ps. 5:2 as his parallels. And on this psalm’s cut clause *cor meum et caro mea* he comments at v. 3b, **the clause the missal cuts**: a second witness that *caro* here is positive, outside the appointed extract. **At the Introit he is corroboration and probably dependence on Augustine, not a second independent vote.** And **no qualifying cultural afterlife of the Introit’s wording stands in the corpora searched**: every use confirmed in the optical text is straight devotional or liturgical reception.

### Collect <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span>

*Custódi, Dómine, quǽsumus, Ecclésiam tuam* *Oratio* marginal no. 1573

**The pointing is load-bearing.** The semicolon after *mortálitas* is a semicolon and not a comma, and it divides the prayer into its two real parts: a causal clause, *quia sine te lábitur humána mortálitas*, and the double petition that clause grounds. The two limbs of the petition are yoked by an *et … et* straddling *abstrahátur* and *dirigátur*, and the first *et* sits before *abstrahátur* rather than before *tuis*, which is what makes the pair read as a pair. By the same *auxíliis* the Church is drawn back from what harms and steered toward what saves: withdrawal and direction, one instrument.

**Its object is the Church, and its diagnosis is mortality.** Not sin, not the world, not the enemy: what the prayer says is wrong is that human mortality slips when God is not holding it. **The Church is named here and nowhere else in the ten elements** — though the first-person plurals of Introit, Alleluia, Secret and Postcommunion are corporate in their own way, which softens any contrast built on that count. The formulary’s only other words for sin are this prayer’s *nóxiis* and the Secret’s *delictórum*; *peccatum* occurs nowhere in the ten elements at all.

**This prayer is received three ways, and they are three kinds of reception rather than three votes on a meaning.** **Honorius** cites the prayer by incipit in *Gemma animae* IV. **Godfrey of Admont** glosses it word by word inside his homily on this Mass, by interpolation: *quia sine te labitur humana mortalitas in elatione, tuis semper auxiliis abstrahatur a noxiis superbiae et extollentiae mentis, et ad salutaria, vere humilitatis bona, te ducente dirigatur*. And **Schuster** comments on the prayer continuously, at more length than Guéranger. Godfrey carries the bound recorded at the Introit — his Mass is not this one.

**Alcuin and Wibald of Corvey both have this Collect’s clauses in their own texts.** An exact-phrase index over the *Patrologia Latina* returns them inside a prayer of Alcuin, *De usu Psalmorum* VII, and inside a letter of Wibald of Corvey to Eugenius III, *Epistola* CCCLXXV. **PL 101 and PL 189 stand unopened at both places**, so what either writer makes of the clauses is not known here.

Guéranger makes one interpretive move on this Collect: that the abyss of vice the Church’s children would fall into without help is specifically the Epistle’s catalogue — a cross-element reading. **But it is an association he asserts in one sentence rather than argues, with no witness behind it.** His remarks on the Secret and the Postcommunion are one sentence each and are paraphrase rather than exegesis.

**What the English witness does not carry.** The Cummiskey rendering turns *propitiatióne perpétua* into “by thy constant mercy” and *tuis semper auxíliis* into “may thy grace,” so both the noun *propitiatio* and the adverb *semper* disappear from the English, and the measurement in the synthesis is accordingly made on the Latin. The English itself stands on the printed page: this Collect was read at printed p. 425 of the registered 1861 scan, whose bytes were hash-matched before a page was opened, and it reads there word for word and point for point as it is printed here, as do the Secret and the Postcommunion at p. 427. That is one digitisation of the 1861 book and not a collation of two copies, and who made the translation is not established, so the edition is named and no translator is.

### Epistle <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>

*Fratres: Spíritu ambuláte* *Léctio Epístolæ beáti Pauli Apóstoli ad Gálatas* — Gal. 5, 16-24 marginal no. 1574

#### Where the lesson is cut from, and what the cut costs

The pericope **replaces** Paul’s own connective. Clementine Gal. 5:16 reads *Dico autem: Spiritu ambulate*, and the appointed Epistle reads *Fratres: Spíritu ambuláte*. *Dico autem* ties v. 16 back to the liberty of v. 13 — where Paul’s own vocative *fratres* stands, inside the sentence the pericope drops — and to the law fulfilled in love at v. 14. **The missal’s *Fratres* displaces the connective rather than supplementing it**, and supplies at v. 16 an address the apostle had used three verses earlier.

It also ends two verses before the unit’s own closing inclusio. Verse 25’s *Si Spiritu vivimus, Spiritu et ambulemus* returns v. 16’s verb in the hortatory subjunctive — and **in Greek the two verbs differ**, so the inclusio is tighter in the Latin than in the Greek, which is a fact about the Vulgate’s rendering. **The appointed excerpt therefore ends on crucifixion rather than on walking**, and v. 25 is the incipit of the *next* Sunday’s lesson, printed on the facing pages of the same book.

#### Two catalogues that are not built alike

*Ópera carnis* is plural with a plural verb; *fructus Spíritus* is singular with a singular verb, and **the asymmetry stands in the Greek as well**, so it is not a Latin artefact. The vice list is left explicitly open (*et his simília*) and the fruit list is closed with a verdict (*Advérsus huiúsmodi non est lex*).

**The list lengths, and their mechanism.** Latin: seventeen vices, twelve fruits. Tracked Byzantine Greek: seventeen vices, nine fruits. The critical text its apparatus records: fifteen and nine. Three precise consequences follow.

1.  **The Latin fruit list exceeds every tracked Greek form by exactly three.** The pair *patiéntia*/*longanímitas* answers the single Greek word for long-suffering — **Jerome says so himself**, as he says that *continéntia* renders *enkrateia* — and *modéstia* and *cástitas* answer nothing in either the Byzantine text or its apparatus. **Any argument built on the number twelve is an argument about the liturgical Latin.**

2.  ***Homicídia* stands in neither a Revised Version nor a modern critical-text English**, because the Byzantine text has the word and the apparatus marks it absent from the critical text — so **the reason is a Byzantine-against-critical divergence and not a peculiarity of the Vulgate.**

3.  **But the Vulgate’s list is not simply the Byzantine list either:** the Byzantine word for adultery has no Latin counterpart, so the Clementine sides with the critical text at that word and with the Byzantine text at “murders.”

The word-by-word alignment of *luxúria*/*impudicítia* against their Greek neighbours is a judgement about renderings and not a count, and it stands more loosely than the counts do. Every critical-text reading named here stands at one remove from a critical edition, none being registered in this repository: the readings come from the inline apparatus of the tracked Greek files.

#### What the commentators actually argue

> **John Chrysostom, *Commentarius in epistolam ad Galatas* cap. V, verse by verse.** **His governing move survives the Greek, and is sharper there:** *horas hoti ou phesi ten sarka entautha, alla ton geode logismon kai chamai syromenon* — **the object is *sarx* and the contrast term *logismos***, an earthly cast of mind dragged along the ground, **not *soma***; NPNF’s “by the flesh, he does not mean the body” is a paraphrase that does not misrepresent the sense, but **the Greek makes a sharper claim about the word the Epistle actually uses**, and it is the Greek that bears on the appointed *caro*. Proved from the vice-list itself, which the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English gives as “hatred, variance, emulations, strife, heresies, and witchcraft … arise merely from a depraved moral choice.” **His argument from the verb holds in the appointed Latin:** “If these things belonged to nature and not to a bad moral choice, his expression, *they practise*, is inappropriate” — and the missal prints *qui tália agunt*. **The Greek confirms two wordings of the appointed Latin against rivals:** his *Ouk eipe, to ergon tou pneumatos, all’, Ho karpos* is verbatim NPNF’s “He says not, the work of the Spirit, but, the fruit,” and his lemma at the list’s close is *Qui autem sunt Christi* — **the missal’s reading, against Augustine’s *Qui sunt in Christo Iesu***, so on this clause the appointed Latin is the better witness to Paul. **And at the lesson’s last verse he insists the crucifixion is not the destruction of the flesh**, *Ou gar de ten sarka aneilon: epei pos emellon zen?* — exact discipline — and **no other witness read here states so directly that the appointed v. 24 is not ascetical.** **No other exposition follows the appointed Epistle verse by verse as this one does**, with lemmata at vv. 16, 17, 18, 19–21, 22 and 24 and continuing to v. 25, where the missal’s pericope stops.

**Chrysostom’s Greek on the first half of the pericope** (PG 61:670–673) reads *Sarka gar entautha, ou to soma kalei*: flesh here is the earthbound, slothful, neglected reasoning; **desire belongs to the soul, not to flesh, so a flesh that *desires* cannot be the body**; the body is an instrument, *organon gar estin he sarx*, and *we hate and punish not the iron but the murderer*; the conflict is between two reasonings, virtue and vice, *ouch he psyche kai to soma*. **And at v. 18 he calls the dismissal of the law a great and wonderful praise of it**, set aside not as bad but as less than the philosophy given by the Spirit. **The opponents he answers are unnamed in the text.**

**Chrysostom’s own Greek vice lemma has seventeen items and includes both *pharmakeia* and *phonoi*, so he speaks to *veneficia* and to *homicidia***; *luxúria*, *modéstia* and *cástitas* have no counterpart in the text he expounds, and he says nothing of them. His nine-item fruit lemma is the Greek’s, in the same order Augustine’s and Jerome’s lemmata carry.

> **Ambrosiaster, *Commentaria in Epistolam ad Galatas*, PL 17:367D–368D.** **His reading is governed by two laws rather than by two parts of man.** At v. 17, *Duas leges proponit … unam Dei, alteram peccati*, with *medius homo* standing between them; at vv. 19–21 the works are called the flesh’s by origin, *quia errores isti ex mundo sunt, ex quo et caro*; and at v. 24 *qui sunt Christi* is glossed *christos, id est, unctos* while the crucified flesh is the world, *Hi carnem, id est, mundum crucifigunt*, proved from 1 Jn. 2:15. **His lemma is not the missal’s Vulgate.** His fruit-list has **ten** members, carrying *spes* and *castitas* while lacking *fides* and *patientia*, and his vice-list opens *adulterium, fornicatio, impuritas, obscenitas* where the missal opens *fornicátio, immundítia, impudicítia, luxúria*. **So his exposition counts a different pair of lists, and the missal’s seventeen and twelve are not the lists he expounds.**

> **Marius Victorinus, *In epistolam Pauli ad Galatas*, PL 8:1192B–D.** **He is half a negative.** His text as Migne prints it passes straight from v. 17 to 6:1, so **he comments on none of vv. 18–24.** What he does say at v. 17 is the exact contrary of Chrysostom’s move: *Habet enim motus suos caro, habetque sensus, neque tantum ab anima suscitantur*, argued from water, fire, earth and the other elements, which have their own impulses and no soul at all. **The two comment on the same appointed verse and cannot both be right.** **The lacuna is in the commentary as Migne prints it**, the text standing continuous across the join; whether a critical edition shows the same gap is unchecked, so it is a gap in this printing and not established as Victorinus’s own.

> **Glossa ordinaria on Galatians, PL 114:584B–585B.** **A third position on the clause Chrysostom and Victorinus divide over:** the flesh is *said* to desire by the same idiom that says the ear hears and the eye sees — *Caro dicta est concupiscere, quia hoc secundum ipsam agit anima* — and the cause is in neither alone, *ex utroque enim fit, quia sine utroque delectatio nulla sentitur*. It reads *non perficietis* precisely (*non ait non feceritis … sed non perficietis, id est non opera eorum consensu voluntatis implebitis*), defines being under the law by fear rather than love, and **organises the fruit list by making charity its head from which the members follow**, *Caput praemisit, unde membra sequuntur*. **It is a compilation, filed in the transcription under *Anselmus Laudunensis et schola*, which is the modern attribution of the redaction and not a claim that Anselm wrote these sentences; no sentence in it stands under a named Father’s name.**

> **Augustine, *Expositio epistolae ad Galatas* ss. 45–53, continuous on the whole appointed pericope.** **His own lemma differs from the missal’s at every load-bearing point: thirteen vices** — *animositates* for the missal’s *irae*, *haereses* for *sectae* — **and nine fruits**, and at the exclusion *regnum Dei non possidebunt* where the missal prints *non consequentur*. **So his systematic opposition of the two lists** — *Recte igitur fornicationi opponitur caritas*, point for point — **cannot be mapped onto the missal’s seventeen and twelve without distortion**. **On the counts he refuses the argument altogether**: *Non enim hoc suscepit, ut doceret, quot sint, sed in quo genere* — and he reads *both* lists as open, grounding it on the missal’s own word, *Non enim ait: Adversus haec non est lex, sed: Adversus huiusmodi*. **On v. 16 he makes the argument turn on the mood of the verb**: Paul said not that you shall not *have* the desires of the flesh but that you shall not *fulfil* them — and though his lemma reads *concupiscentias carnis ne perfeceritis*, the argument survives into the missal’s *non perficietis*, which is still about fulfilling. **On v. 17 he denies that the verse removes free choice**, the verse being addressed to those who will not hold the grace of faith — an anti-Pelagian-shaped argument drawing on Rom. 5–8, none of it appointed. At the connective the pericope cuts he reads *Spiritu ambulate* as the answer to v. 15’s biting and devouring, naming *humilitas et mansuetudo* the first and great gift of the Spirit — **and *mansuetúdo* is in the appointed fruit list, so that link the appointed text can carry**. At the last verse he grounds the crucifixion of the flesh in **chaste fear**, the fear of the chaste wife against the adulteress’s, through Ps. 118:120.

> **Jerome, *Commentarii in epistolam ad Galatas* lib. III, ad 5:16–24.** **He rules on the missal’s list count in his own voice:** *Sed sciendum non plus quam quindecim carnis opera nominata* — fifteen vices, with *adulterium*, *impudicitia* and *homicidia* recorded as additions found *in Latinis codicibus*, and the items numbered in his own text — **so the 1962 Epistle prints two of the three he rejects, in a list of seventeen**. The bound: this is Jerome on the Latin codices he knew about 386–388, not a judgement about the Clementine’s descent, and nothing is said about how the 1962 text got its count. **On the missal’s own word *vitiis* he is its defender, not its corrector:** *Ubi Latinus interpres vitia posuit, in Graeco pathemata, id est, passiones leguntur*, and *desideria* was added *ut non naturam corporis videretur in spiritualibus viris negare, sed vitia* — **which also explains Augustine’s divergent lemma *passionibus***: two renderings of one Greek noun, not two different texts. **His philology of the vice-list is Greek-anchored and checkable**: *aemulatio* = *zelos*; *rixae* renders *eritheia* and not *mache*; *haeresis* from *helesthai*, choosing. **He distinguishes *modestia* from *continentia*** — the former belonging to the perfect, the latter to those still on the way — **which is not the medieval *continentia*/*castitas* pair** Anthony and Aquinas share, but a different distinction. On v. 17 he answers the free-choice objection by a **tripartite anthropology**, the soul standing in the middle between flesh and spirit — **the same difficulty Chrysostom raises in Greek and the same answer, a convergence with nothing showing dependence**. On v. 18 he tells a Latin reader that ***spiritus* there stands without the article and so is the Holy Spirit**, a distinction Greek marks and Latin cannot — *qui arthra penitus non habemus*; the 1962 book’s lower-case *spiritu* at v. 18 and capital *Spiritus* at v. 22 are the printers making visible what he says the language cannot. **He transmits Origen verbatim and partly withdraws from him**: at v. 24 he records Origen construing *Christi* with *carnem* — those without law crucified the flesh *of Christ* — and declines it, *ut non carnem Christi, sed suam eos crucifixisse dicamus*; and on the fruit’s being unnamed he transmits Origen’s reading that the works are *manifest* and the fruit not, so it must be sought with labour, while saying at 5:19–21 that on reflection the named vices seem to him plain sense. **Origen stands here only through Jerome, a checked report of an unchecked source.** **And on the clause the list closes with, he and Augustine converge on one proof-text**, 1 Tim. 1:8–10, that the law is not laid down for the just — **the firmest reception *Adversus huiúsmodi non est lex* has here, and convergence rather than dependence**.

**Six accounts of the two lists’ asymmetry stand, and they are six.** The textual reading: the vice list is left open (*et his simília*) and the fruit list closed with a verdict. Augustine, against the argument from counts altogether, reads *both* lists as open on the missal’s own *huiúsmodi* — pointing the opposite way. Origen through Jerome: the works are called *manifest* and the fruit not, so the fruit must be sought with labour. Chrysostom: evil works come from us alone, while the good need God’s kindness too — **and Jerome’s own reason differs from his, vices ending in themselves where virtues sprout and overflow, the Greek account being about grace and the Latin about fecundity.** **The Glossa alone gives the list a structure**, and it answers the question the others leave open: charity is the head from which the members follow, so the fruit list is not a list of coordinates at all. **And Ambrosiaster grounds *fructus* rather than *opera* in the law of the spirit that fructifies them** — a reason different again from Chrysostom’s and from Jerome’s — **but his own lists are not the missal’s, so his reason stands while his count is of other lists.**

> **Thomas Aquinas, *Super Galatas* cap. 5 lect. 5 and 6.** **He expounds the missal’s own lists, and is the only witness read here that does.** He orders the twelve fruits by what each perfects — interior, exterior, what is above us, what is below us — and reads the number as answering Apoc. 22:2, *ex utraque parte lignum vitae afferens fructus duodecim*; **the Apocalypse link is his own move.** He groups the seventeen vices by object, derives *veneficia* from *venenum*, and reads *homicidia* *cordis et operis*, of the heart as well as of the deed, citing 1 Jn. 4. **He raises the objection Chrysostom raises — that several listed vices are plainly not bodily — and answers it by a different route:** Augustine’s definition at *De civitate Dei* XIV.2, that whoever lives according to himself lives according to the flesh, so that *caro* here stands for the whole man. Augustine’s own wording at *De civitate Dei* XIV.2 stands unopened here, and what is given above is Aquinas’s report of it.

> **Anthony of Padua, *Sermo Dominica XIV post Pentecosten*.** **He left a sermon headed for this very Sunday, and it is partly a false friend.** He states his own Mass’s texts explicitly: his Introit is *Inclina, Domine, aurem tuam ad me* — the 1962 **Fifteenth** Sunday’s Introit — and his Gospel is Luke 17:11–19, the ten lepers. His whole sermon is built on leprosy and cleansing, and his stated reason for pairing Epistle with Gospel (*in evangelio fit mentio de leprosis … et in epistola ponuntur vitia, ex quibus provenit lepra peccati*) is a rationale for a pairing **the 1962 missal does not make**. **His exposition of the Epistle’s own words stands on the appointed text**; the pairing of Gal. 5 with a cleansing narrative, and everything resting on *Inclina, Domine*, belongs to his Mass and not to this one.

**Anthony’s substantive moves on the Epistle.** He glosses *idolorum servitus* as **avarice** rather than the cult of images; he moralises *veneficia* into *suggestiones daemonum et adulationes peccatorum, quae nocere non possunt nisi ad sanguinem, idest animae consensum, pervenerint*; and he defines all twelve fruits individually — **passing over *homicidia* with no gloss at all.** His definitions of the last two, *continentia, quae a licitis abstinet; castitas, quae recte utitur licito*, are **verbatim** the pair Aquinas independently attributes to the Gloss, from two separately retrieved and separately hashed artifacts — **which is real evidence of a common medieval source.** His etymologies carry the moral sense he draws from a word rather than its descent: *fornicatio, idest formae necatio* reads the sin as the killing of the form, which is his gloss on the appointed word and not its philology. His inner quotations from the Gloss and from Isidore are **leads**, neither having been opened, and are attributed to *his use* of them and not to Isidore or the Gloss; the same holds for the definition of charity he ascribes to Augustine, which the edition’s own note transmits through Peter Lombard and which reads differently in the printed Augustine, **so it is not Augustine’s exact words.** And his text prints three of the fruits in a different order from the missal’s — *patientia, longanimitas, bonitas, benignitas, mansuetudo* against *patiéntia, benígnitas, bónitas, longanímitas, mansuetúdo* — which is a fact about two texts and **not evidence about which Vulgate recension the missal descends from.**

**And one fact bears on every join of Epistle and Gospel.** The library’s sole registered treatment of Gal. 5:16–24 is Anthony’s sermon, and **Anthony’s Sunday numbering runs one ahead of the 1962 missal’s, consistently across all three of his registered sermons.** A major medieval preacher expounding this Epistle does so beside a different Gospel. So every cross-element candidate joining Epistle and Gospel is a claim about how the 1962 formulary reads when taken as a whole, and **not** a claim about how the tradition read these two texts together.

Augustine’s and Jerome’s Galatians commentaries stand here **as transcriptions uncollated against Migne or a critical edition**. Theodoret’s Galatians commentary is registered only at 3:16–22 and so reaches no appointed verse, and **no Pauline commentary of his stands in either Greek collection reached.** Cassian, Pelagius’s *Expositio*, Nicholas of Lyra, Denis the Carthusian and Cornelius a Lapide on Galatians are unswept.

### Gradual <span class="nodecor">(*Grad.*)</span>

*Bonum est confídere in Dómino* *Graduale* — Ps. 117, 8-9 marginal no. 1575

Unlike the Introit and the Offertory, **the Gradual takes complete verses and cuts nothing**, so there is no omission at all — one of only three places in this formulary where the appointed wording is the Clementine’s exactly. Context: vv. 6–7 twice call the Lord *adjutor* and name *homo* as what need not be feared, so v. 8’s *quam confídere in hómine* generalises a contrast the psalm has just made concrete; and v. 1’s *quoniam bonus* supplies the adjective the Gradual’s *Bonum est* turns into a comparative formula. The tracked Clementine prints *Alleluja.* at the head of v. 1, which bears on any claim that Ps. 117 has no superscription — it is a liturgical rubric-word rather than an ascription, and it is not numbered separately.

**The princes are read four ways, and a question stands beside the four.** **Cassiodorus, in Latin** (transcription of PL 70, inspected, uncollated), observes that **the two appointed verses are governed by anaphora** — *Et nota quod in principiis amborum versuum eadem verba geminavit. Quae figura dicitur anaphora* — exactly on the appointed wording; reads the pair as **carnal against spiritual** rather than a moral climb, citing Jas. 4:13–15, Phil. 2:13 and Jer. 17:5 against presumption of the will; and lets *princeps* cover **both the devil and the good angel**, *Sic et bonus angelus dictus est princeps, sicut in Daniele legitur*. **Theodoret, in Greek** (PG 80:1812, same bounds as the Introit’s Theodoret material), has *archontes* who are **earthly rulers whose authority is temporary**: God is good, wills the good, can do what he wills and has imperishable authority, while men are corruptible by nature and changeable in purpose; his preface makes them the persecuting rulers, demagogues, kings and generals whom the gentile believers outlasted. **His psalm heading is the same *Allelouia* the Clementine prints**, a small convergence, and **his lemma reads the missal’s ablative**, one data point against the accusative variant below without settling it. **Augustine and Cassiodorus both reach Michael in Daniel, so between those two the shared proof-text is probably dependence and not independent invention** — and **the division on what the princes are** sets a Greek reading against three Latin ones on the same two verses.

> **Augustine, *Enarratio in Ps. CXVII* §4, on vv. 8 and 9.** **He reads the two verses as a single escalating argument**, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English: “let not my friend present himself unto me as a good man, so as to bid me repose my hope in himself … Nor let any one, who may in a certain sense be styled a good angel, be regarded by myself as one in whom I ought to put my trust … For angels also are called princes, even as we read in Daniel, Michael, your prince.” The second term is progressively strengthened — bad men, good men, good angels — and still loses.

> **Prosper of Aquitaine, *Expositio Psalmorum*, PL 51:333B–C.** *Etiam si bonus homo, aut bonus angelus adiuvet, non in eis, inquit, confidam; sed in Domino, qui me per illos iuvat*; and at v. 9, *Principes et homines et angeli possunt intelligi: sed in neutris spes collocanda est; quia potestas non est nisi a Deo* (Rom. 13:1). **His frame for the passage is Eph. 6:12, read three verses earlier, and that is what licenses the angelic sense**, which v. 9 does not carry by itself. **Three verses later he quotes the Offertory’s own words** — *ut gustemus et videamus quam suavis est Dominus* — so a Father joins the Gradual’s psalm to the Offertory’s verse in one sentence. This *Expositio* is largely an epitome of Augustine’s *Enarrationes*, and how much of it is Prosper’s own is unsettled.

> **Arnobius the Younger (*attributed*), in Ps. CXVII, PL 53:505A–C.** **The man not to be trusted is oneself, and the confidence forbidden is confidence in free choice:** *Habes quidem liberum arbitrium, sed noli de eo praesumere; de Deo praesume, quia vinci non potest, nam liberum arbitrium vinci potest*, with the predestinarian objection raised and answered in the same passage. **Augustine’s anti-Pelagian books, which Cassiodorus names at this verse, stand unopened here**, and **the grace-and-free-will reading of the verse is attested in a fifth-century Latin psalm commentary in its own right**, and not only through the cross-reference Cassiodorus makes.

> **Godfrey of Admont, on the appointed verses, PL 174:579D–580A.** **A fourth account of what the princes are, and the only one that keeps them inside the man who prays:** the princes and the servants are the interior and the exterior senses — *Interiores sensus nostri sunt principes, exteriores sensus servi nostri* — and the comparison itself he reads as *melius est, imo optimum confidere in virtute Dei, quam praesumere in suis meritis*. **His Fourteenth Sunday is not the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday**, the bound recorded at the Introit.

> **Bruno the Carthusian (*attributed*), *Expositio in Psalmos*, PL 152:1249D–1250B.** **He is the one witness read here who asks whether the Gradual’s *quam* is comparative at all, and he answers twice.** First he denies that the word is set down as a comparative: on that reading the verse means that trusting the Lord by enduring tribulation is useful and looking to the persecutor for bodily safety is not, with Jer. 17:5 as proof. Then he grants the comparative, **but only if *principes* are heavenly spirits and spiritual men**, on the ground that God can help without them and they cannot help without God. **This is a grammatical judgement about the appointed words themselves**, and the first proposal below stands or falls with his second answer.

**And one compilation carries the angelic reading forward without adding a vote to it.** **Peter Lombard** (PL 191:1035B–C) makes v. 8’s man *homo carnalis, cui salus vana est* and, under the siglum *\[Aug.\]*, v. 9’s princes *angelis qui dicuntur principes. Unde in Daniele legitur Michael princeps* — **he is the *Magna glosatura*, a sigla-marked compilation.** **So what looks like three witnesses for the angelic reading may be one position with three carriers:** Lombard’s siglum puts it under Augustine’s name with Daniel as warrant, Prosper’s exposition is largely an Augustine epitome, and Prosper’s reason is Lombard’s reason. **Augustine’s own *Enarratio* on this psalm, held here, would settle it and stands unopened.**

The Latin witness reads the accusative *sperare in Dominum … in principes* where the missal prints the ablative *in Dómino … in princípibus*. **One modern transcription cannot tell whether that is Augustine’s psalter or the transcriber’s.**

**One cross-relation, and Augustine does not make it.** His escalation to good angels makes the Gradual’s exclusion absolute rather than moral, which is the Gospel’s own logic at *Nemo potest duóbus dóminis servíre*. **He does not cite Mt. 6:24 here**, and no witness read here makes the join.

**Chrysostom’s exposition of this psalm is not in the Greek collection that carries his *Expositiones in Psalmos*.** That text is a fragment ending at PG 55:194 and covering Pss. 4–12 and 43–44 only, under a title that promises the whole. So the only Greek reading of the Gradual is Theodoret’s, with the bounds stated above. **Bellarmine on this psalm stands outside what is held here:** the one Bellarmine passage this repository registers falls at Ps. 33 vv. 2–3, reaching no appointed verse of any element, and is in an abridged Victorian English translation rather than the Latin.

### Alleluia <span class="nodecor">(*All.*)</span>

*Veníte, exsultémus Dómino* no separate heading; reference inline — Ps. 94, 1 marginal no. 1576

**The whole psalm, and where its reception went.** Psalm 94 calls the assembly to praise, names God king above all gods and shepherd of his flock, and then turns abruptly to warning: today if you shall hear his voice, harden not your hearts. **The chant takes v. 1 alone** and drops the psalm title *Laus cántici ipsi David*, which the Clementine prints as part of that verse, pointing the clause with a comma where the Clementine has a semicolon.

> **Augustine, *Enarratio in Ps. XCIV* §2–3.** **He takes the imperative *Venite* as the load-bearing word and denies that it means motion**, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English: “It is not by place, but by being unlike Him, that a man is afar from God … If therefore by unlikeness we recede from God, by likeness we approach unto God.” And *exsultemus* is specified as joy *in the Lord* precisely to exclude a worldly joy the same word would cover: “if there were not in this life a wicked joy which is to be distinguished from a righteous joy, it would be enough to say, Come, let us rejoice; but he has briefly distinguished it.”

**The Latin recovers something the translation loses entirely.** Augustine closes the argument on a jingle built from the appointed verb itself — *Pie debes Domino exsultare, si vis securus mundo insultare*: you must piously *exult* in the Lord if you would safely *exult over* the world. The English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series, renders this “piously joy in the Lord, if you dost wish safely to trample upon the world,” and the play vanishes. **The play depends on the missal’s own word *exsultémus*.**

**This exposition’s centre of gravity is vv. 7–11, *Hodie si vocem eius audieritis*, which the Alleluia does not appoint**, and Augustine’s argument there closes on the oath of exclusion from rest. **That ending belongs to the psalm and not to the chant, which stops at v. 1.** The same limit governs the canonical relationship: Hebrews quotes Ps. 94:8–11 at 3:7–11, repeats the opening at 3:15, and expounds it again through 4:1–11; but **the formulary appoints v. 1 and no more, and the appointed text does not say that its single verse carries the whole psalm’s warning.** And the same Clementine renders the quotation differently in the two places — Ps. 94:9 has *in irritatione* where Heb. 3:8 quoting it has *in exacerbatione* — **so the Vulgate’s psalter and its Hebrews do not agree verbally.**

**The Alleluia is the least lexically integrated element of the formulary.** Its only substantive tie to any other element is *salutári* — **and that tie is to three composed orations rather than to a lesson**; *exsult-* and *iubil-* occur nowhere else in the ten elements, and it shares no other content word beyond *Dominus* and *Deus*. **No lexical warrant joins the Alleluia’s joy to the Gospel’s anxiety**: the Epistle’s *gaudium* and the Alleluia’s *exsultémus* are different roots in different elements with nothing joining them, and no witness read here reads the one as the resolution of the other.

**Cassiodorus and Theodoret disagree about the one word the chant contributes.** **Cassiodorus, in Latin** (PL 70 transcription, inspected, uncollated), reads *iubilémus* as the psalm’s own invitation to the **jubilus** — wordless praise beyond syllables — and takes *Venite* through Is. 29:13, a people that draws near with the mouth; he also glosses *salutari* plainly of salvation, which quietly underlines how little else the verse carries. **Theodoret, in Greek** (PG 80, same bounds as the Introit’s Theodoret material), hears in the same verb **the shout of victors**, an epinician: the praise of those who have already conquered, and he composes the psalm in the person of King Josiah foreseeing his reform, from 4 Kgs 22–23 — **his own reconstruction and not what the psalm states**; he also reports the psalm **untitled among the Hebrews**, against the Clementine’s printed title. **They disagree about what *iubilémus* means.** **The *Deo salutári nostro* resonance:** the collocation is rare, and its only New Testament occurrence is the Magnificat’s *Deus salutari meo*.

**Four further readers of this psalm stand.** **A homily of the Chrysostomic corpus** (PG 55:615–618, read in Greek from a transcription, **marked spurious in the apparatus and cited here as of that corpus and not securely as Chrysostom**) makes the psalm a summons to repentance and confession rather than to festal praise — *Metanoias gar ho paron bios, apeiles de kai kriseos he mellousa parousia* — proved from Hezekiah turning to the wall and from the Ninevites, whose sackcloth prevailed where purple, diadem and throne did not. **Arnobius the Younger** (PL 53:462D–463C): *exsultemus non in delectationibus vanis, sed in Domino; antequam praeoccupemur, nos praeoccupemus faciem eius in confessione peccatorum nostrorum*, with a warning against attending to Christ *in forma servi* as though he were a mere man. **Bruno the Carthusian** (*attributed*, PL 152:1142A–1143A): the title is *non est David, sed Christi*; the *Venite* addresses unbelieving Jews of the sixth age; *iubilo, id est nimia et intensa interiori laetitia exsulto*; and the following half-verse is read twice, once from court manners and once eschatologically, *Secundus autem adventus recte facies dicitur, eo quod tunc cunctis Christus manifestabitur*. **Peter Lombard** (PL 191:875A–876A) allots the appointed verse’s three verbs to three faculties — *Venite, fide et exsultemus mente, Domino, non in saeculo, Iubilemus voce, Deo salutari nostro Iesu* — **which is his own arrangement and is a reading of the chant’s own words** — while carrying the classical jubilus formula at exactly the appointed verb, *Gaudium sit tantum, quod verbis dici non possit, quod tamen intus conceptum, vox extra testetur*. **His sigla attribute the surrounding material to Augustine, Cassiodorus, Haymo and Alcuin, so that formula is almost certainly Cassiodorus’s wordless jubilus carried forward and not a third account.**

**All four read the psalm penitentially, and every one of them reaches that reading through v. 2, *Praeoccupemus faciem eius in confessione*, which the Alleluia does not appoint** — Bruno’s turning on the very word *faciem* the chant never sings. **The convergence is about the psalm and not about the chant, which stops at v. 1**, the same limit that stands at the Introit for Augustine and Cassiodorus.

No later use of this chant’s wording can be tied to Ps. 94:1 at all: the King James wording of the verse stands at five psalms — Pss. 66:1, 81:1, 95:1–2, 98:4, 98:6 and 100:1 — and every use confirmed in the corpora swept was a music programme or a service listing.

### Gospel <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>

*Nemo potest duóbus dóminis servíre* *Sequéntia sancti Evangélii secúndum Matthǽum* — Mt. 6, 24-33 marginal no. 1577

#### Where the lesson stands in Matthew 6

**The pericope is cut out of a continuous argument at both ends.** It begins bare at v. 24, with no connective particle, immediately after the treasure and eye sayings (*Ubi enim est thesaurus tuus, ibi est et cor tuum*, v. 21), and stops at v. 33 without v. 34, the passage’s own conclusion. *Sollícitus* governs the discourse four times, at vv. 25, 28, 31 and 34, and **the pericope carries three of them, ending on the promise of v. 33 rather than on the restriction to today.** That is also what lets the Communion take the Gospel’s last verse as its own antiphon.

**The pericope contains the second half of a repetition internal to Matthew 6 and not the first.** The Father’s foreknowledge of need at v. 32, *Scit enim Pater vester*, restates v. 8, which stands in the instruction on prayer the pericope does not include — and the Lord’s Prayer stands between the two members, asking for the kingdom’s coming and for bread. Matthew supplies that proximity; nothing in the appointed formulary points to it.

**Both sayings have Lucan parallels in different settings, and Luke orders the material the other way round.** Luke places the two-masters saying at 16:13, after the unjust steward, and adds *servus*; Matthew has it inside the Sermon without *servus*. Luke’s *corvos* and *cellarium, neque horreum* differ from Matthew’s *volatília cæli* and *hórrea*. And the ordering is reversed: in Matthew treasure-and-heart precedes the anxiety discourse and in Luke it follows — **which bears directly on whether Mt. 6:24 is heard as the close of the treasure unit or the head of the anxiety unit.** The appointed pericope’s boundary at v. 24 takes it as a head.

#### What the commentators actually argue

> **Augustine, *De sermone Domini in monte* II.47–58, continuous direct commentary on the appointed pericope.** **He reads v. 24’s asymmetry as deliberate:** Christ says of the second master *contemnet* and not *odio habebit* because, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English, “almost no one’s conscience can hate God; but he despises, i.e. he does not fear Him, as if feeling himself secure in consideration of His goodness.” **The move is a claim about which of two verbs the text uses, and the appointed Latin preserves exactly that contrast — but its premise is the sentence Augustine afterwards withdrew**, at *Retractationes* I.19, naming this very passage. **The observation about the two verbs stands on the appointed text and the reason he gave for it does not stand on his own later judgment**, and both are printed below. **Two things in the same locus are his own and stand unchecked:** the remark that “mammon” is Punic for gain, which was checked against no Semitic authority; and the importation of “the prince of this world” from Jn. 12:31 to identify the second master, **which the appointed verse does not say.**

> **Jerome, *Commentarii in Matthaeum*, on the appointed verses.** **He draws the distinction on which any non-ascetical reading of the Gospel depends:** *non dixit, qui habet divitias, sed qui servit divitiis. Qui enim divitiarum servus est, divitias custodit, ut servus; qui autem servitutis excussit iugum, distribuit eas, ut dominus* — **Aquinas repeats the same distinction in almost the same words**, *aliud est habere divitias ut dominus, aliud ut servus*, and whether he drew on Jerome is not established. **He states the limit against over-reading in one sentence anchored inside the appointed bounds:** *labor exercendus est, sollicitudo tollenda* (ad v. 25), with *De praesentibus ergo concessit debere esse sollicitos qui futura prohibet cogitare* at vv. 31ff — **the second of the two standing inside the appointed bounds** — and a third independent Father setting the same limit. **He expressly rebuts allegorising the birds**, naming those who read them as angels and ministering powers, and refutes them from the *a fortiori* itself: *Si hoc ita est, ut intelligi volunt, quomodo sequitur dictum ad homines: Nonne vos magis pluris estis illis? Simpliciter ergo accipiendum* — **a ground internal to the appointed verse**. **His comment on the lilies is aesthetic, not allegorical:** *quod sericum, quae regum purpura, quae pictura textricum potest floribus comparari?* **He gives the other etymology of *mammonae*:** *Mammona sermone Syriaco divitiae nuncupantur* — **two Fathers, two eastern languages, no check against a Semitic authority here; the finding is that they disagree**. **And he records a variant the missal does not print**, *In nonnullis codicibus additum est neque quid bibatis* at v. 25, the missal’s shorter form being the one he treats as his own.

> **Hilary, *Commentarius in Matthaeum*, on the appointed verses.** **He is the Latin tradition’s allegorist at this pericope, and he argues for it:** the literal sense does not cohere with what precedes — *Quae consequuntur, non satis propositionibus congruunt. Numquid et unius domini servus non possit circa vestem esse cibumque sollicitus?* — and he reads the whole pericope as doctrine about the resurrection of the body, allegorising every appointed image: **birds the unclean spirits; the cubit the risen body raised to the measure of the perfect man (Eph. 4:13); lilies the brightness of the angels; the hay burnt tomorrow the gentiles.** **His hay-as-gentiles reading ends in a bodily eternity of the damned no other witness states**, and the birds-as-unclean-spirits reading is the one place where his allegory works *against* the pericope’s consoling sense. **At the verse the Mass lifts out he reads plainly**, grounding *Haec enim omnia gentes inquirunt* in unbelief rather than appetite — *Gentium igitur est, infidelitatis istius cura angi* — **the one place where his resurrection reading and the plain sense converge**; his exposition runs on to v. 34, which the lesson excludes. **His lemma lacks the last step of Chrysostom’s escalation**, reading *cras in ignem mittitur* where the missal prints *cras in clíbanum* — **the step Chrysostom’s rhetoric turns on is not in every ancient Latin witness**, and the medieval Gloss knows the variant, *Alii libri habent in ignem, vel in acervum, qui habet speciem clibani*. **He is not refuted by Jerome:** Jerome’s target is a reading of the *birds* as angels, Hilary’s angels are the *lilies*, and that Jerome names Hilary among the Latin commentators he had read is not evidence that he has him in view here.

> **Thomas Aquinas, *Super Matthaeum* cap. 6 lect. 3–5.** **He fastens on the other member of the same four-verb figure Augustine reads, and explains it from the same asymmetry:** *Et non dicit, diliget, quia Diabolus naturaliter diligi non potest. Deus naturaliter diligitur; Diabolus vero sustinetur* — **complementary to Augustine’s *contemnet* observation, and both checkable against the appointed Latin, which prints all four verbs.** He reads the whole saying as a claim about ultimate ends, *impossibile est quod animus feratur ad duos fines simul et semel*, and **on the cubit he reads an experimental proof of providence**: *Hic probat experimento, quod sicut providet avibus, ita et nobis … Unde augmentum non est nobis ex nobis, sed ex Deo: unde de providentia Dei non debetis desperare.* The measure of a man’s stature is not in his own power, so growth is from God and not from ourselves, and the anxiety the pericope forbids is on that ground demonstrably vain. **He independently names the sect Augustine’s controversy leaves unnamed:** *destruit hic error Euticharum, qui dicebant quod viri apostolici non debebant laborare*, answered from 2 Thess. 3:10 — **which does not close the lead**, *De haeresibus* standing unopened; *Euticharum* is the text’s spelling and is reproduced as printed. **And *Super Matthaeum* ascribes the lilies-as-angels reading to Jerome**; the reading is **Hilary’s**, and the *Catena aurea* — Thomas’s own compilation — attributes it correctly to *Hilarius in Matth.* **The slip is most likely the reporter’s** — *Super Matthaeum* is a *reportatio* — **and belongs to that text and not to Thomas.**

> **Augustine, on the birds and the lilies.** **He explicitly forbids allegorising them**, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English: “these examples are not to be treated as allegories, so that we should inquire what the fowls of heaven or the lilies of the field mean: for they stand here, in order that from smaller matters we may be persuaded respecting greater ones,” with the unjust judge as his comparison case. **The Latin tradition is namedly divided at these images** — Hilary allegorises them, Jerome and Augustine forbid it — and one of the two principal Latin Fathers commenting on this very pericope says its central images are not to be taken allegorically. His restraint is specific: he bars allegorising *these examples*, not the pericope as a whole, and he is at the same time willing to read “seek first the kingdom” as a doctrine of ordered ends.

> **John Chrysostom, *Homiliae in Matthaeum* 21–22.** **He reads the illustrations as rhetorically engineered.** The birds are chosen *because* they are irrational, deliberately in place of Moses, Elijah or John — in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English, “that He might touch them more to the quick … He has cut off from them every excuse”; and the lilies are progressively degraded — lilies, then “grass of the field,” then “which today is,” then “cast into the oven” — so the *a fortiori* grows at every step. **The appointed Latin fits word for word:** *fænum agri, quod hódie est, et cras in clíbanum míttitur … quanto magis vos módicæ fídei?*, and he calls the rebuke “not want, but poverty, of faith.”

**Where both Fathers comment they agree the images are arguments and not figures, and they differ in what the argument is.** Augustine grounds the inference in the **rank** of the creature — in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series, “a rational being such as man has a higher rank in the nature of things than irrational ones.” Chrysostom grounds it in the **vileness** of the creature and in the deliberate rhetorical descent, and gives the anthropological ground in terms of *gift* rather than rank, in the English of the same series: “you, to whom He gave a soul, for whom He fashioned a body … for whose sake He gave up His only begotten Son.” **The difference is not a contradiction, but the middle terms differ and the two yield different homiletic conclusions from the same verses:** the argument from lesser to greater is common to both Fathers, and the term that carries it is not.

**Both Fathers independently set a limit against over-reading the pericope.** Augustine at II.57–58: Christ kept money bags, Paul worked with his hands, the church at Antioch stored food against Agabus’s famine, and the whole precept reduces to the rule, in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series, “that even in looking after such things we should think of the kingdom of God, but in the service of the kingdom of God we should not think of such things.” Chrysostom reaches the same limit by a different route on v. 26, in the English of the same series: “He said not, we must not sow, but we must not take thought; neither that one ought not to work, but not to be low-minded.” **Where the claim must rest inside the appointed bounds, Chrysostom’s sentence is the better one**, because Augustine’s material there is anchored partly in v. 34, which this lesson excludes, while Chrysostom’s is anchored in v. 26, which it includes.

#### The Latin transmission of this Gospel, and the sentence its author withdrew

> **Augustine, *Retractationes* I.19, PL 32:617.** **He withdraws the sentence the Latin tradition repeats most often at this Gospel’s first verse:** *Item quod dixi, Nullius enim fere conscientia Deum potest odisse (Lib. 2, c. 14, n. 48), non video fuisse dicendum. Multi enim sunt de quibus scriptum est: Superbia eorum qui oderunt te (Psal. LXXIII, 23).* **The self-citation fixes the work as *De sermone Domini in monte* II.14.48**, his exposition of *aut unum sustinébit, et álterum contémnet* — **so the strongest single patristic move on this Gospel is one its author afterwards refused.** *De serm. Dom.* II.14.48 itself stands unopened here, and the withdrawal is reported from the *Retractationes* alone.

**Five later Latin witnesses repeat the withdrawn sentence, and one denies it outright.** Rabanus, the Glossa, Christian of Stavelot, Paschasius and the exposition printed under Bede’s name all carry it **without Augustine’s qualifier *fere***; **the Glossa alone flags the retraction**; **Rabanus prints *ferre* for *fere* and pseudo-Bede prints *Nullius enim scientia Deum odisse ferre potest***, and *scientia* for *conscientia* cannot be anyone’s theology. **The pattern is transmission**, and not five authors each hardening Augustine on his own account. And **Bruno of Segni contradicts the proposition from Scripture**: *Quis enim servit mammonae, nisi avarus? Avarus igitur Deum contemnit et odio habet. Unde hoc? Quia idolis servit*, from Eph. 5:5. **So “no one can hate God” is neither the tradition’s settled reading here nor Augustine’s own last word on it.**

> **Christian of Stavelot (Druthmarus), *Expositio in Matthaeum* cap. XIII, PL 106:1316C–1319A.** **The most literal-minded of the Latin witnesses read here.** He answers the objection that Paul commands *serve one another* by the Greek distinction of *latreusis* and *douleusis*, quoting both words in Greek characters; identifies the two masters as the devil and God; glosses mammon as Syriac for riches, with *Aliud est habere divitias, aliud servire divitiis*; **reads the lilies with no allegory at all**, on the bare ground that no linen matches the lily’s whiteness, no dye the rose, no purple the violet; explains Solomon as the example because the Jews knew no richer king though richer men existed; and supplies the elided noun at Mt. 6:33 with its limit, *haec, subauditur necessaria, adiicientur … Ad necessitatem, non ad superfluitatem*.

> **Paschasius Radbertus, *Expositio in Matthaeum*, PL 120:306B–307A and 312C–313A.** **He grounds the impossibility of two masters in the baptismal renunciation** — no one enters the font until he renounces the devil, all his works and all his pomps — and calls the relapsed Christian a foolish soldier throwing away immortal arms. **He excepts the Trinity from the rule**, *Pater, et Filius, et Spiritus sanctus, non tres Domini*, because service fails only where a discordant will intervenes. **And at Mt. 6:33 he denies that *primum* marks order at all:** *Primum autem, non ad ordinem referendum puto … magis ad quantitatem referendum est, ut extra hoc nihil quaerendum sit*, calling the sequence *praeposterus* and explaining that the kingdom was put first to soothe the hearing before justice was brought in as the laborious thing, and reading the promise through use and enjoyment, *his utamur in via, quatenus illo fruamur in patria*. **So the word the Communion antiphon promotes to its head is contested, and Paschasius stands against the Augustine the compilations transmit.**

> **Rabanus Maurus, on the appointed verses, PL 107:836B–839A.** **At these verses he compiles rather than composes.** The material runs *(Hier.)* for the mammon gloss and the flowers and *(Aug.)* for the two masters and for *Quaerite primum*, with **one** sentence of his own marked *(Maur.)*. The Augustine he transmits supplies the sharpest surviving formula for the Communion’s word — *Cum enim dicit illud primum, significavit quod hoc posterius quaerendum est, non tempore, sed dignitate* — **but that sentence was read inside his compilation and Migne’s sigla are editorial.**

> **Glossa ordinaria on Matthew, PL 114:105A–107A.** It reports mammon as also the name of a demon set over riches — **and reports it as hearsay, *Dicitur hoc nomen esse daemonis***; grounds the two masters in baptism exactly as Paschasius does; **records a variant at Mt. 6:30**, *Alii libri habent: in ignem vel in acervum qui habet speciem clibani*; and **alone of the compilations flags that Augustine withdrew the sentence about hating God.** **The variant is a fact about the Gospel text and no Latin witness was checked for whether any actually prints *in ignem***; one recognition error in this transcription prints *(Psal. III)* where the psalm is the seventy-third.

> **The Matthew exposition printed under Bede’s name, PL 92:34D–36A.** It raises the question whether bodily food belongs to the soul and answers it by taking *anima* at Mt. 6:25 as this present life, *cuius retinaculum est alimentum istud corporeum*; carries the anti-allegorical line *Ista vero documenta de avibus et liliis non allegorice ponuntur*; and **reads the Communion’s own verb precisely**, *Non dixit nobis dari, sed adiici, quia nobis in intentione aeternitas, in usu vero debet esse temporalitas*.

> **Bruno of Segni, *Commentaria in Matthaeum*, PL 165:121B–123C.** **The sharpest counter-witness at this Gospel.** Besides denying that the covetous man can fail to hate God, he raises a literal objection nobody else here raises — a man plainly *can* serve many masters, *non solum duobus dominis, sed et multis dominis servire non est impossibile*, which is why the verse must be read of these two in particular; defines serving riches as fearing to spend them; reads the lilies literally; and **restricts the whole prohibition of anxiety to apostles, their successors and those aiming at perfection.** **So his exposition is addressed to a narrower audience than a Sunday congregation.** His own *Expositio in Psalmos*, which reaches all four appointed psalms, stands unread.

> **Godfrey of Admont, *Homilia* LXXXIII, PL 174:580C–583D — his Fifteenth Sunday, and the far end of the Latin division.** He takes the whole prohibition of anxiety as concerning **spiritual** food and clothing, not visible food, drink or dress but the invisible nourishment and clothing of soul and spirit, so that *quid manducetis* asks what taste of contemplation one may have; widens mammon past riches to whatever a man sets his heart on and loves more than God; makes the kingdom to be sought first the heart, which ought always to be God’s kingdom; makes **the birds the good angels**, who neither sow by reading nor reap by taking the fruit of reading nor gather into barns by recollecting it into memory; makes **the lilies the evil spirits**, who do not spin because a spinner draws the thread from left to right and they draw everything they do the other way; and makes **Solomon Christ**, Solomon’s name being interpreted the peaceable one. **He raises the objection his own allegory creates and answers it, so he is a self-aware instance of the allegorising side and not a naive one.** **His spiritual-food reading depends on the pericope running past Mt. 6:33, where this lesson stops, and he is preaching to monks about *lectio* and not to a Sunday congregation.**

**So the Latin division at the images is sharper than a two-way split.** On one side the Augustine the compilations transmit, pseudo-Bede repeating him, Christian of Stavelot refusing allegory on aesthetic grounds and Bruno of Segni reading the lilies literally; on the other Godfrey, allegorising every image and defending the allegory against his own objection. **That is not a difference of emphasis.** Godfrey’s proof text for the pride of the fallen angels is Ps. 73:23, **the same verse Augustine gives in the *Retractationes* as his reason for withdrawing the sentence about hating God.**

> **Augustine, *De opere monachorum* §27, §30, §31 — a *controversy* over these verses.** **This runs against the obvious devotional reading.** Certain monks quoted these very verses to argue that they need not work, and Augustine wrote against them, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English: “let these persons, who from perverse understanding of the Gospel labor to pervert apostolic precepts, either take no thought for the morrow, even as the birds of the air; or let them obey the Apostle”; “against the Apostle of Christ, they recite a Gospel of Christ.” The identification of those monks as the Euchitae stands in the *Augustinus de Haeres.* citation, and *De haeresibus* was not retrieved, so it remains an unverified lead.

**Four of the *Catena aurea*’s authorities on this Gospel stand checked against the underlying works and the rest do not.** The appointed Gospel occupies cap. 6 lect. 16–21, drawing on Augustine, Jerome, Hilary, Chrysostom, Gregory, Rabanus, Remigius and the Gloss. **The Catena cites *Chrysostomus in Matth.* and *Chrysostomus super Matth.* as different things:** the first is the genuine *Homiliae in Matthaeum*, cross-matched sentence for sentence against the independently retrieved Homily 22; **the second is the anonymous *Opus imperfectum in Matthaeum*, a fifth-century work with Arian features whose attribution to Chrysostom is universally rejected.** **So a citation of “Chrysostom” on Mt. 6:33 drawn from the *super Matth.* formula names a Doctor of the Church for a text that is not his.** **Four of its attributions hold against the underlying works** — the Chrysostom of lect. 19, the *De opere monachorum* of lect. 17, and **every *Hilarius* and *Hieronymus* excerpt on the appointed Gospel, checked sentence by sentence against the independently retrieved work**, which the Catena reproduces faithfully. Those excerpts stand against the same Migne recensions the Catena drew on, so what agrees is the transmission and not the Migne text’s own correctness. **Its Gregory and its Remigius are unverified leads**, neither work opened. **Rabanus and the Gloss stand directly at Migne above**, where what they are is visible — compilations whose sigla are editorial.

### Offertory <span class="nodecor">(*Off.*)</span>

*Immíttet Angelus Dómini* *Ant. ad Offertorium* — Ps. 33, 8-9 marginal no. 1578

**The psalm is anchored by its own superscription to a named deliverance narrative — and the narrative does not fit the name.** *Davidi, cum immutavit vultum suum coram Achimelech*. The tracked Douay prints the cross-reference in the text as “\[1 Kings 21.\],” which in Douay book-naming is modern **1 Samuel 21**. **Read whole, that chapter shows Achimelech the priest at Nobe in vv. 1–9 and the change of countenance at v. 13 before Achis, king of Geth**; the tracked CPDV reads *Abimelech* at the superscription. **The divergence stands unexplained here**, no source for the common scholarly explanation being registered. It bears materially, because Augustine’s Eucharistic reading below is anchored in David *before Achis* while the Latin superscription names Achimelech. So the angel who encamps stands in a psalm whose superscription reads it off a concrete escape, and *eripiet eos* at v. 8 repeats the *eripuit me* of v. 5. **The antiphon stops one clause short of the psalm’s beatitude.**

**Cassiodorus reports the psalm’s alphabetic acrostic and locates the appointed verses within it.** His *Divisio psalmi* states it: *Per totum psalmum verba prophetae sunt, alphabeti Hebraei litteras, excepta sexta, in capitibus versuum per ordinem ponentis*, tagging every verse with its letter — **HETH at the Offertory’s first verse and TETH at its second** — and on the skipped letter, *Ego enim non inveni Patrum definitam de hac parte sententiam*. He is a sixth-century Latin reader reporting about the Hebrew, and no Hebrew psalter stands behind this guide, so what stands is his report and no statement about the Hebrew text. Bellarmine, the one other registered witness noting the structure, does so at vv. 2–3, reaching no appointed verse, in an abridged Victorian English translation.

> **Augustine, *Enarratio in Ps. XXXIII* **Sermo II**.** **The passage stands in Sermo II and is absent from Sermo I entirely.** **He rejects a rival reading and defends the one the missal prints**, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English: “So it is written, brethren, not as some bad copies have it, *The Lord shall send His Angel round about them that fear Him* … but thus, *The Angel of the Lord shall send round about them that fear Him*.” He then identifies that Angel as Christ, “the Angel of the great Counsel.” The 1962 Offertory prints *Immíttet Angelus Dómini* — *Angelus Dómini* nominative subject of *immíttet* — **the very reading he defends.** His Latin runs: *mendosi codices: Immittet Angelum Dominus … sed sic: Immitte\[t\] Angelus Domini …*

**Augustine’s text reads *et ERUET eos* where the missal prints *et ERIPIET eos*** — **so the two agree exactly on the point in dispute, which noun is the subject, and differ on the following verb**, and the missal’s text is therefore not simply Augustine’s. What stands between them is a convergence at the disputed word: the appointed text and a Father’s defended reading agree on which noun governs the verb. **The missal’s capital *Angelus* is an orthographic fact**, and no textual history joins the two readings by descent.

> **Cassiodorus, *Expositio in Psalterium*, at Ps. 33 vv. 8–9.** **His lemma reads *Immittet angelum Dominus* — exactly the reading Augustine denounced as belonging to faulty copies.** **So the variant Augustine fought was still standard in the principal medieval Latin psalm commentary, and the 1962 Offertory follows Augustine against Cassiodorus** — a convergence and not a descent, nothing tracing transmission. **He does not identify the angel with Christ**: he reads *Immittet* as deliberately covert — *propter insolentiam humanae fragilitatis non palam facit, sed occultis immissionibus operatur* — and turns to the moral sense, *si vis te angelum fieri, fac quod praecipit … Tunc enim spiritu angeli sumus, quando ministri supernae voluntatis efficimur*. **He is positive evidence that a Latin reader could take the verse otherwise.**

> **Cassiodorus, on *gustáte et vidéte* in the same exposition.** **His eucharistic reading is anchored in the appointed verbs themselves, where Augustine’s is anchored in the superscription the antiphon does not print:** *Gustate non pertinet ad palatum, sed animae suavissimum sensum, qui divina contemplatione saginatur. Nam ut ipsum gustum intelligeres, sequitur, videte, quod utique non ad os pertinet* — and he reads it eucharistically from the verb, citing Jn. 6:53 and the life-giving flesh taken from the Virgin. **So the reading is available to a hearer of the antiphon alone**, lifting the limit Augustine’s route imposes; and it converges with Cyril of Jerusalem’s “trust not the judgment to your bodily palate” — in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, second series, vol. 7 — reached in Latin and without dependence on the Jerusalem catechesis.

> **Augustine, on *gustáte et vidéte* in the same exposition.** **He reads v. 9 as openly Eucharistic**, decoding the psalm’s superscription: the David who, in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series, “was carried in his own hands” before Achish is Christ holding his own Body — *cum commendaret ipsum corpus suum et sanguinem suum, accepit in manus suas quod norunt fideles* — so “taste” is set against the incomprehension of Jn. 6:52. **His reading is anchored in the psalm’s superscription, and the Offertory antiphon prints no superscription**, so his reasoning stands as reception of the psalm and not as something a hearer of the antiphon alone could recover.

> **Arnobius the Younger (*attributed*), in Ps. XXXIII, PL 53:368C–369B.** **He makes two different claims at this verse.** The **Eucharistic** gloss: *Gustate corpus vitae, et videte quam suavis est Dominus: Vitam enim habet in semetipso, qui manducaverit carnem eius, et biberit sanguinem eius* (Jn. 6) — **a Latin witness beside the two already held here, and unlike them a psalm commentary reading the appointed verse in place rather than a mystagogical reuse.** And the **paschal** identification of the angel: *misit angelum suum in circuitu timentium corpus eius, et revolvit lapidem a monumento* (Mt. 28), with the poor man who cried read as Christ on the cross. **The second depends on repointing *timentium eum* as those fearing *for* his body, which is not what the missal’s Latin says**.

**And two further readers reach the angel and the verse.** **Godfrey of Admont** (PL 174:580A–B) makes the angel the gift of grace itself — *Angelus iste, quem Deus in circuitu timentium se immittit, donum gratiae illius est, sine cuius munere homo nunquam videre et gustare valet quoniam suavis est Dominus* — under the bound recorded at the Introit. **And Prosper of Aquitaine quotes this Offertory’s own words inside his exposition of the Gradual’s psalm**, *ut gustemus et videamus quam suavis est Dominus*: **a Father joining the two chants’ psalms in one sentence, which is a fact about his exposition and not about the formulary.** **So the angel of this chant now carries four accounts — Christ, the moral sense, the angel of the sepulchre, and the gift of grace — and the formulary adjudicates none of them.**

**Two fourth-century mystagogical witnesses attest the verse as the Church’s own communion invitation, and the 1962 Mass does not use it that way.** Cyril of Jerusalem, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, second series vol. 7, reports it actually being chanted at the communion: “ye hear the chanter inviting you with a sacred melody to the communion of the Holy Mysteries, and saying, O taste and see that the Lord is good. Trust not the judgment to your bodily palate.” Ambrose, in vol. 10 of the same series, cites it to identify what is eaten: “Taste and see that the Lord is good, blessed is the man that hopes in Him. In that sacrament is Christ.” **In the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday the same verse stands at the Offertory, before the consecration, and this Mass’s Communion is a different text entirely.** Note also that **Ambrose quotes the verse *with* the clause *beatus vir qui sperat in eo* which the Roman Offertory omits**, which sharpens rather than weakens the observation that the antiphon cuts at *Dóminus*. **Cyril’s authorship of the Mystagogical Catecheses is disputed in modern scholarship**, and both witnesses speak at Jerusalem and Milan and not at Rome.

**And the displacement is attested as living practice in a surviving Western rite.** The Mozarabic *Missale mixtum* prints *Gustate et videte quam suavis est Dominus* as the *ad accedentes* chant at communion time, cued by incipit thirty-four times across the book — **as liturgical fact**, in a searched optical layer of poor quality, and reading *quam* where the Roman chant has *quoniam*.

**The Offertory’s argument runs close to the Gospel’s two verses past its own cut.** Ps. 33:10–11 (*Timete Dominum … Divites eguerunt, et esurierunt; inquirentes autem Dominum non minuentur omni bono*) and Mt. 6:32–33 both set seeking God against want of food and both promise that those who seek will not lack. **The likeness is one of argument and not of wording, and neither text quotes the other.** The Latin verbs differ, and **the Gospel’s own *inquírunt* at v. 32 is used of the gentiles negatively where the psalm uses the participle positively**, which cuts against reading it as a verbal link. **And the appointed excerpt does not contain the psalm verses in question at all**, so the resemblance is between the Gospel and the psalm and not between two appointed texts.

**The chant’s other Roman use.** Ps. 33:8–9 has **exactly two Roman uses in the searched Missal**, the second appointed immediately after the Canaanite-woman Gospel ending *O mulier, magna est fides tua*. **That second use stands outside a whole-word search of the tracked Missal**, whose optical text breaks *Immittet* across a line end, and it was reached by reading the recorded line range instead.

### Secret <span class="nodecor">(*Sec.*)</span>

*Concéde nobis, Dómine, quǽsumus* *Secreta* marginal no. 1579

**One victim, two genitives.** The two *et* clauses are parallel predicates of the one subject *hæc hóstia salutáris*, and the second genitive is objective in a different way from the first: the victim is *purgátio* *of our offences* and *propitiátio* *of thy power* — **the appeasing of God’s power, not a power belonging to the propitiation.** At the offering, the protection the Introit and Collect asked for becomes propitiation. **Guéranger construes *propitiatio potestatis* the same way**, independently agreeing with this guide’s own collation; and **Schuster reads *tuae propitiatio potestatis* the same way**, as the appeasing of the divine omnipotence.

**Transmission.** The Secret stands with the Collect in Gelasianum Book III sect. X and has a second, penitential home at sect. XLI — with the narrowing recorded above, that the second home is a feature of the Vatican manuscript’s arrangement rather than of the Gelasian tradition at large.

**No exegetical reception of this prayer stands anywhere the exact-phrase index of the whole *Patrologia Latina* reaches.** *Propitiatio potestatis* returns three hits there and *purificent semper et muniant* two, **and every one of the five is a sacramentary printing the prayer** — the Gelasian at two sections, the Gregorian at *Dominica I ante natalem Domini* (reading *purgatio peccatorum* for *delictorum*), and the saint-adapted Gelasian recension for St Andrew’s vigil (reading *nos, Domine*). **The same index queried for the Collect’s phrases does return non-liturgical hits, so the silence belongs to these two prayers and not to the instrument.** **The index reaches Migne’s orthography and no other, and only phrases unbroken in the unit it indexes**, so a witness writing *propiciatio* or breaking the phrase across a line stands outside the negative; no Greek or vernacular corpus was searched, and the *Corpus Orationum* concordance stands unconsulted. **What the two prayers do have is modern comment at paraphrase length:** Schuster treats all three orations of this Mass and Guéranger gives the Secret and the Postcommunion a sentence each. **The Collect, alone of the three, has a medieval reception, and it is set out above.**

### Communion <span class="nodecor">(*Comm.*)</span>

*Primum quǽrite regnum Dei* *Ant. ad Communionem* — Mt. 6, 33 marginal no. 1580

#### The one substantive act this formulary performs on its Bible text

**The Communion departs from Mt. 6:33 in exactly four ways**, verified against the Clementine, the Lucan parallel and the tracked Greek: *ergo* is dropped and *Primum* promoted to head the clause; *et iustítiam eius* is omitted; *hæc* is dropped before *ómnia*; and *dicit Dóminus* is added. **That tag stands in none of Clementine Mt. 6:33, Clementine Lk. 12:31, or the tracked Greek at Mt. 6:33.** And **Luke, unlike the antiphon, keeps *iustitiam eius*, so the omission is not explained by conflation with the Lucan form.**

Two of the four bear theologically. Adding *dicit Dóminus* **turns a saying inside a sermon into an address made to the communicant at the moment of reception**; and dropping *et iustítiam eius* leaves the kingdom to be sought without the justice the Gospel names beside it.

> **Augustine, *De sermone Domini in monte* II.53, II.55, II.56, on *primum*.** **He argues an ordering of importance and not of time**, in the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers* English: “in using that word *first*, He has indicated that this is to be sought later, not in point of time, but in point of importance.” **A separately hashed thirteenth-century Latin witness carries the same sentence as *non tempore, sed dignitate***, which answers that English exactly. He makes the point by denying the sequence the word invites, in the same English — “He did not say, *Then* seek such things.” He offers an inversion test at II.55 and ties the phrase back to Mt. 6:24 at II.56.

**And the word the recasting promotes is contested.** **Paschasius Radbertus denies that *primum* marks order at all** — *non ad ordinem referendum puto … magis ad quantitatem referendum est* — against the *non tempore, sed dignitate* the compilations transmit under Augustine’s name. His is the earliest reading of that word recorded here.

**The verb the antiphon keeps when it drops everything else has readers too.** Pseudo-Bede: *Non dixit nobis dari, sed adiici, quia nobis in intentione aeternitas, in usu vero debet esse temporalitas.* Christian of Stavelot supplies the elided noun and its limit, *subauditur necessaria … Ad necessitatem, non ad superfluitatem.* Paschasius reads the promise through use and enjoyment. **And Godfrey of Admont reads *regnum Dei* at this verse as the heart**, which ought always to be God’s kingdom — a fourth thing done with the phrase the Communion sings. **Every one of them comments on Mt. 6:33 in its Gospel place, and no witness read here comments on the antiphon as such**, and the one reader who comes to it as an antiphon, Schuster, **reaches back for the *et iustitiam eius* the recasting removed** and glosses the justice from the Gospel verse.

Augustine’s exegesis of Mt. 6:33 is his own, and it makes word order carry the doctrine. **The missal’s Communion antiphon prints *Primum* at its head**, where the Gospel verse carries it third; Augustine expounds the verse and not the antiphon, and his exposition runs on to v. 34, which the appointed pericope excludes.

#### And the Communion is a later substitution at this Mass

**Five independent witnesses converge on the antiphon being secondary in this position.** ***Primum quaerite* is not a late composition:** it is an ancient Roman antiphon standing at the *Ninth* Sunday in four ninth-century antiphonaries, while the Fourteenth Sunday’s Communion in those books is *Panis quem ego dedero*. **So the antiphon is secondary in this slot only, and not in the repertory.** **And the substitution broke a pair**: *Panis quem ego dedero* travels with the Offertory *Immittet Angelus* at *both* its occurrences in those books, the Thursday of the third week of Lent and this Sunday, so putting *Primum quaerite* here separated two chants the older books kept together. **That evidence is the modern database of Hesbert’s *Antiphonale Missarum Sextuplex* and not Hesbert’s pages.**

The other four. First, the appointed text itself: this is the formulary’s one substantive departure from its Bible text. Second, Guéranger: “The Communion-anthem, taken from the Gospel which now is assigned to this Sunday, was not the one primitively used; the ancient liturgists make no mention of it in its present position, nor is it to be found there in any of the manuscripts consulted by Blessed Thomasi.” Third, the ninth-century chant apparatus: Wilson’s index shows *Primum quaerite* occurring as a Communion at exactly one locus, whose chant set is the 1962 *Ninth* Sunday’s, the *Protector noster* set there taking *Panis quem ego*. Setting that against the 1962 book shows **a rotation rather than a single replacement**: in that apparatus the 1962-Ninth set takes *Primum quaerite*, the 1962-Fourteenth set *Panis quem ego*, and the 1962-Fifteenth set *Qui manducat*, while 1962 assigns *Qui manducat* to the Ninth, *Primum quaerite* to the Fourteenth and *Panis quem ego* to the Fifteenth.

**A control from within the same Office, checkable in the same pages.** Guéranger prints this Sunday’s Magnificat antiphon at Vespers as *Quaerite primum regnum Dei, et justitiam ejus, et haec omnia adjicientur vobis. Alleluia.* **That antiphon retains both *et iustítiam eius* and *haec* — exactly the two clauses the Mass’s Communion drops — and leaves *primum* in its Matthaean position after the verb.** The *Breviarium Romanum* itself was not opened; this is a within-one-book control, the same editor printing both forms.

**Guéranger’s judgment rests on Tommasi’s Antiphonary and “the ancient liturgists,” neither consulted here**. Antiphon texts are routinely shortened for chant and *dicit Dóminus* is a common formulaic tag, so both changes may be conventional rather than expressive, and that count was not run. Two of the three 1962 assignments in the rotation were verified against repository text; **the Fifteenth Sunday’s Communion was not**, no leaf for it existing here. What the older books establish is **what the older pairing was, not when the substitution happened.** **The exchange is later than those antiphonaries, later than the Ottobonianus margin and later than Honorius, and earlier than the Pustet Missal of 1862**; **when it was made, no witness read here says, and no witness explains why.** **And the recasting is not proper to the 1962 edition:** the same form is printed in the Pustet Missal of Ratisbon, 1862, which this repository tracks as public-domain text — which shows the recasting is old and says nothing about why it was made. That the Communion selects the imperative rather than the pericope’s three prohibitions **may be an artefact of taking the final verse** rather than a choice among the pericope’s parts.

**No witness connects the omission of *et iustítiam eius* to the Epistle**, and Paul’s “not under law” and Matthew’s *iustítia* are not the same concept.

### Postcommunion <span class="nodecor">(*Postcomm.*)</span>

*Puríficent semper et múniant* *Postcommunio* marginal no. 1581

**The figure, and the noun held to the end.** *Ad perpétuæ … ducant salvatiónis efféctum* splits the genitive around the verb, exactly as the Thirteenth Sunday’s Postcommunion split *redemptiónis ætérnæ … augméntum* around its own — the same figure, one Sunday apart, and in both cases the noun the prayer is reaching for is held back to the end. This is also **the formulary’s only reach past this life**: *perpétuæ salvatiónis* is the one phrase in the ten elements that looks beyond the present.

***Semper*, a second time.** The Collect asked *tuis semper auxíliis*; this prayer opens *Puríficent semper*. Of the fifteen formularies collated in this repository, three carry *semper* in an oration and **this is the only one that carries it twice** — a statement about fifteen collated formularies and not about the Missal.

**Transmission, and it is the sharpest structural point in the oration dossier.** This prayer does not come from the same place as the other two, and **its Gelasian base is materially narrower than theirs**: it is attested for Rheinau and Gerbert but **not** for S. Gallen, Pamelius or Menard, since S. Gallen omits the whole appended series. *Purificent semper* stands in the floating series of unassigned Postcommunions appended after the Canon, in exactly the form the 1962 book prints, and separately in a saint-adapted recension in the Vigil Mass of St Andrew at Book II sect. **LXVIII**. Which recension is prior is not settled by anything read, and the pages carrying the floating series were read in text layers only.

**One measured fact about its vocabulary.** *Salvatio* as a noun occurs **once** in the forty-five orations of the fifteen collated formularies, and it is here — a count over those records and not over the *Missale Romanum*.

### The Secret and the Postcommunion: no exegetical reception in any period

**The Collect stands outside this negative**: Honorius cites it by incipit, Godfrey glosses it word by word and Schuster comments on it continuously, as the Collect’s own treatment above sets out. **For the Secret *Concede nobis, Domine* and the Postcommunion *Purificent semper et muniant* the negative stands across a whole-corpus exact-phrase index.** *Propitiatio potestatis* and *purificent semper et muniant* return five hits between them across the *Patrologia Latina*, and every one is a sacramentary printing the prayer; the same index returns non-liturgical hits for the Collect’s phrases, **so the silence belongs to these two prayers and not to the instrument.** Four continuations of the two prayers occur in no Latin work retained here.

**These are anonymous composed Roman orations, and the patristic commentary genres read here — psalm expositions, scriptural homilies, epistle commentaries — do not take liturgical orations as their object.**

**That negative reaches Migne’s orthography alone, and only phrases unbroken in the unit the index covers**, so a witness writing *propiciatio* or breaking the phrase across a line stands outside it, and no literal search excludes paraphrase, a different incipit, or an oration cited by its Sunday rather than by its words. **No Greek or vernacular corpus was searched for any of the three.** Three further bodies of material stand unopened here: the ancient sacramentaries’ modern editions as commentary sources; Amalarius, Walafrid Strabo, Rupert of Deutz, Durandus and the other medieval liturgical expositors; and the Usuarium *Corpus Orationum* concordance. **Schuster alone of that list is open**, **and he comments on all three orations without supplying any patristic or medieval witness for these two.**

**And Guéranger is a reception witness only and never a text witness.** His printed Latin differs from the 1962 typical edition at four points: he prints *millia* for *mília*; a **comma** where the typical edition prints the **semicolon** the Collect’s pointing turns on; lower-case *angelus*; and *Concede nobis, Domine, quaesumus:* with a colon and a capitalised *Hostia*, plus the short conclusion *Per Dominum* at both Secret and Postcommunion where the typical edition prints the longer form at the Postcommunion. **None of the appointed Latin above is his.** The last of the four is the weakest as evidence, since hand-missal presentations abbreviate conclusions as a matter of course.

## Source-Grounded Synthesis Across the Propers

### C1. One phrase carries the Mass, and the element that repeats it is the element that cuts it

*Regnum Dei* stands verbatim in three appointed elements and carries one argument across them. The Epistle names who is *excluded* from the kingdom, *qui tália agunt, regnum Dei non consequéntur*; the Gospel commands that it be *sought first*; and the Communion repeats that command as *the Lord’s own word at the moment of reception*. The negative and the positive are two faces of one claim about the kingdom, not two positions set against each other.

Four textual facts sharpen it. *Regnum Dei* is **rare in Matthew**: four verses in the whole Gospel (6:33, 12:28, 21:31, 21:43) against twenty-four carrying *regnum caelorum*, with Mt. 6:33 the first of the four. It is the **only** occurrence of the phrase in Galatians, **but Paul uses it elsewhere**: 1 Cor. 6:9–10, Eph. 5:5 and 1 Cor. 15:50 form a small closed set of Pauline parallels closing vice lists with the same exclusion, **none of the three is appointed, and nothing in the ten elements points to them**; their standing is as the canonical context of the appointed wording. **The tie survives into the Greek**, both texts reading *basileia theou*, which the Epistle-to-Gospel *servitus* / *servire* bridge does not. And across the fifteen 1962 formularies this repository has facsimile-collated, *regnum Dei* occurs in **this formulary alone**, at exactly three loci, independently reproduced in a second provider’s collation.

**The formulation is about exact forms, and dropping those two words makes it false.** *Regnum* is the only exact form reaching three or more of the ten elements that is neither a divine name nor a function word — but at stem level *salut-* and *salv-* reach three elements too, and are substantive. The two roots partition the formulary rather than competing: *regnum* in the read and sung word, *salut-* in the three orations and one chant.

**And the element that repeats the phrase is the element that cuts it.** *Iustítia* stands **once** in the whole appointed Latin, in the Gospel’s last verse — and the Communion, quoting that very verse, drops it. In Matthew that word closes an arc the chapter opens with at 6:1, **though the arc is a property of the Vulgate’s reading there, the tracked Byzantine text reading *alms*.**

At the hinge stands Augustine’s reading of *primum*, carried in Latin by a second and separately hashed thirteenth-century witness, and it is now contested. The Augustine the compilations transmit reads *primum* as an ordering of importance and not of time — *non témpore, sed dignitáte* — making the point by denying the sequence the word invites, in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series: “He did not say, *Then* seek such things”; and he ties the phrase back to Mt. 6:24 at *De sermone Domini in monte* II.56. **Paschasius Radbertus denies that *primum* marks order at all**: *non ad ordinem referendum puto … magis ad quantitatem referendum est, ut extra hoc nihil quaerendum sit*. The verb the antiphon keeps has its own readers — pseudo-Bede’s *Non dixit nobis dari, sed adjici*, Christian of Stavelot’s *subauditur necessaria … Ad necessitatem, non ad superfluitatem*, Paschasius’s *his utamur in via, quatenus illo fruamur in patria* — and Godfrey of Admont reads *regnum Dei* at this verse as the heart, which ought always to be God’s kingdom. **Schuster, commenting on the antiphon itself, reaches back for the clause the recasting removed** and glosses the justice from the Gospel verse rather than from the antiphon.

What the omission does is remove the formulary’s only occurrence of *iustítia*. The cross-formulary count runs over fifteen collated formularies, a small and non-random set, and is a fact about them rather than about the *Missale Romanum*. A Communion antiphon drawn from the day’s own Gospel is ordinary practice, so were this one’s *regnum Dei* a purely mechanical carry-over the three-element spine would reduce to a two-element one plus an echo — which would still leave the Epistle’s occurrence standing, since it comes from a different book and a different pericope. And the one medieval preacher the library registers on this Epistle expounds it beside a different Gospel.

### C2. The flesh that is crucified is not the body that is fed, and the tradition now says so four ways in two languages

The two lessons are **lexically disjoint**: *caro*/*carn-* stands five times in the Epistle and nowhere else in the formulary; *corp-* stands twice in the Gospel and nowhere else. **This is one unit, not a contrast.** The point is not that Epistle and Gospel disagree about the body but that they use different words for different things, and that reading them together dissolves the appearance of disagreement. **A reading built as “Epistle mortifies against Gospel provides” has inverted it.** **And the Introit’s cut is what keeps the division clean:** its psalm verse stops at *in atria Domini*, one clause before the Clementine’s *cor meum et caro mea exsultaverunt in Deum vivum* — the one appointed element that would have given *caro* a positive sense, severed exactly before it, and Cassiodorus comments on that very clause outside the appointed extract.

**Four positions now stand on what *caro* is at Gal. 5:17, and they are genuinely four.** **Chrysostom’s Greek denies desire to the flesh**: *sarka gar entautha, ou to soma kalei* — flesh here is the earthbound, slothful reasoning; desire belongs to the soul, so a flesh that desires cannot be the body; the body is an instrument, *organon gar estin he sarx*, and *we hate and punish not the iron but the murderer*; the conflict is between two reasonings and not *ouch he psyche kai to soma*. **Marius Victorinus** argues the contrary from the soulless elements: *Habet enim motus suos caro, habetque sensus, neque tantum ab anima suscitantur*. **The Glossa ordinaria** makes the ascription a manner of speaking about a joint act — *Caro dicta est concupiscere, quia hoc secundum ipsam agit anima* — with the cause in neither alone, *ex utroque enim fit*. **Ambrosiaster** shifts the ground entirely: not two parts of man but two laws, *unam Dei, alteram peccati*, with *medius homo* standing between them, the flesh’s works so named by origin (*quia errores isti ex mundo sunt, ex quo et caro*), so that at v. 24 the crucified flesh is the world. **Chrysostom and Victorinus comment on the same appointed verse and cannot both be right.**

**And Jerome supplies the argument from the missal’s own word.** Where the Latin translator put *vitia* the Greek reads *pathemata*, *passiones*, and the Apostle added *desideria* **precisely so as not to seem to deny the nature of the body in spiritual men, but its vices**: *ut non naturam corporis videretur in spiritualibus viris negare, sed vitia*. **A Father defending the appointed word on exactly this ground is better evidence for this claim than any of the others, because it is anchored in the word the missal prints.** Chrysostom adds at the lesson’s last verse that those of Christ did not destroy the flesh — *epei pos emellon zen?* — and that the desires, though they trouble, rage in vain. Aquinas reaches the same conclusion by a different route again, through the definition he cites from Augustine’s *De civitate Dei* XIV.2, that whoever lives according to himself lives according to the flesh, so that *caro* here stands for the whole man — **Aquinas’s citation of that work, and not Augustine’s own wording, the *De civitate Dei* standing unopened behind it.**

**And the pericope itself supplies the convergence these routes point to.** The lesson names only *caro* and *spiritus* and leaves the soul unmentioned; **Chrysostom in Greek and Jerome in Latin independently raise that difficulty and answer it the same way** — the soul lies between and makes the body spiritual or earthly by what it does with it. **The two agree, and nothing shows dependence.**

Augustine supplies a leg from the other direction, and the reception is *controversial*. Certain monks quoted these very verses to argue that they need not work, and *De opere monachorum* was written against them, in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series: “let these persons, who from perverse understanding of the Gospel labor to pervert apostolic precepts, either take no thought for the morrow, even as the birds of the air; or let them obey the Apostle.” The *Catena aurea* points to that work, and the work itself carries the passage. The identification of those monks as the Euchitae stands in *De haeresibus*, which is unopened, and remains an unverified lead.

Gal. 5:24’s *carnem suam crucifixérunt* is read by some as including the body proper, and the Vulgate elsewhere uses *caro* for the body simply; the disjunction is a fact about these two pericopes as printed, **not a lexical law**. **Ambrosiaster’s Latin lemma is not the missal’s** — his fruit-list has ten members, carrying *spes* and *castitas* and lacking *fides* and *patientia*, and his vice-list opens differently — **so his exposition counts a different pair of lists, and the missal’s seventeen and twelve are not the lists he expounds.** Marius Victorinus’s text as Migne prints it passes from v. 17 to 6:1, so he comments on none of vv. 18–24, and whether a critical edition shows the same gap was not checked. Chrysostom’s Greek is a TLG transcription uncollated against *Patrologia Graeca* 61. The Latin vice- and fruit-lists the missal prints are longer than the lists the Greek Fathers expound, so no Greek witness here speaks to *luxúria*, *modéstia* or *cástitas*, words his own list does not contain.

### C3. The tradition is namedly divided about the Gospel’s images, and its most-repeated sentence at the Gospel’s first verse was withdrawn by the man who wrote it

**The division is named, and it is not a difference of emphasis.** On the anti-allegorical side: the Augustine the compilations transmit — *ista documenta non sicut allegorica discutienda sunt* — repeated by the exposition printed under Bede’s name; **Christian of Stavelot refusing allegory at the lilies on frankly aesthetic grounds**, no linen matching the lily’s whiteness, no dye the rose, no purple the violet; Jerome forbidding the birds as angels on a ground internal to the appointed verse, *Simpliciter ergo accipiendum*; and Bruno of Segni reading the lilies literally. On the allegorical side, at its far end: **Godfrey of Admont makes the birds the good angels, the lilies the evil spirits and Solomon Christ**, and then raises and answers the objection his own allegory creates — so he is a self-aware instance and not a naive one. **Hilary allegorises every appointed image into doctrine of the risen body**, holding that the literal reading does not cohere, *non satis propositionibus congruunt*. Chrysostom reads the images as arguments too, but rhetorically engineered ones, the illustrations degraded step by step so the *a fortiori* grows at each — and **where the Fathers agree they still differ**, Augustine’s middle term being the creature’s **rank** and Chrysostom’s its **vileness**, given in terms of *gift* rather than of rank.

**And the sentence the Latin tradition repeats most often at this Gospel’s first verse was withdrawn by its author.** *Nullius enim fere conscientia Deum potest odisse* is retracted at *Retractationes* I.19, where Augustine names *De sermone Domini in monte* II.14.48 — his exposition of the appointed Gospel’s first verse — and gives Ps. 73:23 as his reason, *Superbia eorum qui oderunt te*. **Five later Latin witnesses repeat the sentence without his qualifier *fere*** — Rabanus, the Glossa, Christian of Stavelot, Paschasius and the exposition printed under Bede’s name, one of them so damaged (*scientia* for *conscientia*) that the corruption can only be transmission — **the Glossa alone flags the retraction**, and **Bruno of Segni denies the proposition outright**: the covetous man does hate God, because he serves idols, from Eph. 5:5. **“No one can hate God” is therefore neither the tradition’s settled reading at Mt. 6:24 nor Augustine’s own last word on it.**

**The Communion half, and it is this claim’s second appointed element.** The formulary appoints Mt. 6:33 twice: once as the Gospel’s last verse and again, recast, as the Communion antiphon — the seventh element standing on the sixth locus. On that one verse nobody argues from a lesser example. The reading the compilations transmit under Augustine’s name makes *primum* an ordering of importance and not of time; **Paschasius denies that it orders anything**, and elsewhere in the same place calls the sequence *praeposterus*, the kingdom put first to soothe the hearing before justice is brought in as the laborious thing. **So the two appointed elements that carry Matthew 6 in this Mass receive figure refused at the images and doctrine read at the imperative — and the sentence the missal lifted out to say at the moment of reception is the one on which the tradition reads doctrine and then divides about a single adverb.**

**Beneath it, a textual observation.** The Gospel opens on a conclusion whose premises are not read — the treasure and the eye at Mt. 6:19–23 — and closes one verse before Matthew’s own close at v. 34; and **the supplied incipit narrows the audience to the disciples where Mt. 5:1 has the crowds present.**

**The missal’s recasting promotes *Primum* to the head of the antiphon** — exactly where the argument transmitted under Augustine’s name makes word order carry the doctrine, and no witness read here comments on the antiphon as such. That promotion bears twice over: it measures the extent and antiquity of the recasting, and it separates the two registers in which the appointed Matthaean text is read.

**The Latin Fathers commenting on this very pericope are divided about whether its central images may be allegorised at all** — Hilary allegorises them, Jerome and Augustine forbid it — and page 1’s allegorical row prints that division under the names that hold it.

**Bruno of Segni restricts the whole prohibition of anxiety to apostles, their successors and those aiming at perfection, and Godfrey preaches it to monks about *lectio***, so two of the witnesses read the pericope as addressed to a narrower audience than the Sunday congregation. A Communion antiphon drawn from the day’s Gospel is ordinary practice, so this second element may be a mechanical carry-over. That bears on how *interesting* the join is; it does not dissolve it, because the lesson and the reception are two ritual moments whatever produced the antiphon. **Both elements that carry this claim stand in Matthew 6**, and no third element from another book stands behind it.

### C4. Three strands, three schedules, four reckonings — and the strand that never moved

*The orations of this Mass stand one Sunday later in the Gregorian books and two later in the Frankish Gelasian; the chants carry this Sunday’s own number in the ninth-century antiphonaries; and the Epistle and Gospel stand under a reckoning counted from St Lawrence and were never cut anywhere but where 1962 cuts them.* **No witness says that this Mass stood at a different Sunday**: what moved is one strand of it, and the three strands moved by different amounts or not at all.

**The orations.** Gelasianum Book III runs sixteen sections, and sects. I–XVI correspond to the 1962 Fifth through Twentieth Sundays on a numeration base that counts from Trinity; each section’s 1962 identity was fixed from its own printed collect or Secret on the page image, never from position. **Across sects. I–XI Rheinau, S. Gallen, Gerbert and Menard stand two Sundays later, and across sects. XII–XVI they stand four**, the offset surviving a documented interpolation and resuming a new constant rather than dissolving; **Pamelius stands one later across all sixteen without a break, and Menard agrees with Rheinau, S. Gallen and Gerbert at every readable section and never with him.** This Mass’s own orations are sect. X, inside the run of two. **Gerbert himself is open at his printed p. 173**, headed *Dominica XVI* — in two independently produced Internet Archive optical layers that agree verbatim, under eighteenth-century long-s type and with no page image behind them — and the offset is **already present at the series’ first section**, so nothing inside the series produces it. The Gregorian Hadrianum heads this Mass *DOMINICA .XV. POST PENTECOSTEN*, one later than 1962, **confirmed across eleven consecutive Masses on page images**, and **its sect. XXXIII carries all three orations of this Mass word for word.**

**The chants did not travel with them.** The ninth-century antiphonary formulary carrying this Introit, Gradual and Offertory is numbered *Dominica XIV* — **the same number 1962 gives it** — and the Ottobonianus margin reproduces those formularies **one number above** the sacramentary headings they stand against, at twenty-five of twenty-five slots across five consecutive Masses, the bracketed Alleluia cues matching nothing in 1962. **The cue block whose Introit is *Protector noster* attaches to Wilson’s sect. XXXI, *DOMINICA .XIII. POST PENTECOSTEN*, whose Collect is the 1962 Twelfth Sunday’s:** an offset of two, read on a page image where the superscript reference letters stand before the section numbers and not as part of them.

**The readings never moved.** The *Liber Comitis* as Migne prints it pairs *Fratres, spiritu ambulate, usque concupiscentiis* with *Nemo potest duobus dominis servire, usque adiicientur vobis* under *Dominica v post sancti Laurentii* — **the same incipits and the same termini the 1962 book prints.** So the Epistle–Gospel pair is not among the things that moved.

**Two different mechanisms stand behind the instability.** Wilson’s is a numeration base, *post Pentecosten* against *post Octavas Pentecostes*. **Schuster’s is a Roman cycle built around the feast of St Lawrence**, whose count “was not everywhere the same,” the Lawrence cycle succeeded at Rome by cycles around St Cyprian and St Michael, these feasts serving “as so many milestones … and had no special connection with the saint whose name they bore.” **Four series are now tracked and two of them disagree with each other by one:** the Comes calls this Sunday the fifth after St Lawrence and Schuster’s title for the formulary is *Quarta post Sancti Laurentii*. **Neither mechanism has been shown to produce the offsets Wilson collates, and the arithmetic joining them has not been done.**

**And the reception shows the same displacement twice, independently.** Honorius reads this formulary in one twelfth-century chapter whose Introit, Collect, Epistle and Offertory are the 1962 Fourteenth Sunday’s while his Gospel is the Thirteenth’s and his Gradual, Alleluia and Communion are the Fifteenth’s — **three chants of five displaced, not five** — and he reads the whole formulary twice more, *sub lege* under Ezechias and *sub gratia* under Theodosius, and a third time as calendar, tying the Offertory to the September night office’s *historia* of Tobias and Raphael and to Michaelmas. **Godfrey of Admont expounds this formulary’s Introit, Gradual, Alleluia psalm and Offertory in order and closes by praying its Collect — and his Gospel is the ten lepers.**

Four of the five Gelasian books are unopened at the locus, and for those four the disagreement rests on Wilson’s apparatus; the fifth, Gerbert, is open only in optical text. His footnote at sect. X covers **“the Collects and Secret” only**, and the 1962 Postcommunion has a materially narrower Gelasian base, attested for Rheinau and Gerbert but **not** for S. Gallen, Pamelius or Menard. **The Gelasian displacement is therefore a claim about two of the formulary’s ten elements, and prose treating the three orations as equally attested from that side would be wrong.** The four books agreeing are not four independent witnesses: Gerbert’s principal manuscript cannot now be traced and Wilson holds its “Gelasian” portions to be a revision based on the Gregorian type, while Pamelius is itself an edition of the Gregorian book — **so its reading and the Gregorian numbering are not two witnesses.** **Schuster does not corroborate the displacement:** his three pages on this Mass name no station, no sacramentary siglum and no manuscript, and do not say the Communion is secondary. The antiphonary side is a modern database and not Hesbert’s printed pages, which are unreachable here for a recorded rights reason; the 1962-side identifications are against a repository derivative and not a facsimile collation; the *Liber Comitis*’s own date and provenance are **not established**; and the run of the two-Sunday offset breaks at Dominica XXII and XXIII for a reason nobody has established.

### C5. The formulary makes exactly one substantive exchange, it is at the Communion, it is older than this book, and it broke a pair

The Communion departs from Mt. 6:33 in exactly four ways, verified against the Clementine, the Lucan parallel and the tracked Robinson-Pierpont Greek: *ergo* is dropped and *Primum* promoted to head the clause; *et iustítiam eius* is omitted; *hæc* is dropped before *ómnia*; and *dicit Dóminus* is added. That tag stands in none of Clementine Mt. 6:33, Clementine Lk. 12:31 or Robinson-Pierpont Mt. 6:33. **Nor does the Lucan parallel supply the fronted *primum***: the tracked Byzantine Luke carries neither *proton* nor *kai ten dikaiosynen autou*, so any comparison with Luke stands on the Clementine’s Latin alone and is attributed to it.

Two of the four bear theologically. Adding *dicit Dóminus* turns a saying inside a sermon into **an address made to the communicant at the moment of reception**. Dropping *et iustítiam eius* leaves the kingdom to be sought without the justice the Gospel names beside it.

**The antiphon is secondary in this slot only, and not in the repertory.** *Primum quaerite* is an ancient Roman antiphon standing at the **Ninth** Sunday in four ninth-century antiphonaries, where the Fourteenth Sunday’s Communion is *Panis quem ego dedero*. **And that Communion travels with the Offertory *Immittet Angelus* at both its occurrences** — the Lenten Thursday and this Sunday — **so substituting *Primum quaerite* here broke a pair the older books kept together.** Five witnesses now converge on the substitution: the appointed text itself, this being the formulary’s one substantive departure from its Bible text; Guéranger’s report that the antiphon “was not the one primitively used; the ancient liturgists make no mention of it in its present position”; the Ottobonianus cue block whose Introit is this Mass’s own and whose Communion is *Panis quem ego*; Honorius, whose Communion here is *Panis, quem ego dabo*; and the antiphonary formularies themselves. **Guéranger’s judgment is confirmed in substance from a source he did not have.**

The strongest single control comes from within the same Office and anyone can check it in the same pages: Guéranger prints this Sunday’s Magnificat antiphon at Vespers as *Quaerite primum regnum Dei, et justitiam ejus, et haec omnia adjicientur vobis. Alleluia.* **That antiphon retains both *et iustítiam eius* and *haec* — exactly the two clauses the Mass’s Communion drops — and leaves *primum* in its Matthaean position after the verb.**

**No witness explains why the exchange was made, and no witness dates it.** What is fixed is that it is later than the ninth-century antiphonaries, later than the Ottobonianus margin and later than Honorius, and earlier than the Pustet Missal of Ratisbon, 1862, which prints the recast form already. Guéranger’s judgment rests on Tommasi’s Antiphonary and “the ancient liturgists,” neither consulted. Antiphon texts are routinely shortened for chant and *dicit Dóminus* is a common formulaic tag, so both changes may be conventional rather than expressive, and that count was not run. **The antiphonary evidence is a modern database and not Hesbert’s pages.**

**Two further negatives, and they bound how the Communion may be read.** First, **the Communion carries no eating vocabulary**: the appointed text is *Primum quaerite regnum Dei*, and the verbs of eating that the Offertory’s *Gustate* makes central belong to the Offertory alone and to the Eucharistic reception the antiphon accompanies; nothing in the Communion’s own wording invites a Eucharistic reading. Second, **the law and *adversus* language is confined to the Epistle**: the Communion’s one addition, *dicit Dóminus*, is an address to the communicant and not a legal exclusion. Both negatives are bounded to the appointed elements.

### C6. Two vocabularies partition the Mass, and the silence they produce is a genre fact everywhere but in the readings

*Salut-* and *salv-* occur four times, once each in the Collect (*salutária*), the Alleluia (*salutári*), the Secret (*salutáris*) and the Postcommunion (*salvatiónis*), **and in none of the six other elements**; *propitia-* twice, Collect and Secret only; *semper* and *perpétu-* twice each, Collect and Postcommunion only. **At stem level *salut-* and *regnum* are the only substantive ties reaching three or more elements, and the two partition the formulary cleanly:** *regnum* in the read and sung word, *salut-* in the three orations plus the one chant and in no reading at all. **The people’s texts state the problem as service, trust and anxiety; the priest’s prayers state it as expiation and rescue.**

**The silence about temporal goods is a fact about the genre.** No Collect, Secret or Postcommunion of any of the fifteen 1962 identities this repository has collated — forty-five orations — asks for food, drink or clothing, so this Sunday’s silence about the three goods its Gospel names is not a distinctive of this formulary. **And Roman orations of this season are not uniformly non-temporal:** the Fourth Sunday’s Collect asks that *mundi cursus pacifice nobis tuo ordine dirigatur*, the Nuptial Mass’s Postcommunion asks *longaeva pace*, Trinity Sunday’s asks *ad salutem corporis et animae*, and the Eleventh Sunday’s asks *subsidium mentis et corporis*. **So these orations are silent about temporal goods generally and not only about the Gospel’s three.**

**The singularity lies on the reading side.** Among the same fifteen identities, the Fourteenth Sunday is the **only** one whose appointed texts name eating, drinking *and* clothing together — *manducetis*, *manducabimus*, *esca*, *bibemus*, *induamini*, *operiemur*, *vestimento*, all in the Gospel. **So the objects of petition and the objects of promise differ, and what is distinctive about this Mass is what it reads and not what it asks.**

**And the address differs with the vocabulary.** The three orations never command the assembly, their material addressed to God being otherwise subjunctival; **the one command the Mass repeats at the moment of reception is the promise’s own *Quǽrite*.**

The fifteen identities behind that count are a small and non-random set, so it is evidence about them and not about the *Missale Romanum*. **And *salutáris* of a victim, *salutári* of God, *salutária* of things that save and *salvatiónis* of a state are one root in four senses**, so the four-fold count is a fact about the root and not about a single idea. “Temporal” against “spiritual” is a judgment and not a measurement.

### What this formulary does not say

**The Holy Spirit is named in exactly one of the ten appointed elements.** The *spiritu-* stem occurs five times in the Epistle and nowhere else; the Collect, Secret and Postcommunion never name the Spirit. The printed rubric appoints the Preface of the Most Holy Trinity, so the Mass *as celebrated* names the Spirit where its proper does not, and that is how far into this formulary a Spirit-centred reading reaches. Beyond that, across the same ten elements: no parousia, resurrection or judgment vocabulary past the Epistle’s *regnum Dei non consequéntur* and the Postcommunion’s *perpétuæ salvatiónis*; no saint named and no Marian reference; no penitential, fasting or almsgiving language, despite a seventeen-item vice list; one angel named, in the Offertory, and no other created spirit; and the Church named **once**, in the Collect — though the first-person plurals of Introit, Alleluia, Secret and Postcommunion are corporate in their own way, which softens any contrast built on that count.

**And two apparent ties are not ties.** *Conte-* joins the Epistle’s *contentiónes* to the Gospel’s *contémnet* and *oper-* joins its *ópera carnis* to *quo operiémur*, in each case across two different verbs; and the Introit’s *gloria* is the doxology cue and not the psalm’s word. **No further substantive tie joins two of the ten elements at token level.**

### Two joints the appointed texts do not make

**The Alleluia is not joined to the rest by its own vocabulary.** Its only substantive tie to any other element is *salutári*, and *exsult-* and *iubil-* occur nowhere else in the ten. **No lexical warrant joins the Alleluia’s joy to the Gospel’s anxiety**: the Epistle’s *gaudium* and the Alleluia’s *exsultémus* are different roots in different elements with nothing joining them. **Nor does the reception supply it**: the medieval and later readers of this psalm reached here — a homily of the Chrysostomic corpus, Arnobius the Younger, Bruno the Carthusian and Peter Lombard — all read it penitentially, and every one of them reaches that reading through v. 2, *Praeoccupemus faciem eius in confessione*, which the Alleluia does not appoint.

**Whether the Epistle and the Gospel were ever read together in the tradition.** **The lectionary answers half of it and no witness answers the rest.** The *Liber Comitis* pairs these two lessons at these two extents, so the pairing is old; but the library’s sole registered treatment of Gal. 5:16–24 is Anthony of Padua’s sermon, and his Sunday numbering runs one ahead of the 1962 missal’s, consistently across all three of his registered sermons. **A major medieval preacher expounding this Epistle does so beside a different Gospel.** Every cross-element claim joining Epistle and Gospel is therefore a claim about how the 1962 formulary reads when taken as a whole, and not a claim about how the tradition expounded these two texts together.

## The Propers: Notable and Quotable

Five afterlives of wording from the scriptural propers, each a use that moves the phrase into a register its psalmist or evangelist did not occupy.

> **Communion and Gospel, Mt. 6:33 — the political kingdom**
>
> **Appointed text and locus.** *Ant. ad Communionem*, marginal no. 1580: *Primum quǽrite regnum Dei, et ómnia adiciéntur vobis, dicit Dóminus* — and the same verse closes the appointed Gospel.
>
> **Later text and loci.** Kwame Nkrumah’s motto, “Seek ye first the political kingdom and all other things shall be added unto you.” UK Hansard, HC 3 Dec 1962, debate “Central Africa,” Humphry Berkeley, reporting the inscription on the statue outside the Parliament building in Accra; and HL 19 Dec 1962, col. 1203, Lord Milverton, calling it “a famous place in Accra.” *The Spark* (Accra), no. 159, 5 Nov 1965, on the Ghana Co-operative Movement.
>
> The motto is verbal dependence and not echo: it preserves Matthew’s imperative, its object-plus-addition structure and the King James diction, substituting only “political kingdom” for “kingdom of God” and dropping “and his righteousness,” and it keeps the word *first*, which the Lucan parallel does not carry. **None of the three attestations reads “all these things shall be added,”** which is the King James’s own wording and the form most often quoted secondhand: the two 1962 speakers, in different Houses sixteen days apart, both give “all things will be added unto you,” and *The Spark* gives “all other things shall be added unto you.”
>
> **The turn.** Eschatological counsel against anxiety becomes a nationalist priority-of-sequence argument: take state power first, development follows. The redirection was contested in its own time — Berkeley reports the inscription approvingly, Milverton lists it among the causes of the Federation’s failure. The Mass’s closing antiphon has become, by 1962, a monumental inscription in Accra and a political doctrine debated in both Houses at Westminster. *The Spark* reports what “Osagyefo has repeatedly said” and the two 1962 speakers report an inscription, so what these three witness is the motto’s circulation and not its coinage, which none of them dates. That the antiphon drops *et iustítiam eius* where the motto drops “and his righteousness” is a coincidence.

> **Gospel, Mt. 6:24 — the rule against divided loyalty**
>
> **Appointed text and locus.** Gospel, marginal no. 1577: *Nemo potest duóbus dóminis servíre: aut enim unum ódio habébit, et álterum díliget: aut unum sustinébit, et álterum contémnet. Non potéstis Deo servíre, et mammónæ.*
>
> **Later text and loci.** *Everhart v. Searle*, 71 Pa. 256, 258 (1872), which opens on “the authority of Holy Writ” and quotes the whole antithesis; *United States v. Mississippi Valley Generating Co.*, 364 U.S. 520, 549–550 (1961), grounding 18 U.S.C. §434 in “the Biblical admonition that no man may serve two masters, Matt. 6:24, a maxim which is especially pertinent if one of the masters happens to be economic self-interest.” In the same line: *Knights of Pythias v. Withers*, 177 U.S. 260 (1900); *SEC v. Capital Gains Research Bureau*, 375 U.S. 180 (1963); *NLRB v. Health Care & Retirement Corp.*, 511 U.S. 571 (1994).
>
> The dependence is explicit and self-declared: *Everhart* names the source, “the authority of Holy Writ,” and *Mississippi Valley* prints the Matthaean locus itself. The courts quote the King James against the Vulgate’s *aut unum ódio habébit … aut unum sustinébit, et álterum contémnet.*
>
> **The turn.** Institutional and legal. In Matthew the sentence claims the impossibility of divided religious allegiance and is urged as a reason not to be anxious. In law it becomes a **prophylactic rule of positive obligation that operates without proof of wrongdoing**: the rule “is not intended to be remedial of actual wrong, but preventive of the possibility of it” where “no fraud \[was\] meditated and no injury done.” **A saying about God and money is made to disqualify a dual agent who has in fact behaved honestly**, and it does that work from *Everhart* in 1872 to the Supreme Court in 1961.
>
> Two attributions inside those opinions belong to other mouths. The “not intended to be remedial of actual wrong” sentence is *Everhart* *quoting Hare and Wallace*, not the court speaking in its own voice; and “the authoritative declaration” is the *Court of Claims’* phrase, quoted by the Supreme Court, and not the Supreme Court’s own.
>
> **Two lines of use run side by side and are not the same line.** The conflict-of-interest line from *Everhart* to *Mississippi Valley* stops at the general maxim; a separate and larger body of state and bankruptcy decisions — at least eighteen opinions, at least six of them printing the Matthaean citation expressly — quotes the mammon clause and names Matthew, so the second master is not left unnamed in American law. **Identity is delicate throughout:** the King James antithesis and the mammon clause are word-identical at Lk. 16:13, so only an express Matthaean locus secures a candidate, and at least one opinion prints “Luke 16, 13” instead.

> **Epistle, Gal. 5:23 — “against such there is no law,” by pun**
>
> **Appointed text and locus.** Epistle, marginal no. 1574: *Advérsus huiúsmodi non est lex.*
>
> **Later text and loci.** *Johnston v. Commonwealth*, 22 Pa. 102, 111 (1853), construing a Sunday-observance act: “visiting and administering to the sick and destitute … Certain it is, that against such there is no law, and they may be performed on any day.” Its companion case *Omit v. Commonwealth*, 21 Pa. 426 (1853), supplies the statutory language. *Sullens v. State*, 191 Miss. 856 (1941), on the criminal contempt of a newspaper editor: “the brambles of bad taste which fringe the border lines of discretion. Against such there is no law.”
>
> Neither court names Galatians, and both reproduce the clause word for word in the King James form — “against such there is no law,” where the Vulgate as appointed reads *Advérsus huiúsmodi non est lex* — which is unattributed borrowing and not citation.
>
> **The turn, and it is a pun.** **The pivot is on the word “law.”** Paul’s point is that the twelve fruits of the Spirit stand outside the *condemning* reach of the Mosaic law; *Johnston*’s is that works of charity and necessity stand outside the *prohibiting* reach of a Pennsylvania penal statute against Sunday labour; *Sullens* pushes it further, to conduct improper yet legally uncompellable. A theological claim about justification is converted into a canon of statutory construction. It is the gallery’s only Epistle entry, and the two opinions are the only two the American case-law corpus carries.

> **Gospel, Mt. 6:28–29 — Solomon was not arrayed**
>
> **Appointed text and locus.** Gospel, marginal no. 1577: *Consideráte lília agri quómodo crescunt: non labórant, neque nent. Dico autem vobis, quóniam nec Sálomon in omni glória sua coopértus est sicut unum ex istis.*
>
> **Later text and loci.** *The Progressive Farmer* (Winston/Raleigh, N.C.), 15 Jan 1889, p. 8, “Solomon and his glory,” reprinting the rural editor of *Farm, Field and Stockman*: the soprano, then bass, then alto, then tenor each declare in turn that “Solomon … was not arrayed,” until “the feelings of the congregation had been harrowed up sufficiently and our sympathies all aroused for poor Solomon, whose numerous wives allowed him to go about in such a fashion even in that climate” — and when “like one of these” finally arrives, “by ‘these’ one naturally concluded that the choir was designed.” **The joke does not end there:** it goes on to ask whether Solomon wore “a Prince Albert or cutaway coat … an eye-glass and mustache, his hair cut Pompadour,” and answers “No, most decidedly.” *The Spanish American* (Roy, N.M.), 11 Apr 1908, p. 9, “A freeze out,” credited to the *Illustrated Sunday Magazine*: at Hayville a singer breaks down at “Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed,” and a voice from the freezing audience remarks, “Bad fix for Solomon on a night like this.”
>
> Both quote the clause explicitly, the 1889 piece naming it “that scriptural poem,” and both turn on the King James wording and on its being interrupted before “like one of these.” The 1889 piece’s “the lilies of the field” is Matthew’s phrase and not Luke’s, which is what fixes the identity; the clause itself is word-identical at Lk. 12:27, so the 1908 item is identity-neutral standing alone.
>
> **The turn.** **Humorous, and in both cases the humour is produced by the wording itself rather than attached to it.** The 1889 sketch is a joke about syntax: a comparative clause severed from its second term turns a statement about Solomon’s inferiority to a wildflower into a statement that Solomon was *unclothed*, the delayed “these” is then mis-referred to the choir, and **the appointed verse finishes as a satire on the tailoring of an aesthetic congregation.** In 1908 the printed em-dashes inside the singer’s quotation are the typographic mark of the interruption the joke turns on. The setting is the occasion and the appointed wording is what is played with.
>
> **Both items are reprints** — the 1889 piece credits *Farm, Field and Stockman* and the 1908 piece the *Illustrated Sunday Magazine*, and the wording quoted above is the reprinting paper’s and not either original’s — and they are two attestations of one joke-type rather than two independent inventions.

> **Gradual, Ps. 117:9 — princes, or railroads**
>
> **Appointed text and locus.** Gradual, marginal no. 1575: *Bonum est confídere in Dómino, quam confídere in hómine. V Bonum est speráre in Dómino, quam speráre in princípibus.*
>
> **Later text and locus.** *Semi-weekly interior journal* (Stanford, Ky.), 19 Nov 1895, p. 2, a travel letter datelined “Atlanta, Nov. 16”: “It is better to serve the Lord than to put confidence in princes or railroads, especially the latter. I thought when I got on the ‘Exposition Flyer,’ my trials were ended, but alas for human hopes.” The engine then broke down midway between stations five miles apart and refused to turn a wheel, a flagman had to go two and a half miles afoot for another, and the writer reached Chattanooga two and a quarter hours late.
>
> The writer quotes the second half of the appointed Gradual verse almost intact, unattributed, and then adds to it — which only works if the reader recognises where the sentence was going to stop.
>
> **The turn.** **Humorous and idiomatic, achieved by a single appended noun.** The psalm’s antithesis is between divine and human reliability, with “princes” standing for the highest human power; the letter keeps the grammar and slips a railway company into the princes’ place, so a verse about the vanity of political trust becomes a traveller’s grumble about the Exposition Flyer. “Especially the latter” is what makes it a redirection rather than a quotation: it ranks the railroad below princes. In the American case-law reports and the American English-language newspapers the Gradual is the least quoted element of this formulary.
>
> **The letter’s verb is “serve” where the King James reads “trust,”** and it stands so on the printed page, the writer’s or the compositor’s substitution rather than a fault of transcription. The rest matches King James Ps. 118:9 word for word, and the identity is clean, the near-twin “Put not your trust in princes” being another psalm and reading differently. The paper is an unremarkable local one and the writer is unnamed: the entry’s value is the neatness of the redirection, not the standing of its author.

## The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities

> **What this section is**
>
> Everything below is exploratory editorial and AI proposal. None of it is sourced historical intent, attributed teaching, or documented reception, and none of it should be quoted as any of those. Each proposal joins at least two precisely named appointed elements and ends on the strongest thing that stands against it. **No connection here is claimed to be unknown, unprecedented, first, or authored by the model**; where a proposal and a checked witness conflict, the witness wins. > **Editorial proposal — P1. Goods are ordered, not refused**
>
> **Anchors:** Introit <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>, *mélior est dies una in átriis tuis super mília*; Gradual <span class="nodecor">(*Grad.*)</span>, *bonum est confídere in Dómino, quam confídere in hómine*; Gospel <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>, *nemo potest duóbus dóminis servíre* and *non potéstis Deo servíre et mammónæ*; Communion <span class="nodecor">(*Comm.*)</span>, *Primum*; Collect <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span>
>
> **Mechanism:** The formulary states one choice in two grammatical registers. Six comparatives of degree in four Latin constructions stand in exactly three of the ten elements — the Introit’s *melior … super mília*, the Gradual’s *Bonum est … quam* twice, the Gospel’s *plus quam* twice, its *Nonne … magis pluris* and its *quanto magis* — while the Epistle, the three orations, the Alleluia, the Offertory and the Communion carry none. The readings state the same choice **exclusively**: *hæc enim sibi ínvicem adversántur*, no man can serve two masters, you cannot serve God and mammon. **The Gospel sits on both sides of the split**, its *Ideo* making the exclusive premise the ground of the comparative argument, and the comparative register is what keeps the exclusive one from reading as contempt for created goods.
>
> **Fruit:** A doctrine of ordered preference preached without contempt for creation, and the reason this Mass can command an absolute exclusion and still promise that all things shall be added.
>
> **What the element-by-element reading misses:** Read one element at a time, the chants look like decoration around two hard sayings. Read together, the two registers are doing different grammatical work on the same choice.
>
> **Controlling limit:** **The defeater is not a modern philological worry: a named medieval expositor stands on both sides of it.** The *quam* of Ps. 117:8–9 may render a Hebrew idiom of *exclusion* rather than of degree, and *super mília* is a Hebraism too, so two of the six comparatives may not be comparatives at all — only the Gospel’s four are Greek-derived and immune. **Bruno the Carthusian asks exactly that question about exactly these words, and answers it twice**: first that the word is not set down as a comparative, so that trust in the Lord is useful and recourse to the persecutor is not; then that it is comparative, **but only if the princes are heavenly spirits and spiritual men**, on the ground that God can help without them and they cannot help without God. His wording is not quoted here, at either answer, the passage standing in a column this guide may not quote until a page image is read. **So this proposal is stated as contingent on the comparative reading of the Latin *quam*, and on Bruno’s second answer rather than his first; nothing in a Latin distribution can settle which is right.** Secondly, the lexical evidence reaches only part of the anchor set — **the Communion and the Collect are thematic members here and not lexical ones**, and that is said rather than blurred. Thirdly, this must not be built as “the chants soften the readings”: it is one unit, not a two-sided contrast.

> **Editorial proposal — P2. What man cannot add**
>
> **Anchors:** Gospel <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>, Mt. 6:27 *Quis autem vestrum cógitans potest adícere ad statúram suam cúbitum unum?* and Mt. 6:33 *adiciéntur*; Communion <span class="nodecor">(*Comm.*)</span>, *adiciéntur*; Collect <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span>, *quia sine te lábitur humána mortálitas*; Epistle <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>, *ut non quæcúmque vultis, illa faciátis*
>
> **Mechanism:** One verb carries the exchange. The Gospel denies that any man can *add* a cubit to his stature and then promises that all things *shall be added*; the Communion repeats the promise alone; and the Collect and the Epistle supply the same incapacity in other words. **What man cannot add to himself, God adds to him**, and the reversal is in the morphology — active of the man who cannot, passive of the God who does.
>
> **Fruit:** A doctrine of grace read off a single verb, and the reason the Mass’s petition and its promise are one movement rather than two.
>
> **What the element-by-element reading misses:** Both occurrences of the verb sit inside one pericope, so an element-by-element reading files them as a feature of Matthew rather than as the axis on which the Collect’s diagnosis and the Communion’s promise turn.
>
> **Controlling limit:** **More of this is documented reception than proposal, and the proposal is what is left when that is subtracted.** Aquinas already reads the cubit as an argument from providence at the same verse; pseudo-Bede reads the Communion’s own verb, *Non dixit nobis dari, sed adiici*; and Christian of Stavelot supplies its elided object with its limit, *Ad necessitatem, non ad superfluitatem*. **What remains exploratory is only the cross-element join** — the incapacity at v. 27 to the promise six verses later, to the Communion’s repetition of the promise alone, and to the Collect’s *sine te lábitur humána mortálitas*. **Neither the Collect nor the Epistle uses the verb**, so two of the four members join doctrinally and not lexically, **a weaker join than P4’s and stated as such.** A second edition of the 1962 book spells the root with a doubled *-ii-* in all three places; the difference does not touch the argument, and that reading was taken from an optical text layer with **no page image opened for it.**

> **Editorial proposal — P3. Propitiation and salvation saturate the prayers the people do not sing**
>
> **Anchors:** Collect <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span>, *propitiatióne perpétua*; Secret <span class="nodecor">(*Sec.*)</span>, *tuæ propitiátio potestátis*; Postcommunion <span class="nodecor">(*Postcomm.*)</span>, *perpétuæ … salvatiónis efféctum*; Alleluia <span class="nodecor">(*All.*)</span>, *Deo salutári nostro*
>
> **Mechanism:** The three orations and the Alleluia carry vocabulary no other element does — *propitiátio* twice, the *salut-* and *salv-* root four times, *semper* twice, *perpétu-* twice — so **the sung and read elements state the problem in the language of service, trust and anxiety while the priest’s own prayers state it in the language of expiation and rescue.** The Alleluia’s *salutári* is the single word tying the day’s one purely jubilant chant into that thread.
>
> **Fruit:** The Mass has two registers of address, and a reader who follows only the readings hears one of them. It also explains why the Alleluia, otherwise the least integrated element of the formulary, is not simply decorative.
>
> **What the element-by-element reading misses:** Read in order, the orations are three short prayers in a conventional idiom. Read together and against the chants, they are the only place in the Mass where the problem is named as expiation.
>
> **Controlling limit:** **Severe, and it cannot be closed here.** This is the ordinary vocabulary of Roman collects, so the saturation may be a fact about the genre and not about this Mass — and **the counter-test that would settle it cannot be run**: a lexical concordance over the orations of the whole Missal would have to run against an optical layer that demonstrably undercounts, so its result would not be evidence. What can be said is bounded to the fifteen formularies collated here: the substantive *propitiátio* appears in the appointed Latin of **no other** of them, independently reproduced in a second provider’s collation, **but the wider *propiti-* sweep hits eleven of the fifteen**, so the claim is about the substantive and **collapses if restated about the propitiation word-family generally.** **And the four-fold *salut-* count is one root in four senses** — of a victim, of God, of things that save and of a state. Finally, **this proposal is invisible in the guide’s own English witness**: the registered 1861 rendering drops *propitiátio* at the Collect, turns it into a verb at the Secret, and drops both *semper* and the substantive at the Postcommunion, so **every measurement supporting it is conducted on the Latin**. That is how the 1861 translator rendered the three prayers, read on the printed page at pp. 425 and 427, and not a doubt about what he wrote.

> **Editorial proposal — P4. The hope-formula lies past the cut, and is handed on at another Mass**
>
> **Anchors:** Introit <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>, Ps. 83:10–11a with vv. 2–3a; Gradual <span class="nodecor">(*Grad.*)</span>, Ps. 117:8–9; Offertory <span class="nodecor">(*Off.*)</span>, Ps. 33:8–9a
>
> **Mechanism:** The formula *beatus … qui sperat* stands in exactly two verses of the Clementine Vulgate, and they are the two psalms this Mass takes its Offertory and its Introit from: *beatus vir qui sperat in eo* at Ps. 33:9b and *beatus homo qui sperat in te* at Ps. 83:13, the two differing in what is hoped in, so that no one quoted form stands at both. It lies **past the Introit antiphon’s cut at v. 11a and past its psalm verse’s cut at v. 3a**; it lies **one clause past the Offertory’s cut at v. 9a**, in the second half of the very verse the antiphon ends on; and **the Gradual alone is uncut and sings the hope vocabulary outright**, the *sper-* and *confid-* stems standing twice each in the Gradual block and not at all in the Introit. Three appointed excerpts stop short of the formula and a fourth sings the vocabulary it is made of. **The Introit belongs because the formula lies beyond both its boundaries, not because it uses hope vocabulary — which it does not.**
>
> **Fruit:** The formulary’s chief coherence is a shared editorial boundary, and it is verifiable rather than thematic. It also gives the Gradual, the day’s plainest maxim, a structural role it does not obviously have.
>
> **The control the corpus supplies:** **Six Sundays earlier the same Psalm 33 verse is appointed with the hope clause this Offertory stops before.** The Eighth Sunday after Pentecost’s Communion prints Ps. 33:9 whole — *Gustate et videte, quoniam suavis est Dominus: beatus vir, qui sperat in eo*. Across the twenty-one collated text records covering fifteen distinct 1962 identities, the denominator **Appendix: Scope and Qualifications** states, *sperat in eo* stands in the appointed Latin of the **Eighth Sunday only** and *gustate* in the Eighth and the Fourteenth only — a count over those collated records and not over the Missal. So the beatitude of the one who hopes is not absent from the repertory; it is assigned to the other Mass.
>
> **Controlling limit:** **The strongest limit is that the antiphons’ extents are inherited chant tradition and nothing establishes that anything was cut in order to suppress it.** Duller explanations are available — metrical length, the ordinary bounds of an antiphon — and **the chant repertories that would show whether these extents are stable across the tradition were not reached, this repository holding none.** Secondly, *confidere* is a trust word rather than a hope word strictly: a stricter count of *spes* vocabulary alone finds *sperare* twice in the Gradual and nothing else, a weaker statement. Thirdly, the Eighth Sunday’s Communion prints Ps. 33:9 whole with the hope clause in the Pustet Ratisbon 1862 and the Venice 1570 text layers, both tracked here and both searched rather than inspected, **neither collated against a page image.** Fourthly, Ambrose quotes the fuller verse *with* the hope clause in the fourth century, which sharpens the observation about the Roman cut but says nothing about why it was made.

> **Editorial proposal — P5. Two chants, two divisions: the Fathers disagree at the Offertory and at the Gradual, and the formulary resolves neither**
>
> **Anchors:** Offertory <span class="nodecor">(*Off.*)</span>, Ps. 33:8–9a, *Immíttet Angelus Dómini … gustáte et vidéte*; Gradual <span class="nodecor">(*Grad.*)</span>, Ps. 117:8–9, *quam speráre in princípibus*
>
> **Mechanism:** The Fathers divide at each of the two chants, and the two divisions are **independent** of each other; the Mass prints both without adjudicating. At the **Offertory** the angel is read four ways — Augustine’s Christ, the Angel of great counsel; Cassiodorus’s covert working and moral sense, on the very lemma Augustine denounced; Arnobius’s angel of the sepulchre; Godfrey’s gift of grace — while the same verse is attested by the *Mystagogical Catecheses* and by Ambrose as the Church’s *communion* invitation, and the 1962 book assigns it to the *offertory*. At the **Gradual** Augustine escalates the excluded second term to **good angels** — “for angels also are called princes,” in the English of the *Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, first series — making the exclusion absolute rather than moral, against Theodoret’s temporary earthly rulers and Godfrey’s interior and exterior senses. **The angel who is the Mass’s rescuer at one chant is the Mass’s excluded object of trust at the other, and the Fathers reached each position separately.**
>
> **Fruit:** A cross-chant tension the Fathers themselves hold and the formulary’s own arrangement stages; and a way to treat the Offertory’s angel that neither flattens the christological identification nor asserts it.
>
> **What the element-by-element reading misses:** Each division is reported under its own chant as a local disagreement. Set side by side, they are the same word doing opposite work at two moments of one Mass.
>
> **Controlling limit:** **Both divisions are deeper than when this was drafted, and one of them may be shallower than it looks.** **Arnobius’s angel of the sepulchre depends on repointing *timentium eum* as those fearing *for* his body, which is not what the missal’s Latin says.** **And the Gradual’s angelic reading may be one position with three carriers rather than three witnesses**: Peter Lombard puts it under Augustine’s name with Daniel as warrant, Prosper’s exposition is largely an epitome of Augustine, and Prosper’s reason is Lombard’s reason — **so no votes are counted here, and the check that would settle it, Augustine’s own *Enarratio* on that psalm, has not been made.** Bruno the Carthusian’s question is a fifth thing and is grammatical rather than doctrinal. **The cross-relation between the Gradual’s absolute exclusion and the Gospel’s *Nemo potest duóbus dóminis servíre* is this guide’s observation and not Augustine’s: he does not cite Mt. 6:24 at that locus**, and the Gospel is accordingly not an anchor here. The Offertory half carries three further limits: Augustine’s text differs from the missal’s at the following verb, *éruet* against *erípiet*, which is evidence that the missal’s text is **not** simply his; the missal’s capital *Angelus* is orthography and **does not encode** his identification; and the attribution of the *Mystagogical Catecheses* to Cyril of Jerusalem is disputed in modern scholarship and that question was not swept.

> **Editorial proposal — P6. The one verb the Mass appoints twice, with its object reversed**
>
> **Anchors:** Introit <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>, the psalm verse, *concupíscit, et déficit ánima mea in átria Dómini*; Epistle <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>, *caro enim concupíscit advérsus spíritum* and *carnem suam crucifixérunt cum vítiis et concupiscéntiis*
>
> **Mechanism:** *Concupiscere* is appointed three times in two elements with its object reversed. In the Introit’s psalm verse the soul longs *toward* the courts of the Lord and faints for them; in the Epistle the flesh desires *against* the spirit, and what is crucified is the flesh *with its desires*. **One verb states both the movement the Mass asks for and the movement it asks to be delivered from**, and the difference between them is the direction of the longing rather than its intensity.
>
> **Fruit:** Desire is not the enemy in this formulary; its object is. That gives the Epistle’s mortification a positive term inside the same Mass and keeps the crucifixion of the flesh from being read as the extinction of longing.
>
> **What the element-by-element reading misses:** The Introit’s occurrence sits in the psalm verse, which an element-by-element reading treats as the antiphon’s setting rather than as appointed vocabulary; the Epistle’s two are read as Pauline anthropology. Nothing brings the three together.
>
> **Controlling limit:** **Two limits, both real, and a third that forbids the reinforcement one would want.** The two senses are the ordinary senses of an ordinary Latin verb, **so the opposition may be a coincidence of the Vulgate’s vocabulary before it is a compositional choice, and no compiler’s intention is claimed.** The Introit’s occurrence is in the **psalm verse** and not the antiphon, so it is liable to the objection that a psalm verse is not appointed in the same sense the antiphon is. **And the second lexical leg that would strengthen it may not be used here:** *ánima* stands in the same Introit clause, three words from *concupíscit*, and again in the Gospel with the valuation reversed — **but that is an Introit–Gospel conjunction, which the precedent search behind this section did not reach, so it is recorded in `research/scope.md` as a lead and is not an anchor here.**

### Evidence-state discipline

### Search limits

**A search that finds nothing has not established that nothing is there; it has established what the search did.** Five traps met in this research resolve successfully and *wrongly*, and every negative in this guide is bounded against them.

**Optical hyphenation.** A whole-word search over the tracked Missal misses an occurrence of the Offertory broken across a line end, and a search of forty-seven newspaper issues for a two-word phrase returned zero while the phrase was present, broken the same way. **The bound on the missal payloads is worse than that:** four appointed phrases tested return zero from all nine payloads while the same books demonstrably print them, so **single words and fragments carry weight there and multi-word phrases carry almost none**, and no whole-phrase negative over those artifacts is reported here as absence.

**A phrase index that knows one orthography.** The exact-phrase index of *Patrologia Latina* makes a new class of negative statable — that a phrase does not occur in that whole collection — **but only in Migne’s orthography and only where the phrase is unbroken in the unit indexed.** **No patristic or medieval exegesis of the Secret *Concéde nobis, Dómine* or of the Postcommunion *Puríficent semper et múniant* stands anywhere that index reaches**, every hit on their distinctive phrases being a sacramentary printing the prayer — **two of the ten appointed elements with no documented exegetical reception in any period.** **The Collect stands outside that negative**, Honorius citing it by incipit, Godfrey of Admont glossing it word by word and Schuster commenting on it continuously. The negative on those two prayers carries exactly the bound above.

**Phrase-search degradation.** The newspaper service’s quoted-string search **does not do exact-phrase matching for stopword-heavy strings; it degrades to an AND of terms.** This was established by calibration, one test query returning tens of thousands of items whose first-ranked result did not contain the clause at all. **Every count from that corpus in this guide is an AND count and never a phrase count, and every negative is bounded to the ranked results actually checked.** **That corpus is thin after 1922 and effectively ends about 1963, and only English-language American newspapers were searched**: no British, Irish, Continental or Latin-American press, and no non-English, book, periodical or musical-repertory corpus, was searched for any element. The case-law corpus is **overwhelmingly American** and no Commonwealth sweep was run.

**A partial file that looks complete.** Two collections have now been met in which a work’s title promises the whole and the file carries a fragment — Cassiodorus in one web library, and Chrysostom on the Psalms in a Greek one. **A title is not a table of contents.**

**The shared incipit, the running head, and the fused reference letter.** *Protector noster aspice, Deus* opens this Mass’s Introit, another Sunday’s Gradual and a composed collect that quotes no psalm at all, so **a search hit on an incipit proves nothing on its own.** A running head in either of Wilson’s editions names the section the page *reaches*, not the one it opens in, and two loci in this guide were misread that way before being corrected against the section headings themselves. **And an optical layer fuses a superscript footnote reference letter with the section numeral that follows it**, which is how a section CXXXIII was manufactured out of *cXXXIII* and how the Ottobonianus chant cues were briefly attached to the wrong Mass. **Both were corrected against page images.**

**Three summary states are distinguished on page 2 and they are not the same thing**, which is the distinction this appendix owes that sheet. *Summarised by choice* is a public-domain source this guide could quote and does not, generally because its bytes are not retained and the wording would be this guide’s own. *Summarise-only* is a source whose rights are recorded *unresolved* — a recorded non-determination and not a restriction, treated conservatively here as though it were one. *Summary compelled* is a source whose rights are recorded *restricted*.
