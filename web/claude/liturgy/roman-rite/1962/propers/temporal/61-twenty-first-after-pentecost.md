The war God bounds, the debt forgiven from the heart, and the Church’s exodus:<br>
a study of the proper in three readings

## The Will None Resists, and a Servant Who Would Not Forgive {#sec:opening}

The Mass of the Twenty-first Sunday after Pentecost, *Dominica vigesima prima post Pentecosten*, opens with a creed spoken under sentence of death. Its Introit is taken from the prayer of Mardochai in the Greek chapters of the Book of Esther (Esth 13:9–11), prayed when the king’s decree, obtained by Aman, had condemned the Jews of the Persian empire to destruction. The 1861 Philadelphia hand missal renders its first sentence “All things, O Lord, are in thy power: and no one can resist thy will”; the antiphon goes on to confess God the maker of heaven and earth and of all that the compass of heaven contains, and ends *Dominus universorum tu es*. Its verse is the first of the longest psalm, “Blessed are the undefiled in the way, who walk in the law of the Lord.” The Collect asks God to keep his family by his continual mercy, *continua pietate*, free under his protection from every adversity and devoted to his name in good works. The Epistle, from the last chapter of Ephesians, arms the Church: “our wrestling is not against flesh and blood; but against principalities and powers, against the rulers of the world of this darkness,” and so the Christian is to take the whole armour of God, “that you may be able to resist in the evil day.” The Gradual answers with the opening of Ps 89: “Lord, thou hast been our refuge” from generation to generation, “from eternity and to eternity thou art God.” The Alleluia sings the first verse of the Exodus psalm, “When Israel went out of Egypt, the house of Jacob from a barbarous people.”

The Gospel is the parable of the unforgiving servant (Mt 18:23–35). A king settles accounts with his servants; one owes ten thousand talents, cannot pay, is ordered to be sold with his wife and children, falls down and begs, “Have patience with me, and I will pay thee all,” and is let go with the whole debt forgiven. Going out, he meets a fellow servant who owes him a hundred pence, takes him by the throat, hears the same words, “Have patience with me, and I will pay thee all,” and casts him into prison. The king calls him back: “Thou wicked servant, I forgave thee all the debt, because thou besoughtest me: shouldst not thou then have had compassion also on thy fellow servant, even as I had compassion on thee?” He hands him to the torturers until he pays all the debt. “So also shall my heavenly Father do to you, if you forgive not every one his brother from your hearts.” The Offertory then tells, in a narrative of its own, how Satan asked to tempt Job, “and power was given him by the Lord over his possessions, and over his flesh,” in the words of the 1861 hand missal. The Secret offers the sacrifice by which God willed to be appeased and to restore salvation by his powerful mercy. The Communion takes three half-verses of the eleventh strophe of Ps 118: the soul that has hoped in God’s word asks, “when wilt thou judge those who persecute me?” and begs his help. The Postcommunion asks that the food of immortality received with the mouth may be followed with a pure mind.

A sovereign will that no one resists, and an adversary who asks for power and is given it; a debtor forgiven without measure who will not forgive a little; a people brought out of Egypt that asks when its persecutors will be judged. Three questions rise from these texts, and each gives one of the readings that follow. The first asks who the Christian’s enemy is, and how the adversary’s work can stand within a will that none resists. St Gregory the Great answers from Job that God looses the enemy and bridles him, permitting for the proving of his servant what the devil asks for his ruin; St John Chrysostom, from the Epistle, that the spirits who work in the persecutor are the real enemy, so that the wronged man is to turn his anger on the devil and not on his brother. The second asks what the king’s mercy requires of the forgiven, and whether a pardon once given can return. Chrysostom answers that the servant revoked the gift himself by his cruelty; St Augustine, that God calls back what he remitted from the one who will not forgive, and that forgiveness from the heart leaves room for correction given in love. The third asks when the judgment that the Communion asks for will come, and where that leaves the people that sings this Mass in the meantime. Augustine hears in the plea the martyrs’ cry under the altar, answered with long-suffering until their brethren are complete; Chrysostom reads the girded loins of the Epistle as the girding of the Passover eaten on the night of the Exodus, and the Christian life as a march toward departure. Every element of the formulary has a place in each reading, and each closes with four senses of its own, literal, allegorical, moral and anagogical.

The Sunday is of the second class in the Proper of Time of the 1962 *Missale Romanum*. In 2026 it falls on 18 October, which the calendar gives to St Luke the Evangelist, a feast of the second class. A Sunday of the second class is preferred to a feast of the same class that is not a feast of the Lord (*Rubricae generales* 16), and St Luke is commemorated wherever the rubrics admit an ordinary commemoration: in every low Mass and in a conventual Mass (RG 108, 111 b; *Rubricae generales Missalis* 434 b). There his Collect, Secret and Postcommunion follow the Sunday’s, each under its own conclusion (RGMR 437 a), and nothing else of his formulary comes with them, not even the Preface of the Apostles (RGMR 483). A sung Mass that is not conventual, such as a parish *Missa cantata*, admits no such commemoration (RG 111 a; RGMR 434 a). The date carries a second addition. The eighteenth is the penultimate Sunday of October, the day the general rubrics give to the missions, and on it the oration *pro Fidei propagatione* is joined to the Collect, the Secret and the Postcommunion of the Mass, each time under one conclusion with the Sunday’s prayer, in every Mass, sung or low (RGMR 444 c, 445–446, 453), unless the Ordinary of the place has appointed another day for the missions. Its Collect begins *Deus, qui omnes homines vis salvos fieri*, the words of 1 Tim 2:4, and so it is prayed on this Sunday beside an Introit that says no one can resist God’s will. Neither the commemoration nor the added oration belongs to the formulary studied here. The Mass is said in green (RG 127 b), with the Gloria, with the Credo the rubrics require on every Sunday (RGMR 475 a), and with the Preface of the Most Holy Trinity, which the Missal prints as a direction after the Secret and the general rubrics appoint for the Sundays of the second class outside the Christmas and Easter seasons (RGMR 494 b).

## The Appointed Texts {#sec:appointed-texts}

The Latin below is that of the 1962 typical edition, *Missale Romanum* (Typis Polyglottis Vaticanis), pp. 413–415, nos. 1699–1708, read on the page images of the CMAA facsimile. Every element’s wording already stands in the Pustet *Missale Romanum* of Ratisbon, 1862 (pp. 354–355); the two books differ only in spelling, accent, pointing, the form of the citations, the length of the printed conclusions, and the additional orations that the 1862 book prints under the rubrics of its day and the 1962 book no longer carries. The English of the scriptural elements is the Douay–Rheims Bible in Bishop Challoner’s revision, which translates the Clementine Vulgate at the Missal’s own Vulgate numbering. It is given at the canonical verses, so that wherever a chant departs from its verse the English and the Latin part company; each such place is named beneath the text. Where the Douay–Rheims cannot carry the Missal’s form at all, as in the Offertory and the Communion, the English is that of the anonymous translation printed in *The Roman Missal, Translated into the English Language for the Use of the Laity* (Philadelphia: Eugene Cummiskey, 1861), which also supplies the English of the three orations. Neither English is an approved liturgical translation of the 1962 Missal. Psalms are cited in the Missal’s numbering; the Hebrew numbering follows in parentheses.

### Introit {#proper-introit}

*Antiphona ad Introitum*, printed citation *Esth. 13, 9 et 10-11*; verse Ps 118:1 (Heb. 119). Missal p. 413, no. 1699.

> *In voluntáte tua, Dómine, univérsa sunt pósita, et non est qui possit resístere voluntáti tuæ: tu enim fecísti ómnia, cælum et terram, et univérsa quæ cæli ámbitu continéntur: Dóminus universórum tu es. <span class="nodecor">Ps. 118, 1</span> Beáti immaculáti in via: qui ámbulant in lege Dómini. ℣. Glória Patri.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Esth 13:9–11, the source of the antiphon</span>
>
> And said: O Lord, Lord, almighty king, for all things are in thy power, and there is none that can resist thy will, if thou determine to save Israel. Thou hast made heaven and earth and all things that are under the cope of heaven. Thou art Lord of all, and there is none that can resist thy majesty.

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Ps 118:1, the verse</span>
>
> Blessed are the undefiled in the way, who walk in the law of the Lord.

The antiphon is not a continuous text of the Vulgate, and the Douay–Rheims above translates Mardochai’s prayer as the Clementine Vulgate gives it, not the Missal’s antiphon. The Vulgate reads *in ditione enim tua cuncta sunt posita*; the Missal begins *In voluntate tua … universa sunt posita*, so that the will which no one resists is named twice, in the first clause and in the second. The Missal puts *resistere voluntati tuae* in another order, adds *enim* and *omnia* (*tu enim fecisti omnia*), reads *universa quae … continentur* for *quidquid … continetur* and *Dominus universorum tu es* for *Dominus omnium es*. It leaves out the vocative *Domine, Domine rex omnipotens*, the condition *si decreveris salvare Israel*, “if thou determine to save Israel”, and the closing *nec est qui resistat maiestati tuae*. The 1861 hand missal renders the antiphon’s opening sentence “All things, O Lord, are in thy power: and no one can resist thy will.” The Vulgate counts the psalm’s title, *Alleluia*, and the letter *Aleph* inside its first verse; the Missal’s verse begins after them. After the *Gloria Patri* the antiphon is repeated (RGMR 427).

### Collect {#proper-collect}

*Oratio*. Missal p. 414, no. 1700.

> *Famíliam tuam, quǽsumus, Dómine, contínua pietáte custódi: ut a cunctis adversitátibus, te protegénte, sit líbera; et in bonis áctibus tuo nómini sit devóta. Per Dóminum nostrum Iesum Christum, Fílium tuum: Qui tecum vivit et regnat in unitáte Spíritus Sancti Deus: per ómnia sǽcula sæculórum. R. Amen.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Cummiskey, The Roman Missal (1861), XXI Sunday after Pentecost, Collect</span>
>
> Preserve thy family, O Lord, we beseech thee, by thy constant mercy: that under thy protection, it may be freed from all adversity; and by the practice of good works, continue devoted to thy name. Thro’.

“By thy constant mercy” stands for *continua pietate*, the unbroken *pietas* of God toward his household; “by the practice of good works” for *in bonis actibus*. The Missal prints this conclusion in full, with the response sign before *Amen*, here written R. On 18 October 2026 the oration *pro Fidei propagatione* is joined to the Collect under its one conclusion in every Mass, and in a low or conventual Mass the Collect of St Luke follows under its own (below).

### Epistle {#proper-epistle}

*Léctio Epístolæ beáti Pauli Apóstoli ad Ephésios*. Eph 6:10–17. Missal p. 414, no. 1701.

> *Fratres: Confortámini in Dómino, et in poténtia virtútis eius. Indúite vos armatúram Dei, ut possítis stare advérsus insídias diáboli. Quóniam non est nobis colluctátio advérsus carnem et sánguinem: sed advérsus príncipes, et potestátes, advérsus mundi rectóres tenebrárum harum, contra spirituália nequítiæ, in cæléstibus. Proptérea accípite armatúram Dei, ut possítis resístere in die malo, et in ómnibus perfécti stare. State ergo succíncti lumbos vestros in veritáte, et indúti lorícam iustítiæ, et calceáti pedes in præparatióne Evangélii pacis: in ómnibus suméntes scutum fidei, in quo possítis ómnia tela nequíssimi ígnea exstínguere: et gáleam salútis assúmite: et gládium spíritus, quod est verbum Dei.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Eph 6:10–17</span>
>
> Finally, brethren, be strengthened in the Lord and in the might of his power. Put you on the armour of God, that you may be able to stand against the deceits of the devil. For our wrestling is not against flesh and blood; but against principalities and powers, against the rulers of the world of this darkness, against the spirits of wickedness in the high places. Therefore, take unto you the armour of God, that you may be able to resist in the evil day and to stand in all things perfect. Stand therefore, having your loins girt about with truth and having on the breastplate of justice: And your feet shod with the preparation of the gospel of peace. In all things taking the shield of faith, wherewith you may be able to extinguish all the fiery darts of the most wicked one. And take unto you the helmet of salvation and the sword of the Spirit (which is the word of God).

The Missal opens the lesson with the bare address *Fratres* where the Vulgate’s verse begins *De cetero, fratres*, which the English renders “Finally, brethren”. The Missal spells *exstinguere* for the Clementine’s *extinguere*; every other word agrees with the Vulgate. The lesson stops at verse 17. In the letter Paul goes on at once to ask for prayer “at all times in the spirit” and for himself, “for which I am an ambassador in a chain” (6:18–20); the Mass does not read those verses.

### Gradual {#proper-gradual}

*Graduale*, Ps 89:1–2 (Heb. 90), respond and verse. Missal p. 414, no. 1702.

> *Dómine, refúgium factus es nobis, a generatióne et progénie. ℣. Priúsquam montes fierent, aut formarétur terra et orbis: a sǽculo et usque in sǽculum tu es, Deus.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Ps 89:1–2, without the title</span>
>
> Lord, thou hast been our refuge from generation to generation. Before the mountains were made, or the earth and the world was formed; from eternity and to eternity thou art God.

The Missal reads *a generatione et progenie*, “from generation and offspring”, where the Clementine reads *a generatione in generationem*; the English, which renders the Vulgate, cannot show the difference, and the 1861 hand missal renders the Missal’s respond with the same words as the Douay. The Gradual leaves out the psalm’s title, *Oratio Moysi, hominis Dei*, “A prayer of Moses the man of God”. The verse agrees with the Vulgate.

### Alleluia {#proper-alleluia}

Ps 113:1 (Heb. 114:1). Missal p. 414, no. 1703.

> *Allelúia, allelúia. ℣. <span class="nodecor">Ps. 113, 1</span> In éxitu Israël de Ægýpto, domus Iacob de pópulo bárbaro. Allelúia.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Ps 113:1</span>
>
> When Israel went out of Egypt, the house of Jacob from a barbarous people:

The verse agrees with the Vulgate, without its title *Alleluia*. The Vulgate’s Ps 113 joins in one psalm the Hebrew Pss 114 and 115; the verse sung here is the first of the Exodus psalm that goes on to the sea that fled and the Jordan turned back.

### Gospel {#proper-gospel}

*Sequéntia sancti Evangélii secúndum Matthǽum*. Mt 18:23–35. Missal p. 414, no. 1704.

> *In illo témpore: Dixit Iesus discípulis suis parábolam hanc: Assimilátum est regnum cælórum hómini regi, qui vóluit ratiónem pónere cum servis suis. Et cum cœpísset ratiónem pónere, oblátus est ei unus, qui debébat ei decem mília talénta. Cum autem non habéret unde rédderet, iussit eum dóminus eius venúmdari, et uxórem eius, et filios, et ómnia quæ habébat, et reddi. Prócidens autem servus ille, orábat eum, dicens: Patiéntiam habe in me, et ómnia reddam tibi. Misértus autem dóminus servi illíus, dimísit eum, et débitum dimísit ei. Egréssus autem servus ille, invénit unum de consérvis suis, qui debébat ei centum denários: et tenens suffocábat eum, dicens: Redde quod debes. Et prócidens consérvus eius, rogábat eum, dicens: Patiéntiam habe in me, et ómnia reddam tibi. Ille autem nóluit: sed ábiit, et misit eum in cárcerem donec rédderet débitum. Vidéntes autem consérvi eius quæ fiébant, contristáti sunt valde: et venérunt, et narravérunt dómino suo ómnia quæ facta fúerant. Tunc vocávit illum dóminus suus: et ait illi: Serve nequam, omne débitum dimísi tibi, quóniam rogásti me: nonne ergo opórtuit et te miseréri consérvi tui, sicut et ego tui misértus sum? Et irátus dóminus eius, trádidit eum tortóribus, quoadúsque rédderet univérsum débitum. Sic et Pater meus cæléstis fáciet vobis, si non remiséritis unusquísque fratri suo de córdibus vestris.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Mt 18:23–35</span>
>
> Therefore is the kingdom of heaven likened to a king, who would take an account of his servants. And when he had begun to take the account, one was brought to him, that owed him ten thousand talents. And as he had not wherewith to pay it, his lord commanded that he should be sold, and his wife and children, and all that he had, and payment to be made. But that servant falling down, besought him, saying: Have patience with me, and I will pay thee all. And the lord of that servant being moved with pity, let him go and forgave him the debt. But when that servant was gone out, he found one of his fellow-servants that owed him an hundred pence: and laying hold of him, he throttled him, saying: Pay what thou owest. And his fellow-servant falling down, besought him, saying: Have patience with me, and I will pay thee all. And he would not: but went and cast him into prison, till he paid the debt. Now his fellow servants seeing what was done, were very much grieved, and they came, and told their lord all that was done. Then his lord called him: and said to him: Thou wicked servant, I forgave thee all the debt, because thou besoughtest me: Shouldst not thou then have had compassion also on thy fellow servant, even as I had compassion on thee? And his lord being angry, delivered him to the torturers until he paid all the debt. So also shall my heavenly Father do to you, if you forgive not every one his brother from your hearts.

The Missal opens with the liturgical formula *In illo tempore*, names the speaker and his hearers, *Dixit Iesus discipulis suis parabolam hanc*, and so replaces the Vulgate’s first word, *Ideo*, “Therefore”. That word ties the parable to Peter’s question, “Lord, how often shall my brother offend against me, and I forgive him? till seven times?”, and to the answer, “till seventy times seven times” (18:21–22), which the Mass does not read. The Missal reads *suffocabat* where the Clementine reads *suffocavit* (v. 28), and spells *milia* and *venumdari*; “throttled” renders either verb. The Gospel cross before the title is printed in the Missal and not reproduced here. After the Gospel the Missal prints the cue *Credo*.

### Offertory {#proper-offertory}

*Ant. ad Offertorium*, printed citation *Iob 1*. Missal p. 415, no. 1705.

> *Vir erat in terra Hus, nómine Iob: simplex et rectus, ac timens Deum: quem Satan pétiit, ut tentáret: et data est ei potéstas a Dómino in facultátes et in carnem eius: perdidítque omnem substántiam ipsíus et filios: carnem quoque eius gravi úlcere vulnerávit.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Cummiskey, The Roman Missal (1861), XXI Sunday after Pentecost, Offertory</span>
>
> There was a man whose name was Job, and that man was simple and upright, and fearing God: and Satan asked to tempt him; and power was given him by the Lord over his possessions, and over his flesh: and he destroyed all his substance, and his sons: and he wounded his flesh with a grievous ulcer.

The antiphon is a narrative in the chant’s own words, not a verse of Job. Its first clause shortens Job 1:1, which the Douay renders “There was a man in the land of Hus, whose name was Job, and that man was simple and upright, and fearing God, and avoiding evil.” What follows summarises the testing: *quem Satan petiit, ut tentaret* has no verbal counterpart in the book; *data est ei potestas … in facultates et in carnem eius* gathers the two permissions, “all that he hath is in thy hand” (1:12) and “he is in thy hand, but yet save his life” (2:6); *perdiditque omnem substantiam ipsius et filios* sums up the four messengers of 1:13–19; and *carnem quoque eius gravi ulcere vulneravit* stands for 2:7, “struck Job with a very grievous ulcer”. The 1861 English is that book’s own rendering of the Missal’s Latin; it leaves out *in terra Hus*. The Missal prints the antiphon alone.

### Secret {#proper-secret}

*Secreta*. Missal p. 415, no. 1706.

> *Súscipe, Dómine, propítius hóstias: quibus et te placári voluísti, et nobis salútem poténti pietáte restítui. Per Dóminum nostrum.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Cummiskey, The Roman Missal (1861), XXI Sunday after Pentecost, Secret</span>
>
> Mercifully receive, O Lord, these offerings, by which thou art pleased to be appeased, and restore us to salvation by thy powerful goodness. Thro’.

*Quibus et te placari voluisti* says that God willed to be appeased by these offerings, and *et nobis salutem potenti pietate restitui* that by them he willed salvation to be restored to us; the English turns the second infinitive into a petition. “Powerful goodness” stands for *potenti pietate*, the same *pietas* the Collect asked to be continual. After the Secret the Missal prints the direction *Præfatio de Ssm̃a Trinitate*.

### Communion {#proper-communion}

*Antiphona ad Communionem*, Ps 118:81, 84 and 86 (Heb. 119). Missal p. 415, no. 1707.

> *In salutári tuo ánima mea, et in verbum tuum sperávi: quando fácies de persequéntibus me iudícium? iníqui persecúti sunt me, ádiuva me, Dómine Deus meus.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Cummiskey, The Roman Missal (1861), XXI Sunday after Pentecost, Communion</span>
>
> My soul hath looked to be saved by thee, and hath relied on thy word: when wilt thou judge those who persecute me? The wicked ones have long persecuted me, help me, O Lord my God.

The Communion sings three half-verses of the eleventh strophe of Ps 118, the strophe *Caph*, and changes each. Where the Vulgate reads *Defecit in salutare tuum anima mea: et in verbum tuum supersperavi*, the Douay “My soul hath fainted after thy salvation: and in thy word I have very much hoped”, the Missal has no verb of fainting, *In salutari tuo anima mea*, and the simple *speravi*. Of verse 84, “How many are the days of thy servant: when wilt thou execute judgment on them that persecute me?”, it sings only the second question. Of verse 86, “All thy statutes are truth: they have persecuted me unjustly, do thou help me”, it sings only the second half, reads *iniqui*, “the wicked”, for the Vulgate’s adverb *inique*, “unjustly”, and adds *Domine Deus meus*. The 1861 English is that book’s own rendering of the Missal’s Latin; its “long” has no word in the Latin.

### Postcommunion {#proper-postcommunion}

*Postcommunio*. Missal p. 415, no. 1708.

> *Immortalitátis alimóniam consecúti, quǽsumus, Dómine: ut, quod ore percépimus, pura mente sectémur. Per Dóminum nostrum.*

<!-- end of quotation -->

> <span class="smallcaps">Cummiskey, The Roman Missal (1861), XXI Sunday after Pentecost, Postcommunion</span>
>
> Having received the food of immortality, we beseech thee, O Lord, that what we have taken with our mouths we may receive with pure souls. Thro’.

The English renders *sectemur* by “receive”. The Latin verb means to follow after or pursue: what the mouth has taken, the pure mind is to go after. The prayer has the shape of a prayer the priest says after his Communion in the Order of Mass, *Quod ore sumpsimus* (*Ordo Missae*, no. 1130), which also sets what the mouth has taken beside a pure mind.

### The orations added on 18 October 2026

On this date the rubrics add two sets of prayers to the Sunday’s three orations; neither belongs to the formulary. The oration *pro Fidei propagatione*, its Collect *Deus, qui omnes homines vis salvos fieri*, Secret *Protector noster, aspice, Deus* and Postcommunion *Redemptionis nostrae munere vegetati* (printed in the Missal’s *Tabula orationum*, p. 427, nos. 1806–1808), is joined to each Sunday oration under one conclusion in every Mass, unless the Ordinary has appointed another day for the missions (RGMR 453). The commemoration of St Luke, his Collect *Interveniat pro nobis*, Secret *Donis caelestibus da nobis* and Postcommunion *Praesta, quaesumus, omnipotens Deus* (pp. 689–690, nos. 3883, 3889 and 3891), follows under its own conclusion in a low or conventual Mass only (RG 108, 111; RGMR 437 a).

## Each Element in Its Setting {#sec:each-element}

Seven of the ten elements are Scripture and three are the Church’s own orations. The Introit joins a prayer from the Greek chapters of Esther to the first verse of Ps 118; the Gradual, the Alleluia and the Communion take verses from three psalms, the Communion returning to Ps 118; the lessons are St Paul’s letter to the Ephesians and St Matthew’s Gospel; and the Offertory tells the opening of the Book of Job in words of its own. Each element is set out below in its own context, with what its words say and what the liturgical commentators who expound it say of it, before the three readings of the whole.

### Introit: Mardochai’s prayer, and the blessed way

The Book of Esther tells how a Jewish girl of the exile became queen of Assuerus, king of Persia, at Susa, and how her cousin and guardian Mardochai would not bow down to Aman, the king’s favourite. Aman obtained a royal decree that every Jew in the empire should be put to death on one day. The Hebrew book tells of Mardochai’s mourning in sackcloth and ashes and Esther’s fast before she went in to the king unbidden; it gives no prayer. The prayers of Mardochai and of Esther stand in the chapters that the Vulgate prints after the end of the Hebrew book (Esth 10:4–16:24), which St Jerome translated from the Greek and set apart; the *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s article “Esther” reports them as most probably translations from a Hebrew or Chaldaic original of which no trace remains in the Hebrew Bible. They are part of the Book of Esther in the Church’s canon. The first of these chapters opens with a colophon that dates the bringing of “this epistle of Phurim” to the fourth year of the reign of a Ptolemy and Cleopatra (11:1).

“Mardochai besought the Lord, remembering all his works” (13:8). His prayer begins with the confession that the Introit takes: “O Lord, Lord, almighty king, for all things are in thy power, and there is none that can resist thy will, if thou determine to save Israel” (13:9). He confesses God the maker of heaven and earth and Lord of all (13:10–11). Then, in lines the Mass does not sing, he tells God that it was not pride that kept him from worshipping Aman, for he would have kissed the steps of his feet for Israel’s salvation, “But I feared lest I should transfer the honour of my God to a man” (13:12–14). He asks mercy on a people whose enemies “resolve to destroy us” (13:15), reminds God of “thy portion, which thou hast redeemed for thyself out of Egypt” (13:16), and asks that God turn their mourning into joy, “that we may live and praise thy name, O Lord, and shut not the mouths of them that sing to thee” (13:17). “And all Israel with like mind and supplication cried to the Lord, because they saw certain death hanging over their heads” (13:18). The Missal’s antiphon keeps the confession and drops the condition “if thou determine to save Israel”. What Mardochai said of God’s power to save one threatened people the Introit says of God’s will without condition: in it all things are placed, and no one resists it.

No Father of the first centuries wrote a commentary on Esther. St Rabanus Maurus expounded the book in the ninth century, from the Hebrew chapters only, passing over the Greek additions, which included this prayer. Yet when he comes to the king’s edict hung up in Susa he reads it as the decree of the heavenly king, whose rule reaches the whole world, and he describes that king in the words of Mardochai’s prayer, citing Esther 13: in his power all things are placed, and none can resist his will. The pride of the world, he concludes, is overcome by the power of God, as Christ said, “I have overcome the world” (Jn 16:33). Aman, whose name Rabanus interprets as iniquity, stands for the powerful of this world who persecute those who will not give them divine honour, and prefigures the enemies of the Church (*Expositio in librum Esther*, preface, VI, XI and XIII). St Thomas Aquinas takes the same verse as his authority that nothing can happen outside the order of the divine government (*Summa theologiae* I q. 103 a. 7, *sed contra*), an argument the first reading follows.

The liturgical commentators hear the antiphon in more than one voice. For William Durandus the Church speaks with the voice of Mardochai, who prayed in sackcloth and ashes, the arms of penance; and because God is Lord of all, he frees those who serve him from the hand of the devil and helps them in their war against him, as he freed Mardochai from the hand of Aman (*Rationale* VI.138). Rupert of Deutz hears the voice of the soldier of the virtues, who in his trial falls to the earth with Job and says, “The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away” (*De divinis officiis* XII.21). Honorius Augustodunensis reads the Sunday’s chants from the history of Esther, through which God’s people is delivered from the danger hanging over it (*Gemma animae* IV.90). Lucien Fromage, continuing Dom Guéranger’s *The Liturgical Year*, says that the holy men who live in a world reduced, like Job on the dunghill, to the extremity of wretchedness “begin by making their own the sublime prayer made, by Mardochai, for his people, who were doomed to extermination”. Bl. Ildefonso Schuster draws from the same prayer what he calls the secret of the Church’s history: the evil done by the malice of creatures with God’s permission enters the plan of Providence, which reaches its highest ends through its bitterest enemies, “Like the devil, who, despitefully winnowing the grain, renders a helpful service to the great Householder” (*The Sacramentary* III, pp. 178–179).

The verse is the first of Ps 118, the longest of the psalms, whose twenty-two strophes of eight verses follow the Hebrew alphabet and praise God’s law. It opens with a beatitude, “Blessed are the undefiled in the way, who walk in the law of the Lord.” St Augustine begins his sermons on the psalm from the desire for blessedness that all men share: “if thou wouldest be blessed, be undefiled”; and he asks in what way, “save in the law of the Lord?” Yet he will not let the undefiled forget that they are debtors: the saints who walk in the Lord’s ways still say, “If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves” (1 Jn 1:8; *Enarrationes in Psalmos* 118, sermon 1.1–3). Cassiodorus notes that *immaculati* is plural and said of the faithful, not of Christ, who alone is spotless by nature, and that the saints become undefiled when they receive the gifts of pardon, *quando indulgentiae munera consequuntur*; the way is the Saviour himself, who said, “I am the way” (*Expositio Psalmorum*, Ps 118:1).

### Collect: the family kept by continual mercy

The Collect is the Church’s own composition. It calls the Church God’s family, *familiam tuam*, and asks two things of his *continua pietas*: that under his protection the family be free from every adversity, *a cunctis adversitatibus*, and that it be devoted to his name in good works. Its *te protegente* and its adversities stand beside the Gradual’s refuge and the Epistle’s adversaries; its *pietas* returns in the Secret’s *potenti pietate*. Honorius reads it as the prayer of God’s people in the history of Esther, asking that God guard his family (*Gemma animae* IV.90). Fromage finds in it the Church’s preference for peace, “because, that furnishes her with undisturbed freedom for paying to her God the united homage of religion and good works”, and joins it to the close of Mardochai’s prayer, which asked that the people might live to praise God’s name. Schuster hears two requests: protection against the wiles of the devil, who raises up obstacles material, moral and spiritual to turn men from a righteous life, and the spirit of *pietas*, the gift of the Holy Spirit by which the mind is drawn to the service of God and to good works (*The Sacramentary* III, p. 179).

### Epistle: the armour of God

The lesson is the close of the letter to the Ephesians. Paul writes as a prisoner, “the prisoner of Jesus Christ” (3:1), “a prisoner in the Lord” (4:1), and calls himself in the verses that follow the lesson “an ambassador in a chain” (6:20). The letter’s last instruction is a call to arms. “Be strengthened in the Lord” places the strength in God; the armour is “the armour of God”; the enemy is the devil, whose “deceits”, *insidias*, are what the Christian must stand against. Then comes the sentence on which the lesson turns: “our wrestling is not against flesh and blood; but against principalities and powers, against the rulers of the world of this darkness, against the spirits of wickedness in the high places.” The Christian is to resist “in the evil day” and, having done all, to stand. The armour is named piece by piece: the girdle of truth, the breastplate of justice, the shoes of the gospel of peace, the shield of faith that quenches “all the fiery darts of the most wicked one”, the helmet of salvation, and the sword of the Spirit, “which is the word of God”. In the letter the call goes on to prayer “at all times in the spirit” (6:18), which the Mass does not read.

St John Chrysostom notes that Paul says not “fightings” but “wiles”: “this enemy is at war with us, not simply, nor openly, but by ‘wiles’”; the devil “never proposes to us sins in their proper colors”. “By ‘evil day’ he means the present life”; to stand is to stand “after we have slain”, for many who won have fallen again (*Homilies on Ephesians* 22). St Jerome reads the whole armour as Christ himself: by the armour of God, he says, we are to understand the Saviour, so that “Put on the armour of God” says the same as “Put on the Lord Jesus Christ” (Rom 13:14): *quasi dixerit, Induite Dominum Jesum Christum* (*Commentarii in Epistolam ad Ephesios* III, PL 26, col. 543). He adds that the wars of Israel against the nations, David against Goliath, Josue, the Judges and the Kings, were written as a figure of this spiritual war. St Thomas Aquinas makes the armour the gifts and virtues, the girdle temperance, the breastplate justice and the shield faith; and to the objection that men plainly do attack us he answers that flesh and blood attack us not principally but as moved by a higher agent, the devil, *a superiore movente* (*Super Epistolam ad Ephesios* 6, lect. 3–4).

Durandus says that even the well armed cannot perfectly resist unless God helps them, and finds there the reason for the Gradual’s refuge (*Rationale* VI.138); Rupert hears the Epistle comfort the soldier whom the Introit showed fallen with Job (XII.21). Fromage, like Jerome, makes the armour first Christ’s: the prophets describe the Leader himself girded, helmeted, in breastplate and shield and with the sword of the spirit, so that “We should almost think we were here having a list of our own arms; well, yes, but they are his, first” (Is 11:5; 59:17; Wis 5:19–20; Apoc 2:16). The evil days are many in every life, he says, but “there is one evil day, evil beyond all the others: it is the last day, the day of judgment” (*The Liturgical Year* XI, pp. 459–460). Schuster calls faith the most effectual defence against the devil, and says that God allows some of the saints to sustain this combat “in order that their faith may be still further purified and strengthened”, “This is precisely the history of Job” (*The Sacramentary* III, p. 179).

### Gradual: the eternal God our refuge

The Gradual sings the first two verses of Ps 89, which the Vulgate entitles *Oratio Moysi, hominis Dei*, “A prayer of Moses the man of God”. The title is not sung. The psalm goes on, in verses the Mass leaves aside, to set the shortness and misery of human life against God’s eternity: a thousand years in his sight are as yesterday (v. 4), and man is grass that flourishes in the morning and withers by evening. The two verses the Gradual sings speak only of God: he has been our refuge *a generatione et progenie*, and before the mountains were made, or the earth and the world formed, *a saeculo et usque in saeculum tu es, Deus*.

St Augustine, reading the title, calls Moses “the Minister of the Old, and the Prophet of the New Testament”, and takes “from one generation to another” as either every generation or the two, the old and the new. He does not believe the psalm “was entirely the composition of that Moses”, since it lacks the turns of phrase of Moses’ songs, and holds that the great servant’s name is used “for the sake of some intimation”. On the second verse he observes that the psalmist does not say “thou wast” and “thou shalt be” but puts the verb in the present, “intimating that the substance of God is altogether immutable”, and concludes: “Behold then the eternity that is our refuge, that we may fly thither from the mutability of time, there to remain for evermore” (*Enarrationes* 89.1–3). St Robert Bellarmine, in his *Explanation of the Psalms*, notes that the Hebrew word for refuge “signifies a well fortified house, placed on an eminence”, and that those who dwell in God dwell “in a city fortified by faith, hope, and charity”; he too marks the present tense, “Thou art God”, “in order to show the true eternity of God, in which there is no past or future”. On the title’s ascription to Moses, the Doctor admits in his general prefaces that those named in the titles of the psalms may have written them, but holds the more probable opinion to be that David wrote them all, as he says Augustine maintains. He and Augustine differ only in that Bellarmine leaves Moses’ authorship open as a tenable opinion; the title itself names Moses, and the Scriptural Date and Location appendix sets out the witnesses.

Cassiodorus reads the first verse through the title: Moses, venerable *in typo sanctae Ecclesiae*, gives thanks that God was his refuge from his exposure as an infant to the murmuring of the people, and he speaks for every church, since to the whole Church, *quae in hac saeculi tempestate versatur*, the Lord is always the safest refuge (*Expositio Psalmorum* 89.1). The exposition of the psalms printed under the name of St Athanasius finds in the first verse a proem drawn from benefits already given, under Pharaoh, under Josue and in Babylon, and in the second the confession that the Lord coexisted with the Father before all creation, *eum ante omnia opificia Patri coexsistere* (PG 27, col. 395). Durandus says that the soldier wearied or wounded has his refuge in God alone (VI.138). Fromage hears in the Gradual and the Alleluia the Church preparing its last exodus from the world (p. 463), a phrase the third reading takes up; Schuster, the assurance that the God who helped Israel and the early Church is “still the same God, faithful and unchanging” (p. 180).

### Alleluia: Israel out of Egypt

The verse is the first of the psalm *In exitu Israel*, which remembers the Exodus and the entry into the land: “The sea saw and fled: Jordan was turned back. The mountains skipped like rams” (Ps 113:3–4). In the Vulgate the psalm runs on into what the Hebrew numbers as Ps 115. The verse names the two ends of the going out: Israel, the house of Jacob, and Egypt, “a barbarous people”. Bellarmine explains that on leaving Egypt the Jews began to be a people under God’s special rule, and that the Egyptians are called barbarous because they spoke a strange language. Schuster notes that Egypt was far richer in civil culture than Israel, and that Scripture calls it barbarous by the measure of the spirit; he adds that the psalm was among those sung at the Passover meal (p. 180).

St Augustine teaches that the deeds of the Exodus “happened unto them for ensamples” (1 Cor 10:11). Egypt, a name he understands as affliction or oppression, stands for this world, from which no one withdraws in heart without God’s mercy, as Israel did not leave Egypt without his help (*Enarrationes* 113.1–4; the third reading gives his words). Cassiodorus takes the going out as the moment when we are released from the bonds of sin, *a peccatorum vinculis*, and freed from the host of the Egyptians, that is, of the demons; we truly become Israel when the pomps of this world begin to leave our minds (*Expositio Psalmorum* 113.1). St John Chrysostom, expounding the same verse, observes that God first shows his power and his benefits, freeing his people from a cruel enemy, and only then asks for worship, where men wish first to rule and only afterwards to do good (*Expositio in Psalmum* 113.1).

### Gospel: the king’s reckoning

The parable closes the discourse of Mt 18, spoken after Jesus and the disciples have come to Capharnaum (17:23) and before he leaves Galilee (19:1). The chapter begins with the question who is greatest in the kingdom and goes on to the little ones, scandal, the lost sheep and the brother who sins, who is to be rebuked first alone, then before witnesses, then before the Church. Peter then asks how often he must forgive his brother, “till seven times?”, and hears “till seventy times seven times” (18:21–22). “Therefore”, the Vulgate goes on, “is the kingdom of heaven likened to a king”. The Mass begins the parable without Peter’s question, so that it stands on its own as a word of Jesus to his disciples.

The story moves in three scenes. In the first the king begins his reckoning, *voluit rationem ponere*, and a servant is brought in who owes ten thousand talents, a sum no servant could repay; the second reading gives the hundred pence’s value and Augustine’s reckoning of the difference. The king orders him sold with his wife, his children and all he has; the servant falls down and asks, “Have patience with me, and I will pay thee all”; and the lord, moved with pity, lets him go and forgives the debt. He asked for time and was given remission. In the second the same servant goes out and finds a fellow servant who owes him a hundred pence, takes him by the throat, *suffocabat*, and hears the very words he had spoken, “Have patience with me, and I will pay thee all”; and he will not, but casts him into prison until he pays. In the third the fellow servants, grieved, tell their lord; the lord calls the servant “wicked”, reminds him that he was forgiven “because thou besoughtest me”, asks whether he should not have had compassion as he had received it, and in anger delivers him “to the torturers until he paid all the debt”. The sentence that closes the Gospel applies the whole to the hearers: “So also shall my heavenly Father do to you, if you forgive not every one his brother from your hearts.”

St Jerome explains that it is *familiare … Syris et maxime Palaestinis* to join a parable to what they say, so that a precept the hearer cannot hold by itself is held by its likeness; and he states the parable’s argument as a question: *Si … dominus servo debitori decem millium talentorum tam facile dimisit, quanto magis servi conservis suis debent minora dimittere?*, if the lord so easily forgave his servant ten thousand talents, how much more ought servants to forgive their fellow servants lesser debts? (*Commentarii in Matthaeum* III, PL 26, cols. 132–133). St Hilary of Poitiers says that the parable teaches pardon without measure through the grace of the Gospel, and that the Lord himself expounded its whole meaning (*Commentarius in Matthaeum* 18.10–11). The liturgical commentators who reach this Gospel read it toward the judgment and the Lord’s Prayer: Rupert hears in the servant’s debt the petition *Dimitte nobis debita nostra* (*De divinis officiis* XII.22); Durandus says that on the day of judgment the Lord will settle accounts with us (*Rationale* VI.139); Honorius, that Christ the true king will come then to judgment and require of each his deeds (*Gemma animae* IV.93). Schuster calls the parable “the most authoritative commentary on those words of the Lord’s Prayer: ‘Forgive us our trespasses as we forgive them that trespass against us’” (p. 180).

### Offertory: the man of Hus

The Offertory tells the first two chapters of Job in a narrative of its own. In the book, Job of the land of Hus, which the Lamentations join to Edom (Lam 4:21), is “simple and upright, and fearing God, and avoiding evil” (Job 1:1). When the sons of God come to stand before the Lord, Satan comes among them; the Lord asks, “Hast thou considered my servant, Job?”, and Satan answers, “Doth Job fear God in vain?” The Lord gives him leave over all that Job has, “only put not forth thy hand upon his person” (1:6–12). Job loses his oxen, his asses, his sheep, his camels, his servants and his sons and daughters, and says, “the Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away … blessed be the name of the Lord. In all these things Job sinned not by his lips” (1:21–22). At a second council Satan is given Job’s flesh, “but yet save his life” (2:6), and strikes him “with a very grievous ulcer, from the sole of the foot even to the top of his head” (2:7). The antiphon gathers this into a single movement: the just man, the adversary’s petition, the power given by the Lord, the loss of goods and sons, the wound in the flesh. It says that Satan *petiit*, asked, a verb the book does not use, and it says nothing of Job’s answer.

St Thomas Aquinas, expounding the book according to its literal sense, holds that it tells a thing that happened and not a parable; that Satan can harm *non quantum vult sed quantum permittitur*, not as much as he wills but as much as he is permitted; and that the will to harm is his own while the power comes only from God (*Expositio super Iob* 1). St Gregory the Great, whose *Moralia* on Job is the great Latin commentary, finds two wills in the trial: the devil asked for it that Job might fall short, the Lord in kindness permitted it, and Job went forward instead (Preface 7). The first reading follows that answer.

Fromage makes the Offertory the centre of the Sunday: it “contains the ruling idea, which runs through this twenty-first Sunday after Pentecost” (p. 455). The other commentators expound the antiphon itself. Durandus says that the devil is conquered above all by patience (VI.138); Rupert, that what befell Job in history befalls the soldier of the virtues in his moral life, when the tempter, permitted by a hidden dispensation of God, strikes him (XII.21); Honorius, that Job signifies Christ, who suffered for us (IV.90–91); Sicard of Cremona, that Job is the example of the brave warrior (*Mitrale* VIII.21); for Schuster, Job “is considered as being a figure of Christ crucified” (p. 178). Fromage and Schuster both print or describe the verses of Job’s lament that the older chant books sang after the antiphon; the 1962 and 1862 Missals print the antiphon alone.

### Secret: the sacrifice by which God willed to be appeased

The Secret asks God to receive the offerings *propitius*, graciously, and names what they are: the sacrifices by which he willed to be appeased, *quibus et te placari voluisti*, and by which he willed salvation to be restored to us by his powerful mercy. The prayer makes the appeasing God’s own will, and the restoring the work of his *pietas*. Schuster explains that the Eucharistic sacrifice both satisfies divine justice for us and restores us to the salvation in which God created our first parents, and that the attribute *potens* given to God’s mercy fits a mercy that “has triumphed over divine justice itself” (Jas 2:13; p. 181). Fromage says that the salvation of the world and of each man “is, virtually, ever in the august Sacrifice, whose power restores man, by appeasing God” (p. 470).

### Communion: the plea of the strophe Caph

The eleventh strophe of Ps 118, each of whose verses begins in Hebrew with the letter *Caph*, is the lament of a man worn out with waiting. His soul faints for God’s salvation; his eyes fail for God’s word, “When wilt thou comfort me?”; he has become “like a bottle in the frost”; he asks, “How many are the days of thy servant: when wilt thou execute judgment on them that persecute me?”; the wicked have told him fables, and have persecuted him unjustly; “They had almost made an end of me upon earth: but I have not forsaken thy commandments”; “Quicken thou me according to thy mercy” (vv. 81–88). The Communion keeps from this strophe the hope in God’s salvation and word (v. 81, without its verb *Defecit*), the question when God will judge the persecutors (v. 84, without its first question), and the cry for help against the wicked (v. 86, without its first half), and adds *Domine Deus meus*. What it sings is hope, a question about the judgment, and a plea for help; the fainting, the counting of days and the near death of the strophe are left unsung.

Who the persecutors are, and when the judgment asked for will come, are the questions on which the Fathers read the verse most differently, and the readings below take them up at length. The liturgical commentators hear in the Communion, first, hope: Durandus says that patience avails nothing without hope (VI.138); Fromage, that “An unflagging hope ever accompanies the admirable patience of holy Church”, so that persecutions “never interrupt her prayer” (p. 470). Honorius, reading it in the history of Esther, hears thanks that God has done judgment on the persecutors (IV.90). Schuster says that the spiritual enemies whose judgment is asked are judged already, by their own refusal of the light, “Qui non credit, jam judicatus est” (Jn 3:18; p. 181).

### Postcommunion: the food of immortality

The Postcommunion calls what has been received *immortalitatis alimonia*, the food of immortality, and asks that what the mouth has received the pure mind may follow, *pura mente sectemur*. Its pairing of the mouth and the mind, of the outward reception and the inward pursuit, is the pairing of the Order of Mass’s *Quod ore sumpsimus*. Schuster calls the Eucharist “the food which nourishes us for all eternity” and reads the petition as asking that sacramental Communion bind “our mind, our heart, and our will to Jesus” (p. 181); Fromage: “Now that we have been nourished by the food of immortality, let us live on it, with all the evidence of a soul that is made pure” (p. 470).

## The War God Permits and Bounds: The Adversary on a Leash and the Armour That Is Christ {#lane-armour-under-providence}

<span id="sec:armour-under-providence" data-label="sec:armour-under-providence"></span>

Heard from the Introit and the Offertory, the Mass sets two sentences side by side that seem to contradict each other. The first says that all things are placed in God’s will and that no one can resist it. The second says that Satan asked to tempt a just man, and that power over his goods and his flesh was given him by the Lord. Between them the Epistle names the Christian’s enemy: not flesh and blood, but principalities, powers and spirits of wickedness, against whom he must stand in the evil day clothed in the armour of God. At the end the Communion asks when God will judge those who persecute the singer. St Gregory the Great and St John Chrysostom answer the question these texts raise together. Gregory, expounding Job, finds two wills at work on the one man: the devil’s, which asks for his ruin, and God’s, which permits the trial for his proving and keeps the tempter bridled. Chrysostom, expounding the Epistle, finds the real enemy behind the human one: the spirits that work in persecutors, against whom alone the Christian may turn his anger. Read with them, the formulary describes a war that is fierce and real but bounded. The adversary asks before he strikes and strikes only as far as he is let; the human persecutor and the offending brother are not the enemy; the armour is God’s, and St Jerome says it is Christ; and the will that no one resists is the ground of the soldier’s confidence, not the cause of his trouble.

### Two sentences that seem to collide

The Introit’s sentence is absolute: *In voluntate tua, Domine, universa sunt posita, et non est qui possit resistere voluntati tuae*. In the Book of Esther it is said by a man under sentence of death, whose people a royal decree has handed over to its enemy; he confesses the will that no one resists while the enemy’s will seems to have prevailed. The Offertory tells a story that sharpens the question. Job is *simplex et rectus, ac timens Deum*, and yet *Satan petiit, ut tentaret: et data est ei potestas a Domino*. The verb *petiit* gives the adversary a will of his own, and the passive *data est … a Domino* says where his power came from. If no one resists God’s will, how does Satan come to ask for what God’s servant does not deserve, and why is it given? Either the Introit says too much, and the adversary is a second power whose will God must reckon with; or the Offertory makes God the author of Job’s ruin. The Epistle adds a third term. The Christian has an enemy, and is told not to mistake him: *non est nobis colluctatio adversus carnem et sanguinem*. And the Communion, sung after a Gospel that demands forgiveness of the brother, asks, *quando facies de persequentibus me iudicium?*

### The petition and the permission: St Gregory the Great

St Gregory’s *Moralia* on Job begins with the question why so many strokes fell on a man who kept every virtue, and answers it with two wills: “Him the adversary sought indeed to obtain that he might prove deficient in godliness, but obtained that he might prove a proficient therein. The Lord in loving-kindness permitted that to be done, which the devil in his wickedness required; for when the enemy had got leave to have him with the purpose of destroying him, by his temptations he effected that his merits were augmented” (Preface 7). The play in his Latin is on one verb with two prefixes, *ut deficeret* and *ut proficeret*: the devil sought that Job might fall short, and obtained that he might go forward. The petition is the devil’s and the permission is God’s, and the outcome follows the permission. Job therefore “became the intermediate subject of the contest between God and the devil” (Preface 8): the enemy, in putting forth his strength on Job, “entered the lists against God”.

On the first permission, “all that he hath is in thy hand: only put not forth thy hand upon his person” (Job 1:12), Gregory bids the reader “mark in the Lord’s words the dispensations of heavenly pity, how He lets go our enemy, and keeps him in; how He looses, and yet bridles him. He allows him some things for temptation, but withholds him from others.” The Lord gives up Job’s substance and keeps his person, and later gives that too, “yet He does not loose the enemy to every thing at once, lest he should crush His own subject by striking him on every side”. Trials are dealt out by seasons, “that what by coming all together would destroy, may when divided be borne up against”, as St Paul says, “God is faithful, Who will not suffer you to be tempted above that ye are able” (1 Cor 10:13). Gregory adds another sense of the same words: the Lord knew “that His soldier was brave”, and chose to divide his contests with the enemy so that he might come out of each “the more incomparable conqueror” (*Moralia* II.19). The adversary of the Offertory is on a leash, and the hand that holds it measures every stroke.

Gregory answers the Introit’s claim as well, though from another verse of Job, “Who hath resisted Him, and had peace?” (Job 9:4). Resistance to God happens, he says, but it never succeeds and never has peace: “whereinsoever there is resistance made to the Creator, that agreement in peace is broken up”. The apostate angel, “banished, has to bear the burthen of himself”; Adam, refusing obedience, lost even the peace of his body. “Not that our frailty does resist His unchangeable decree, but what it has not the power to accomplish, it yet attempts” (*Moralia* IX.5). Rupert of Deutz, citing the Introit’s words in his treatise on God’s omnipotence (he gives them as St Paul’s), completes them from this verse of Job and from Gregory’s exposition of it: no one resists God’s will and has peace (*De omnipotentia Dei* 9). Read with Gregory, the Introit does not deny that creatures set themselves against God. It says that none succeeds, and that the one who tries breaks only his own peace.

### The will to harm and the power to harm: St Thomas Aquinas

St Thomas states in a single distinction what Gregory tells in a story. In his literal exposition of Job he holds that Satan can harm *non quantum vult sed quantum permittitur*, not as much as he wills but as much as he is permitted: the will to harm is in every evil being from itself, the power only from God. When Satan “went forth from the presence of the Lord” (Job 1:12), Thomas reads it as going out from the intention of Providence while unable to escape its order (*Expositio super Iob* 1). In the *Summa* he takes the Introit’s own verse as his authority. Asking whether anything can happen outside the order of the divine government, he answers from Esther: “It is written (Esther 13:9): ‘O Lord, Lord, almighty King, all things are in Thy power, and there is none that can resist Thy will’”. An effect can fall outside the order of some particular cause, but not outside the order of the universal cause, and “as God is the first universal cause, not of one genus only, but of all being in general, it is impossible for anything to occur outside the order of the Divine government” (I q. 103 a. 7).

The two Doctors make the two sentences of the Mass agree without softening either. The Offertory’s *petiit* is the adversary’s will, which is his own and evil; its *data est … a Domino* is his power, which is God’s and measured. The Introit’s will that no one resists is the order to which even the petition belongs. Gregory speaks of lost peace, Thomas of an order that cannot be escaped, and Bl. Ildefonso Schuster, commenting on this Mass, of malice that Providence turns to its own ends; the three formulas agree on the one point the reading needs. In this sense the adversary is not a second power. He is a creature whose hatred is real and whose reach is lent.

### The enemy behind the enemy: St John Chrysostom

If the war is real and bounded, who is the enemy? The Epistle answers, and St John Chrysostom presses the answer hard. Paul speaks of “wiles” because the devil “never proposes to us sins in their proper colors”; he fights “not simply, nor directly, but with much wiliness”. The rulers of this darkness are incorporeal, and the Christian overcomes them only by becoming what they are not: “How then, you may say, are we to wrestle with the darkness? By becoming light. How with the ‘spiritual hosts of wickedness’? By becoming good.” Then Chrysostom gives the sentence its sharpest point. Paul, he says, wrote to disciples who would have many persecutors, and told them in effect, “imagine not … that it is they who war with you. They that really war with you, are the spirits that work in them. Against them is our conflict” (*Homilies on Ephesians* 22). The persecutor is the instrument, and the enemy is the spirit that moves him.

From this Chrysostom draws a rule that he states with deliberate violence: “If any one hath an enemy, if any one hath been wronged by him, if any one is exasperated, let him collect together all that wrath, all that fierceness, and pour it out upon the head of the devil. Here wrath is a good thing, here anger is profitable, here revenge is praiseworthy.” “Hath any one struck thee? Bear malice against the devil, and never relinquish thy hatred towards him” (Hom. 22). The wrath is not abolished but redirected, from the brother who wronged you to the adversary who moved him. The same Father, preaching on the parable of this Mass’s Gospel, makes the turn again and joins it to the Offertory’s Job: “For what can be more wicked than the devil; yet nevertheless, even hence have we a great opportunity of approving ourselves; and Job showeth it. But if the devil hath become a cause of crowns, why art thou afraid of a man as an enemy?” (*Homilies on Matthew* 61.5). The devil, who asked for Job’s ruin, became the occasion of his crown. A man who wrongs us can do no worse.

On the gospel of peace with which the feet are shod Chrysostom adds the paradox that holds the reading together: “this war which we have against them, puts an end to another war, that, namely, which is between us and God; if we are at war with the devil, we are at peace with God” (*Homilies on Ephesians* 24). The Christian who stands against the adversary stands with God; the one who makes his brother the enemy has mistaken the field.

St Thomas, answering the objection that men plainly do persecute us, which he draws from another verse of the same psalm, *Multi qui persequuntur me* (Ps 118:157), allows that they do, but says that flesh and blood attack us not principally but as moved by a higher agent, *a superiore movente, sc. a diabolo* (*Super Epistolam ad Ephesios* 6, lect. 3). Chrysostom and Thomas agree that the spirits are the real enemy; Thomas adds that men are their instruments, which keeps the persecutor answerable for what he does.

### The armour that is Christ

The armour is *armatura Dei*, God’s armour, and the lesson opens with *Confortamini in Domino, et in potentia virtutis eius*: the strength is lent as the adversary’s power is lent. St Jerome draws the conclusion that the armour is the Lord himself. To put on the whole armour of God, he says, is to put on Christ: *quasi dixerit, Induite Dominum Jesum Christum*, “But put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ” (Rom 13:14), so that the girdle of truth, the breastplate of justice, the shield of faith and the rest are each Christ (*In Ephesios* III, PL 26, col. 543). And he reads the wars of Israel against the nations, David’s against Goliath, Josue’s and the Judges’ and the Kings’, as written for us, a figure of the spiritual war. St Thomas names the pieces as the gifts and virtues, the girdle temperance, the breastplate justice and the shield faith; the continuation of *The Liturgical Year*, commenting on this Mass, holds that the prophets describe the same pieces as Christ’s own before they are ours: “well, yes, but they are his, first” (XI, p. 460). Jerome’s identification and Thomas’s are different emphases, the one Christological and the other moral. The soldier is clothed in Christ, and Christ in him becomes virtue.

The Gradual gives the soldier his fortress. St Robert Bellarmine explains that the Hebrew for refuge “signifies a well fortified house, placed on an eminence”, where the tenants are secure from enemies, beasts, flood and storm, and that those who dwell in God dwell “in a city fortified by faith, hope, and charity” (*Explanation of the Psalms*, Ps 89:1). Durandus, expounding the Gradual in the Mass of his Church, says that because even the well armed cannot perfectly resist unless God helps them, the Church flees to God her refuge (*Rationale* VI.138). The refuge that is God before the mountains were made is the same will that the Introit says none resists: what threatens the soldier lies inside it, and so does he.

### The persecutors of the Communion: St Hilary of Poitiers

The Communion asks, *quando facies de persequentibus me iudicium? iniqui persecuti sunt me*. Sung after a Gospel that commands forgiveness from the heart, the plea seems to ask God to punish the singer’s enemies. St Hilary of Poitiers, commenting on the verse, identifies the persecutors in the Epistle’s terms. The prophet, he says, desires that judgment be done on those who persecute him, *a diabolo scilicet, ministrisque ejus. Sunt enim spiritales nequitiae in caelestibus*: on the devil, that is, and his ministers, for there are spirits of wickedness in the heavenly places (Eph 6:12). What he asks is that, being made wholly holy and pleasing to God, he may crush the devil within himself, *conterat intra se diabolum, conterat adversantes sibi inimicasque virtutes, et fidei suae animae subdat nequitias spiritales*; not yet trusting his own nature, he asks *ut per misericordiam Dei judicium in inimicos suos sit*. This is the power the Lord gave his apostles, *Hanc potestatem judicii Dominus apostolis suis dedit*, when he gave them power “to tread upon serpents and scorpions, and upon all the power of the enemy” (Lk 10:19) (*Tractatus in Psalmum* 118, *Caph* 5, PL 9, cols. 573–574). Hilary himself sets the Communion’s verse beside the Epistle’s. The judgment asked for is not a curse on men but the power to tread the adversary down.

Hilary knows the last judgment and says so: *Judicium post consummationem saeculi Propheta esse non ignorat*, as Ps 1:5 shows; and he knows that the devil himself is to be judged then, *Sed scit et diabolum in resurrectionis saeculo judicandum*. Why then does he take the prophet to be asking now for a judgment within this life? He draws it from the first half of the verse, which the Communion does not sing, “How many are the days of thy servant?”: the prophet knows himself to be a man of few days, *cum se paucorum nunc dierum secundum corporalem vitam sciat esse*, and so asks for what he may have in them. The exposition of the psalm printed under the name of St Athanasius, on the same unsung question, says likewise that the days of men on earth are few, in which he prays that Satan be crushed at once under his feet (PG 27, col. 495; Rom 16:20). Hilary’s identification of the persecutors and of the power asked stands on the words the Communion sings; his “now” rests on the half it leaves out.

### Every element in the war

Each element of the formulary takes its place in this reading. The Introit is the soldier’s confession before the battle: Mardochai, under sentence and refusing to give a man the honour due to God, confesses the will that holds all things. St Rabanus Maurus reads the edict of the heavenly king in Esther as ruling the whole world and overcoming the pride of its persecutors, and Durandus hears in the Introit the Church in Mardochai’s voice, freed from the hand of the devil as Mardochai was from Aman’s. Its verse, “Blessed are the undefiled in the way, who walk in the law of the Lord”, names the road on which the soldier keeps his footing; St Augustine finds the way in the law of the Lord. The Collect asks the protection that Gregory’s bridle supplies: that the family be free *a cunctis adversitatibus, te protegente*, and Schuster reads it as a prayer against the obstacles the devil raises to turn men from good works. The Epistle names the enemy and the armour, and the Gradual the fortress.

The Alleluia sings the first deliverance from an oppressor, Israel brought out from a barbarous people. Cassiodorus reads the going out as release from the host of the Egyptians, *id est a daemonum turba*, and St John Chrysostom on the same verse observes that God first freed his people from a cruel enemy and only then asked their worship. The Gospel, which the next reading takes as its centre, enters here at its end: the servant who will not forgive is handed *tortoribus*, and St Thomas glosses the torturers as the demons to whom he is subjected, *subiicitur daemonibus* (*Super Matthaeum* 18). The fellow servant who owed a hundred pence was never the enemy; Chrysostom’s question, why fear a man as an enemy when the devil himself has become a cause of crowns, is asked of this parable.

The Offertory is the reading’s centre: the petition, the permission, the measured loss. Rupert of Deutz, commenting on the Offertory in the Mass he expounds, says that what happened to Job in history happens in the moral life to every soldier of the virtues, whom the tempter strikes when a hidden dispensation of God permits him (*De divinis officiis* XII.21); Durandus, that the devil is conquered above all by patience (VI.138). The Secret offers the sacrifice by which God willed to be appeased, and Chrysostom’s sentence on the gospel of peace explains why that offering belongs in a war: to be at war with the devil is to be at peace with God. The Communion, read with Hilary, asks for the power to crush the adversary. The Postcommunion names what the soldier has received, the food of immortality, and asks that the pure mind follow what the mouth has taken; the armour that is Christ is put on, in the end, by eating.

### The strongest difficulty

The reading meets three objections. The first is that it makes God the author of Job’s ruin. Gregory and Thomas answer it by keeping the two wills apart: the will to harm is the devil’s own and evil, the permission is God’s and good, and God permits what the devil asks only so far as it serves the proving of his servant. Gregory’s *ut deficeret* and *ut proficeret* carry the whole answer: the same trial is the devil’s attempt at Job’s fall and God’s means of his advance.

The second is that Chrysostom’s redirection excuses the persecutor. If the spirits are the real enemy, is the man who strikes me innocent? Chrysostom does not say so. He tells the wronged man where to direct his wrath, not that the wrong was none, and in the same homily on the parable he says that the one who injures us harms himself. St Thomas keeps the persecutor’s part by calling men instruments moved by the devil, not principal agents but agents still. And Chrysostom’s “here revenge is praiseworthy” is aimed at the devil alone; it licenses no vengeance on any man, and the next reading shows how hard the Gospel presses that limit.

The third concerns the judgment itself. Hilary asks for power in this life to crush the devil and his ministers, and says that what the prophet now asks is not the judgment he knows is to come at the resurrection, *non illud utique quod in resurrectionis saeculo meminit futurum*. St Augustine hears in the same verse the martyrs’ cry for that final judgment, when vengeance falls on the Church’s persecutors; St Robert Bellarmine hears a plea for the condemnation of the wicked who persecute and tempt. The Fathers differ in what is asked, a present power or the last judgment, and in whom the plea names, not only in the time. Of the others only Cassiodorus names Hilary’s persecutors, the devil with his ministers, and he joins them to the judgment at the end of the world. The answers are compatible, not identical: Hilary affirms the judgment at the end, and the men whom Augustine and Bellarmine see persecuting are, in Thomas’s terms above, instruments moved by the devil. The comparison at the end of the study sets out where each finds his time.

### The four senses of this reading

Literal.

: Mardochai, under Aman’s sentence, confesses that all things are placed in God’s will and that no one can resist it. Paul, a prisoner, arms the Ephesians against principalities and powers and spirits of wickedness, not against flesh and blood. The Book of Job tells that Satan asked leave to try a just man and was given power over his goods and his flesh, and no further. The psalmist, hoping in God’s word, asks when God will judge his persecutors and begs his help.

Allegorical.

: The armour of God is Christ, whom the Christian puts on (Jerome); the wars of Israel are figures of the Church’s spiritual war; Job, whose name Gregory reads as “grieving”, bears the type of the Redeemer together with his Body (*Moralia*, Preface 14 and 16); the people under sentence whom God delivers from Aman is the Church delivered from the devil (Rabanus, Durandus).

Moral.

: Stand, and do not mistake the brother or even the persecutor for the enemy; pour the wrath on the devil, and on him alone (Chrysostom). Take every trial as measured by the hand that bridles the tempter, and turn it by patience from the devil’s attempt at your fall into God’s means of your advance (Gregory, Durandus).

Anagogical.

: The adversary, bridled now, is to be judged at the resurrection, as Hilary affirms; and the soldier who has stood in the evil day is crowned, the devil having become, in Chrysostom’s word, a cause of crowns.

## The King’s Reckoning and the Forgiven Debtor: Mercy Received Without Measure, Given From the Heart {#lane-debt-forgiven-from-the-heart}

<span id="sec:debt-forgiven-from-the-heart" data-label="sec:debt-forgiven-from-the-heart"></span>

Heard from its Gospel, the Mass is a lesson in what the king’s mercy requires. The parable ends with a sentence on every hearer: *Sic et Pater meus caelestis faciet vobis, si non remiseritis unusquisque fratri suo de cordibus vestris*. St John Chrysostom and St Augustine read it as the condition of a pardon already given. Every hearer is the servant who owed ten thousand talents and was forgiven beyond what he asked, and the only thing the king asks in return is that the forgiven forgive. Chrysostom finds that the cruel servant revoked his own pardon by seizing his fellow; Augustine hears God say that he will call back what he remitted, as the covenant of the Lord’s Prayer states; both insist that the forgiveness be given from the heart and not with the lips alone. Read with them, the rest of the formulary surrounds the parable with prayers for the mercy it describes. The Introit’s verse blesses the undefiled, whom Augustine still calls debtors; the Collect asks God’s continual mercy over his family and its devotion in good works; the Epistle forbids the forgiven man to make his brother the enemy; the Secret offers the sacrifice by which God willed to be appeased; and the Postcommunion asks that what the mouth has received the pure mind may follow, as the Gospel asks that what the lips forgive the heart may forgive.

### The sentence that ends the Gospel

The difficulty lies in the parable’s last two verses. The king had said, *omne debitum dimisi tibi*, “I forgave thee all the debt”; now, angry, he hands the servant to the torturers *quoadusque redderet universum debitum*, “until he paid all the debt”. The same whole debt that was forgiven is exacted again. And Jesus applies the sentence to his hearers with the words “So also shall my heavenly Father do to you”. Two questions follow. What is the condition on which the king’s mercy stands, and how is it to be met? And can a pardon once given return? If it can, forgiveness from God seems never secure; if it cannot, the king’s second sentence seems to contradict his first. The Fathers answer the first question together and the second in words that differ.

### Ten thousand talents and a hundred pence

Everything in the parable depends on the disproportion of the two debts: ten thousand talents against a hundred pence. The hundred pence are *centum denarios*, and a denarius is the day’s wage for which the householder of another parable hires his labourers, “a penny a day” (Mt 20:2): the fellow servant owes about a hundred days’ labour, a real debt but one a working man could in time repay. Augustine, who supposes the ten thousand talents to be “at least ten thousand sins”, says that a hundred denarii “perhaps are a thousand asses”, and asks, “But what was this to ten thousand talents?” (*Sermon* 83.3). Chrysostom draws the lesson at once: “Seest thou how great the difference between sins against man and against God? As great as between ten thousand talents, and a hundred pence, or rather even much more.” The difference arises “both from the difference of the persons, and the constant succession of our sins. For when a man looks at us, we stand off and shrink from sinning: but when God sees us every day, we do not forbear” (*Homilies on Matthew* 61.1). Augustine makes every hearer both debtors at once: “every man is at once God’s debtor, and hath also some brother a debtor to himself. For who is there who is not God’s debtor, but he in whom there can be found no sin? And who is there who hath not a brother his debtor, but he against whom no one hath sinned?” (*Sermon* 83.2). St Jerome puts the argument in a single question, from the greater to the lesser: if the lord so readily forgave his servant ten thousand talents, *quanto magis servi conservis suis debent minora dimittere?* (*In Matthaeum* III, PL 26, col. 133).

Augustine goes further and finds the Law itself in the numbers. The Law is given in ten precepts; the ten thousand talents and the hundred pence are both built on ten, so that “in both numbers you will find every kind of sin included” (*Sermon* 83.6). He also reckons from St Luke’s genealogy seventy-seven generations from Christ back to Adam, the number of the Lord’s answer to Peter (Mt 18:22), which the Mass does not read. These reckonings are Augustine’s own. What they serve in his sermon is the point the parable makes without them: there is no sin that the forgiven may refuse to forgive. “And so let us be ready to forgive all the trespasses which are committed against us, if we desire to be forgiven” (83.4).

### The threat that was mercy, and the gift beyond the request

The king’s first command, that the servant be sold with his wife and children, looks cruel. Chrysostom says it was “Not of cruelty, nor of inhumanity … but of unspeakable tenderness. For it is His purpose to alarm him by this threat, that He might bring him to supplication, not that he should be sold.” The king did not forgive before the reckoning because he wished the servant to learn “from how many obligations He is delivering him, that in this way at least he might become more mild towards his fellow servant” (61.3). The servant asked for little: “Have patience with me, and I will pay thee all.” He wanted time. And the lord, moved with pity, gave him remission. “Seest thou again surpassing benevolence? The servant asked only for delay and putting off the time, but He gave more than he asked, remission and forgiveness of the entire debt” (61.3). The servant’s promise to pay all was empty; the lord did not hold him to it.

Then comes the scene the parable has prepared. The fellow servant falls down and says the same words, *Patientiam habe in me, et omnia reddam tibi*, and the Gospel repeats them exactly. The forgiven servant hears his own prayer and refuses it. Chrysostom marks the repetition: “he did not regard even the words by which he had been saved (for he himself on saying this was delivered from the ten thousand talents), and did not recognize so much as the harbor by which he escaped shipwreck” (61.4). The fellow servants who saw it were grieved; “They therefore who did not owe, partook of the grief” (61.4).

### Who revokes the pardon?

The king’s second sentence exacts the debt he had remitted. Who revoked the pardon? Chrysostom puts the question to the servant himself as he seizes his fellow: “What doest thou, O man? perceivest thou not, thou art making the demand upon thyself, … and revoking the sentence and the gift?” When the lord then pleads with the servant and excuses himself, “being on the point of revoking his gift”, Chrysostom corrects the phrase: “or rather, it was not he that revoked it, but the one who had received it”. The delivery to the torturers is “for ever; for he will never repay”. And Chrysostom notes what this means for the gifts of God, which Paul calls “without repentance” (Rom 11:29): “wickedness has had such power as to set aside even this law. What then can be a more grievous thing than to be revengeful, when it appears to overthrow such and so great a gift of God” (61.4).

Augustine puts the same sentence in God’s mouth: “For I have forgiven thee first; do thou at least forgive after that. For if thou wilt not forgive, I will call thee back, and put upon thee again all that I had remitted to thee. For the Truth doth not speak falsely; Christ neither deceiveth, nor is deceived” (*Sermon* 83.7). For Chrysostom the receiver revokes the gift; for Augustine God calls it back. The two formulations do not contradict each other: the one looks at the cause, the servant’s cruelty, and the other at the judge who ratifies it. Jerome calls the closing sentence fearful for this reason: *Formidolosa sententia, si juxta nostram mentem sententia Dei flectitur atque mutatur*, a fearful sentence, if God’s sentence bends and changes according to our own mind; and he says that it overturns every pretence of a feigned peace, *omnem simulationem fictae pacis evertit* (*In Matthaeum* III, col. 133). St Rabanus Maurus, commenting on the parable, repeats Jerome’s sentence and adds one he gives under St Gregory’s name: what was forgiven us through penance is exacted of us again if we do not forgive from the heart what is done against us (*Commentaria in Matthaeum*, on Mt 18:35).

St Thomas Aquinas takes up the question these formulations raise and answers it more narrowly. The last verse, he says, seems to suggest that forgiven sins return, as Origen held; but that cannot be so, since the remission of sins has its efficacy from the sacraments. Forgiven sins are said to return *per ingratitudinem*, through ingratitude (*Super Matthaeum* 18). Thomas does not deny what Chrysostom and Augustine say of the servant’s fate; he denies that the sins once remitted come back as the same sins: they are said to return through ingratitude. The ungrateful man makes himself a debtor again, by a new sin, and the parable sets that debt at the size of the gift refused. The hearer is not told that a grudge undoes his baptism; he is told that to refuse mercy after receiving it is to make himself again a debtor of the whole.

### From the heart

The sentence ends with three words that the Fathers do not let pass: *de cordibus vestris*. Chrysostom says that the Lord requires two things here, “both to condemn ourselves for our sins, and to forgive others; and the former for the sake of the latter, that this may become more easy (for he who considers his own sins is more indulgent to his fellow-servant); and not merely to forgive with the lips, but from the heart” (61.5). Augustine: “Say not with the tongue, ‘I forgive,’ and put off to forgive in the heart; for by His threat of vengeance God showeth thee thy punishment. God knoweth where thou speakest. Man can hear thy voice; God looketh into thy conscience.” And he adds a sentence that reverses the ordinary preference: “Better is it that thou shouldest be violent in words, and forgive in the heart, than in words be soft, and in the heart relentless” (*Sermon* 83.7).

Augustine also shows what “from the heart” does not forbid. A boy who sins and asks forgiveness, and sins again, and asks again, may still be corrected: “Let us reprove with words, and if need be with scourges; but let us withal forgive the sin, and cast away the remembrance of it from the heart. For therefore did the Lord add, ‘from your hearts,’ that though through affection discipline be exercised, gentleness might not depart out of the heart.” The patient cries out under the surgeon’s knife, and is cut; “This is not cruelty”, Augustine says of the surgeon (83.8). Forgiveness from the heart forbids rancour, not correction.

The same sermon names the place where every Christian makes this promise daily: “Forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors.” “What debts of thine? All, or a certain part? Thou wilt answer, All. So then do thou with thy debtor. This then is the rule thou layest down, this the condition thou speakest of; this the covenant and agreement thou dost mention when thou prayest” (83.4). Bl. Ildefonso Schuster calls the parable “the most authoritative commentary on those words of the Lord’s Prayer”, and adds, “The measure of divine compassion is fixed by ourselves, and fully corresponds to that which we may have used towards our neighbours” (*The Sacramentary* III, p. 180).

### Who is the debtor?

The Fathers disagree on one point of identification, and the disagreement shapes the reading. Some interpreters, St Jerome reports, took the debtor of ten thousand talents to be the devil; Origen had suggested as much in a higher sense he did not fix. Jerome rejects it outright: *Scio quosdam istum qui debebat decem millia talenta, diabolum interpretari … nec Ecclesiasticae interpretationis est, nec a prudentibus viris recipiendae* (col. 133), a reading neither of the Church nor to be accepted by prudent men. Chrysostom and Augustine make the debtor every hearer; Augustine says that “every man begins from Baptism; he goes out free, the ‘ten thousand talents’ are forgiven him; and when he goes out, he will soon find some fellow-servant his debtor” (83.7). St Thomas makes the king God and the servants every man, and he finds the title in Job: “Hast thou considered my servant Job?” (Job 1:8). Rabanus, following an allegorical line of his own, reads the debtor as the people of the Jews bound by the ten commandments and the fellow servant as the Gentiles; he too then turns to every hearer with Jerome. The moral identification is the one the parable’s last sentence demands, *unusquisque fratri suo*: the debtor is each of us, and the fellow servant each brother.

### The reckoning and its end

*Voluit rationem ponere*: the king willed to settle accounts. St Thomas says that this reckoning begins when God first sends some tribulation and ends at the day of judgment (*Super Matthaeum* 18; the third reading gives his Latin); the liturgical commentators who expound the parable at its own place in their books read it of the judgment too, Durandus saying that on that day the Lord will settle accounts with us (*Rationale* VI.139), Honorius that Christ the true king will then come and require of each his deeds (*Gemma animae* IV.93). St Alphonsus Liguori, preaching on the verse *tradidit eum tortoribus*, calls it “a description of the sentence of the eternal death”: those who die in sin, he says, can make no satisfaction in the other life and remain debtors to the divine justice for ever (*Sermons for All the Sundays*, Sermon L). He agrees with Chrysostom’s “for ever; for he will never repay”. The continuation of *The Liturgical Year*, commenting on this Mass, sets beside the Gospel the petition of the *Dies irae*, “grant us the gift of forgiveness, before the Day of reckoning cometh”, and says: “We are all of us, in fact, that negligent servant, that insolvent debtor” (XI, pp. 464–466).

### The whole Mass around the parable

The other elements of the formulary pray for, warn of, or embody the mercy the parable describes. The Introit confesses a king whose will holds all things; the people for whom Mardochai prays is under sentence, and in lines the Mass does not sing he asks, “have mercy on thy people” (Esth 13:15). The verse blesses the undefiled who walk in the law, and Augustine, expounding it, will not let them forget the apostle’s “If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves” (*Enarrationes* 118, sermon 1.3); Cassiodorus says that the saints become undefiled *quando indulgentiae munera consequuntur*, when they receive the gifts of pardon. The undefiled of the Introit are forgiven debtors.

The Collect asks for the whole household the *continua pietas* that the king showed his servant, and asks that the family be devoted *in bonis actibus*. Augustine names the two works of mercy that answer the Gospel, “Forgive, and ye shall be forgiven: give, and it shall be given unto you” (Lk 6:37–38; *Sermon* 83.2), and the good works the Collect asks include both. The Epistle, read beside the parable, forbids the forgiven man to make his brother the enemy, and Chrysostom himself joins the two: the man who has been wronged is to pour his anger on the devil, not on the one who wronged him (*Homilies on Ephesians* 22). The Gradual names God the refuge of generation after generation, and Augustine says he became our refuge “ever since we turned to Thee”; the Alleluia sings Israel released from Egypt, and Augustine adds that “no man is turned away in heart from this world, unless aided by the gift of the Divine mercy”. The release of the Exodus and the release of the debtor are both the work of mercy.

The Offertory’s Job is the man who bore injury without sin, and Chrysostom, in the homily on this parable, sets him before the hearer as proof that even the devil’s malice can become a cause of crowns, so that no man need be feared as an enemy (61.5; quoted in the first reading). St Thomas, expounding the servant’s “Have patience with me”, sets beside it a cry of Job’s (Job 10:20). The Secret offers the sacrifice by which God willed to be appeased, *quibus et te placari voluisti*; in the parable the lord was appeased by a prayer, and Chrysostom reminds the servant of “God, whom by mere prayer thou hast reconciled” (61.4). Schuster reads the Secret’s *potenti pietate* as the mercy that “has triumphed over divine justice itself” (p. 181).

The Communion’s plea for judgment on persecutors, sung after this Gospel, would seem to undo it. Augustine answers in two places. Of anger he says: “A man lusteth to be avenged, and Christ is not yet avenged, the holy martyrs are not yet avenged. Still doth the patience of God wait, that the enemies of Christ, the enemies of the martyrs, may be converted. … Forgive therefore; from the heart forgive” (*Sermon* 58.8). And of the martyrs who in the Apocalypse cry to be avenged he asks “whether they pleaded against the men themselves or against the dominion of sin”, and answers that “it is a genuine avenging of the martyrs, and one full of righteousness and mercy, that the dominion of sin should be overthrown”. That dominion is overthrown partly by men’s amendment and partly by the condemnation of the incurable; and he points to St Paul, who in bringing his own body into subjection avenged the martyr Stephen “in his own person” (*De sermone Domini in monte* I.22.77). The plea for judgment and the command to forgive ask the same thing, the end of sin’s rule, and the first way it ends is the persecutor’s conversion.

The Postcommunion asks that what the mouth has received, *quod ore percepimus*, the pure mind may follow. Chrysostom’s “not merely … with the lips, but from the heart” and Augustine’s “Say not with the tongue … and put off to forgive in the heart” are said of the Gospel’s forgiveness. The prayer asks the same agreement of mouth and mind of the one who has received the sacrament, and so the Mass ends where the parable ends, at the heart.

### The strongest difficulty

The reading has to hold two things together. The first is the parable’s severity. Augustine and Chrysostom do not soften it: the debt returns, God calls it back, the delivery to the torturers is for ever. St Thomas’s distinction does not weaken that severity but places it: sins once remitted do not return as the same sins, but ingratitude makes a man a debtor again. Thomas tells the hearer that a grudge does not undo his baptism; Augustine and Chrysostom tell him that refusing mercy after receiving it puts the whole debt back on him. Both are true of the parable, and each keeps the other from being misheard.

The second is the reach of “from the heart”. The command could be taken to forbid every correction, every refusal, every justice. Augustine’s sermon refuses that reading: discipline may be exercised “through affection”, as the surgeon cuts to cure, so long as gentleness does not depart from the heart. What the command forbids is the remembrance kept against the brother, the forgiveness of the lips that hides a refusal of the heart, what Jerome calls *simulatio fictae pacis*.

### The four senses of this reading

Literal.

: Jesus tells his disciples a parable: a king forgives a servant an unpayable debt on his plea for time; the servant refuses a fellow servant a small one on the same plea; the king calls him wicked and hands him to the torturers until he pays all; so will the heavenly Father do to those who do not forgive their brothers from the heart. In Matthew the parable answers Peter’s question how often to forgive (18:21–22), which this Mass does not read.

Allegorical.

: The king is God (Thomas), and in Rabanus’s allegory Christ; the ten thousand talents are the sins forgiven every man in baptism (Augustine); the fellow servants who grieve and tell their lord are the members who suffer with one member that suffers (Thomas); the remission given on the servant’s prayer is the Church’s daily “Forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors”, which Augustine calls a covenant.

Moral.

: Forgive every debt, as one forgiven far more, and forgive it from the heart, not with the lips alone (Chrysostom, Augustine); correct when correction is owed, but without rancour (Augustine); remember your own sins, and the brother’s will seem small.

Anagogical.

: The king’s reckoning, begun in tribulation, is completed at the judgment (Thomas; Durandus; Honorius), when God’s measure will be the measure we used (Schuster); the debtor who would not forgive remains a debtor for ever (Chrysostom; Alphonsus); and the Church asks before that day, in the words Fromage sets beside this Gospel, for “the gift of forgiveness, before the Day of reckoning”.

## The Church’s Exodus toward the Judgment: A People on the March Asks When {#lane-last-exodus-of-the-church}

<span id="sec:last-exodus-of-the-church" data-label="sec:last-exodus-of-the-church"></span>

Heard from the Communion, the Mass asks a question of time. The singer has hoped in God’s salvation and in his word, and asks, *quando facies de persequentibus me iudicium?*, “when wilt thou judge those who persecute me?” The Epistle bids the Ephesians stand and resist *in die malo*, girded about the loins; the Gospel’s king *voluit rationem ponere*, willed to settle accounts; the Alleluia sings Israel going out of Egypt; the Gradual confesses the God who is *a saeculo et usque in saeculum*; and the Postcommunion names what has been eaten, *immortalitatis alimonia*, the food of immortality. St Augustine hears in the Communion’s question the cry of the martyrs in the Apocalypse, who ask how long until God judges, and are told to wait until the number of their brethren is complete. St John Chrysostom reads the girded loins of the Epistle as the girding with which Israel ate the Passover on the night of the Exodus, and calls the present life “nothing else than a march and an encampment”. Read with them, the formulary is the song of a people that has been brought out of Egypt and has not yet arrived. It asks when the judgment will come, and it is answered that the judgment is the end toward which it travels, and that until then it remains on earth, persecuted and helped, fed on the way with the food of immortality.

### The question the Communion sings

The Communion takes three half-verses from a strophe of Ps 118 whose speaker is worn out with waiting. It leaves out his fainting, his failing eyes, his shrivelled body and his near death, and it leaves out the first question of verse 84, “How many are the days of thy servant?” What it sings is a hope, a question and a cry: *In salutari tuo anima mea, et in verbum tuum speravi: quando facies de persequentibus me iudicium? iniqui persecuti sunt me, adiuva me, Domine Deus meus*. The question is not how long the singer will live. It is when God will judge. The difficulty is plain once the question is heard after the Gospel. The parable has just told every hearer to forgive his brother from the heart; the Communion asks for judgment on persecutors. Is the Church asking for vengeance? And if the judgment is the last judgment, why ask when, when the Lord told the disciples, “It is not for you to know the times or moments” (Acts 1:7)?

### The martyrs’ plea and the long-suffering: St Augustine

St Augustine reads the strophe as the voice of the whole people of God. The singer who hopes in God’s salvation is “the chosen generation, the royal priesthood, the holy nation, the peculiar people, longing for Christ from the origin of the human race even unto the end of this world”; the first ages had saints who desired his Incarnation, “but these times, since He hath ascended into heaven, have Saints who desire His manifestation to judge the quick and the dead”. He says this of the soul’s failing toward God’s salvation, whose verb the Communion omits; the salvation itself, *in salutari tuo*, is sung. Of the words the Communion does sing, *in verbum tuum speravi*, he says: “that is, of Thy promise; a hope which causeth us to await with patience that which is not seen by those who believe” (*Enarrationes* 118, *Caph* 81).

On verse 84 Augustine first names the voice: “In the Apocalypse, these are the words of the Martyrs, and long-suffering is enjoined them until the number of their brethren be fulfilled.” The martyrs under the altar cry, “How long, O Lord (Holy and True), dost thou not judge and revenge our blood on them that dwell on the earth?”, and are told “that they should rest for a little time till their fellow servants and their brethren, who are to be slain even as they, should be filled up” (Apoc 6:10–11). The Communion’s question is that cry, and its answer is that answer: the judgment will come, and before it the Church waits. Augustine then reads the two questions of the whole verse in order. “The body of Christ then is asking concerning its days, what they are to be in this world”; and “when he had enquired concerning the days, he added also respecting the judgment, showing indeed that the Church would exist on earth until the judgment, when vengeance shall fall upon Her persecutors” (118, *Caph* 84). The first of those two questions is the one the Communion leaves unsung, and Augustine’s “until the judgment” comes from the order of the two. On the half that is sung, his comment gives the martyrs’ plea answered by long-suffering, and the vengeance that falls at the judgment on the Church’s persecutors.

On verse 86, whose second half the Communion sings, Augustine says that “the whole sense dependeth upon the foregoing”, and gives the cry for help its object: “help Thou me, that I may struggle for the truth even unto death” (*Caph* 86). The wicked persecuted “nothing save the truth”, he says, leaning on the first half of the verse, “All thy statutes are truth”, which the Communion leaves out.

### Not a curious question: Cassiodorus, St Robert Bellarmine and the Greek exposition

Augustine is not alone in hearing the sung question as a desire for the end. Cassiodorus says that the whole strophe is sung by *peregrinus in hac terra populus*, a people on pilgrimage in this land, confessing its desire for the Lord’s coming and the persecutions of the proud. On verse 84 the company of the saints, knowing that the members of the Lord will be subject to the devil’s temptations until the end of the world, asks when this world will end, that the vengeance prepared for the persecutors, *id est diabolo cum ministris*, may be completed; and he adds the sentence that settles the point, *non quia vitae suae tempus volebat agnoscere: sed celeritatem futuri iudicii postulabat*. The singer was not asking to know the length of his own life; he was asking for the swiftness of the judgment to come. He sets the question beside the Lord’s answer to the apostles that the times are not theirs to know (Acts 1:7), and explains it by the blessed people’s longing for the end of evils, which it could see only after the end of this world (*Expositio Psalmorum* 118:84).

St Robert Bellarmine reads the sung half the same way: “When will you judge and condemn those that persecute and tempt me, that my persecution may cease on their condemnation? He makes no curious inquiries as to the number of the days of his life, or of those of the world, he merely expresses his desire of life everlasting, when all the wicked … shall cease to harass the just” (*Explanation of the Psalms*, Ps 118:84). On the question *quando*, then, Augustine, Cassiodorus and Bellarmine agree: it asks for the judgment at the end, and the hope that asks it waits with patience.

The exposition of the psalms printed under the name of St Athanasius does not answer *quando*, but it points the first and last sung verses toward the same end. God’s *salutare*, it asks, what else can it be *nisi adventus Domini?*, and the word in which the singer hopes is the promise, *in promissionem* (PG 27, cols. 494–495). On the sung cry *iniqui persecuti sunt me* it says that the man persecuted unjustly, *gnarus se in divino tribunali mercedem accepturum*, knowing that he will receive his recompense at the divine tribunal, *gestit illud tempus videre*, longs to see that time (col. 495). Its comment on verse 84 is said of the unsung question about the servant’s few days, and there it asks that Satan be crushed at once under his feet, as the first reading notes beside Hilary. The exposition thus holds both: the adversary crushed now, and the tribunal longed for at the end.

### Israel out of Egypt: the Alleluia and the Epistle’s girded loins

The Alleluia gives the waiting people a name and a road: *In exitu Israel de Aegypto*. Augustine teaches that “no Christian consider himself alien to the name of Israel”; that Egypt “is often used for an emblem of this world; from which we must spiritually withdraw”; and that “as they did not depart thence, until freed by Divine help; so no man is turned away in heart from this world, unless aided by the gift of the Divine mercy”. That going out, he says, “is fulfilled in every faithful one in the daily travailings of the Church, in this end of the world” (*Enarrationes* 113.3–4). Cassiodorus takes the going out as release from the bonds of sin and from the host of demons, and says that we truly become Israel *cum pompae mundi huius a nostra coeperint mente discedere*, when the pomps of this world begin to leave our minds (*Expositio Psalmorum* 113.1).

St John Chrysostom finds the same Exodus in the Epistle. On *State ergo succincti lumbos vestros in veritate* he asks why the Israelites ate the Passover girded, with shoes on their feet and staves in their hands, and answers that the guise was that of wayfarers: “we were ready for our journey, we were just about to go forth out of Egypt to the land of promise and we were ready for our exodus”. “This then is the historical type. But the reality is this; we too eat a Passover, even Christ … We too ought to eat it, both sandalled and girded. And why? That we too may be ready for our Exodus, for our departure hence.” The desert lies ahead, and the devil in it, “to the end that those who have been saved out of Egypt, those who have passed the Red Sea … may be taken and destroyed by him”; but “We have manna; yea rather not manna, but far more than manna”, and “the leader of this our Exodus is not Moses, but Jesus” (*Homilies on Ephesians* 23). Chrysostom himself joins Paul’s girded loins to the Exodus and the Passover. And he draws the moral that governs this reading: no one builds a splendid house in an encampment, and “The present life is nothing else than a march and an encampment” (Hom. 23).

### The evil day, the eternal refuge and the king’s reckoning

The Epistle’s *dies malus* has two readings that this reading holds together. Chrysostom says, “By ‘evil day’ he means the present life” (Hom. 22). The continuation of *The Liturgical Year* grants that evil days are many in every life and in the world’s history, “But, for every man, and for the world at large, there is one evil day, evil beyond all the others: it is the last day, the day of judgment” (XI, p. 459). The two are one road: the whole march is an evil day, and its end is the day that ends all others.

The Gradual names the God toward whom the march goes and in whom it shelters. *A saeculo et usque in saeculum tu es, Deus*: Augustine and Bellarmine both mark the present tense, and Augustine calls that eternity the refuge to which we fly from the mutability of time (*Enarrationes* 89.3; quoted under the Gradual above); the exposition printed under St Athanasius’s name finds in the first verse the benefits God gave his people under Pharaoh, under Josue and in Babylon, a refuge from generation to generation of Israel’s own exodus and exile (PG 27, col. 395). Fromage hears in the Gradual and the Alleluia together the Church “preparing the last exodus of the Church, which is leaving this world turned infidel, and is hastening to the true land of promise” (p. 463). The psalm the Gradual begins goes on, in verses the Mass does not sing, to the thousand years that are as yesterday and to the shortness of man’s life; the Gradual sings only the refuge and the eternity.

The Gospel’s king gives the march its end. *Qui voluit rationem ponere cum servis suis*: St Thomas says that this reckoning begins when God first sends some tribulation and ends at the day of judgment, *Finis huius rationis erit in die iudicii; principium quando primo inducit aliquam tribulationem* (*Super Matthaeum* 18). Honorius Augustodunensis and William Durandus, expounding the parable in their books, read the reckoning of the judgment (*Gemma animae* IV.93; *Rationale* VI.139), and Fromage, at this Mass, sets beside the Gospel the *Dies irae*’s plea for forgiveness before the day of reckoning (pp. 464–465), as the second reading shows. The reckoning the parable describes is the judgment the Communion asks for, and the tribulations of the march are its beginning.

### The rest of the Mass on the road

The Introit is spoken by a people under sentence of death in a foreign empire, far from the land, and in lines the Mass does not sing Mardochai reminds God of “thy portion, which thou hast redeemed for thyself out of Egypt” and asks “that we may live and praise thy name, O Lord” (Esth 13:16–17). Fromage reads that closing petition as the reason for the Collect’s prayer for peace: the Church asks to be free of adversity so that she may give God “the united homage of religion and good works” (p. 456). The verse of the Introit blesses those who walk, *qui ambulant*, in the law of the Lord, and the Collect asks God to keep his family on the way *a cunctis adversitatibus*.

The Offertory’s Job is the Church on the road. St Gregory reads Job’s name, “grieving”, as setting forth “either our Mediator’s Passion, or the travails of Holy Church, which is harassed by the manifold toils of this present life” (*Moralia*, Preface 16), and Schuster says that the history of Job “is repeated again and again throughout the history of the Church” (p. 179). The Secret offers the sacrifice by which God wills salvation to be restored, *salutem … restitui*: the salvation the Communion hopes in, *in salutari tuo*, is given on the way as well as at its end. And the Postcommunion names the food: *immortalitatis alimonia*. Chrysostom’s “we too eat a Passover, even Christ” and his “far more than manna” are said of the Epistle’s girded loins; the Postcommunion names the same food in its own words, the nourishment of those who will not die, eaten by a people still on the march. Bellarmine’s gloss on the Communion’s question, “his desire of life everlasting”, is the desire this food answers.

### The strongest difficulty

Two objections meet this reading. The first is that it sets a cry for vengeance after a Gospel of forgiveness. Augustine answers it from the martyrs themselves, in the two passages the second reading quotes (*De sermone Domini in monte* I.22.77; *Sermon* 58.8): the martyrs plead against the dominion of sin, not against the men, and “they might lawfully wish for the end of this world, in which they had endured such martyrdoms”; and God’s patience still waits for the conversion of Christ’s enemies. What this reading adds is the time. The long-suffering enjoined on the martyrs is God’s patience with their persecutors, so the Church that asks when the judgment will come forgives while it waits, because the delay is the time of the persecutors’ conversion.

The second objection is that another Father reads the same verse otherwise, and he does. St Hilary of Poitiers, whom the first reading follows, asks for a power given in this life to crush the devil and his ministers, and says that this is not the judgment of the resurrection, which he affirms: what the prophet now asks is *non illud utique quod in resurrectionis saeculo meminit futurum* (*Tractatus in Psalmum* 118, *Caph* 5, PL 9, cols. 573–574). Augustine hears the martyrs’ cry for that final judgment, “when vengeance shall fall upon Her persecutors”, the men who persecuted in the Church “nothing save the truth” (*Caph* 84, 86); Bellarmine, a plea that God “judge and condemn those that persecute and tempt me”; Cassiodorus joins Hilary’s persecutors, the devil with his ministers, to the judgment at the end of the world. The Fathers differ in what is asked and in whom the plea names, not only in the time, which each takes from the verse’s unsung first question. Their answers are compatible without being the same: Hilary affirms the judgment that Augustine and Bellarmine await, and the men who persecute are, in the terms the first reading takes from St Thomas, instruments moved by the devil. This reading follows the end-directed answer, with Augustine’s martyrs, Cassiodorus’s swift judgment and the Athanasian exposition’s longing for the divine tribunal, without making it the only one.

### The four senses of this reading

Literal.

: Israel went out of Egypt, from a barbarous people. Mardochai prays for a people under sentence of death. Paul tells the Ephesians to resist in the evil day with their loins girt about with truth. The psalmist, hoping in God’s word, asks when God will judge his persecutors and begs his help. The king in the parable wills to settle accounts with his servants.

Allegorical.

: The Exodus is the Church’s going out of the world, led not by Moses but by Jesus, with Christ for its Passover (Chrysostom); the Church whose saints desire Christ’s manifestation to judge asks when the judgment comes, as the martyrs asked, and is told to wait (Augustine); the eternal God is its refuge from the mutability of time (Augustine).

Moral.

: Live as on a march and in a camp, and build nothing in the desert that you must leave (Chrysostom); withdraw in heart from Egypt, which no one does without God’s mercy (Augustine); forgive while God’s patience waits for the conversion of enemies (Augustine).

Anagogical.

: The judgment the Communion asks for, at which the king’s reckoning, begun in tribulation, is completed (Thomas); the land of promise toward which the last exodus hastens (Fromage); the food of immortality fulfilled in the life everlasting that Bellarmine hears the singer desire.

## The Three Readings Compared {#sec:comparison}

The three readings hear the same ten texts and agree on much. All three read the formulary under one sovereign God who is refuge: the Introit’s will that no one resists and the Gradual’s God who is before the mountains stand behind each of them. All three hear the Gospel’s mercy and the Communion’s plea for judgment together, and none lets the plea undo the command to forgive. All three draw on St John Chrysostom, who expounds both the Epistle and the Gospel, and on St Thomas Aquinas, whose distinctions serve each in turn: the will and the power to harm in the first, the sins said to return through ingratitude in the second, and the reckoning that ends at the judgment in the second and third. The second and third rest on St Augustine, and the first and third draw on St Gregory the Great. Where they differ is in the question each asks of the texts, the element each hears first, and the answer each gives.

### Where the readings meet

The first and second readings meet at a sentence of Chrysostom’s. In his homily on the Epistle he tells the man who has been wronged to pour all his wrath on the devil; in his homily on the parable he asks why a man who has seen the devil become a cause of crowns should fear another man as an enemy. The first reading takes the first half of that thought: the enemy is the adversary, not the brother. The second takes the other half: the brother is the fellow servant whose small debt the forgiven man must forgive. The two are complementary, and they are distinct. The first reading’s argument stands on the Introit, the Epistle, the Offertory and the Communion, and would stand without the parable; the second’s stands on the parable, and would stand without the Offertory’s permission. The first answers a question about God’s government; the second, a question about the forgiven man’s heart.

The second and third readings meet at Augustine’s sermon on anger, where the martyrs not yet avenged and the command “from the heart forgive” stand in one sentence. The second reading uses it to show that the Communion’s plea does not undo the Gospel; the third, to show that the waiting it imposes is the time of God’s patience with persecutors. Both hold that the Church that asks for judgment forgives in the meantime. They differ in where they place the weight: the second on the debt and its condition, the third on the time and its end.

### Where they differ

The sharpest difference lies in the Communion, and it is a difference between Fathers, not only between readings. St Hilary asks for a power given in this life to crush the devil and his ministers, and says that this is not the judgment of the resurrection, which he affirms. St Augustine hears the martyrs’ cry for the final judgment, when vengeance falls on the Church’s persecutors; St Robert Bellarmine, a plea for the condemnation of the wicked who persecute and tempt; Cassiodorus joins Hilary’s persecutors, the devil with his ministers, to the judgment at the end of the world. They differ in the thing asked and in the persecutors named, and also in the time. Each draws his time from the question the Communion does not sing, “How many are the days of thy servant?”: Hilary hears in it a man who knows his days are few; Augustine, the body of Christ asking about its days in the world; Cassiodorus and Bellarmine, no question about the length of a life at all. The exposition printed under St Athanasius’s name holds both answers: on that unsung question it asks that Satan be crushed at once, and on the sung cry it longs to see the time of the divine tribunal. The answers are compatible, though they are not the same. Hilary affirms the last judgment and the devil’s judgment at the resurrection; only he and Cassiodorus name the persecutors as the devil with his ministers, while Augustine’s and Bellarmine’s persecutors are men, whom the first reading, with St Thomas, calls instruments moved by the devil. Hilary’s identification of the persecutors stands on the sung words, his “now” on the unsung question; Augustine’s martyrs’ plea answered by long-suffering stands on the sung words, his “until the judgment” on the order of the two questions. The first reading follows Hilary’s identification and the power he asks for; the third follows the end-directed answer. The difference is real, and it divides no doctrine.

A second difference lies in the Gospel’s sentence. The second reading keeps three formulations side by side: Chrysostom’s servant who revokes his own pardon, Augustine’s God who calls back what he remitted, and Thomas’s sins that do not return as such but are said to return through ingratitude. The first two are complementary in their own grammar, the one looking at the cause and the other at the judge; Thomas’s is narrower than either, and he names Origen as holding the stronger view he denies. The third reading reads the same king’s reckoning toward its end at the judgment, with Thomas, and does not need to decide the question of return.

A third lies in the armour. St Jerome makes the armour of God Christ himself; Thomas makes it the gifts and virtues; the continuation of *The Liturgical Year* makes it first Christ’s and then ours. The first reading holds Jerome’s identification and Thomas’s together as the Christological and the moral sides of one gift. The third reading draws from the same Epistle not the armour but the girded loins, which Chrysostom reads as the girding of the Passover.

### What each reading meets in the texts

Each reading meets a demanding feature of the formulary that the others leave in the background. The first takes up the Introit’s absolute sentence beside the Offertory’s *petiit* and *data est … a Domino*, the hardest theological knot in the Mass, and answers it with Gregory’s two wills and Thomas’s distinction of will and power. The second takes up the Gospel’s reversed pardon and its *universum debitum*, and answers it without softening the sentence or letting it unsettle the efficacy of God’s forgiveness. All three take up the plea for judgment sung after a Gospel of forgiveness. The first answers it from Hilary, for whom the persecutors are the devil and his ministers; the second from Augustine, for whom the martyrs plead against the dominion of sin and not against the men; the third, again from Augustine, by the time the plea opens, the delay of the judgment in which God’s patience waits for the persecutors’ conversion. Read together, the three readings show one Mass in which the God who bounds the adversary is the king who forgives the debt, and the people that stands in the evil day is the people that goes out of Egypt toward his judgment.

### Esther 13:9–11, the Introit {#sec:date-esther}

The Introit’s prayer belongs to the chapters of Esther that the Vulgate prints after the end of the Hebrew book (10:4–16:24), which St Jerome translated from the Greek and set apart; the *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s “Esther” (vol. 5, 1909) says that no trace of them is found in the Hebrew Bible and that they are most probably translations from a Hebrew or Chaldaic original. The story is set at the court of Assuerus, king of Persia, in Susa (1:2; 11:3); the prayer is said there, in the days of Aman’s decree. The date shown is that article’s for the writing of the book, placed at the end of the reign of Xerxes I or under his son Artaxerxes I; the article calls it an early dating and allows a later one. The chronology record applies the book’s date to the Greek chapters as well, and it dates no event of the story. The colophon at 11:1, which names the fourth year of a Ptolemy and Cleopatra as the time this “epistle of Phurim” was brought, is the book’s own note on its Greek form and is not the date shown.

### Job 1–2, the Offertory {#sec:date-job}

The Offertory narrates Job 1:1, 1:12, 1:13–19 and 2:6–7 in words of its own; the chronology record answers every locus it cites with the same claim for the whole book. The date shown is the modern critical judgment of the introduction to Job in the *New American Bible, Revised Edition*, summarised there as a probable period of writing; the record holds no traditional attribution and no date for Job’s life. St Gregory the Great reports that some held Moses to be the writer and others one of the prophets, says that it is superfluous to ask who wrote it since the Holy Spirit is its author, and then judges it the more probable that Job himself, who bore the conflict, also told of his victory, speaking of himself as of another, as Moses does (*Moralia*, Preface 1–3). The land of Hus, where the story is set, is not the place of writing, which is unknown.

### Psalms 89, 113 and 118, the Gradual, Alleluia, Introit verse and Communion {#sec:date-psalms}

None of the four psalm elements has an occasion in the record, and the “Historical setting” row says so for the Gradual, the Introit’s verse and the Communion. The composition date shown for all four is the bound that the introduction to the Psalms in the *New American Bible, Revised Edition* sets for the whole Psalter, within which, it says, no individual psalm can be dated securely.

Ps 89 is titled *Oratio Moysi, hominis Dei*, “A prayer of Moses the man of God”, a title the Gradual does not sing. The chronology record holds no attribution for it, and no date is shown for Moses. The title names Moses. St Augustine holds that the psalm was not “entirely the composition of that Moses”, since it lacks the expressions of Moses’ songs (*Enarrationes* 89.2). St Robert Bellarmine, in the general prefaces of his *Explanation of the Psalms*, admits as acceptable, and in the preface that survives only in manuscript as not unlikely, the opinion that those named in the titles, Moses among them, wrote their psalms; in both he prefers as the more probable the opinion that David wrote all the psalms, which he gives to St Augustine and St John Chrysostom. Neither Doctor makes Moses the whole psalm’s author; they differ only in that Bellarmine still admits Moses’ authorship as a tenable opinion. The exposition of the psalms printed under St Athanasius’s name says that Moses wrote the prayer and handed it on for those who would come after (PG 27, col. 395). No witness read names an occasion or a date.

Ps 113 names no author. The row for the Alleluia dates the event the psalm remembers, the going out of Egypt, not the psalm: the preferred reckoning is the Vulgate’s statement that the Temple was begun in the four hundred and eightieth year after the Exodus (3 Kgs 6:1), and the alternative, kept beside it, is the Septuagint’s figure, reported in the *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s “Biblical Chronology” (vol. 3, 1908).

Ps 118 has no author in its title. The tradition attributes it to David: Haydock’s note at the first verse says that it is generally attributed to him and records several proposed occasions without settling one. The reign shown is David’s in the “usual chronology” of the *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s “David” (vol. 4, 1908), marked disputed because the article reports a later dating beside it. It is the reference point of the attribution and not a date of the psalm. The same rows serve the Introit’s verse, Ps 118:1, and the Communion’s three half-verses.

### Matthew 18:23–35, the Gospel {#sec:date-matthew}

The parable is told in the discourse of Mt 18, after the arrival at Capharnaum (17:23) and before Jesus leaves Galilee (19:1). The record holds no date for that discourse, and the “Event” row says so; the king, the servants and their debts belong to a parable and have no date. The composition dates shown are the *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s, from “Gospel of Saint Matthew” (vol. 10, 1911) and “The New Testament” (vol. 14, 1912), all marked disputed; the first of these reports the tradition that Matthew wrote in Hebrew in Palestine, according to Eusebius when he was leaving it. The comparison row is the modern critical horizon of the introduction to Matthew in the *New American Bible, Revised Edition*, which concerns the Greek Gospel; it is shown beside the default answer and does not enter it. For the place where that Gospel was composed, the introduction calls Antioch, the capital of the Roman province of Syria, a plausible suggestion, not a settled fact.

### Ephesians 6:10–17, the Epistle {#sec:date-ephesians}

Paul names himself (1:1) and writes as a prisoner (3:1; 4:1) and “an ambassador in a chain” (6:20). The first range shown is that of the *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s “Epistle to the Ephesians” (vol. 5, 1909), which sets the letter in an imprisonment no earlier than the captivity at Caesarea and reports that the greater number of interpreters take it as a circular letter; the year is from the table in its “St. Paul” (vol. 11, 1911), which places the letter in the Roman captivity with Philemon, Colossians and Philippians. Both are marked disputed. The comparison row is the hypothesis that the introduction to Ephesians in the *New American Bible, Revised Edition* offers as one possible explanation of the letter’s style and teaching, a later disciple developing Paul’s thought; it is shown beside the default answer and does not enter it.

## Appendix: Scope and Qualifications {#sec:scope}

**Edition, formulary and occurrence.** The study treats the Mass *Dominica vigesima prima post Pentecosten*, II classis, of the *Missale Romanum*, editio typica, Typis Polyglottis Vaticanis 1962, pp. 413–415, nos. 1699–1708, in the universal 1962 calendar and no particular calendar. Its occurrence on 18 October 2026, its precedence over the feast of St Luke, the commemoration of that feast in low and conventual Masses and its omission in a sung Mass that is not conventual, the oration *pro Fidei propagatione* added in every Mass on the penultimate Sunday of October, the colour, the Credo and the Trinity Preface rest on the Missal’s own calendar and general rubrics (RG 16, 91, 108, 111, 127 b; RGMR 434, 437 a, 444–446, 453, 475 a, 483, 494 b). Whether a local Ordinary has appointed another day for the missions, and any diocesan, titular, dedication or institute calendar or local external solemnity, are local facts not applied here. The added orations are named by incipit and locus and not reproduced; they are not part of the formulary.

**Text control.** Every Latin form of the ten proper elements was read on page images of the CMAA facsimile of the typical edition and collated with the Pustet Missal of 1862, in which every element’s wording already stands; the collation, the conclusion forms and the comparison with the Clementine Vulgate are recorded in `propers/verified.md`. No witness read outside liturgical books carries the Introit’s antiphon in the Missal’s form, the Missal’s opening of the Communion or its added *Domine Deus meus*, or the wording of the Offertory’s narrative; the Gradual’s *a generatione et progenie* stands in Cassiodorus’s lemma as transcribed, and the Gospel’s *suffocabat* in St Jerome’s lemma in Migne. No chant manuscript, Old Latin or Roman psalter was consulted. The Missal prints the Offertory antiphon alone; the verses that older chant books sang after it are not part of this formulary.

**English.** The scriptural English is the Douay–Rheims in Challoner’s revision at the canonical verses. Where the Missal departs from the Vulgate (the Introit’s antiphon, the Epistle’s opening, the Gradual’s *a generatione et progenie*, the Gospel’s opening, the Offertory’s narrative, and the Communion’s shortened and altered verses) that English does not render the Missal’s Latin; the Offertory and Communion are given in the English of the 1861 Philadelphia hand missal, which also supplies the opening sentence of the Introit and the three orations. None of these is an approved liturgical translation of the 1962 Missal. The English of the Fathers, Doctors and commentators is that of the published translations named in the References. Where the Latin of St Jerome, St Hilary, St Thomas or Cassiodorus is printed, the English beside it is a gloss of that Latin and not a translation from a named edition.

**Reception.** Each appointed passage was searched in the Latin and Greek Fathers, the later Doctors and saints, and the liturgical commentators available to this production, and the witnesses read are those named in the References at their exact loci. St Hilary’s commentary on Ps 118 and St Jerome’s on Matthew and Ephesians were read on the page images of Migne’s *Patrologia Latina*, and the exposition of the psalms printed under St Athanasius’s name on the page images of Migne’s *Patrologia Graeca*, whose attribution of that work is the printing’s. The other witnesses were read in text layers, transcriptions or web texts; St John Chrysostom’s Greek exposition of Ps 113 is described and not quoted. Rupert of Deutz, Honorius Augustodunensis, Sicard of Cremona and William Durandus are cited for what they say of the Introit, Collect, Epistle, Gradual, Offertory and Communion, which the Mass they expounded shared with this formulary although it read another Gospel; Rupert, Honorius and Durandus are cited on this formulary’s Gospel at the chapters where their books expound it. Bl. Ildefonso Schuster and the continuation of *The Liturgical Year* comment on this formulary as the 1962 Missal gives it. Origen is cited only for the view St Jerome rejects and St Thomas names. The sentence St Rabanus gives under St Gregory’s name was not checked in Gregory’s own works. Among the witnesses not reached are St Ambrose on Ps 118, St Hilary and St Robert Bellarmine on Ps 118:1, Greek commentators on Pss 89 and 118 other than the exposition named above, Theophylact and Cornelius a Lapide on Ephesians, Hugh of Saint-Cher, St Augustine’s *De spiritu et littera*, Amalarius on the Offertory’s verses, and St Anthony of Padua’s Sunday sermons.

**Chronology.** The rows of Scriptural Date and Location come from the Scripture chronology corpus, through the record generated for this formulary: its default profile’s answer for every element and, for the Gospel and the Epistle, a declared comparison under its critical profile. The record holds no occasion for any of the psalms, no attribution for Ps 89 or for Job, and no date for the telling of the parable; it is audited in `research/scope.md`.

**Rights.** The Latin of the formulary is published on the basis of its public-domain antecedent, the Pustet Missal of 1862 (17 U.S.C. 103(b)). The Bibles, the 1861 English, Migne’s texts and the other editions and translations quoted from the References are in the public domain in the United States. Cassiodorus and St Thomas on Job are quoted briefly from public-domain Latin in web transcriptions. The *New American Bible* introductions are summarised, not reproduced.

**Records.** The collation is in `propers/verified.md`, the occurrence and rubrics in `research/context.md`, the search, loci, disagreements and negative results in `research/scope.md`, and the reasoning behind the three readings in `research/interpretations.md`; all were reviewed independently before this study was written.
