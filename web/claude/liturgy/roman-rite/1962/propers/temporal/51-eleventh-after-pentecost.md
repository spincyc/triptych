## The Propers: Themes and Movement {#triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

> **Governing thesis**
>
> Four of this formulary’s ten texts stop exactly where a human contribution would begin. The Collect asks God to add what prayer does not presume to ask; the Epistle’s last words are grace not made void, and the missal cuts before the Apostle’s labour; the Communion antiphon drops the imperative *da ei* and keeps the filling of the barns; and in the Gospel the man who is healed says nothing at all until the string of his tongue has been loosed by somebody else. What holds the day together is not a theme but a grammar: God acts first, and the texts decline to describe what we add.

The book prints the ten texts in one order and the argument can be read in four stages:

1.  A holy place that turns out to be a people, and a body that flowers again. <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>, <span class="nodecor">(*Grad.*)</span>, <span class="nodecor">(*All.*)</span>

2.  What exceeds merit, and what exceeds request — with the lesson cut one clause short. <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span>, <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>

3.  *Ephphetha*: one word, and a mouth that will not close. <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>

4.  The healed man’s psalm, the accepted gift, the overflowing press. <span class="nodecor">(*Off.*)</span>, <span class="nodecor">(*Sec.*)</span>, <span class="nodecor">(*Comm.*)</span>, <span class="nodecor">(*Postcomm.*)</span>

English quoted in this section is the Douay–Rheims (Challoner) for scriptural propers and the 1861 Cummiskey hand missal for orations, as throughout; the witnesses are identified at each element in the commentary and in the references.

### 1. A holy place that is a people, and a body that flowers again

The Introit is built, not quoted. The missal’s own reference — *6-7 et 36* — announces that three clauses have been cut from three places in Psalm 67 and welded: God in his holy place; God who makes the like-minded dwell in a house; he will give power and strength to his people. Read in that order the antiphon performs a small argument. It names a place, and then says the place is inhabited, and then says the strength belongs to the inhabitants. St. Augustine put the point sharply on the second clause: God is contained by no place, so we should not look for him outside ourselves, but rather, dwelling in the house of one mind, deserve that he should dwell in us — and that place, he adds, grace builds, not merits.

One word of the Introit is a small marvel of transmission, and it is argued at the element below. The chant sings *unánimes*, “of one mind,” where every Bible of the Latin west reads *unius moris*, “of one manner.” Augustine did not have the chant’s word; he supplied it as his gloss. Peter Lombard passed the gloss on; Bellarmine read it back into the verse. Cassiodorus alone lemmatises it as text. The Introit sings what began as an explanation.

The Gradual and the Alleluia carry that forward in two different registers. The Gradual is Psalm 27:7 with v. 1 for its versicle — thanksgiving first, the earlier cry second, exactly the inversion the Introit performs on its own psalm — and its central words are *reflóruit caro mea*, my flesh has flowered again. Augustine glosses that in four words: *id est, et resurrexit caro mea*. Cassiodorus gives the prefix a history (the flesh flowered in the virginal conception and re-flowered in the Resurrection); Aquinas gives it a span from Adam to Christ; Bellarmine gives it a face (“restored … to the bloom of youth, health, joy, and beauty”). Theodoret declines the whole reading and hears a hunted man’s recovered vigour. The day sings the verse without settling the question. The Alleluia then does what a festal chant does: it summons the singing — take a pleasant psalm with the harp — and it does so immediately before the Gospel in which a man is given back the power of speech.

### 2. What exceeds merit, and what exceeds request

The Collect is two clauses of address and two of petition, and the two halves are exactly matched. God is addressed as exceeding both *mérita* and *vota* — what suppliants deserve and what suppliants ask — out of the abundance of his kindness. Then: pardon what conscience fears, and add what prayer does not presume. Below what we deserve, God does not exact; above what we ask, God supplies. The Latin makes *orátio* itself the thing that does not presume, which is a stranger and better sentence than the 1861 hand missal’s “we dare not presume to ask”; the divergence is declared at the element. St. Thomas states the principle without commenting on the prayer: *Deus multa nobis praestat ex sua liberalitate etiam non petita*.

The Epistle sets the same asymmetry in the first person. Paul delivers what he received — died, buried, raised, seen — and then puts himself at the end of the witness list as the untimely born, the least of the apostles, not worthy of the name. And then the lesson’s last words: *grátia autem Dei sum id quod sum, et grátia eius in me vácua non fuit*. That is where the missal stops, and where it stops is the single most consequential fact about this reading. The verse continues, in the Bible, “but I have laboured more abundantly than all they. Yet not I, but the grace of God with me” — and every classical exposition of Pauline grace built on this text turns on those unread words. Augustine’s proof of free will is the labour; Gregory’s prevenient-and-subsequent scheme hangs on the single word *mecum*; Aquinas derives *gratia cooperans* from the same clause. The cut is old — the same mid-verse ending stands in the Missals of 1570 and 1862 — and its motive is undocumented. Two things follow. A guide may not say that the Fathers expound this lesson’s teaching on grace: they expound one clause further. And the truncation cannot be pressed into a denial of cooperation, since the very authors whose key clause is missing are the ones who teach it.

### 3. *Ephphetha*: one word, and a mouth that will not close

The Gospel is a single healing told almost entirely in gestures. Jesus is in Gentile country throughout — Tyre, Sidon, the Decapolis — and the man is brought to him by others. He is taken out of the crowd; fingers are put in his ears; there is spittle, and a touch on the tongue; there is a look upward and a groan; and then one Aramaic word, which the evangelist translates: *Ephphetha, quod est adaperíre*. St. Bede reads the fingers as the Holy Spirit, proving it from Luke and Matthew on the finger of God, and the spittle as divine wisdom instructing the mouths of those being catechised; he reads the sigh not as need but as example. The seventh-century commentary passing under Jerome’s name reads the whole itinerary as salvation crossing from Judaea to the Gentiles and names what the loosed mouth first says: *Credo in Deum Patrem omnipotentem* — and the missal, at the end of this Gospel and no other in the formulary, prints the rubric *Credo*.

Then the closing paradox: he charged them to tell no one, and the more he charged them the more they published it. St. Augustine treats only this, and takes it as a rebuke to lazy preachers. And the word itself walked out of the Gospel into the sacraments: St. Ambrose says plainly that the Church’s rite of opening comes from this passage, and the *Rituale Romanum* was still printing *Ephpheta, quod est, Adaperire* over the ears of infants in the century that produced the missal.

### 4. The healed man’s psalm, the accepted gift, the overflowing press

Immediately after that Gospel the Offertory sings *clamávi ad te, et sanásti me* — I cried to thee, and thou hast healed me. It is the day’s one proper spoken by somebody already healed, and it arrives at the moment the gifts are set out. A caution belongs here rather than in an appendix, because it changes the claim: the entire checked tradition, Latin and Greek alike, reads that healing of the Resurrection, on the strength of the psalm’s title, the dedication of David’s house. Jerome states it in one line; Augustine asks who healed a man who was never sick, and answers that the flesh was healed when it rose. To hear the verse as the deaf-mute’s thanksgiving is to read it against the tradition, not with it.

The Secret then asks one gift to do two things — be acceptable to God, and be a support to the frailty of those offering it — and the Communion, uniquely in this formulary, quotes not a psalm but the wisdom of Israel: honour the Lord with thy substance and with the firstfruits, and thy barns shall be filled and thy presses run over with wine. Bede reads the firstfruits at three levels, and the middle one is the day’s own Epistle in other words: a man honours God with his substance who credits every good he has to grace and not to his own powers. The Postcommunion closes by refusing to divide the beneficiary: help of mind *and* body, and, saved in both, *gloriémur* — may we glory — in the fullness of the heavenly remedy. Schuster draws the consequence that the body’s contact with the Body of Jesus is a pledge of the final resurrection, and with that the day’s last word rejoins the first: power and strength given to his people.

## The Propers: Detailed Commentary

The commentary proceeds element by element, in the order the missal prints them. It opens with the complete appointed formulary, so that every claim made afterwards can be checked against the text it is made about; then it reports what two 1962 editions actually print and where the appointed text differs from the Bible’s; then it treats each element in turn, moving from the complete canonical context of the passage, through the direct exegesis of witnesses who had it in front of them, to later reception — explaining what each witness actually argues and preserving disagreement where the tradition disagrees.

### The appointed formulary in full

Everything the 1962 *Missale Romanum* prints for this Mass, in the order it prints it: the Latin of the Vatican *editio typica* exactly as the controlling facsimile has it, each element under its own printed heading, scriptural reference and marginal number, and beneath it the English of an identified public-domain witness. This is the canonical full research edition; a reader who came for the texts has them here.

**Formulary.** *DOMINICA UNDECIMA post Pentecosten*, *II classis*, printed pp. 390–392, marginal nos. 1542–1551. Green follows the general rubrics for Sundays after Pentecost and is not printed on those pages. The formulary begins immediately after the Postcommunion of the Tenth Sunday (no. 1541) and ends immediately before the heading *DOMINICA DUODECIMA post Pentecosten* (no. 1552). Nothing between those boundaries is omitted here. It appoints no Tract, Sequence, second oration, blessing or ritual text, and no second Collect, Secret or Postcommunion.

#### 1. Introit <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>

> ***Ant. ad Introitum* — Ps. 67, 6-7 et 36 — marginal no. 1542**
>
> *Deus in loco sancto suo: Deus qui inhabitáre facit unánimes in domo: ipse dabit virtútem et fortitúdinem plebi suæ. <span class="nodecor">Ps. ibid., 2</span> Exsúrgat Deus, et dissipéntur inimíci eius: et fúgiant, qui odérunt eum, a fácie eius. ℣. Glória Patri.*

> **English: Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Ps. 67:6–7 and 36 in full, with the fragments the antiphon takes in bold, and Ps. 67:2**
>
> **v. 6** Who is the father of orphans, and the judge of widows. **God in his holy place:** **v. 7** **God who maketh men of one manner to dwell in a house:** Who bringeth out them that were bound in strength; in like manner them that provoke, that dwell in sepulchres. **v. 36** God is wonderful in his saints: the God of Israel is **he who will give power and strength to his people.** Blessed be God.
>
> *Ps.* (v. 2) Let God arise, and let his enemies be scattered: and let them that hate him flee from before his face. ℣. *Glória Patri.*

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** Two declarations belong beside this text. *First*, the antiphon is a centonisation, which is why the missal’s own reference reads *6-7 et 36*: it takes the closing clause of v. 6, the opening clause of v. 7, and the central clause of v. 36, so the three Douay verses are printed whole with the sung fragments marked rather than stitched into a seamless English sentence the project would itself have composed. *Second*, at v. 7 the antiphon sings *unánimes* — “those of one mind” — where the Clementine, and therefore the Douay, reads *unius moris*, “of one manner.” The Douay cannot be quoted for the chant’s word, and this guide does not supply a rendering of its own; the older-psalter reading behind *unánimes* is discussed in the element commentary below. The doxology cue *Glória Patri* belongs to the Ordinary rather than to this formulary and is a scriptureless liturgical formula; no English is supplied for it here.

#### 2. Collect <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span>

> ***Oratio* — marginal no. 1543**
>
> *Omnípotens sempitérne Deus, qui, abundántia pietátis tuæ, et mérita súpplicum excédis et vota: effúnde super nos misericórdiam tuam; ut dimíttas quæ consciéntia métuit, et adícias quod orátio non præsúmit. Per Dóminum nostrum.*

> **English: Cummiskey (Philadelphia, 1861), Collect of this formulary, printed p. 418**
>
> O Almighty and eternal God, who, in the abundance of thy goodness, exceedest both the merits and requests of thy suppliants; pour forth thy mercy upon us: and both pardon what our consciences dreadeth, and grant such blessings as we dare not presume to ask. Thro’.

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** One shifted subject, declared and left standing. The Latin’s last clause makes the *prayer* the thing that does not presume — *quod orátio non præsúmit*, “what prayer does not presume” — and asks God to *adícias*, to *add* it. The 1861 English makes the petitioners the ones who dare not ask. The rendering is quoted because it is the registered public-domain witness; the argument built on the prayer’s grammar is conducted on the Latin, in the element commentary below. The 1861 “dreadeth” after “consciences” is as printed.

#### 3. Epistle <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>

> ***Léctio Epístolæ beáti Pauli Apóstoli ad Corínthios* — 1 Cor. 15, 1-10 — marginal no. 1544**
>
> *Fratres: Notum vobis fácio Evangélium, quod prædicávi vobis, quod et accepístis, in quo et statis, per quod et salvámini: qua ratióne prædicáverim vobis, si tenétis, nisi frustra credidístis. Trádidi enim vobis in primis, quod et accépi: quóniam Christus mórtuus est pro peccátis nostris secúndum Scriptúras: et quia sepúltus est, et quia resurréxit tértia die secúndum Scriptúras: et quia visus est Cephæ, et post hoc úndecim. Deínde visus est plus quam quingéntis frátribus simul, ex quibus multi manent usque adhuc, quidam autem dormiérunt. Deínde visus est Iacóbo, deínde Apóstolis ómnibus: novíssime autem ómnium tamquam abortívo, visus est et mihi. Ego enim sum mínimus Apostolórum, qui non sum dignus vocári Apóstolus, quóniam persecútus sum Ecclésiam Dei. Grátia autem Dei sum id quod sum, et grátia eius in me vácua non fuit.*

> **English: Douay–Rheims (Challoner), 1 Cor. 15:1–10, to the point at which the lesson ends**
>
> Now I make known unto you, brethren, the gospel which I preached to you, which also you have received and wherein you stand. By which also you are saved, if you hold fast after what manner I preached unto you, unless you have believed in vain. For I delivered unto you first of all, which I also received: how that Christ died for our sins, according to the scriptures: And that he was buried: and that he rose again the third day, according to the scriptures: And that he was seen by Cephas, and after that by the eleven. Then was he seen by more than five hundred brethren at once: of whom many remain until this present, and some are fallen asleep. After that, he was seen by James: then by all the apostles. And last of all, he was seen also by me, as by one born out of due time. For I am the least of the apostles, who am not worthy to be called an apostle, because I persecuted the church of God. But by the grace of God, I am what I am. And his grace in me hath not been void …

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** Two boundaries, both the missal’s. The lesson opens on the missal’s own address: where it prints *Fratres:*, the Clementine reads *Notum autem vobis fácio, fratres* — Paul’s “brethren” stands mid-clause, and the Douay accordingly begins “Now I make known unto you, brethren.” And the lesson ends mid-verse: the Douay’s v. 10 continues “but I have laboured more abundantly than all they. Yet not I, but the grace of God with me,” and the missal cuts before it, so the reading’s last word is grace not made void, not the Apostle’s labour. The same ending stands in the 1570 and 1862 Missals; it is the received lectionary boundary. Neither clause is supplied here.

>
> **v. 7** The Lord is my helper and my protector: **in him hath my heart confided, and I have been helped. And my flesh hath flourished again, and with my will I will give praise to him.** **v. 1** A psalm for David himself. **Unto thee will I cry, O Lord: O my God, be not thou silent to me:** lest if thou be silent to me, I become like them that go down into the pit.

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** Three declarations. *First*, the chant opens *In Deo sperávit* where the Clementine reads *in ipso sperávit* — “in him,” resuming the verse’s own opening *Dóminus adiútor meus* which the chant does not sing — so the Douay’s “in him” renders a pronoun the chant replaces with the divine name. *Second*, the versicle sings *clamávi*, “I have cried,” where the Clementine has the future *clamábo* and the Douay accordingly “will I cry.” *Third*, the versicle’s closing *ne discédas a me*, “depart not from me,” has no counterpart in the Clementine verse and therefore none in the Douay; the gap is declared and left in Latin. One American-edition difference falls inside the unsung clause printed above: the 1899 printing reads “lest thou be silent” where this e-text reads “lest if thou be silent.”

#### 5. Alleluia <span class="nodecor">(*All.*)</span>

> **Alleluia, no separate heading — Ps. 80, 2-3 — marginal no. 1546**
>
> *Allelúia, allelúia. ℣. <span class="nodecor">Ps. 80, 2-3</span> Exsultáte Deo adiutóri nostro, iubiláte Deo Iacob: súmite psalmum iucúndum cum cíthara. Allelúia.*

> **English: Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Ps. 80:2–3 in full, with the sung words in bold**
>
> **v. 2** **Rejoice to God our helper: sing aloud to the God of Jacob.** **v. 3** **Take a psalm,** and bring hither the timbrel: the pleasant psaltery **with the harp.**

**Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** The verse condenses Ps. 80:3: the Clementine reads *Súmite psalmum, et date tympanum; psaltérium iucúndum, cum cíthara*, and the chant drops the timbrel and the psaltery, attaching *iucúndum* — “pleasant” — to the psalm where the Bible attaches it to the psaltery. The Douay verse is therefore printed whole with the sung fragments marked; read in the chant’s own order they say: take a pleasant psalm with the harp. No English join is composed for it.

>

> **English: Douay–Rheims (Challoner), Mk. 7:31–37**
>
> And again going out of the coasts of Tyre, he came by Sidon to the sea of Galilee, through the midst of the coasts of Decapolis. **Where the registered English does not answer the Latin.** The missal’s *In illo témpore: Exiens Iesus de fínibus Tyri* names a subject Mark does not name at v. 31 and drops the evangelist’s *íterum* — the “again” that ties this journey back to the earlier Tyre episode — which the Douay’s opening “And again going out” renders. The liturgical incipit is the missal’s own and is not translated here. One transliteration difference is declared rather than harmonised: the missal prints *Ephphetha*, with the second aspirate, and the Douay prints “Ephpheta.”

**Rubric as printed:** *Credo.* Printed run-on, right-aligned at the end of the Gospel’s last line. A direction, not a text: the Creed itself is in the Ordinary.

#### 10. Postcommunion <span class="nodecor">(*Postcomm.*)</span>

> ***Postcommunio* — marginal no. 1551**
>

### The text: what two 1962 books actually print

The Latin above was collated at 300 and 600 dpi — 1200 dpi for the Gospel’s unaccented opening line — against the CMAA facsimile of the Vatican *editio typica* (printed pp. 390–392), and independently against page images of the Benziger *editio iuxta typicam* of the same year (printed pp. 385–386). The two books are not two photographs of one page. They are two editions of one revision, and they differ in nine places. Nothing below is blended; the typical edition governs every form printed in this guide.

| Introit close | *℣. Glória Patri.* and stop | *℣. Glória Patri. Deus.* — the printed cue to repeat the antiphon |

Two of these bear on the commentary rather than on the eye. The typical edition’s comma pair around *abundántia pietátis tuæ* marks that ablative as parenthetical to *qui … excédis*, which is exactly the reading the argument below makes of the prayer; Benziger’s single comma leaves it open whether the ablative attaches forward or back. And *adícias* against *adiícias* is the same contracted/uncontracted class that separates the two editions elsewhere; the 1862 Pustet hand missal prints *adjicias*, so three books spell one verb three ways. The Secret’s conclusion is the short *Per Dóminum.* in both witnesses. No reading in either was unclear enough to need a third witness, and no substitution was made in either direction.

#### Eleven places where the appointed text is not the Bible’s text

Eleven divergences were verified directly and are textual observations, not inferences. They are gathered once here; the commentary returns to each where it bears on an argument. This is the point at which the Latin has to be on the page, because an argument about wording cannot be conducted in translation.

Negative results belong with that table and are as much a finding as the divergences. The Alleluia’s first verse (Ps. 80:2) and the Offertory’s first verse (Ps. 29:2) match the Clementine word for word; so do the Gospel from v. 32 onward and the Epistle from v. 2 to the cut in v. 10, orthography aside. One further question is deliberately *not* published as a divergence: at Mk. 7:34 the missal and the 1862 Pustet print *suspíciens in cælum*, while the two Clementine transcriptions consulted print *suscípiens*. Those two transcriptions may not be independent of each other, and no printed Clementine facsimile was collated for the verse, so the guide records an unresolved lead rather than a finding.

#### Three consequences the collation forces on this commentary

*First*, at Ps. 67:7 the chant’s *unánimes* is not a corrupt *unius moris*. It is the reading of an older Latin psalter, and one checked expositor — Cassiodorus — lemmatises exactly that word. The Introit therefore sings a text that Augustine, Hilary, Jerome, Peter Lombard and Bellarmine did not have in front of them; what they have instead is *unius moris* or *unius modi* with *unanimes* supplied as a *gloss*. The Introit sings the gloss. That is argued at the element.

*Second*, the Gradual’s versicle ends *ne discédas a me*, and no checked witness expounds those words, because no checked witness has them: all of them expound *ne síleas* and *nequándo táceas*. Any reception attached to the Gradual’s closing plea is reception of a different clause, and this guide says so where it uses it.

*Third*, the Epistle stops mid-verse, at *et grátia eius in me vácua non fuit*. The words the missal does not read are the words on which the entire classical Latin argument about grace and free will turns. That is not a curiosity; it changes what may honestly be claimed about this lesson’s reception, and it is treated at length at the Epistle.

### Introit <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>

*Deus in loco sancto suo* *Ant. ad Introitum* — Ps. 67, 6-7 et 36, with *Ps. ibid.,* 2 marginal no. 1542

The complete appointed Latin has already been printed above, in liturgical order and beside the same English; the commentary quotes it again only where an argument turns on the wording.

**The whole psalm, and what the antiphon takes from it.** Psalm 67 is a long processional: God arises, his enemies scatter, he rides through the desert at the head of his people, Sinai trembles, rain falls on an inheritance, kings flee, a train of captives is led up to a mountain God chose for his dwelling, and the psalm ends in a summons to sing to him who mounts above the heaven of heavens. The antiphon takes three clauses out of that and welds them: the last words of v. 6 (*Deus in loco sancto suo*), the first words of v. 7 (*Deus qui inhabitáre facit … in domo*), and the middle of v. 36 (*ipse dabit virtútem et fortitúdinem plebi suæ*). What is dropped is instructive. Verse 6 begins with God as father of orphans and judge of widows; v. 7 continues with prisoners brought out and rebels left in a waterless place; v. 36 begins *Mirábilis Deus in sanctis suis* and ends *benedíctus Deus*. The centonisation keeps the housing and the strengthening and lets the orphans, the prisoners and the benediction go.

> **St. Hilary of Poitiers, *Tractatus super Psalmum* 67, §§2, 8, 37 (CSEL 22)** The earliest Latin exposition of the psalm, and it reads the whole thing as prophecy of Christ. On the psalm verse the missal sings — *Exsúrgat Deus* — Hilary is explicit that the rising asked for is the Resurrection: *resurrectionis enim nunc in deo tempus optatur*. At vv. 6–7 his lemma is *unimores*, a hyper-literal Latin for the Greek, and he is the first witness to weld the verse to the Jerusalem community of Acts: *ubi unius moris ac uoluntatis pacificus unanimisque conuentus — erat enim secundum Actus apostolorum credentium anima et cor unum*. At v. 36 he takes the giving of *virtus* and *fortitudo* as the resurrection of the body: God is wonderful in his saints *quos in uitae resurrectionem excitabit aeternos*, and gives strength to a people *quae inmortalis effecta intra se uitam uirtutemque contineat*.

> **St. Augustine, *Enarrationes in Psalmos* 67, §§2–3 (v. 2), §7 (vv. 6–7), §44 (v. 36; NPNF sec. 40)** Augustine’s lemma at v. 7 is *unius modi*, and he glosses it at once with the word the Introit sings: *unius modi habitare facit in domo*, that is, *unanimes, unum sentientes: iste est locus sanctus Domini*. He then makes the philological observation that the Greek admits either *modi* or *mores*, and notices that the Greek carries no prefix answering the *in* of *inhabitare*. The doctrinal point is the one the Introit’s first two clauses now make in sequence: God is contained by no place, so the holy place he is in is the one he makes — *ne quaeramus eum absque nobis; sed potius unius modi habitantes in domo, mereamur ut habitare etiam ipse dignetur in nobis* — and that place is built by grace and not by merits. On v. 2 he regards the petition as already answered: in the public-domain English of the NPNF translation, “Already this has come to pass: Christ has risen up … and His enemies have been dispersed through all nations.” On v. 36 the Introit’s last limb becomes resurrection doctrine, and he proves it out of the chapter the day’s own Epistle comes from: *Seminatur enim hoc corpus in infirmitate; surget in virtute* (1 Cor. 15:43), *virtutem resurrectionis eius* (Phil. 3:10), and *fortitudinem autem, qua inimica destruetur mors*. *Material negative, checked in the full Latin:* Augustine does **not** cite Acts 4:32 here. The Acts link that later readers assume is Augustinian is Hilary’s and Cassiodorus’s.

> **St. Jerome, *Tractatus de Psalmo* 67 (Morin)** A third track. Jerome’s lemma is *unius moris*, and he records what the Hebrew has: *In hebraeo autem habet “Dominus inhabitare facit monachos in domo”: id est, in quibus non cohabitat peccatum*. The monk hears *monks* in the verse. His exposition is accordingly moral rather than ecclesial — *Qui unius moris sunt, et non mutantur … Peccator autem mutatur*; *ubicumque sanctitas est, ibi habitaculum Dei est*. On v. 2 he states a double key openly: *specialiter* of the Lord, *ut resurgat a mortuis, et dispergat inimicos suos, hoc est diabolum et exercitum eius*, and *generaliter* whenever we are in tribulation. *Negative:* the homily breaks off at v. 31 and never reaches v. 36; the *Commentarioli* on this psalm begin only at v. 10.

> **Cassiodorus, *Expositio Psalmorum* 67, ad vv. 6, 7, 36** The finding that governs this element. Cassiodorus is the one checked expositor whose lemma is the Introit’s own text: *Deus, qui habitare facit unanimes in domo*. With that word in front of him he restores the Acts citation Augustine had dropped — *Multitudinis autem credentium erat cor unum et anima una* — adds Ps. 132:1, and identifies the house ecclesially: *In domo vero Ecclesiam significat*. At v. 36, however, he parts from Hilary and Augustine and takes the strength given to God’s people without any resurrection at all: *Dominus dabit fidelibus suis virtutem patientiae, et fidei fortitudinem, ut possint ad aeterna praemia pervenire*. The eschatology survives only as the *praemia*; the gift itself has been moralised into patience and faith.

> **Theodoret of Cyrus, *Interpretatio in Psalmos* 67, ad vv. 7 and 36 (PG 80, cols. 1380, 1400) — Greek-tradition sample** Theodoret reads the Septuagint’s “single-mannered” as those who hold one aim and choose to care for virtue only. There is no Acts citation, no monastic reading, and at v. 36 no resurrection: the saints are lovers of virtue whose lives make God be praised, on the model of Matthew 5:16, and the strengthening is providential. *Source limit:* the only English of this commentary is in copyright, so Theodoret is summarised here and nowhere quoted.

> **Peter Lombard, *Commentarium in Psalmos* 67, ad v. 6 (PL 191); St. Robert Bellarmine, *Explanatio in Psalmos* 67, tr. John O’Sullivan (Dublin, 1866), pp. 196–201** The conveyor belt and its terminus. Lombard’s exposition is Augustine with the gloss embedded and tagged — *unius moris … id est unanimes, unum sentientes … Hunc autem locum gratia aedificat, non meritis* — which is how the schools received both the word and the anti-Pelagian point. By Bellarmine the Acts reading is simply standard: the dwellers are “of one mind, one will,” and he quotes “One soul, one heart.” At vv. 35–36 he follows Augustine rather than Cassiodorus: God “will endow his elect with true and real immortality.”

**What the chant did to the tradition.** Set those lemmata side by side and the Introit’s second clause turns out to be a patristic gloss that became a liturgical text. Augustine reads *unius modi* and explains it by *unanimes*; Lombard transmits the explanation; Bellarmine reads the explanation back into the verse. Cassiodorus alone has *unanimes* as text. The Roman chant sings it. This guide does not assert a line of descent from Cassiodorus’s psalter to the Introit — no *Psalterium Romanum* witness was collated for it — but the coincidence is exact and is the reason the Douay cannot be quoted for the sung word.

**And a disagreement the formulary does not resolve.** The Introit’s last clause is read two ways by witnesses of equal standing. Hilary, Augustine and Bellarmine hear the resurrection of the body in it; Cassiodorus hears patience and faith; Theodoret hears providential strengthening. The day’s Epistle is 1 Corinthians 15, and Augustine reaches his reading by quoting that very chapter — but he is expounding a psalm, not this Mass, and nothing documents that anyone paired them. The disagreement is left standing.

**One material negative.** St. Thomas’s psalms commentary stops at Psalm 54; there is no Thomistic exposition of Psalm 67 or Psalm 80, and the guide does not supply one from elsewhere in his corpus.

### Collect <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span>

*Omnípotens sempitérne Deus, qui, abundántia pietátis tuæ* *Oratio*, composed oration marginal no. 1543

**Where the prayer comes from.** All three orations of this Mass travel together as a set, and they are old. The set stands complete in the Old Gelasian sacramentary (Vat. Reg. lat. 316, ed. H. A. Wilson, Oxford 1894), at p. 228, as the seventh of the sixteen unassigned Sunday masses of Book III — *Item alia Missa*, tied to no numbered Sunday at all. Wilson’s own footnote records that the Frankish Gelasians assign the mass to the *thirteenth* Sunday after Pentecost, and that Pamelius’s Gregorian prints it at the twelfth. The Gregorian Hadrianum (ed. Wilson, HBS 49, 1915) does assign it: at p. 172, chapter XXX, *DOMINICA .XII. POST PENTECOSTEN*. Through this whole stretch the Gregorian series runs one Sunday ahead of 1962, so the Hadrianum’s twelfth is the 1962 eleventh.

**A bounded negative that matters.** The Secret and the Postcommunion are older still and stand in the Veronese (“Leonine”) collection. The Collect is **not there**. The whole of Feltoe’s text was searched for *abundantia pietatis*, *merita suppli-*, *adicias* and *adjicias*, *excedis*, and all seven occurrences of *praesumit*; none is this prayer, and Wilson’s Gelasian margins cite no Leonine parallel for it either. The Collect’s oldest verified witness is the Gelasian. That is a bounded result about the books actually searched, not a claim about a prayer’s absolute age.

**The chant cues, and why no design argument follows.** Wilson’s apparatus to the Hadrianum reports the marginal chant cues of the Ottobonianus manuscript (Vat. Ottob. lat. 313). The antiphon set of this 1962 Mass — *Deus in loco sancto suo*, *Exaltabo te Domine*, *Honora Dominum* — is cued there against *Dominica X post Pentecosten*, two masses before the orations it now accompanies; Gregorian Dominica XII itself carries the cues *Respice … in testamentum* and *Panem de caelo*, which are the 1962 Thirteenth Sunday’s chants. The pairing of the *Honora Dominum* Communion with the *abundantia pietatis* Collect is therefore a product of a later realignment of two independently moving cycles. Anything this guide observes about how the day’s chants and orations illuminate each other is an observation about the book as it now stands, never a recovered intention.

**The grammar, which is the prayer.** *Omnípotens sempitérne Deus, qui, abundántia pietátis tuæ, et mérita súpplicum excédis et vota.* The two objects are not a doublet. *Merita* are what a suppliant has; *vota* are what a suppliant asks. God is addressed as exceeding both, and the ablative *abundántia pietátis tuæ* — set off by a comma pair in the typical edition — names the reason. Then the petition, and its two purpose clauses: *ut dimíttas quæ consciéntia métuit, et adícias quod orátio non præsúmit*. The first asks remission of what conscience fears; the second asks God to *add* what prayer does not presume. The subject of *præsúmit* is *orátio*: it is the prayer, not the petitioner, that does not presume. The registered 1861 English makes the petitioners the ones who “dare not presume to ask,” which is a fair devotional rendering and a different sentence; the guide quotes the witness and argues on the Latin, as declared at the element above. The structure is therefore symmetrical and complete: below what we deserve, God does not exact; above what we ask, God supplies.

**An echo, labelled as an echo.** The Vulgate of Ephesians 3:20 addresses God as *qui potens est omnia facere superabundánter quam pétimus aut intellégimus*. The two clauses map onto the Collect’s two — giving beyond what we ask, giving beyond what we understand to ask. There is no quotation and no documented dependence; it is reported as a verbal echo and nothing more.

**Doctrinal illumination, not a source.** No patristic or early-medieval commentary on the *wording* of these three orations was found, which is the expected result for composed Roman prayers of this age; the earliest wording-level commentary located is modern. Two Thomistic loci nevertheless illuminate the second petition exactly. *Summa theologiae* II–II q. 83 a. 2 ad 3 states the principle the Collect prays: *Deus multa nobis praestat ex sua liberalitate etiam non petita* — God grants us much unasked, out of his liberality. And q. 83 a. 16 treats whether the prayer of a sinner is heard, concluding that it is heard *non quasi ex iustitia … sed ex pura misericordia*, with a *sed contra* that quotes Augustine on the publican: *si peccatores non exaudiret Deus, frustra publicanus dixisset: Domine, propitius esto mihi peccatori*. Neither article is about this Collect. Both are about what the Collect asks.

> **Dom Prosper Guéranger, *The Liturgical Year* … *Time after Pentecost*, vol. II, 2nd ed., tr. Laurence Shepherd (1909), Eleventh Sunday, pp. 272–274** Guéranger keys the Collect to the publican — and does so on historical grounds, citing Rupert of Deutz, *De divinis officiis* XII, 11, for an older arrangement that read Luke 18 on this Sunday. On the prayer itself: the Church, “with motherly tenderness, comes and takes up his faltering prayer, and gives it her own eloquence … granting them what they, poor sinners, are too afraid to presume to ask for.” That is reception of the second petition, and it reads *orátio* the way the 1861 missal does.

> **Blessed Ildefonso Schuster, *The Sacramentary* (*Liber Sacramentorum*), tr. Levelis-Marke, vol. III (1927), pp. 124–125** Schuster calls the Collect “a perfect liturgical gem” and says that “this short petition from the Sunday Liturgy contains a complete treatise on prayer.” The judgment is worth recording because it is a judgment about compression: three clauses that between them state who prays, what prayer cannot reach, and what God does about the shortfall.

**Afterlife in the reformed books.** The prayer did not stop in 1962. It is used verbatim, with the same contracted *adícias*, as the Collect of *Dominica XXVII per annum* in the postconciliar *Missale Romanum*. That reuse was verified in two independent unofficial Latin transcriptions; no free scan of the official *editio typica tertia* was located, so the official locus remains a lead and the postconciliar book is not quoted here. The guide reports the reuse at that strength and no higher.

### Epistle <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>

*Fratres: Notum vobis fácio Evangélium* *Léctio Epístolæ beáti Pauli Apóstoli ad Corínthios* — 1 Cor. 15, 1-10 marginal no. 1544

**The complete literary context.** First Corinthians 15 is the last of the letter’s problem chapters and the only one that argues a doctrine rather than settling a practice. Some at Corinth were saying *resurréctio mortuórum non est* (v. 12). Paul answers in three movements: he restates the gospel he delivered and the witnesses who saw the risen Christ (vv. 1–11); he shows what follows if the dead are not raised — empty preaching, empty faith, false witness, the dead perished (vv. 12–19); and he expounds Christ as firstfruits, the order of the resurrection, the manner of the risen body, and the swallowing up of death (vv. 20–58). The appointed lesson is the first movement, minus its last verse and minus the last clause of v. 10. It is therefore the *premiss* of the chapter read without the argument it was laid down to support — which is precisely what makes it usable as a Sunday lesson: the assembly hears the creed and the witness list, not the polemic.

**The oldest layer of reception is not about grace.** Long before anyone argued about merit from v. 10, this passage was the anti-docetic proof text. St. Irenaeus quotes the formula verbatim inside the argument that Christ truly suffered (*Adversus haereses* 3.18.3). Tertullian presses it further, in the public-domain English of the Ante-Nicene Fathers: “since you are told that He died according to the Scriptures … no otherwise than in the flesh, you will also allow that it was in the flesh that He was raised from the dead. For the very same body which fell in death, and which lay in the sepulchre, did also rise again” (*De resurrectione carnis* 48). *Bounded note:* *Adversus Marcionem* 5 engages this chapter only from v. 12, so it is not a witness to the appearance list and is not cited as one.

> **St. John Chrysostom, *Homiliae in epistulam I ad Corinthios* 38, §§2–3, 5, 7 (NPNF 1/12, tr. Chambers, 1889)** Chrysostom’s first observation is grammatical and it controls everything after it: Paul says *delivered* and *received*, not *taught* — “neither here does he say, I said to you, nor, I taught you, but uses the same expression again, saying, *I delivered unto you that which also I received*.” The gospel is transmission, not invention. The centre of the homily is anti-Manichaean: because Christ was sinless, his dying “for our sins” can only be the body’s death — “if being without sin He died, He died — not the death of sin … but the death of the body” — and *secúndum Scriptúras* pins that sense, with the burial clinching it. The appearance list he reads as a chain ordered by credibility, Cephas “the most credible of all”; and he notices that Paul writes *fallen asleep*, not *dead*, so that even the verb preaches resurrection. On v. 8 the self-abasement is calculated: “This is rather an expression of modesty than anything else.” On v. 10 — the lesson’s last words — he catches the reserve exactly: “he said not, I have displayed a diligence worthy of His grace, but, *it was not found vain*.” Chrysostom’s instinct throughout is **moral**. Augustine’s, on the same words, will be **dogmatic**.

> **Ambrosiaster, *Commentaria in epistulam ad Corinthios primam*, ad 15:1–11 (PL 17)** The earliest Latin commentary on the lesson, and the one with a text-critical surprise in it. On vv. 1–2 the stakes are total: those who desert the tradition *perdiderunt quod crediderunt; omnis enim spes credentium in hoc sensu est, quia mortui resurgent*. *Secundum Scripturas* he refers to the Old Testament, chiefly Isaiah 53, with an excursus on prophetic past tenses; the third day he takes from Hosea 6:3 read *sub persona populi*, adding *In Christo enim omnes resurrexisse, sicut in Adam omnes mortuos esse, nemo fidelium denegat*. James is singled out *ut multifaria apparentia fidem resurrectionis firmaret*. *Abortivus* he explains as *extra tempus natus* — Paul received the apostolate when Christ had already been received into heaven with his flesh. On v. 9, *tempore enim minor est, non dignitate*; on v. 10, *Ne se extulisse jactanter putaretur, statim se humiliat*. **And his lemma for the lesson’s last clause is not the Vulgate’s.** He reads *Et gratia ejus **pauper** in me non fuit* — “his grace was not poor in me” — an Old Latin variant that Cornelius a Lapide still knew, a millennium later, as “St. Ambrose’s” reading. The missal’s *vácua* is not the word the first Latin commentator had.

> **Theodoret of Cyrus, *Interpretatio in xiv epistulas S. Pauli*, in 1 Cor. 15 (PG 82, cols. 347–352; Migne’s Latin column quoted)** Theodoret states the chapter’s occasion first: impostors were denying the bodily resurrection, and cross, passion and resurrection all happened *propter resurrectionem communem*; hence *si enim non creditur resurrectio corporum, supervacanea est praedicatio Evangelii*. He builds the witness list as an ascending argument from testimony — the five hundred are *a suspicione alienum* and checkable, since *multi manent usque adhuc*. His reading of *abortivus* is his own and is the harshest in the tradition: Paul *abortivo fetui seipsum comparat, qui in hominum catalogo non recensetur* — the aborted foetus is not entered in the register of men. On v. 10 he catches Paul’s double bind: *Divitias occultare conatur, et aperire cogitur … Occultat enim miracula, et solum laborem ostendit, atque hunc quoque dicit esse divinae gratiae*. *Rights limit:* the only modern English of this commentary is in copyright; the Latin column is quoted instead.

> **St. Thomas Aquinas, *Super I ad Corinthios*, cap. 15, lectio 1, nn. 888–911** **Authenticity first, because this corpus has a seam in it.** The *lectura* on 1 Corinthians survives with a gap filled from Peter of Tarantaise; that supplement covers 7:15–10:33, and the authentic *reportatio vulgata* resumes at chapter 11. Chapter 15 is therefore Thomas’s own. Lectio 1 covers vv. 1–11 entire and lectio 2 begins at v. 12, so the whole 1962 pericope sits inside one lecture. Thomas divides the four things delivered — death, burial, resurrection, appearance — gives three reasons the burial is named at all, and explains the omission of the women’s appearances with a principle of forensic economy: *noluit ponere testimonia nisi authentica*. He preserves Augustine’s judgment that the true reading at v. 5 is *duodecim* and that *undecim* is *corruptum … vitio scriptorum* — the missal prints *úndecim*, and the guide prints what the missal prints while recording that the tradition mostly defends the harder number. On *abortivus* he does what a scholastic does and distinguishes three senses at once: *vel quia nascitur extra tempus debitum, vel cum violentia educitur, vel quia non perducitur ad debitam quantitatem* — and applies all three to Paul, including, remarkably, the second: converted by coercion, which he turns into an argument *contra haereticos, qui dicunt quod nullus debet cogi ad fidem, quia Paulus coactus fuit*. On the lesson’s own last words: *ex me nihil sum, sed id quod sum, gratia Dei sum … Et dicit “id quod sum,” quia homo sine gratia nihil est*. *Rights limit:* Larcher’s English is modern and in copyright; the Latin is quoted instead.

#### The lesson stops one clause short of the argument the tradition built on it

This is the finding that most changes how the Epistle may be preached from, and it is a textual observation before it is anything else. The missal reads to *et grátia eius in me vácua non fuit* and stops. The Clementine verse continues: *sed abundántius illis ómnibus laborávi: non ego autem, sed grátia Dei mecum*. Now consider what hangs on the words that are not read.

| **Witness** | **The argument** | **Which words it needs** |
|:--------------|:------------------------|:--------------------------|
| St. Augustine, *De gratia et libero arbitrio* 5.12 | Paul’s merit was real but *evil* — *Meritum enim fuit quidem in apostolo Paulo, sed malum*; then the balance: *nec gratia Dei sola, nec ipse solus, sed gratia Dei cum illo* | the proof of free will is *plus omnibus illis laboravi*; the correction is *non ego autem, sed gratia Dei mecum* |
| St. Gregory the Great, *Moralia in Iob* 16.25 | prevenient and subsequent grace: *Superna ergo pietas prius agit in nobis aliquid sine nobis, ut, subsequente quoque nostro libero arbitrio, bonum quod iam appetimus agat nobiscum* | the whole schema hangs on one word: *Non enim diceret **mecum** si … subsequens liberum arbitrium non haberet* |
| St. Thomas Aquinas, *Super I Cor.* c. 15 lect. 1 n. 909 | *gratia cooperans* is derived here: God *non solum infundit gratiam … sed etiam movet ad bene utendum gratia infusa* | *non autem ego, sed gratia Dei mecum* |
| St. John Chrysostom, Hom. 38 §7 | the humility “with reserve” turns on a seesaw of boast and retraction | “laboured more abundantly … yet not I” |
| Ambrosiaster; Cornelius a Lapide | separate lemmata and comments; a Lapide’s anti-Reformation argument rests on *with* me, not *in* me | both omitted clauses |

Two conclusions follow, and the second is the one worth having. *First*, a guide may not say that the Fathers expound this lesson’s teaching on grace, because their classic expositions quote past its last word. What they expound is verse 10 entire. *Second*, the truncation is not a 1962 decision. The same mid-verse ending stands in the Venice Missal of 1570 and in the Pustet Missal of 1862, so it is the received lectionary boundary of the Roman rite, of undocumented motive and considerable age. What the Church actually reads on this Sunday ends on *grátia … vácua non fuit*: grace not made void, with the labour and the *mecum* left unread. The guide notes, and refuses to press, the fact that the very tradition whose key clause is cut — Augustine, Gregory, Thomas — affirms human cooperation; a lection boundary is not a doctrinal statement, and this one cannot be made into a denial of what those authors teach.

#### One word, three readings, and a sixfold catalogue

*Tamquam abortívo* is where this pericope’s reception genuinely disagrees with itself, and the disagreement is substantive rather than verbal. Ambrosiaster and Chrysostom read it temporally: born out of time, that is, brought to apostolic birth after the ascension and Pentecost. Theodoret reads it juridically: a thing not entered in the register of men, the language of worthlessness rather than of chronology. Aquinas adds the violent sense — *cum violentia educitur* — and makes Paul’s conversion on the Damascus road a case of it. Cornelius a Lapide, writing in the seventeenth century and read here in the public-domain English of Cobb (1908), catalogues six readings and rejects at least one of them (Baronius’s theory that Paul was a supernumerary member of the Sanhedrin). The Douay’s “as by one born out of due time” takes the first sense only; the guide notes that the Latin will bear all three and does not choose among them.

**Two further material negatives.** The *Summa theologiae*’s treatise on grace, I–II qq. 106–114, never cites 1 Cor. 15:10 verbatim: the files were searched for *gratia Dei sum id quod sum*, *gratia Dei mecum*, *vacua*, and *abundantius*, and q. 111 a. 2’s *sed contra* rests on Augustine’s treatise instead. The verse enters Thomas’s doctrine of grace through the *lectura*, not through the *Summa*. And no Augustinian exegesis of *abortivus* said of Paul was found in site-scoped searches of his Latin corpus; what Augustine does with the adjacent verses is homiletic — *Sermo* 279, 5–6 calls Paul *modicus*, “least and last, like the hem of the Lord’s garment” that healed the woman with the issue of blood, and *Enarratio in Ps. 70*, sermo 1, shows him preaching on an Apostle-reading that contained v. 9 in his own day’s liturgy.

**Where the Summa does use the lesson.** *Summa theologiae* III q. 55 a. 3 cites vv. 6–7 as appearances of which the Gospels make no mention, and III q. 57 a. 6 ad 3 presses v. 8 dogmatically: the Damascus vision *veritatem resurrectionis non probaret nisi ipsum verum corpus visum fuisset ab eo*. The appearance-list is doing evidentiary work about the risen body, which is what Paul laid it down to do.

### Gradual <span class="nodecor">(*Grad.*)</span>

*In Deo sperávit cor meum* *Graduale* — Ps. 27, 7 et 1 marginal no. 1545

**The chant inverts the psalm.** Psalm 27 opens with a cry of a man afraid of being ignored — *Ad te, Dómine, clamábo: Deus meus, ne síleas a me: nequándo táceas a me, et assimilábor descendéntibus in lacum* — and reaches its thanksgiving only at v. 7, after the imprecation of vv. 3–5. The Gradual prints *7 et 1*: the body of the chant is the thanksgiving and the versicle is the cry that historically came first. As at the Introit, the liturgy sings the arrival and then the approach.

**And it alters the words at four points.** *In Deo* for the psalm’s *in ipso* (the pronoun looks back to an opening *Dóminus adjútor meus* that the chant does not sing, so the chant must supply a name); *confitébor illi* for *confitébor ei*; *clamávi*, past, for *clamábo*, future; and, at the very end, *ne discédas a me*, which has no counterpart anywhere in the Clementine verse. Of these the last is decisive for reception, and negatively: **no checked witness expounds *ne discédas a me*, because no checked witness has it.** Every one of them — Augustine, Cassiodorus, Jerome, Aquinas, Lombard, Bellarmine — expounds *ne síleas* and *nequándo táceas*. What the tradition supplies for the chant’s closing plea is therefore a sense, not a lemma, and the guide says so wherever it uses that sense.

> **St. Augustine, *Enarrationes in Psalmos* 27, §§1, 7** Augustine assigns the whole psalm to Christ: *Ipsius mediatoris vox est, manu fortis in conflictu passionis*. On the versicle’s “be not silent from me” he reads a Christological danger — that the Word be separated from the assumed manhood. And at v. 7 he supplies the flattest and most consequential gloss in the whole tradition of this chant: *Et refloruit caro mea: id est, et resurrexit caro mea*. The flourishing is the Resurrection. He then notices the psalm’s own *ex voluntáte mea*: those freed from the fear of death confess him by free will.

> **Cassiodorus, *Expositio Psalmorum* 27, ad vv. 1, 7** Cassiodorus’s lemma at v. 7 is the chant’s *confitébor illi*. He follows Augustine on the speaker — *Christus Dominus clamat ad Patrem tempore passionis* — and then develops the flower-figure into a two-stage history that Augustine had left implicit: *Refloruit … quia et primo floruit: quippe quae ex Virgine sine peccato tanquam pulcherrimi floris singulare decus emicuit*, with Isaias 11:1 behind it. The flesh flowered in the virginal conception and re-flowered in the Resurrection. *Refloruit ergo significat resurrexit*: the prefix carries the doctrine.

> **St. Jerome, *Commentarioli in Psalmos* 27 (Morin)** Two short notes, both useful. On v. 1, *Lacus significat infernum* — and his lemma is the Gallican one, without *ne discédas*. On v. 7 he records a variant and then a second reading: *Pro carne, Theodotion “cor” interpretatus est. Aliter: quippe consurreximus Christo*. Theodotion read “heart” where the Church’s psalter reads “flesh”; and the flourishing may be read not of Christ’s flesh but of ours, risen with him. *Negative:* there is no Jerome homily on Psalm 27; the *Tractatus* series jumps from Psalm 14 to Psalm 66.

> **St. Thomas Aquinas, *Super Psalmo* 27, nn. 1, 7** Thomas divides the psalm scholastically and then, following the Gloss, gives the versicle to the man Christ praying not to be abandoned by the Godhead: *ne sileas subtrahendo mihi in beneficium resurrectionis, et ne taceas, idest ne deseras in passione*. He also records Jerome’s rendering of the Hebrew, *ne te tacente assimiler*. On v. 7 he gives the flower a salvation-historical span: *In primo homine floruit caro eius per innocentiae puritatem, sed peccando inquinata est. Sed in Christo refloruit in resurrectione*. His lemma for the last clause is *confitébor tibi*, not the chant’s *illi*, and he reads it of the converted will praising freely; he adds that if the verse is said of Christ, *refertur ad membra sua*.

> **Theodoret of Cyrus, *Interpretatio in Psalmos* 27, ad v. 7 (PG 80, col. 1060) — the dissent** Theodoret keeps the psalm where the Antiochene school keeps it: with David in flight from Saul, the Ziphites and Doeg. On v. 7 he understands a man released from the hardships of flight, sprouting and blooming again — bodily vigour recovered because his spirits have recovered. **No resurrection.** He is the one major witness who declines the Christological reading of *reflóruit caro mea*, and the chant is therefore sung under a real disagreement, not a consensus. *Rights limit:* summarised, not quoted; the only English is in copyright.

> **Peter Lombard, *Commentarium in Psalmos* 27, ad v. 7 (PL 191); St. Robert Bellarmine, *Explanatio in Psalmos* 27, tr. O’Sullivan (1866), pp. 68–69** Lombard transmits both readings with their tags: the flesh *quae in Adam defloruit per peccatum* from the interlinear Gloss, and *Vel, caro mea refloruit, id est iterum in resurrectione floruit* correctly attributed to Cassiodorus. Bellarmine popularises the result — “He now passes to foretell the glory of the Lord’s resurrection” — and gives the flower its most concrete form: “My flesh, that had withered up in death, is not only restored to life, but to the bloom of youth, health, joy, and beauty.”

**A one-line development map.** Augustine’s equation (*refloruit = resurrexit*) is taken up by Cassiodorus, who adds the conception-flower; Lombard transmits both with attributions intact; Aquinas recasts them as Adam and Christ, flesh and soul; Bellarmine turns them into devotional description. Jerome supports from the side with Theodotion’s variant and the co-resurrection reading. Theodoret dissents. Hilary is simply absent: the extant *Tractatus super Psalmos* covers Psalms 1, 2, 9, 13, 14, 51–69 and 118–150 with fragments, and Psalm 27 is not among them.

### Alleluia <span class="nodecor">(*All.*)</span>

*Exsultáte Deo adiutóri nostro* Alleluia, no separate heading — Ps. 80, 2-3 marginal no. 1546

**The psalm and the compression.** Psalm 80 is a feast-day psalm: a summons to shout, a recital of the exodus from the burden of Egypt, and then a divine oracle of complaint — my people did not hear me, so I let them go after the desires of their heart. The chant takes only the summons, and condenses even that. The Clementine v. 3 reads *Súmite psalmum, et date tympanum; psaltérium iucúndum, cum cíthara*; the Alleluia sings *súmite psalmum iucúndum cum cíthara*. Two instruments vanish and one adjective moves: *iucúndum* belongs to the psaltery in the Bible and to the psalm in the chant. The result is a line that says, in effect, take a pleasant psalm with the harp — which is a description of what the schola is at that moment doing.

**The reception problem this creates, stated plainly.** Every checked expositor of this verse builds part of his exposition on the timbrel that the chant removes. That is not an objection to the chant; it is a limit on the reception. What follows illuminates Psalm 80:2–3; it does not comment on the Alleluia’s line, which is nobody’s lemma.

> **St. Augustine, *Enarrationes in Psalmos* 80, §§3–5** Augustine takes Asaph as *congregatio* and the title’s winepresses as the Church’s press, separating oil from dregs. On v. 2 he is anti-spectacle: *Exsultate alii circo, vos Deo* — let others shout in the circus; you, to God our helper. On v. 3 he builds an exchange out of the two instruments: *Psalmus spiritalis est, tympanum carnale … accipite spiritalia, et date carnalia*. Receive the spiritual word, give back carnal things — support and mercy to the poor. The psaltery, which sounds from above, is heavenly preaching; the cithara, played below, is earthly works; word and deed have to sound together.

> **Cassiodorus, *Expositio Psalmorum* 80, ad vv. 2–3** Cassiodorus first presses *adiutóri*: an idol *fictitius deus non est adiutor, sed elisor; non sanans, sed vulnerans* — not healing but wounding. The true God heals, and the verb is the one this day’s Offertory sings four elements later. He then etymologises the shout — *Iubilate dictum est a iuvando, id est delectando* — and re-tools Augustine’s exchange ascetically: the drum is a stretched hide, therefore the mortified body, *sic hominum corpus, dum pro Domino tribulatione quatitur, ad superna mandata dulcius temperatur; tunc Deo bene damus tympanum dum eleemosynas facimus, cum ieiuniis corpus affligimus*. Psaltery and cithara — God’s words and human acts — *in unam societatem debeant convenire*.

> **Theodoret of Cyrus, *Interpretatio in Psalmos* 80, ad vv. 2–3 (PG 80, cols. 1520–1521); Bellarmine, *Explanatio in Psalmos* 80, tr. O’Sullivan, pp. 246–247** The literal wing. Theodoret notes the hexaplaric alternatives for the two verbs, takes the jubilation as a victory-shout, and keeps the instruments as instruments, appealing to the account of the temple orchestra in Chronicles. Bellarmine sides with him against Augustine on the occasion — a new-moon festival — and adds, with characteristic bluntness, that “he who sings moodily … gives to understand that he has very little affection for him whose praises he chants.” Against Augustine’s and Cassiodorus’s allegory this is a genuine and unresolved divergence about what the verse is for.

**Two negatives worth printing.** St. Jerome does treat Psalm 80, but not these verses: his homily is a riff on the title, on the three *pro torcularibus* psalms (8, 80, 83) and on the number eighty — *Octavus dies resurrectionis est … in octavo ergo vincimus, et in octogesimo cum Domino congregamur* — and then leaps from the title to v. 5. His famous timbrel allegory (*nos sumus tympanum … quod non habet carnes, nisi quod tenditur*) belongs to his homily on Psalm 149, not here, and is not cited for this chant. And Aquinas, again, is absent: his psalms commentary ends at Psalm 54.

### Gospel <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>

*In illo témpore: Exiens Iesus de fínibus Tyri* *✠ Sequéntia sancti Evangélii secúndum Marcum* — Marc. 7, 31-37 marginal no. 1547

**Where the pericope sits in Mark, and what the incipit removes.** The healing follows the Syrophoenician woman (7:24–30) and precedes the feeding of the four thousand (8:1–9). The itinerary — Tyre, then Sidon, then the Sea of Galilee by way of the Decapolis — keeps Jesus in largely Gentile territory from beginning to end, and it is a long way round: Sidon lies north of Tyre, so the route to the lake is described as a detour. Mark’s own first words are *Et íterum éxiens*: *again* going out, a backward link to the earlier Tyre episode. The liturgical incipit replaces that with *In illo témpore: Exiens Iesus*, supplying a subject Mark does not name at v. 31 and dropping the *íterum*. The lesson therefore begins as a fresh journey where the evangelist wrote a resumption.

**There is no synoptic parallel.** Matthew 15:29–31 is a summary notice of many healings on a mountain: no individual deaf-mute, no fingers, no spittle, no sigh, no *Ephphetha*. That is why the scene surfaces in St. Augustine’s *De consensu evangelistarum* at Book IV — his sweep of Mark-only material — and not in the harmonising Books II–III. Any reception of “the healing of the deaf-mute” is reception of Mark alone.

> **Ps-Jerome, *Commentarius in evangelium secundum Marcum*, cap. VII (PL 30, cols. 611C–612B)** Transmitted under Jerome’s name and not his: the 1842 Oxford editors of the *Catena* already called it spurious, and modern scholarship treats it as an anonymous seventh-century work of the Irish milieu — in any case earlier than Bede. Its reading is corporate from the first line: Tyre is *angustia* and stands for Judaea; *Sidon vero venatio interpretatur, quae fera indomitaque nostra natio est*; the Decapolis is *decalogi mandata*. The itinerary *is* the passage of salvation from Judaea to the Gentiles. The deaf-mute is accordingly not an individual but the race: *Genus humanum per multa membra, ac si unus homo varia peste absumptus enumeratur: in protoplasto caecatur*. The fingers are *verba spiritus*; the spittle is *divina sapientia: quae solvit vinculum labiorum humani generis* — and the author names what the loosed mouth then says: *Credo in Deum Patrem omnipotentem*. The opened ears open *ad hymnos, cantica, et psalmos*. The sigh teaches: *nos gemere docuit, et in coelum thesaurum cordis nostri erigere*.

> **St. Bede, *In Marci evangelium expositio* II, on 7:31–37 (PL 92, cols. 203–206; his lemma spells *Effeta*)** The fullest Latin exposition, and the one the later tradition quarries. Six moves. *(a)* Being taken apart from the crowd is where salvation starts: *Prima salutis janua est infirmum apprehendente Domino de turba seorsum educi*, the sick mind drawn away *a consuetis humanae conversationis moribus*. *(b)* The fingers are the Holy Spirit, and he proves it from Scripture rather than asserting it: *Mittit digitos suos in auriculas, cum per dona sancti Spiritus aures cordis … aperit. Namque digitum Dei appellari Spiritum sanctum testatur ipse Dominus*, comparing Luke 11:20 with Matthew 12:28. *(c)* The spittle is divine wisdom — *Sputum namque Domini saporem designat sapientiae* — and the tongue it touches is a catechumen’s: the Lord acts *cum ad confessionem fidei ora catechizandorum instruit*. *(d)* The look upward names where hearing and speech must be sought; the sigh is not need but instruction: *Ingemuit autem, non quia ipsi opus esset cum gemitu aliquid petere a Patre … sed ut nobis gemendi daret exemplum*. *(e)* Verses 34–35 hold both natures at once: *Suspiciens in caelum quasi homo Deum precaturus ingemuit, sed mox uno sermone quasi divina potens maiestate curavit*. *(f)* Morally the deaf-mute is anyone *qui nec aures audiendis Dei verbis, nec os aperit proloquendis*, and he is carried by those already taught. *One correction this guide makes:* Bede’s closing paragraph on v. 36 is Augustine’s *De consensu evangelistarum* 4.4.5 almost verbatim. Here Bede depends on Augustine — not, as is sometimes assumed for Marcan pericopes, on Gregory.

> **The Victor-of-Antioch tradition, quoted as “Chrysostomus” in the *Catena aurea in Marcum*, cap. 7, lectio 4** Chrysostom wrote no homilies on Mark. The excerpts the *Catena* labels “Chrysostomus” for this pericope belong to the Greek Mark-catena tradition associated with Victor of Antioch; the 1842 Oxford editors say so in the margin and print “Pseudo-Chrys.” The guide names the source that way and does not credit Chrysostom. The move is worth having on its own account: the healing instrument is Christ’s own assumed body, *quod divina virtute ditatum erat corpus divinitati unitum … veniens Christus in seipso perfectionem demonstravit humanae naturae*; and the sigh is *nostram causam suscipiens in seipso, et naturae misertus humanae*. Where Bede makes the sigh an example for us, this tradition makes it a taking-on of our case.

> **Theophylact, *Enarratio in evangelium Marci*, on 7:31–37 (PG 123, cols. 565–568; Migne’s Latin quoted)** The Greek witness, read with Migne’s facing Latin because the only English is in copyright. He takes the affliction as demonic in origin, which is why it follows the exorcism of the Syrophoenician’s daughter, and notes that Jesus leaves Gentile territory promptly lest he be called a transgressor of the law. On the taking aside: *non erat arrogantiae sectator, qui se humiliarat usque ad nostram inopiam*. On the spittle he makes the strongest sacramental point in the tradition: it was done *ut ostenderet omnes partes sanctae carnis suae sanctas ac divinas esse … tametsi superfluitas sit omne sputum, sed in Domino admirabilia erant ac divina omnia*. On the sigh he is openly undecided — twice *fortassis* — between prayer to the Father and pity for human nature under tyranny. His coda, which both the *Catena* and a Lapide lift, converts the command of silence into an ethic: benefactors must not seek applause, and beneficiaries must praise even the unwilling benefactor.

> **St. Augustine, *De consensu evangelistarum* 4.4.5 (NPNF 1/6, tr. Salmond, 1888)** Augustine treats only vv. 36–37, and only one difficulty: if Christ knew they would publish it the more, why forbid it? His answer, in the public-domain English, is that “he desired to give backward ones to understand how much more zealously and fervently they ought to preach on whom He lays the commission to preach, if even men who were interdicted were unable to keep silent.” No allegory of fingers, spittle or sigh anywhere; this is the one place the Latin tradition’s greatest exegete touches the pericope, and it is a note on prescience and preaching.

> **Cornelius a Lapide, on Mark 7:31–37, *The Great Commentary*, tr. T. W. Mossman, vol. 3 (1887; 1891 printing), pp. 408–410** Philology first. The Greek adjective at v. 32, he says, means “speaking with difficulty … stammering,” not born mute; and *Ephphetha* he derives through the Hebrew root with the note that “for which the Syrians use Ephpheta.” He minds decorum at v. 33 (Christ spat on his own finger). Then he absorbs the tradition — Bede twice, including “Where, says Bede, the two natures of the one and the same Mediator between God and man are plainly set forth”; Theophylact on praising benefactors; Augustine on the silence. His own novelty is scholastic and dissenting: the command of v. 36 “was not properly a command, involving a fault if disobeyed, but merely a token of urbanity and modesty … Wherefore they did not commit sin who nevertheless divulged them.” On v. 37 he presses the adverb: it means “beautifully, becomingly, harmoniously … Christ’s whole life was one continuous beneficence.”

#### Four disagreements, kept as disagreements

| **Question** | **Positions** | **Comment** |
|:--------------|:------------------------|:--------------------------|
| Who is the deaf-mute? | Ps-Jerome: the human race in Adam. Bede: any individual soul that neither hears nor confesses. Theophylact: a man historically afflicted by a demon. Victor: Adamic human nature restored in Christ’s own humanity | The corporate and individual readings are not variants of one idea; they yield different sermons and different sacramental applications |
| What is the sigh? | Bede: *exemplum gemendi*, since Christ needs nothing — an anti-subordinationist guard. Victor: Christ taking our cause on himself. Theophylact: prayer *or* pity, openly hedged. Ps-Jerome: he taught us to groan. A Lapide: sympathy *and* genuine impetration | Augustine never touches the sigh at all |
| Why command silence? | Augustine: to shame lazy preachers. Bede: transcribes Augustine. Victor: prudence, not to inflame envy before the time. Theophylact: an ethics of benefaction. A Lapide: it was not a precept | Only a Lapide denies that disobedience was possible |
| Which spelling? | Missal, Benziger, Clementine, *Catena* and PG 123’s Latin: *Ephphetha*. Bede’s lemma: *Effeta*. The 1872 Rituale and the Douay: *Ephpheta* | One Aramaic word, three orthographies, all left as printed |

#### The word left the Gospel and became a rite

This pericope did something no other text in the formulary did: it generated a liturgical ceremony that was still being performed in 1962. St. Ambrose is the earliest witness, and he says outright where the ceremony came from — in the public-domain English of the NPNF translation: “celebrating the mystery of the opening, we said, Epphatha, which is, Be opened … Christ made use of this mystery in the Gospel, as we read, when He healed him who was deaf and dumb” (*De mysteriis* 1.3–4). *De sacramentis* I.1.2–3 describes the gesture from the other side, as a memory of what was done: the priest touched ears and nostrils, “It is a Hebrew word, which rendered into Latin is adaperire, that is, Be opened” — and explains the move from mouth to nostrils on grounds of propriety toward women.

Fifteen centuries later the *Rituale Romanum* still prints it. In the Pustet printing of 1872, at the baptism of infants (pp. 11–12): *Postea Sacerdos digito accipiat de saliva oris sui, et tangat aures et nares infantis: tangendo vero aurem dexteram, et sinistram, dicat: Ephpheta, quod est, Adaperire: deinde tangit nares, dicens: In odorem suavitatis.* The saliva and the ears are the Gospel’s own gestures, carried into the rite; the same ceremony stands in the order for adults at p. 33. Ambrose’s line runs from this pericope into the sacramental books independently of the whole exegetical chain, and it is the reason a reader is more likely to have met the word *Ephphetha* at a font than in Mark.

#### A large negative result, deliberately printed

**St. Gregory the Great does not expound this Gospel.** The complete OCR of PL 75 and PL 76 — the *Moralia* entire, the *Homiliae in Ezechielem*, and the forty *Homiliae in Evangelia* with their indices — was searched case-insensitively and OCR-tolerantly for the word in its three spellings, for *surdum et mutum* and its case-forms, and every “Marc.” citation in both volumes was reviewed (sixty-six of them in PL 76). There is no exposition of Mark 7:31–37 anywhere, and no Gospel homily on the pericope; the one near hit is Herod and the Baptist. Two independent corroborations: the *Catena aurea* gathers no Gregory for this lection, and Bede, who elsewhere leans on Gregory heavily, here transcribes Augustine instead. *Bounds:* the search was OCR-based, and PL 77 — the *Regula pastoralis*, the *Dialogi*, the *Registrum* — was not searched. Two smaller negatives belong beside it: the authentic Jerome *Tractatus in Marci evangelium* jumps from 5:30–43 to 8:1–9 and has no tractate here; and Chrysostom’s Homily 52 on Matthew, which reaches the mountain summary, has no deaf-mute, no spittle and no reference to Mark.

### Offertory <span class="nodecor">(*Off.*)</span>

*Exaltábo te, Dómine* *Ant. ad Offertorium* — Ps. 29, 2-3 marginal no. 1548

**The title does the work.** Psalm 29 carries the superscription *Psalmus cantici in dedicatione domus David*, and the whole Latin tradition reads the psalm through it. The antiphon takes vv. 2–3: extolling, being upheld, enemies not allowed to gloat, a cry, and a healing. It drops *Deus meus* from the opening vocative of v. 3, which every checked lemma carries. What is left is the shortest possible statement of the day’s Gospel in the first person: *clamávi ad te, et sanásti me*.

> **St. Augustine, *Enarrationes in Psalmos* 29 — two expositions (Enarr. I §§1–3; Enarr. II §§1, 6, 11–12)** The first exposition calls the psalm one “of the joy of the Resurrection … not only of the Lord, but also of the whole Church,” and reads v. 2 as praise of God’s own height: *Exaltabo … suscepisti me: sublimitatem tuam laudabo*. The healing at v. 3 is healing from mortality itself. The second exposition — which exists in Latin only, the NPNF series carrying just the first — solves the title’s puzzle: *Modo enim fabricatur domus, id est Ecclesia, postea dedicabitur. In dedicatione apparebit gloria populi christiani, quae modo latet*. The house is being built now and dedicated later; Christ’s risen body is the pattern. And then, on the antiphon’s own last words, the question that the whole later tradition repeats: *Quem sanavit, qui nunquam aegrotavit …? Non, sed mortem carnis portabat, vulnus tuum portabat, sanaturus te de vulnere tuo. Sanata est autem caro. Quando? Cum resurrexit* — with 1 Cor. 15:54 quoted, which is again the day’s Epistle chapter.

> **Cassiodorus, *Expositio Psalmorum* 29, title and vv. 2–3** Cassiodorus compresses the title into a formula: *Domus significat dominici corporis templum; dedicatio vero resurrectionem*. On v. 2 he makes a rhetorician’s note on the chant’s own verb: *Delectasti vero posuit, pro delectare fecisti. Quae figura dicitur hypallage*. On v. 3 he reruns Augustine’s paradox almost verbatim — *Quemadmodum sanatus est, qui non legitur aegrotasse? Sed occulte ille aegrotabat, quando universum hominem sua incarnatione suscepit … quia in illo sanati sumus, sanatum se ipse profitetur* — a textbook demonstration of how much of Cassiodorus is Augustine reorganised.

> **St. Jerome, *Commentarioli in Psalmos* 29, ad titulum; St. Thomas Aquinas, *Super Psalmo* 29, nn. 1–2** Jerome gives the tradition its tersest form in a single sentence: *Dedicatio domus David, resurrectio Salvatoris intellegitur, in qua omnia vitae corpora dedicantur*. Aquinas weighs the letter first — David never dedicated the Lord’s house, and 2 Samuel 5 may supply his own — then decides: *Tamen melius intelligitur quod referatur ad mysterium domus David, idest Christi … dedicationem, quae fuit in resurrectione, quando corpus Christi indutum est gloria immortalitatis. Super corpus Christi continue construitur; et per conversionem fidelium dedicabitur.* That fuses Cassiodorus’s body and Augustine’s Church into a single two-stage scheme. On v. 3 he distinguishes: God heals the whole man, but *Christum vero sanavit a corporali solum passibilitate*.

> **Theodoret of Cyrus, *Interpretatio in Psalmos* 29, title and vv. 2–3 (PG 80, cols. 1069–1072); Bellarmine, tr. O’Sullivan, pp. 73–74** Theodoret argues from the letter that the title cannot be David’s temple, since David neither built it nor fits the words, and concludes that the dedication of the house is the re-making of human nature accomplished when Christ accepted and destroyed death. On v. 2 he has the Master take our firstfruits and refuse to let nature become the demons’ prize. On v. 3 Antiochene precision returns: the cry and the healing fit Hezekiah in the obvious sense, while human nature received its resurrection unasked, out of sheer love of humankind. Even the soberest reader of the four, then, reaches death-destroyed-and-nature-renewed; only the mechanics differ. Bellarmine states the consensus flatly: “In a prophetic sense, it applies literally to Christ. ‘Thou hast healed me’ of the wounds I suffered on the cross … ‘saved me’ by my resurrection,” and adds that all the saints will say it “most completely, in soul and body” on the last day.

**What the convergence is and is not.** On this psalm the Latin and Greek traditions agree, which they did not at the Gradual or the Alleluia: Jerome, Augustine, Cassiodorus, Aquinas, Theodoret and Bellarmine all read the healing of v. 3 as the Resurrection. That convergence is the reason for a caution the guide repeats in the interpretive section: to hear the Offertory as the healed deaf-mute’s own thanksgiving is to read it against, not with, the checked tradition, which puts the words in the mouth of the risen Christ or of the Church in him. *Negative:* there is no Hilary on Psalm 29.

### Secret <span class="nodecor">(*Sec.*)</span>

*Réspice, Dómine, quǽsumus, nostram propítius servitútem* *Secreta*, composed oration marginal no. 1549

**The oldest text in the formulary, and it began somewhere else.** This prayer stands in the Veronese (“Leonine”) collection, ed. Feltoe, p. 142 — inside the October mass *DE SICCITATE TEMPORIS*, a mass against drought, whose preface begs for rain. It is not a Sunday prayer there and has nothing to do with the harvest imagery this Sunday’s Communion carries; it became a Sunday Secret only when the Gelasian gathered the three orations into one mass (Wilson, p. 228), and it was fixed to a numbered Sunday only later still. Three verified variants, recorded and not blended:

| **Witness** | **Reading** | **Character** |
|:--------------|:------------------------|:--------------------------|
| Veronese, Feltoe p. 142 | *Respice quaesumus Dne nostram propitius servitutem … sit nostrae **fragilitati** subsidium* | dative; the older word order *quaesumus Domine* |
| Old Gelasian, Wilson p. 228 | *fragilitatis* in V, R, S and Gerbert’s text (Wilson’s n. 7); **no** *et* before the second *sit* | the 1962 genitive is already Gelasian; the 1962 *et* is not |
| Gregorian Hadrianum, Wilson p. 172 | *fragilitati* in the base text, **fragilitatis** in Ottob. 313 | the manuscript that carries the 1962 reading is the same one whose chant cues sit two masses away |
| Venice Missal, 1570 | *ad nostram … servitutem* | that edition’s own preposition; not the 1962 text and not adopted here |

**The grammar.** *Servitus* is the object of God’s looking, and the prayer asks two things of one gift: *sit tibi munus accéptum, et sit nostræ fragilitátis subsídium*. The first clause is directed at God, the second at the offerers, and the genitive is doing real work — the support is *of our frailty*, that is, support supplied to a weakness that is admitted in the same breath in which the gift is offered. The dative of the older books (*fragilitati subsidium*, “a support for frailty”) says something very slightly different, and the two readings are printed above rather than harmonised.

> **Blessed Ildefonso Schuster, *The Sacramentary*, vol. III (1927), p. 127** Schuster fixes the register of the first noun. The *servitus* of which the Missal speaks, he says, is precisely what the Greeks called *leitourgia* — in his own words, “the sacerdotal ministry.” On that reading the prayer does not ask God to look at a general attitude of subjection but at the liturgical act being performed as the words are said, and the frailty supported is the frailty of those performing it.

### Communion <span class="nodecor">(*Comm.*)</span>

*Honóra Dóminum de tua substántia* *Ant. ad Communionem* — Prov. 3, 9-10 marginal no. 1550

**The one wisdom text of the day, and the missal trims it.** Proverbs 3 is a father’s instruction: trust the Lord and lean not on your own prudence, honour him with your substance, do not despise his correction. Verses 9–10 are the single promise of material increase in the chapter, and they are conditioned on a prior giving away. The antiphon compresses at three points: v. 9 loses *ómnium* and the closing imperative *da ei*, and v. 10 loses the second *tua*. Losing *da ei* is the interesting one, because it removes the verb of donation and leaves the two verbs of filling: *honour … and thy barns shall be filled*. Schuster notices that the antiphon “departs from the usual rule of the classical antiphons” at all — it is not a psalm — and connects it to “these summer Masses, during the time of harvest,” and to the old blessing of firstfruits at *Per quem hæc omnia*.

> **St. Bede, *In Proverbia Salomonis* I, on Prov. 3:9–10 (PL 91, cols. 950–952)** Bede reads the two verses on three levels and the second is the one that matters for this Mass. *Literally*, the alms must come from one’s own and honestly gotten substance, and be given for God’s honour rather than for applause: *Multi multa pauperibus, sed de aliena substantia … tribuunt*. *Spiritually*, the firstfruits are the firstfruits of *every* good work and of every grace received; a man honours the Lord *de sua substantia* who credits all the good he has to grace and not to his own powers and merits, *memor illius verbi: sine me nihil potestis facere* (John 15:5). *Eschatologically*, the filled barns are heavenly satiety — he quotes *satiabor … dum manifestabitur gloria tua* — and the overflowing presses the *interna suavitas aeternorum*; the giver of earthly help is repaid *caelesti remuneratione, ditior dando*, richer for the giving.

> **Rabanus Maurus, *Expositio in Proverbia Salomonis* I (PL 111, cols. c. 688–690); Salvian of Marseilles, *Ad Ecclesiam* I (PL 53, cols. 214–216)** Rabanus prints the full Vulgate lemma and then reproduces Bede’s exposition verbatim, minus a clause: the Carolingian canonisation of Bede’s reading and the route by which it reached the schools. Salvian, writing a good three centuries before Bede, is entirely literal and much sharper. Since everything we have is God’s gift, God calls his own property “ours” precisely so that our giving can earn something: *Cum totum suum sit quod ab eo accepimus, nostrum esse dicit ut demus … Si devotus es, da quasi tuum; si indevotus, redde quasi non tuum.* Give it as yours if you are devout; hand it back as not yours if you are not.

**Bede’s second level and the day’s Epistle say the same thing twice.** *Grátia autem Dei sum id quod sum* is the first-person form of “credit all your good to grace, not to your own powers.” This guide records the convergence as a textual and reception observation and claims nothing about why the two texts stand on one page; the chant-cue evidence at the Collect shows that the Communion and the orations of this Mass reached each other late.

**Negatives, and one lead left open.** Salonius of Geneva’s mystical exposition of Proverbs was searched entire for *Honora*, *substantia*, *primiti-*, *horrea* and *torcular-* and treats these verses nowhere. St. Cyprian, the obvious candidate on almsgiving, quotes Proverbs 20:9, 21:13, 10:3 and 20:7, Sirach 29:12 and Tobias 4 in *De opere et eleemosynis*, and Isaias 58, Job, Tobias and Micheas in *Ad Quirinum* III.1 — but not Proverbs 3:9. Tobias 4:7, “give alms of thy substance,” is the near-twin that the Latin tradition tends to quote instead. Greek patristic use of Proverbs 3:9 was not verified: the Septuagint’s wording differs enough from the Vulgate’s that a phrase search cannot reach it, and the question is recorded as a lead rather than closed.

### Postcommunion <span class="nodecor">(*Postcomm.*)</span>

*Sentiámus, quǽsumus, Dómine, tui perceptióne sacraménti* *Postcommunio*, composed oration marginal no. 1551

**Transmission.** Like the Secret, this prayer predates its Sunday. It stands in the Veronese collection at Feltoe p. 80, in a July mass, in a form with one word the later books drop: *ut in utroque salvati **de** caelestis remedii plenitudine gloriemur*. The Gelasian (Wilson p. 228) has the older word order *Sentiamus, Domine, quaesumus*; the Hadrianum (p. 172) and 1962 read *quǽsumus, Dómine*. The 1962 form was verified against the facsimile; nothing is blended.

**The grammar, and the two things it refuses to separate.** *Tui … sacraménti* is a split genitive governed by *perceptióne*; the subject of *sentiámus* is the assembly, and the verb is deliberately experiential — may we *feel* the help. What is asked for is *subsídium mentis et córporis*, and *in utróque* resumes exactly those two. The prayer will not let the sacrament be a spiritual benefit with bodily side effects, nor the reverse: it names both, saves both, and then asks for something more than benefit. *Gloriémur* is a verb of boasting. The 1861 English renders it “enjoy the full effect,” which keeps the petition and loses the boast; the divergence was declared at the element above and the argument here is conducted on the Latin.

> **Schuster, *The Sacramentary*, vol. III (1927), pp. 127–128** Schuster draws the consequence the prayer’s second noun invites: the body’s contact with the Body of Jesus confers “the grace of the final resurrection.” That is reception of *córporis* and of *in utróque salváti* together, and it is the point at which this Postcommunion joins the resurrection reading that the Introit’s last clause, the Gradual’s *reflóruit*, the Offertory’s title and the Epistle’s kerygma have each carried in their own way. The convergence is a fact about the checked reception of five separate texts. It is not evidence that anyone assembled them for that reason, and the guide does not make it into evidence.

## Source-Grounded Synthesis Across the Propers

What follows rests only on the appointed wording, the documented historical orientation of the texts, and the reception actually checked at its own loci. It draws no conclusion about why these ten texts stand together, because the transmission evidence forbids one: the Old Gelasian carries the three orations as an unassigned Sunday mass, the Hadrianum files them at its own twelfth Sunday, the Frankish Gelasians at the thirteenth, and the Ottobonianus margins cue this Sunday’s antiphons two masses away. Chants and orations reached each other by a realignment nobody documented. Everything below is therefore a statement about the formulary as the 1962 book prints it, never about a compiler’s mind.

**The formulary’s chief coherence is a shared boundary, and it is verifiable.** Four of its texts stop where a human contribution would begin, and in three of the four the stopping is a demonstrable editorial act, not the shape of the source. The Epistle ends mid-verse at *vácua non fuit*, before *sed abundántius illis ómnibus laborávi*. The Communion antiphon drops the imperative *da ei*, so that the two verbs left standing are *honour* and *shall be filled*. The Collect’s second petition asks God to add what *orátio* does not presume — a petition whose grammatical subject is the prayer, not the person praying. And the Gospel’s protagonist contributes nothing whatever until his tongue has been loosed: he is brought, taken aside, touched, and opened, and the first thing recorded of his speech is that it was right. The four boundaries are independently verified in `propers/verified.md`; no witness reads them together, and this synthesis claims only that they are all four there.

**The reception of five separate elements converges on the Resurrection, and the convergence is a fact about the commentators rather than about the Mass.** Hilary, Augustine and Bellarmine read the Introit’s *virtútem et fortitúdinem* of the risen body. Augustine glosses the Gradual’s *reflóruit caro mea* as *resurrexit caro mea*, and Cassiodorus, Lombard, Aquinas and Bellarmine develop it. Jerome, Augustine, Cassiodorus, Aquinas, Theodoret and Bellarmine all read the Offertory psalm’s dedication of David’s house as the Resurrection. The Epistle is the Church’s oldest resurrection kerygma. Schuster reads the Postcommunion’s help to mind and body as the pledge of the final resurrection. Five elements, six centuries of witnesses, one direction — and not one of those witnesses is commenting on this Sunday. The honest statement is that a reader who arrives with the tradition in hand will hear a resurrection Mass here, and that the texts themselves supply that reading only through their reception.

**The tradition genuinely disagrees at four points, and the formulary resolves none of them.** At Introit v. 36 the gift of strength is the resurrection of the body (Hilary, Augustine, Bellarmine), or patience and faith (Cassiodorus), or providential support (Theodoret). At Gradual v. 7 *reflóruit* is the Resurrection for the whole Latin line and recovered bodily vigour for Theodoret. At the Alleluia the instruments are allegory for Augustine and Cassiodorus and instruments for Theodoret and Bellarmine. A fourth disagreement runs through the Gospel: the deaf-mute is the race in Adam (ps-Jerome), or any uncatechised soul (Bede), or a particular man with a demonic affliction (Theophylact), or human nature restored in Christ’s own humanity (the Victor tradition) — and the sigh is an example, a taking-on of our cause, a prayer, or a pity, depending on which of them is speaking. These are not variants of one reading. They yield different sermons, and the guide prints them as four positions rather than one consensus.

**Two of the day’s texts are the patristic tradition’s own language turned into liturgy, which is a different relation from quotation.** The Introit sings *unánimes*, which is Augustine’s gloss on *unius modi* and Cassiodorus’s lemma; the chant therefore carries into the Mass a word the Vulgate does not have and the Douay cannot render. The baptismal *Ephpheta* runs the other way: a word from this Gospel became a rite, attested by Ambrose at Milan around 390 and still printed in the *Rituale Romanum* of 1872 with the same saliva and the same ears. In both cases the traffic between exegesis, chant and ritual is documented; in neither does it license an inference about who put this Mass together.

**The Epistle’s boundary constrains what may be preached from this Sunday, and the constraint is worth stating positively.** What the Church actually reads is a creed received and handed on, a list of witnesses, a man who calls himself untimely born, and the sentence *grátia Dei sum id quod sum*. What she does not read is the labour and the *mecum*. The lection is thus, as read, a statement about the origin of a person and a ministry rather than about the mechanics of cooperation — and Bede, expounding the day’s Communion antiphon three centuries before the scholastics, says the same thing about giving: one honours the Lord from one’s substance by crediting all the good one has to grace and not to one’s own powers and merits. The Epistle and the Communion make one claim in two idioms, personal and economic. That is a source-grounded convergence of two checked expositions, not a claim that either text was chosen for the other.

**The orations do the work that the readings leave undone, and they do it in the plural.** The Collect asks for pardon and for a gift beyond request; the Secret asks that one offering be at once acceptable to God and support to the frailty of the offerers; the Postcommunion asks that the sacrament be felt as help of mind and body, and that, saved in both, we may glory in the fullness of the remedy. Each is a double request, and in each the second half concerns the petitioners’ weakness. The three prayers travelled together from the Old Gelasian onward, which is a documented fact about them and the only unity the formulary can be shown to have been given by anybody. Their shared shape — one act of God, two effects, the second of them ours — is the nearest thing this Mass has to a thesis, and it is legible without any theory of how the chants came to join them.

## The Propers: Notable and Quotable

Four verified afterlives of wording from the scriptural propers, each a use that moves the phrase into a register its author did not occupy. Straight exegesis, devotional reuse, and bare quotation are excluded; they belong in the commentary above. Two of the four come from one Aramaic word, and that concentration is itself the finding: of everything on these three missal pages, *Ephphetha* is what the world took and put to other work. The audit behind the gallery — including a fifth verified candidate held back, and the searches that came up empty — is in `research/scope.md`.

> **Epistle, 1 Cor. 15:8 — William Morris makes an apostle’s apology into an aesthete’s manifesto, 1868**
>
> **Appointed phrase.** *Novíssime autem ómnium tamquam abortívo, visus est et mihi*; Douay–Rheims, “And last of all, he was seen also by me, as by one born out of due time.”
>
> **Later use and locus.** William Morris, *The Earthly Paradise* (London: F. S. Ellis, 1868), the prefatory “Apology,” stanza 4, on p. 2 of the first edition: “Dreamer of dreams, born out of my due time,  Why should I strive to set the crooked straight?” Read in the archive.org scan of the first edition.
>
> **The turn.** Paul’s phrase is an apology for the violence and lateness of his own apostolic vision, made in the act of claiming apostolic standing. Morris keeps the idiom, inserts one word — *my* — and inverts the claim. Untimeliness now means being a medieval dreamer stranded in the industrial nineteenth century, and where Paul’s untimely birth is the ground of a commission, Morris’s is the ground of a disclaimer: he explicitly denies any power to set the crooked straight. The Apostle’s least of the apostles becomes the idle singer of an empty day.
>
> **Rights and limit.** The first edition (1868) and Morris (d. 1896) are long out of copyright. The dependence is on the naturalised English idiom “born out of due time,” shared by the Douay and the King James, not on this Mass lection; Morris cites nothing. The “Apology” is absent from the Project Gutenberg Part II e-text, which is why the first-edition scan was used.

> **Gospel, Mk. 7:34 — “Effeta, The Body Deodorant,” New Jersey, 1912**
>
> **Appointed phrase.** *Ephphetha, quod est adaperíre*, and the baptismal formula that quotes it: *Ephpheta, quod est, Adaperire … In odorem suavitatis* (*Rituale Romanum*, Pustet 1872, pp. 11–12).
>
> **Later use and locus.** A newspaper advertisement for the Willis Pike Co. Laboratories, 129 W. 31st St., New York, in the *Perth Amboy Evening News* (N.J.), 19 June 1912, ed. 2, p. 2: “For That Embarrassing Odor of Perspiration Get a Box of Effeta, The Body Deodorant. ‘Be thou opened unto odor of sweetness.’” The campaign runs in the same paper from 29 May to 22 June 1912 and reappears in the *Norwich Bulletin* (Conn.), 22 June 1912, p. 6. Read in the Library of Congress *Chronicling America* page text.
>
> **The turn.** The product name is the Latin form of the Gospel word — and, at that, the form Bede’s lemma uses — and the slogan splices Christ’s command to the deaf man onto the rite’s own *in odorem suavitatis*, the words said over the nostrils. What is opened in Mark is a pair of ears and a tongue; what is opened in the advertisement, for twenty-five cents a box, is the pores. The copywriter is not being blasphemous so much as commercially literal: he has found a sacramental formula about opening and odour and sold it as a toiletry.
>
> **Rights and limit.** United States newspapers of 1912 are public domain. The copywriter’s proximate source is the baptismal rite rather than the Mass lection — but the rite is quoting this Sunday’s Gospel, and St. Ambrose says so. The 19 June issue is the one in which the scriptural slogan line is legible in the page text, and the wording was read there in context.

> **Introit psalm verse, Ps. 67:2 — Cromwell at Dunbar, 3 September 1650**
>
> **Appointed phrase.** *Exsúrgat Deus, et dissipéntur inimíci eius*; Douay–Rheims, “Let God arise, and let his enemies be scattered.” It is the verse the missal appoints as the Introit’s psalm verse.
>
> **Later use and locus.** At the moment the Scottish line broke, with the sun coming up over the sea, Cromwell was heard to exclaim “Now let God arise, and his enemies shall be scattered.” The eyewitness is Captain John Hodgson, *Original Memoirs … Life of Sir Henry Slingsby, and Memoirs of Capt. Hodgson* (Edinburgh, 1806), pp. 147–48, whose editor footnotes the reference as “Psalm lxviii. v. 1” and who adds, a few lines later, “I heard him say, ‘I profess they run!’” Relayed with the psalter version by Thomas Carlyle, *Oliver Cromwell’s Letters and Speeches* (Tauchnitz, 1861), vol. 2, pp. 332–33. Three years later, opening Barebone’s Parliament on 4 July 1653, Cromwell read the whole psalm aloud; Carlyle notes “We remember it ever since Dunbar morning” (vol. 3, pp. 171–72).
>
> **The turn.** In the Mass the verse is sung by a congregation as the priest comes to the altar. At Dunbar it is not a prayer at all but a battlefield exclamation — the shout of a commander who has just watched a cavalry charge succeed, using the psalm to certify the outcome rather than to ask for it. Three years later the same psalm is deployed politically, as scriptural warrant for a revolutionary assembly. The verse travels from petition to victory-cry to constitutional argument.
>
> **Rights and limit.** Both sources are public domain. Cromwell’s wording is the English psalter and King James tradition, not the Douay. Hodgson’s memoir is remembered speech first printed in 1806, not a contemporary dispatch; Cromwell’s own letter about Dunbar does not contain the phrase. The 1806 page text garbles the word “scattered,” and the reading here is controlled by the editor’s footnote and by Carlyle’s transcription.

> **Gospel, Mk. 7:34 — a command to the deaf becomes the name deaf institutions gave themselves**
>
> **Appointed phrase.** *Ephphetha*, “Be thou opened.”
>
> **Later use and locus.** (a) The Ephphatha Guild, a body of “between fifty and sixty” deaf-mutes attached to St. Luke’s, Scranton, with an affiliated secular “Penny Club” and its own fundraising fairs — *The Scranton Tribune*, 14 November 1895, p. 3, under the heading “EPHPHATHA GUILD SERVICES,” which supplies the derivation itself: the guild “takes its name from the Greek word ‘Ephphatha’ ‘Be opened’ used by the Savior in healing a deaf mute.” (b) The Ephphatha Missions for the Deaf and Blind and the Ephphatha Services Board of the American Lutheran Church, with an annual professional Ephphatha Conference — Council of Organizations Serving the Deaf, *Council Membership Directory 1969* (ERIC ED032704).
>
> **The turn.** A word spoken once, to one man, becomes a durable institutional surname across denominations and three-quarters of a century: dues, guild fairs, boards, a conference in the calendar. The register shifts from miracle to administration. And there is a sharper edge: in the decades of the oralism-versus-sign controversy, deaf people organised themselves under a word that commands the deaf to hear and the mute to speak, and used it as a name for their own society rather than as a prescription addressed to them.
>
> **Rights and limit.** The 1895 newspaper is public domain; the 1969 directory is a freely distributed ERIC document, cited without extended reproduction. Both bodies are church-affiliated, so they qualify as proper names of functioning institutions rather than as sermon reuse. The Scranton paper miscalls the Aramaic word “Greek,” which is reproduced above as printed.

## The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities

> **What this section is**
>
> Everything below is exploratory editorial and AI proposal. None of it is sourced historical intent, attributed teaching, or documented reception, and none of it should be quoted as any of those. Each proposal was tested against the corpus checked for this guide and against a targeted search for its own distinctive conjunction; the anchors, mechanism, search result and controlling limit of each are recorded in `research/scope.md`. “Not located in the checked corpus” is bounded to that corpus and never means that a connection is unprecedented. Evidence, doctrine, and the literal sense of the appointed texts govern; where a proposal and a checked witness conflict, the witness wins.

> **Editorial proposal — The day has two openings, and the second is a harvest**
>
> **Classification:** Not located in the checked corpus.
>
> **Anchors:** Gospel <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>, *Ephphetha, quod est adaperíre*; Communion <span class="nodecor">(*Comm.*)</span>, *implebúntur hórrea tua saturitáte, et vino torculária redundábunt*; Collect <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span>, *effúnde super nos misericórdiam tuam*.
>
> **Mechanism:** Three of the day’s texts describe a container being opened or filled past its own capacity, and they run in one direction. The Collect asks God to *pour out* — a verb of overflow — and then to add what prayer does not presume; the Gospel’s one spoken word is an imperative to open; the Communion ends on presses that run over. Read in sequence, what God opens does not merely function again but produces more than it can hold. The formulary would then have a shape rather than a subject: unstopping, then overflow.
>
> **Fruit:** Healing stops being restoration to a norm. The measure of an opened ear, on this reading, is not that it hears but that what comes out of the man afterwards exceeds him — which is exactly what the crowd’s disobedient publishing does at the end of the pericope.
>
> **What the ordinary reading misses:** Element by element the Communion is an antiphon about almsgiving and the Gospel is a healing narrative, and the Collect’s *effúnde* is read as ordinary petitionary idiom. The three verbs are never set side by side.
>
> **Strongest limit:** The chant-cue evidence at the Collect is decisive against any design claim: the Ottobonianus margins cue this Communion two masses away from these orations, so the pairing is a late accident of two independently drifting cycles. St. Bede’s firstfruits-of-grace reading and Schuster’s harvest reading each control the Communion severally, and neither joins it to the Gospel. And the antiphon’s overflow is conditional on a giving away that the missal’s trimming of *da ei* has removed from view.

> **Editorial proposal — The loosed tongue is given a psalm to say, four elements later**
>
> **Classification:** Not located in the checked corpus.
>
> **Anchors:** Gospel <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>, *solútum est vínculum linguæ eius, et loquebátur recte*; Offertory <span class="nodecor">(*Off.*)</span>, *clamávi ad te, et sanásti me*; Epistle <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>, *grátia eius in me vácua non fuit*.
>
> **Mechanism:** Mark records that the healed man spoke rightly and never records a word of it. The Offertory, sung a few minutes later, is the only proper of the day in the voice of somebody already healed, and it says in the first person precisely what the Gospel has just narrated in the third: I cried to thee and thou hast healed me. The Epistle supplies the grammar for such speech — a man whose account of himself begins from grace and not from himself. The proposal is that the liturgy supplies the missing sentence, and that the supplied sentence is a confession rather than a report.
>
> **Fruit:** Right speech is defined by content, not by fluency. The formulary’s answer to “what does a loosed tongue say?” would be: it says where it came from.
>
> **What the ordinary reading misses:** Taken alone the Offertory is a generic thanksgiving assigned to a Sunday, and *loquebátur recte* is a clinical detail about articulation.
>
> **Strongest limit:** This is the proposal that most directly contradicts the checked tradition, and the contradiction is not marginal. Jerome, Augustine, Cassiodorus, Aquinas, Theodoret and Bellarmine all read Ps. 29’s healing of the Resurrection, on the strength of the psalm’s title; Augustine asks in so many words who healed a man who was never sick. Assigning the verse to the deaf-mute reads it against every witness checked. The nearest material found is Cassiodorus’s remark that idols do not heal but wound — and that falls in his exposition of this day’s Alleluia psalm, not of the Offertory.

> **Editorial proposal — Silence asked of God, and silence ended by God**
>
> **Classification:** Near analogue located.
>
> **Anchors:** Gradual <span class="nodecor">(*Grad.*)</span>, *Deus meus, ne síleas, ne discédas a me*; Gospel <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>, the groan and then *Ephphetha*; Alleluia <span class="nodecor">(*All.*)</span>, *Exsultáte … iubiláte … súmite psalmum*.
>
> **Mechanism:** Three consecutive sung and read elements make an anthropology of sound. The Gradual’s versicle asks God not to be silent; the Alleluia commands a shout; between them the Gospel puts a groan before a word, and ends with a crowd that cannot be silenced. The day would then move through four registers of voice in order — petition that God speak, jubilation, inarticulate groan, and finally right speech — and locate the human voice as something that only exists downstream of a divine one.
>
> **Fruit:** Liturgical song acquires an anthropology: the assembly’s jubilus is not self-expression but the audible form of having been opened, and the groan is given a place inside worship rather than outside it.
>
> **What the ordinary reading misses:** Element by element these are a Gradual, an Alleluia and a miracle story; the sequence of vocal states across them is invisible unless the three are read as one movement.
>
> **Strongest limit:** Two hard checks. The Gradual’s *ne discédas a me* has no patristic lemma at all — every checked expositor has *ne síleas* and *nequándo táceas* — so the closing plea cannot be given a documented reception. And Augustine assigns the whole of Psalm 27 to Christ in the Passion, not to a man asking to be spoken to. The near analogue is real but partial: the seventh-century commentary under Jerome’s name says the opened ears open *ad hymnos, cantica, et psalmos*, joining the healing to psalmody, and Bede supplies the groan as *exemplum gemendi*; neither joins either to this Gradual.

> **Editorial proposal — The shape of the lesson preaches what the lesson does not say**
>
> **Classification:** Not located in the checked corpus.
>
> **Anchors:** Epistle <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>, cut at *et grátia eius in me vácua non fuit*; Collect <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span>, *adícias quod orátio non præsúmit*; Communion <span class="nodecor">(*Comm.*)</span>, the antiphon’s dropped imperative *da ei*.
>
> **Mechanism:** Three independent editorial boundaries in one formulary all fall at the same place — immediately before the point at which a human contribution would be named. The lection stops before Paul’s labour; the Collect makes the prayer, not the petitioner, the thing that does not presume; the antiphon removes the verb of donation and leaves the filling. None of the three is a feature of its source text: each is something the liturgy did to it. The proposal is that the formulary preaches prevenient grace by its cuts rather than by its assertions.
>
> **Fruit:** An account of how a lectionary teaches. What is not read is part of what is heard, and a reader who has noticed the pattern will read the day’s silences as deliberate rather than arbitrary.
>
> **What the ordinary reading misses:** Each boundary looks like a separate accident of chant construction or lectionary convenience, and the Epistle’s mid-verse ending in particular is usually noted, if at all, as a curiosity.
>
> **Strongest limit:** The boundaries are received, not designed here: the same Epistle ending and the same trimmed antiphon stand in the Missals of 1570 and 1862, and no source states a motive for either. More seriously, the tradition whose key clause is cut — Augustine, Gregory, Aquinas — teaches human cooperation with grace on the strength of exactly those unread words. The pattern cannot be pressed into a denial of what they teach, and this guide does not press it.

> **Editorial proposal — A green Sunday that sings a small Easter, with nobody’s intention behind it**
>
> **Classification:** Near analogue located.
>
> **Anchors:** Introit <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>, *ipse dabit virtútem et fortitúdinem plebi suæ*; Gradual <span class="nodecor">(*Grad.*)</span>, *reflóruit caro mea*; Offertory <span class="nodecor">(*Off.*)</span>, the title *in dedicatione domus David* and *sanásti me*; Epistle <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>, the kerygma of 1 Cor. 15.
>
> **Mechanism:** Four elements of this Mass have, independently, a resurrection reading in the checked tradition — and the four readings were made by different men in different centuries, none of them thinking about this Sunday. Set the received expositions side by side and an arc appears across the formulary: the risen body promised (Introit), the flesh flowering again (Gradual), the house dedicated in the Resurrection (Offertory), and the event itself confessed in the lesson. The proposal is that a green Sunday in late summer carries a compact paschal formulary, and that it does so as a *reception effect* rather than as a composition.
>
> **Fruit:** A way of describing liturgical coherence that does not require a compiler. Texts assembled for other reasons can converge because the tradition that reads them converges, and this Mass is a clean specimen of the phenomenon.
>
> **What the ordinary reading misses:** Element by element these are a processional psalm, a thanksgiving, an offertory chant and an epistle. Only the reception makes them one thing, and the reception is not on the missal page.
>
> **Strongest limit:** The arc is not a property of the texts, and two checked witnesses break it: Cassiodorus moralises the Introit’s last clause into patience and faith with no resurrection at all, and Theodoret reads the Gradual’s *reflóruit* of a hunted man’s recovered bodily vigour. Against design, the chant-cue drift recorded at the Collect shows these chants and these orations were not paired when either was composed. And the convergence is partly an artefact of which witnesses survive: it is strongest in the Latin line that runs from Augustine through Cassiodorus to Aquinas.

## Appendix: Scope and Qualifications

**As of 20 August 2026.** Everything below states the boundaries of this guide as at that date. Retrieval mechanics, checksums, query detail, discarded leads, and the full reception, notable-and-quotable and interpretive-proposal audits are in `propers/verified.md` and `research/scope.md` beside this document’s source; they are not repeated here.

**Two editions, one body of research.** This guide is published in two mechanically compiled forms from a single source leaf. The *Full PDF*, which is the canonical edition, prints the complete appointed formulary — the Latin of the 1962 Vatican *editio typica* with the English of the registered public-domain witnesses — and then the full element-by-element commentary. The *Synthesis PDF*, published at the same identity with a `-synthesis` suffix, omits both of those and puts in their place an integrated commentary organised by cross-proper claims. Everything else — the map, the dossier sheet, the two-page thematic movement, the source-grounded synthesis, the notable gallery, the interpretive proposals and this apparatus — is identical in both. Neither is a separate study, and the synthesis companion owns no research, prose or records of its own. Where a paragraph below refers to a table or a collation, the full edition prints it and `propers/verified.md` records it in either case.

**Edition and formulary identity.** The work is the temporal formulary printed *DOMINICA UNDECIMA post Pentecosten*, *II classis*, on pp. 390–392 of the 1962 *editio typica* of the *Missale Romanum* (Vatican City: Typis Polyglottis Vaticanis), with marginal numbers 1542–1551. Green follows the general rubrics for Sundays after Pentecost and is not printed on those pages. The formulary appoints the *Credo* and the Preface of the Most Holy Trinity, and appoints no Tract, Sequence, second oration, blessing or ritual text. Its boundaries are the Postcommunion of the Tenth Sunday (no. 1541) and the heading *DOMINICA DUODECIMA post Pentecosten* (no. 1552).

**Text verification.** All ten elements, their headings, references, wording, accents, punctuation, rank, rubrics and both formulary boundaries were visually collated on 19 August 2026 against the CMAA facsimile of the Vatican typical edition at 300 and 600 dpi — and at 1200 dpi for the Gospel’s unaccented opening line — and independently against page images of the 1962 Benziger *editio iuxta typicam*. The two editions differ at the nine points tabulated in the commentary; those are edition differences, not doubtful readings, and nothing was blended or substituted. The appointed text was separately collated against the Clementine Vulgate, and the eleven places where it diverges are tabulated in the commentary as verified textual observations. One further question is deliberately not published as a divergence: at Mk. 7:34 the missal prints *suspíciens in cælum* where two consulted Clementine transcriptions print *suscípiens*; those transcriptions may not be independent and no printed Clementine facsimile was collated for the verse, so the point is recorded in `research/scope.md` as an unresolved lead. The Internet Archive OCR was used only to locate the formulary and controls no published wording.

**What this guide is not.** It is a hand-missal companion, not a liturgical book, a critical edition, a homily, an assembly sheet for one civil date, or a substitute for the sources cited. It does not treat occurrence, commemorations, particular calendars, or celebration-mode branches for any year; those belong to the edition-specific assembly workflow and require their own evidence.

**Translation and received text.** The project has composed, translated, paraphrased, modernised and adapted nothing. English for the scriptural propers — Introit, Gradual, Alleluia, Epistle, Gospel, Offertory, Communion — is the Douay–Rheims as revised by Bishop Challoner (1749–1752), the English of the Clementine Vulgate the missal prints, quoted from the Project Gutenberg e-text of that revision. English for the three orations is the anonymous rendering in *The Roman Missal translated into the English language for the use of the laity*, first revised edition (Philadelphia: Eugene Cummiskey, 1861), taken from the three lines that book supplies for this formulary and reproduced with its own spelling and its abbreviated conclusion. Both are in the public domain in the United States; neither is an official liturgical translation, and neither was composed for the 1962 books. Where a witness consulted for reception exists only in a translation still under copyright — Theodoret in Hill, the Aquinas *lectura* in Larcher, Theophylact in Stade, Cassiodorus in Walsh — that witness is summarised, or quoted in Latin or from Migne’s Latin column, and never rendered by this project.

**Seven places where the registered English does not answer the Latin, and is not made to.** The Introit sings *unánimes* where the Clementine, and therefore the Douay, reads *unius moris*; the Introit and the Alleluia are centonisations, so their Douay verses are printed whole with the sung fragments marked rather than stitched into project-made joins; the Gradual sings *clamávi* against the Douay’s future and closes with *ne discédas a me*, which has no Clementine counterpart at all; the Offertory drops the *Deus meus* the Douay carries; the Communion drops *ómnium* and *da ei*; the missal prints *Ephphetha* where the Douay prints “Ephpheta”; and the liturgical incipits *Fratres:* and *In illo témpore: Exiens Iesus* are the missal’s own and are not translated. In each case the gap is stated where it falls. The Epistle’s English stops where the lesson stops; the remainder of its verse is described, not printed as appointed text. Two divergences between this e-text and the 1899 American printing of the Douay fall inside verses quoted here — “lest if thou be silent” at Ps. 27:1 and “Then was he seen” at 1 Cor. 15:6 — and are disclosed in the references; every other quoted locus of this Mass agrees word for word with the American edition.

**Search bounds.** Reception was searched in Latin, in Greek both directly and through Migne’s Latin columns, and in English, across the major reasonably accessible patristic, medieval and later corpora relevant to each appointed passage: Hilary’s *Tractatus super Psalmos* in CSEL 22; Augustine’s *Enarrationes*, *De gratia et libero arbitrio*, *De consensu evangelistarum*, and sermons; Jerome’s *Commentarioli* and *Tractatus* in Morin’s editions and the *Commentarius in Marcum* transmitted under his name; Bede on Mark and on Proverbs; Rabanus Maurus on Proverbs; Salvian and Salonius; Gregory the Great in PL 75–76 and the *Moralia*; Cassiodorus’s *Expositio Psalmorum*; Theodoret on the Psalms and on First Corinthians; Chrysostom on First Corinthians and on Matthew; Theophylact on Mark; Ambrosiaster; Irenaeus and Tertullian; Aquinas’s *Super Psalmos*, *Super I ad Corinthios*, *Catena aurea in Marcum* and the *Summa theologiae*; Peter Lombard on the Psalms; Bellarmine; Cornelius a Lapide; Ambrose’s mystagogical works; the 1872 *Rituale Romanum*; and Guéranger and Schuster for modern liturgical commentary. Consequential negative results are reported in the commentary rather than concealed — among them that Gregory the Great nowhere expounds this Sunday’s Gospel, that Aquinas’s psalms commentary ends at Psalm 54 and so touches neither the Introit’s nor the Alleluia’s psalm, that Hilary’s extant tractates omit Psalms 27, 29 and 80, that Jerome’s homily on Psalm 67 stops before the verse the Introit sings, that the *Summa*’s treatise on grace never cites 1 Cor. 15:10, that the Collect is absent from the Veronese sacramentary, and that Salonius and Cyprian do not treat the Communion’s passage. No exhaustive search of Syriac originals, chant manuscripts, untranslated homiliaries, subscription databases or current specialist monographs is claimed, and none of these negatives is a claim about literature outside the corpora named.

**Bounds carried by particular claims.** Modern critical apparatus for the orations — Corpus Orationum, Bruylants, Deshusses’s numbering — was not publicly reachable and was not consulted; every transmission claim rests on the named public-domain editions themselves. The postconciliar reuse of the Collect rests on two secondary Latin transcriptions, the official 2002 locus remaining a lead. Guéranger was verified in the 1909 Stanbrook second edition, which is the edition cited, although the repository’s registered edition of that volume is the Duffy printing of 1900. Peter Lombard was checked on Psalms 27 and 67 only. Greek patristic use of Proverbs 3:9 was not verified, the Septuagint’s wording defeating a Vulgate-phrase search, and is recorded as a lead rather than closed. For the Benziger witness, individual page images were read from the Internet Archive item; acquisition of the exact registered derivative bytes is deliberately not claimed. The gloss-attributed line about the womb of an abortive birth, which circulates under Gregory’s name, was traced only to Hugh of St-Cher’s postill, was not verified in Gregory, and is not published.

**Psalm numbering.** Three series are in use and two of them are routinely both called “modern,” so the dossier sheet prints the two that differ and this appendix supplies the third. *Vulg.* is the Vulgate/Septuagint number, which the missal cites and which the Douay–Rheims carries throughout. *Eng.* is the convention of the King James and Revised Standard versions, which leave the psalm inscription unnumbered and so run a verse behind the Vulgate wherever the psalm carries a title; the dossiers print it beside the Vulgate number. Three of this Mass’s four psalms show that offset; Psalm 27, whose title the Douay counts inside v. 1, shows none. *Heb.* is the Masoretic number of the Nova Vulgata and the New American Bible, which renumbers the psalm but keeps the Vulgate’s verse division: Ps. 67:2, 6–7, 36 Vulg. is Heb. 68:2, 6–7, 36; Ps. 27:1, 7 is Heb. 28:1, 7; Ps. 80:2–3 is Heb. 81:2–3; Ps. 29:2–3 is Heb. 30:2–3. The conversions were taken from the concordance registered with the Douay–Rheims edition used here, not computed by this guide; the concordance marks each of the four psalms as carrying its offset uniformly, so each conversion holds from the head of its psalm.

**Historical bounds.** Dates and places in the page-2 dossiers distinguish traditional attribution from current historical judgment and are orienting rather than probative. On the psalm titles the two witnesses named there differ in emphasis: the 1910 *Catholic Encyclopedia* reports the Biblical Commission’s decree of that year that the titles are older than the Septuagint and that it cannot prudently be denied that David is the Psalter’s chief author, while adding that neither councils nor Fathers make him its sole author; the *NABRE* treats the superscriptions as pre-Christian Jewish tradition whose technical terms are now lost and holds that no psalm can be securely dated. This guide does not settle the authorship or date of any psalm, the date or place of Mark’s composition, the compilation history of Proverbs, or the year of the events Mark narrates. Where the two named witnesses disagree — as they do about Proverbs 1–9 and about Mark’s date — both positions are printed and neither is preferred.

**Rights.** No public-domain conclusion is claimed for the 1962 typical edition. This repository’s rights record for the facsimile finds that edition’s own status unresolved, and more likely than not restored in the United States under 17 U.S.C. §104A to the end of 2057 (that record is `missale-romanum-1962-facsimile-rights-v1.toml`, under `src/sources/inventories/`). What permits the Latin printed here is 17 U.S.C. §103(b): the copyright in a revised edition extends only to what that edition itself contributed and “does not affect or enlarge the scope, duration, ownership, or subsistence of” any copyright in the preexisting material. This formulary is preexisting material. All ten of its appointed elements — in the same order and at the same scriptural references — are printed in the Pustet printing of Ratisbon, 1862, and all ten again in the Venice Missal of 1570, both of which this repository tracks as public-domain text, so the 1962 edition’s copyright does not reach them; the element-level check and its bounds are recorded in `propers/verified.md`.
