# Our Lord Jesus Christ the King: A Study of the Proper in the 1962 Roman Missal

*Expansive study of the 1962 Missale Romanum proper of the feast of Our Lord Jesus Christ the King, last Sunday of October, as said on 25 October 2026: appointed texts, each element in its setting, three readings of the whole formulary with their four senses, a comparison, and the scriptural date and location of its passages*

**Our Lord Jesus Christ the King**

D. N. Iesu Christi Regis

Missale Romanum 1962 · Sanctorale, last Sunday of October · Sunday 25 October 2026

The Lamb who receives the kingdom, the kingdom not from hence, and the kingdom handed over:<br>
a study of the proper in three readings

[TOC]

## The King Bound Before Pilate, and the Lamb Upon the Throne {#sec:opening}

The Mass of Our Lord Jesus Christ the King begins in heaven and comes down, at its Gospel, to a prisoner. Its Introit takes up the song of the angels around the throne in the fifth chapter of the Apocalypse: “The Lamb that was slain is worthy to receive power and divinity and wisdom and strength and honour,” and adds from the first chapter, “To him be glory and empire for ever and ever.” Its verse is the opening prayer of Psalm 71 (72), “Give to the king thy judgment, O God, and to the king’s son thy justice.” The Collect confesses that God willed to restore all things in his beloved Son, *universorum Rege*, the King of all, and asks that the families of the nations, scattered by the wound of sin, *peccati vulnere disgregatae*, be brought under his most gentle rule, *eius suavissimo subdantur imperio*. The Epistle is St Paul’s thanksgiving to the Father “who hath delivered us from the power of darkness and hath translated us into the kingdom of the Son of his love” (Col 1:13), and his hymn to that Son: the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of every creature, in whom all things were created, the head of the body, the Church, the firstborn from the dead, through whom the Father reconciles all things, “making peace through the blood of his cross.” The Gradual returns to Psalm 71: “he shall rule from sea to sea, and from the river unto the ends of the earth … all kings of the earth shall adore him: all nations shall serve him.” The Alleluia sings the second half of Daniel’s vision of one like a son of man: “his power is an everlasting power that shall not be taken away: and his kingdom that shall not be destroyed.”

The Gospel is the first questioning of Jesus by Pilate inside the governor’s hall (Jn 18:33–37). “Art thou the king of the Jews?” Jesus answers with a question, “Sayest thou this thing of thyself, or have others told it thee of me?” Pilate: “Am I a Jew? Thy own nation and the chief priests have delivered thee up to me. What hast thou done?” Then the words on which the feast turns: “My kingdom is not of this world. If my kingdom were of this world, my servants would certainly strive that I should not be delivered to the Jews: but now my kingdom is not from hence.” “Art thou a king then?” “Thou sayest that I am a king. For this was I born, and for this came I into the world; that I should give testimony to the truth. Every one that is of the truth heareth my voice.” The pericope stops there, before Pilate’s “What is truth?” The Offertory gives the Father’s word to his Son in the second psalm, “Ask of me, and I will give thee the Gentiles for thy inheritance, and the utmost parts of the earth for thy possession.” The Secret offers *hostiam … humanae reconciliationis*, the victim of mankind’s reconciliation, and asks that the Son, immolated in the present sacrifice, grant all nations the gifts of unity and peace. The proper Preface gives thanks that the Father anointed his only-begotten Son with the oil of gladness, eternal Priest and King of all things, so that, offering himself on the altar of the cross, he might accomplish the mysteries of man’s redemption, and, all creatures being subjected to his rule, deliver to the Father’s majesty “an eternal and universal kingdom, a kingdom of truth and life; a kingdom of holiness and grace, a kingdom of justice, love and peace” (in the English of the Lasance missal). The Communion is two half-verses of Psalm 28 (29): “the Lord shall sit king for ever … the Lord will bless his people with peace.” The Postcommunion asks that those who have received the food of immortality, and who glory to fight under the standards of Christ the King, may reign with him for ever in the heavenly seat.

The Missal prints this Mass in the Sanctorale, among the feasts of October, under the heading *Dominica ultima octobris*, *D. N. Iesu Christi Regis*, of the first class (printed pp. 697–698). Pius XI instituted the feast by the encyclical *Quas primas* of 11 December 1925 and appointed it to the last Sunday of October, the Sunday that immediately precedes All Saints (n. 28); the Sacred Congregation of Rites approved its Mass and Office the next day, and the Mass was first printed with that decree in the *Acta Apostolicae Sedis*. On Sunday 25 October 2026, which is both the last Sunday of October and the Sunday before All Saints, the feast takes the place of the Twenty-second Sunday after Pentecost with all its rights, and no commemoration of the Sunday is made (RG 17 d; RG 112 b), as the formulary itself directs after each of its three orations. The commemoration of Saints Chrysanthus and Daria, inscribed on this date, is omitted (RG 111 a). The colour is white; the Gloria and the Credo are said, and the Preface is the feast’s own (RGMR 492).

Three questions rise from these texts, and each governs one of the readings that follow. The first is what the Lamb receives. The Introit says that the slain Lamb is worthy *accipere … divinitatem*, to receive divinity, and the Offertory has the Father bid his Son ask for the nations; of the eternal Son both seem to say that he receives what he could never lack. St Hilary of Poitiers answers from the second psalm that the risen Son asks back the glory he had before; St Thomas Aquinas distinguishes a dominion that is his by birth from a dominion he merited by prayer and passion; St Bede sees in the Lamb who dies and the Lion who conquers one Lord, King and Priest by his self-offering. The second is where the kingdom is, and whom it subjects. The bound prisoner says his kingdom is not of this world and not from hence, while the Gradual sings that all kings shall adore him and the Collect asks that the nations be subjected to him. St Augustine answers that the kingdom is not *of* this world but is *in* it, gathered out of every nation; St John Chrysostom, that its empire is from above and needs no servants to defend it; St Robert Bellarmine, that the kings are bidden to subject themselves freely now and will all bend at the judgment. The third is how the kingdom ends. The Preface says the Son will hand the kingdom over to the Father, the Alleluia that his power shall not be taken away, the Communion that he shall sit King for ever. St Augustine answers that the kingdom handed over is the faithful, brought to the contemplation of God; St Hilary, in his own words, that the Son delivers to God not his power but us, made his kingdom by the glorifying of his body. The three readings are complementary: they say how he reigns as man, what kind of kingdom it is and how it claims the nations, and where it is going.

## The Appointed Texts {#sec:appointed-texts}

The Latin below is that of the 1962 typical edition, *Missale Romanum* (Typis Polyglottis Vaticanis), printed pp. 697–698, nos. 3938–3949, and, for the Preface, p. 291, no. 1080, read on the page images of the CMAA facsimile. Every word of every element and of the Preface’s proper body already stands in the formulary’s first publication, with the decree *Urbis et Orbis* of the Sacred Congregation of Rites of 12 December 1925, in the *Acta Apostolicae Sedis* 17 (1925), pp. 666–668. The two printings differ in orthography (the 1925 page writes *ejus*, *Jesu*), capitals and pointing, in the headings of the chants, and in three places of extent: the 1925 page abbreviates every conclusion to *Qui tecum*, which the 1962 page prints at length; it ends the Preface’s proper body at *Et ideo…*, which the 1962 page completes with the common closing that already stands in the Pustet Missal of 1862; and it ends the paschal Alleluia without its last *Alleluia*. The rubrics printed with the formulary are those of the 1960 code and are described here, not reproduced.

The English of the scriptural elements is the Douay–Rheims Bible in Bishop Challoner’s revision, which translates the Clementine Vulgate in the Missal’s own Vulgate numbering. It is given at the canonical verses: where a chant takes part of a verse, the Douay words for that part are given and an ellipsis marks what the Missal leaves out, and each place where the Missal’s form departs from the Vulgate is named beneath the text. The English of the three orations and the Preface is that of the Lasance *New Roman Missal* in the Benziger revision of 1945, pp. 1257–1260 and 813–814, which prints this Mass in Latin and English under the rubrics before 1960 (a commemoration of the occurring Sunday after each oration, and the rank of a double of the first class). None of this English is an approved liturgical translation of the 1962 Missal. Psalms are cited in the Missal’s numbering, with the Hebrew numbering in parentheses.

The antiphon joins two verses of the Apocalypse. From Apoc 5:12 it takes the angels’ acclamation without its opening, “Saying with a loud voice,” and without its last two gifts, “and glory and benediction”; from Apoc 1:6 it takes only the doxology, without “And hath made us a kingdom, and priests to God and his Father” and without the “Amen.” The Missal’s *Ps. 71, 1* counts the psalm’s title, *Psalmus, in Salomonem* (“A psalm on Solomon”), as unnumbered; the Clementine Vulgate and the Douay number the title as verse 1 and print the Introit’s words at the head of their verse 2, which goes on, “To judge thy people with justice, and thy poor with judgment.” After the *Gloria Patri* the antiphon is repeated (RGMR 427).

### Printed for Other Days: The Tract and the Paschal Alleluia {#sec:other-days}

Between the Alleluia and the Gospel the Missal prints two chants that replace the Gradual and Alleluia in other seasons. The first stands under the rubric *In Missis votivis post Septuagesimam, omissis Alleluia et versu sequenti, dicitur*: in votive Masses after Septuagesima, the Alleluia and its verse are left out and this Tract is said (no. 3943, *Ps. 88, 27-28 et 30*). The second stands under *Tempore autem paschali omittitur graduale, et eius loco dicitur*: in Paschaltide the Gradual is left out and this Alleluia is said in its place (no. 3944). The feast is kept on the last Sunday of October, which lies neither between Septuagesima and Easter nor in Paschaltide, so neither chant is ever sung on the feast. Both belong to the votive Mass of Christ the King, the same formulary said on other days (RGMR 308 a 5).

The Tract takes verses 27, 28 and 30 of Psalm 88 (89), leaving out verse 29; the paschal Alleluia’s first verse is the Alleluia verse of the feast, and its second is Apoc 19:16 without the Vulgate’s initial *Et*, which the Douay renders “And.”

## Each Element in Its Setting {#sec:each-element}

Each appointed text is read here first in its own place: the chapter or psalm it comes from, the words the Missal keeps and leaves, and what its principal witnesses say of it. The three readings that follow take these findings up across the whole Mass.

### Introit: the Lamb before the throne {#sec:element-introit}

The antiphon comes from the throne vision of Apoc 4–5. In the right hand of him who sits on the throne John sees a book sealed with seven seals, and no one in heaven or on earth is found worthy to open it. One of the ancients tells him not to weep, for “the lion of the tribe of Juda” has prevailed; John looks and sees “a Lamb standing as it were slain” (5:5–6). The four living creatures and the ancients then sing a new canticle to the Lamb who was slain and has redeemed men to God “out of every tribe and tongue and people and nation” and “made us to our God a kingdom and priests, and we shall reign on the earth” (5:9–10). Only after that do the angels, thousands of thousands, take up the sevenfold acclamation of verse 12 that the Introit sings. The Introit’s second sentence comes from John’s greeting at the head of the book, from Jesus Christ “the faithful witness, the first begotten of the dead and the prince of the kings of the earth, who hath loved us and washed us from our sins in his own blood” (1:5).

Victorinus of Pettau reads the opening of the book as the overcoming of death: none was found worthy save the Lamb, and because he overcame death as testator it was just that he be made heir and possess the human members (on 5:2–5). St Bede reads the Lion and the Lamb as one Lord, a Lamb in dying innocently and a Lion in conquering death (*Expl. Apoc.* I, on 5:5–6). Blessed Ildefonso Schuster, commenting on this Mass’s Introit in 1930, notes that John recalls the Passion, the Lamb “immolated and slain,” before he shows the Lamb’s glory, because “the exaltation of Jesus Christ is, according to St Paul, in proportion to the humiliation he endured” (*The Sacramentary* V, p. 201). The verb that the antiphon sets at its centre, *accipere*, and its strange object, *divinitatem*, receive their fullest treatment in the first reading below.

The verse is the opening prayer of Psalm 71, *Deus, iudicium tuum Regi da: et iustitiam tuam Filio Regis*. The psalm is titled *in Salomonem* and asks for the king a reign of justice to the poor, a peace that lasts as long as the moon, dominion from sea to sea, the homage of the kings of Tharsis, Arabia and Saba, and the blessing of all nations in him; it closes *Defecerunt laudes David, filii Jesse* (71:20). Its witnesses differ on its literal sense. St Augustine holds that the title is figurative, because the psalm says what could not be true of Solomon according to the flesh: Solomon means peacemaker, the true Peacemaker is the Mediator, and the King who is also the King’s Son is Christ, to whom the Father has given all judgment (Jn 5:22; *En. in Ps.* 71.1). Cassiodorus and Theodoret of Cyrus read it the same way, Theodoret adding that Solomon neither ruled the ends of the earth nor ended his life in glory. St Robert Bellarmine gives the literal sense as David’s prayer for his son Solomon, “just anointed king,” who asks not riches but the grace of judging rightly, “for kings were created for the people, not the people for kings”; he allows the reading of Christ beside it, and says the psalm “now begins to pass from Solomon to Christ” only at the verse “he shall continue with the sun” (*Explanatio*, Ps 71). Schuster calls the verse “distinctly Messianic,” the Messianic office conferred upon Jesus (pp. 201–202).

### Collect: all things restored in the Son {#sec:element-collect}

The Collect was composed for the feast and first printed in 1925. Its principal clause, *qui in dilecto Filio tuo … omnia instaurare voluisti*, takes its verb from the Vulgate of Eph 1:10, *instaurare omnia in Christo*, the “re-establishing” or summing up of all things in Christ, and its *dilecto Filio* from the “beloved Son” of Eph 1:6 and the “Son of his love” of Col 1:13. Two witnesses tie that verse of Ephesians to the Epistle. St John Chrysostom, on Col 1:20, says the things in heaven are reconciled because Christ brought man up to the angels, and joins this to “to sum up all things in Christ” (*Hom. in Col.* 3). Theodoret reads the *in ipsum* of Col 1:16 of the work done through the Incarnation and cites *Instaurare omnia in Christo* for it (*In Col.* 1). The title *universorum Rege* names what Col 1:16–17 states of the Son, in whom all things were created and hold together, and what the Preface will call him.

The petition gives the condition of the nations and the remedy. They are *familiae gentium*, the families of the peoples, and they are *peccati vulnere disgregatae*: their scattering is a wound, and its cause is sin. Schuster reads the prayer as inspired by St Paul: sin is “a disruptive force” that ruined the unity of the human race, which Christ the new Adam restores (p. 202). What the Collect asks for them is subjection, but to a rule whose quality it names first, *suavissimo imperio*, “most gentle” or, in the Lasance, “most loving.” How a kingdom not of this world can ask the subjection of nations is the question of the second reading.

### Epistle: translated into the kingdom of the Son {#sec:element-epistle}

Paul writes to the Colossians in bonds (Col 4:3, 18), and the passage stands inside his prayer for them (1:9–11) and before its application to them as once enemies, now reconciled (1:21–23). Its first half is a thanksgiving to the Father, who has made us worthy of “the lot of the saints in light,” delivered us “from the power of darkness,” and “translated us into the kingdom of the Son of his love” (1:12–13). The 1962 page sets the words of verse 13 as the caption of the engraved headpiece above the formulary. The second half is the hymn to the Son as image of the invisible God and firstborn of every creature, in whom, through whom and unto whom all things were made, visible and invisible, thrones and dominations and principalities and powers; as head of the body, the Church, and firstborn from the dead; as the one in whom the fulness dwells, through whom the Father reconciles all things, “making peace through the blood of his cross” (1:15–20).

Chrysostom stresses the verb of verse 13: God did not only deliver us from darkness but translated us into a kingdom, and called it the kingdom of the Son of his love, giving us the same honour as the Son (*Hom. in Col.* 2). In the next homily he insists, on verse 15, that “firstborn” does not mean “first created,” and on verse 18 that “firstborn from the dead” means the firstfruits of the resurrection. St Thomas Aquinas glosses *transtulit* as *ut essemus regnum Dei*, “that we might be the kingdom of God,” and joins to it the Gospel’s *regnum meum non est de hoc mundo* and the Apocalypse’s “thou hast made us to our God a kingdom” (5:10); he assigns the redemption to Christ *inquantum homo, factus … pro nobis sacrificium*, and the remission of sins to him as God (*Super Col.* 1 lect. 3). On verse 18 he makes Christ head of the Church *secundum quod huiusmodi*, as man, first in grace and first in glory, and calls the resurrection a second generation (lect. 5). Theodoret marks the same turn at verse 18, where Paul passes, he says, from theology to the economy, and Christ is our head according to the humanity; at verse 20 he says Christ made our reconciliation by suffering and offering the sacrifice for us, and joined the heavenly to the earthly. Schuster sees in the Epistle Christ’s primacy over all creation, the angels included, founded on the hypostatic union, and his headship of the Church (p. 202).

The Missal’s text departs from the Vulgate only at its frame: it opens *Fratres: Gratias agimus* and closes *in Christo Iesu Domino nostro*.

### Gradual: from sea to sea {#sec:element-gradual}

The Gradual takes Ps 71:8 and 71:11 and leaves out the verses between them, in which the Ethiopians fall down before the king, his enemies lick the ground, and the kings of Tharsis, the islands, Arabia and Saba bring gifts. Its respond is the extent of the dominion, its verse the homage of all kings and the service of all nations.

The Fathers read the geography of verse 8 as the whole earth. For St Augustine “from sea to sea” is from any end of the earth to any other, the Church spread through the whole world, and “from the river” is the Jordan, where the Father’s voice was heard at the baptism and from which the Gospel of the kingdom went out to all nations (*En. in Ps.* 71.11). Cassiodorus adds that the verse cannot belong to Solomon, who reigned in Judaea alone, and that from the Jordan *per totum orbem terrarum saluberrima regula sacri baptismatis emanavit*, the saving rule of holy baptism flowed out through the whole world. Theodoret takes the seas as the outer seas that bound the inhabited world, and the river as the Jordan, from which the preaching “Do penance, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand” began. Bellarmine, following Augustine, prefers the Jordan, where the kingdom had its rise.

Schuster reads the Gradual’s prophecy as being fulfilled while the Church extends her peaceful conquests, and entire only at the judgment (p. 202; his words are given in the second reading). He also treats the Gradual and the Alleluia together as “the double chant after the Epistle,” which sets out a threefold catholicity: of place, *a mari usque ad mare*; of subjects, *omnes reges … omnes gentes*, kings and “wretched pariahs” alike; and of ages, *potestas aeterna … non auferetur* (p. 203). That is the one join between two elements of the Mass that he makes himself. What the kings’ homage means, and when it is given, is developed in the second reading.

### Alleluia: an everlasting power {#sec:element-alleluia}

Daniel dates his vision to the first year of Baltasar, king of Babylon (Dan 7:1). Four beasts rise from the sea; thrones are set, the Ancient of days sits, the books are opened and the beast is slain. Then “one like the Son of man came with the clouds of heaven, and he came even to the ancient of days: and they presented him before him” (7:13), and he is given power and glory and a kingdom that all peoples serve, an everlasting power and an indestructible kingdom (7:14). In the interpretation, “the saints of the most high God” receive the kingdom and possess it for ever (7:18, 27). The Alleluia sings only the second half of verse 14: the power as possessed and lasting, not the giving of it.

St Jerome identifies the son of man coming with the clouds with the stone of Nabuchodonosor’s dream, and reads both of the Son of God’s assumption of human flesh. His reading of the giving of power by the Apostle’s *Qui cum in forma Dei esset* (Phil 2:6–8) belongs to the first reading below, and his argument against Porphyry, that no man can hold a power which no end encloses, to the third (*In Dan.* II, on 7:13–14). On 7:17–18 he holds that the saints receive a heavenly, not an earthly, kingdom: *cesset ergo mille annorum fabula*, let the tale of the thousand years cease. The scholia on Daniel transmitted under the name of Hippolytus say that the Father put all things in subjection to the Son and showed him first-begotten of God, of a virgin and from the dead: King of all in heaven because the Word born before all, of all on earth because made man, of all under the earth because numbered among the dead. Aquinas cites this verse in his exposition of the Gospel, for the origin and the permanence of Christ’s royal power (*Super Io.* 18 lect. 6). Schuster sees its partial fulfilment in the Church’s two thousand years against the empires that have passed away (pp. 202–203).

### Gospel: the king before the governor {#sec:element-gospel}

St John places the scene in the praetorium, the governor’s hall, early on the morning before the Pasch; the accusers will not enter it, so that Pilate goes in and out between them and the prisoner (Jn 18:28–29). The Gospel is his first questioning of Jesus inside. What follows it in John’s account is Pilate’s “What is truth?” (18:38), the choice of Barabbas, the scourging, the crown of thorns and the purple, *Ecce homo*, the chief priests’ “We have no king but Caesar,” and the handing over to be crucified (18:39–19:16).

Each phrase of the dialogue has its own difficulty. Jesus answers Pilate’s question with a question; St Augustine reads it as exposing the vain notion of his kingdom that Gentiles and Jews alike had formed (*Tract. in Io.* 115.1). Verse 36 denies that the kingdom belongs to this world, and the Latin words the denial three times: the kingdom is not *de hoc mundo*; were it *ex hoc mundo*, his servants would strive for him; and now it is not *hinc*. The Douay renders the first two alike, “of this world,” and the third “from hence.” Augustine draws from the words the distinction of “of” this world from “in” it, and of “from hence” from “here” (115.2), which the second reading takes up. “Thou sayest that I am a king” he reads as neither a denial nor a confession of the king Pilate imagines (115.3). “For this was I born” divides the witnesses. Augustine reads it of the temporal nativity, “by being born of the Virgin” (115.4); Chrysostom of the eternal generation, for he who was born a king has all his attributes by generation (*Hom. in Io.* 83.4); Aquinas sets the two readings side by side, the second *sicut Deus ex Deo, ita rex ex rege*, and adds that “for this came I into the world” is the temporal coming even on Chrysostom’s reading (*Super Io.* 18 lect. 6). St Cyril of Alexandria reads “came into the world” as “when I became Man” (*In Io.* XII). The whole dialogue, and the denial at its centre, is the text of the second reading.

### Offertory: ask of me {#sec:element-offertory}

The antiphon is one verse of the second psalm, which Acts 4:25 ascribes to David. The psalm opens with the nations raging and the kings of the earth standing up “against the Lord and against his Christ” (2:1–2); he who dwells in heaven laughs at them; the king speaks: “I am appointed king by him over Sion, his holy mountain,” and recites the Lord’s decree, “Thou art my son, this day have I begotten thee” (2:6–7). The Offertory’s verse follows at once. After it the psalm gives the king a rod of iron (2:9) and turns to the rulers: “And now, O ye kings, understand: receive instruction, you that judge the earth. Serve ye the Lord with fear: and rejoice unto him with trembling” (2:10–11).

The Missal sings only verse 8, but its “Ask of me” is spoken to the Son of verse 7, and the Fathers read the two verses together. Their reading of that pair, of what it means for the eternal Son to ask, is the centre of the first reading; their reading of the kings of verses 10–11 belongs to the second. Schuster, on this antiphon, says that to “the nations and the kings who rise up against” God’s design Christ shows Ps 2:8 as “the solemn deed of investiture which he has received from his Father” (p. 203).

### Secret: the victim of reconciliation {#sec:element-secret}

The Secret, composed for the feast, names the sacrifice by its effect: *hostiam … humanae reconciliationis*, the victim by which mankind is reconciled, in the vocabulary of Col 1:20 and of 2 Cor 5:18–19, “God … hath reconciled us to himself by Christ.” Its relative clause identifies the victim: *quem sacrificiis praesentibus immolamus*, the Son himself, immolated in the sacrifice now offered. And it asks of him the gifts that are the opposite of the Collect’s wound: for all nations, *unitatis et pacis dona*, the gifts of unity and peace. Schuster gives the Secret’s text without comment (p. 204). After it the Missal directs the proper Preface.

### Preface: priest and king {#sec:element-preface}

The Preface, too, was composed for the feast and first printed with the Mass in 1925, in place between the Secret and the Communion; the 1962 Missal prints it among the Prefaces and appoints it for the feast and for the votive Mass of Christ the King (RGMR 492). Its argument runs in one sentence. The Father anointed his only-begotten Son *oleo exsultationis*, the “oil of gladness” of Ps 44:8, which Heb 1:9 applies to the Son, and anointed him both *Sacerdotem aeternum* and *universorum Regem*. The purpose of the anointing is double and ordered: first, that offering himself on the altar of the cross as a spotless and peace-making victim he might accomplish *redemptionis humanae sacramenta*; then, all creatures being subjected to his rule, that he might hand over to the Father’s majesty an eternal and universal kingdom. The verb *traderet* is the “delivered up” of 1 Cor 15:24, “when he shall have delivered up the kingdom to God and the Father.” The kingdom is then named by its goods, in three groups: *veritatis et vitae*; *sanctitatis et gratiae*; *iustitiae, amoris et pacis*.

Schuster’s commentary treats the Preface as a text. Its conception of Christ as Pontiff and King “who reconquers and restores to his Heavenly Father the realm of creation … that God might eternally be all in all” is, he writes, “borrowed from St Paul,” and its compiler gave it the lyrical character of the early eucharistic *Hymnus* (p. 204). Bede, on the clause of Apoc 1:6 that the Introit leaves out, gives the same joining of king and priest by self-offering: *Quia Rex regum et Sacerdos coelestis se offerendo pro nobis nos suo corpori adunavit, nemo sanctorum est qui spiritualiter sacerdotii officio careat*, because the King of kings and heavenly Priest, by offering himself for us, has united us to his body, there is no saint who lacks the office of the priesthood in the spirit (*Expl. Apoc.* I, on 1:6). *Quas primas* joins the kingly office to the offices of redeemer and priest (n. 16). The handing over of the kingdom, and its relation to a reign that never ends, is the text of the third reading.

### Communion: the Lord shall sit King for ever {#sec:element-communion}

Psalm 28 is titled, in the Douay, “A psalm for David, at the finishing of the tabernacle.” It summons the sons of God to give glory to the Lord, and then hears his voice seven times: upon the waters, in power, in magnificence, breaking the cedars of Libanus, dividing the flame of fire, shaking the desert, preparing the stags and discovering the thick woods, while in his temple all speak his glory. It ends with the two verses from which the Communion takes its halves: “The Lord maketh the flood to dwell: and the Lord shall sit king for ever. The Lord will give strength to his people: the Lord will bless his people with peace.” The antiphon keeps the session and the blessing and leaves out the flood and the strength.

The witnesses do not agree on what the flood is, and so they place the session differently. St Thomas Aquinas gives first the plain sense of the Hebrew, *Dominus diluvio sedit*: his judgments were manifest from the beginning, as when he brought the deluge, and he will sit King for ever, judging the peoples in equity. Then he gives three mystical readings: the saints dwelling secure in the Church as in Noe’s ark amid the flood of tribulations; the world and its carnal men, the *diluvium*, brought to dwell in his temple when they are converted, *et sedebit rex in aeternum*; and the baptismal waters, where he dwells by the effect of grace (*Postilla super Psalmos* 28). St Augustine reads the flood as this world, in which the Lord dwells first in his saints, kept in the Church as in the ark (*En. in Ps.* 28.10). St Basil the Great calls the grace of baptism a deluge, and Cassiodorus sees the Holy Trinity dwelling in the waters of baptism; Theodoret, the world drowned by the torrent of lawlessness and made a new creation. Bellarmine reports both the deluge and baptism and will not contradict those who hold them, “being of great weight and high position,” while his own second reading places the flood at the day of judgment (*Explanatio*, Ps 28). Schuster reads the antiphon of the communicant, in whose heart the King is enthroned (p. 204). The third reading follows these witnesses into the session for ever.

### Postcommunion: under the standards of the King {#sec:element-postcommunion}

The Postcommunion begins from what has just been received, *immortalitatis alimoniam*, the food of immortality, and asks a share in the reign: that those who glory to fight under the standards of Christ the King, *sub Christi Regis vexillis militare*, may reign with him for ever *in caelesti sede*. The last clause echoes 2 Tim 2:12, “If we suffer, we shall also reign with him,” which Theodoret cites on Col 1:13, and the Apocalypse’s “we shall reign on the earth” (5:10). Its figure makes the faithful soldiers, as no other text of the Mass does, and it stands a few minutes after a Gospel in which the King says that his servants do not fight. Schuster’s note on the prayer says what the standard is: “The Royal Standard of Jesus Christ is the Cross: Dicite in nationibus: regnavit a ligno Deus; for the kingdom of Christ is that of obedience, humility and sacrifice” (p. 204). The second reading takes up the tension between the standard and the Gospel.

## The Slain Lamb Receives the Kingdom That Was Always His {#lane-lamb-receives-the-kingdom}

The kingship this Mass acclaims belongs to the Son by nature, and it is also received by him: received in the humanity he assumed, asked for and won by his death, and given at his exaltation. What he receives is the nations he has redeemed, and the place where that received kingship is exercised is the sacrifice the Mass offers. The reading begins from the Introit’s verb and the Offertory’s command, and it lets the whole formulary take its order from the Preface, where the anointed Son first offers himself on the cross and only then hands over a kingdom.

### What the Lamb receives {#sec:lamb-question}

The angels of the Introit are not praising God in the abstract. They stand round the throne at the moment the Lamb has taken the sealed book that no one else was worthy to open, and the reason the elders have just given is a history: “thou wast slain and hast redeemed us to God, in thy blood” (Apoc 5:9). Their acclamation names seven things the Lamb is worthy to receive, and the first two that the Introit sings are *virtutem, et divinitatem*: power, and divinity. The Offertory raises the same difficulty from another book. The king set on Sion recites the decree, “Thou art my son, this day have I begotten thee,” and the Lord goes on, *Postula a me, et dabo tibi gentes hereditatem tuam*: “Ask of me, and I will give thee the Gentiles for thy inheritance” (Ps 2:7–8). If the Lamb is the eternal Son, how can he receive divinity? If the one addressed is the Son begotten, why must he ask for anything? The Alleluia sings only the possessing half of Dan 7:14, “his power is an everlasting power,” but the verse it comes from begins “And he gave him power.”

The question was not idle in the Church’s history. St Jerome, expounding that verse of Daniel, writes that the Son of man’s receiving power, honour and kingdom must be understood by the Apostle’s “Who being in the form of God … emptied himself, taking the form of a servant … becoming obedient unto death” (Phil 2:6–8), which the Arians, he says, should have read piously (*In Dan.* II, on 7:13–14). Read without that key, texts of receiving make the Son a creature promoted for his merits. Read with it, they speak of what the eternal Son receives in the nature he took. The Fathers answer the question where the second psalm and Daniel raise it, and their answers carry over to the Lamb of the Introit, who is worthy to receive because he was slain.

### St Hilary: the risen Son asks back the glory he had {#sec:lamb-hilary}

St Hilary of Poitiers reads the decree of Psalm 2 on apostolic authority. St Paul at Antioch had said that God “hath fulfilled … raising up Jesus, as in the second psalm also is written: Thou art my Son: this day have I begotten thee” (Acts 13:33). So Hilary takes the “today” of the decree as the begetting of the firstborn from the dead (*Tractatus super Psalmos* 2.30). The risen Lord’s own words, “All power is given to me in heaven and in earth” (Mt 28:18), show that he asked for what he received; and when the psalm goes on, *Posce a me*, Hilary concludes, *accepit ergo hereditatem gentium, quam poposcit*, he received the inheritance of the nations, which he asked for (2.31). The asking itself Hilary finds in the prayer of the Supper, “Father, the hour is come. Glorify thy Son … As thou hast given him power over all flesh” (Jn 17:1–2).

What was asked, and what was given? Hilary answers with the hymn of Philippians. He who abode in the form of God took the form of a servant, and after the death of the cross he is exalted into the name that is above every name: *in deum namque, quia nullum ultra deum nomen est, prouehitur, eique hoc petenti, id est, ut esset, quod fuerat ante, donatur*, for he is raised into God, since there is no name beyond God, and to him who asks this, namely that he might be what he had been before, it is given (2.33). The receiving is the glorifying of the humanity the Son assumed. The Son loses nothing and gains nothing as God; the servant he became is raised into the glory the Word always had. And the inheritance is not territory. It is the nations baptized and taught, carried over *ex iniusto … iure dominantium in regnum aeternum diuinumque*, out of the unjust right of those who lorded it over them into an eternal and divine kingdom, the inheritance *primogeniti huius ex mortuis*, of this firstborn from the dead (2.31). Hilary is expounding the psalm, not the Epistle, but his words for the inheritance are the Epistle’s: those transferred “into the kingdom of the Son of his love,” and the Son “the firstborn from the dead” (Col 1:13, 18). Later in the same tractate he draws the consequence for the inheritance itself: the kingdom of God is where sin is overcome and death destroyed, and those who become co-heirs of the eternal King must themselves become kings (2.42–43).

### St Augustine and St Thomas: one dominion by two rights {#sec:lamb-two-rights}

St Augustine divides the two verses differently. “To-day have I begotten Thee,” though it may seem spoken of the birth in the flesh, he gives “a divine interpretation”: it is the eternal generation, and “today” signifies the presence of eternity (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 2.6). Only verse 8 is temporal: “Ask of Me” “has at once a temporal sense with reference to the Manhood which He took on Himself, who offered up Himself as a Sacrifice in the stead of all sacrifices, who also maketh intercession for us” (2.7). So Augustine and Hilary agree on verse 8, the asking and receiving of the nations by the Son made man, and differ on verse 7: Hilary reads the decree of the resurrection, Augustine of the eternal generation.

St Thomas Aquinas states Augustine’s division as a principle. The two verses give Christ dominion over the nations *duplici jure: scilicet hereditario, et meritorio*, by a double right, of inheritance and of merit. The right of inheritance is the eternal generation, *hodie genui te, idest aeternaliter*. The right of merit is the asking: God wills that we receive his gifts by asking, *et hoc exemplum voluit ostendere per Christum, quia voluit quod peteret, quod sibi jure hereditario competebat*, and he willed to show this example in Christ, willing that he should ask for what belonged to him by right of inheritance. Christ asks by his prayer, “for them also who through their word shall believe in me” (Jn 17:20), and by his passion, the death that redeems those called to the eternal inheritance (Heb 9:15); and he holds the nations not as ministers hold them, as Peter or Paul did, but as lord, the Son in his own house (Heb 3:5–6; *Postilla super Psalmos* 2, nn. 5–6). The double right removes the scandal of the Offertory. The Son does not ask because he lacks; he asks because the Father wills that even what is owed be received as a gift, and because the asking is his passion.

Cassiodorus gives the rule its widest form. Whenever the Lord Christ asks that something be given him, or another asks it for him, *naturam humanitatis evidenter ostendi*, the nature of his humanity is plainly shown; for the almighty Word, having all that is the Father’s, needs nothing to be given (*Expositio Psalmorum* 71, on verse 1, with Jn 16:15). On Ps 2:8 he applies it: *Quidquid enim in tempore accepit Christus, secundum hominem ea quae non habebat acquirit*; the possession is given because *natura humanitatis accepit, quod semper divina possedit*, the human nature received what the divine always possessed. Theodoret of Cyrus, on the same verse, says that he who is Lord by nature as God *etiam ut homo universorum dominium accipit*, receives also as man the lordship of all; on the Introit’s verse he adds that the psalm teaches both, that as God he has authority over all and as man receives it from his own Father (*Interpretatio in Psalmos* 2; 71). Theodoret parts from Augustine on verse 7, which he will not apply to the Godhead at all, since of that generation the Father says “from the womb before the day star I begot thee” (Ps 109:3): as man Christ receives that voice too.

St Robert Bellarmine hears three generations in verse 7, the eternal, the birth in the flesh and the resurrection, and makes the first the ground of the decree: “to Christ, as being the true and natural Son of God, is due all power in heaven and on earth.” On verse 8 the Father says to him, “You my natural Son, the incarnation of my power raised from the dead, have just right to ask me for power over all nations”; the kingdom so given is “his spiritual kingdom, that is, his Church,” not bounded like David’s or Solomon’s but over all nations, “infidels even included” (*Explanatio*, Ps 2).

### St Bede: the Lamb and the Lion, King and Priest {#sec:lamb-bede}

St Bede reads the Apocalypse itself in the same grammar. The one whom the ancient calls a Lion and John sees as a Lamb is one Lord: a Lamb in dying innocently, a Lion in conquering death. When the Lamb takes the book from the right hand of him who sits on the throne (Apoc 5:7), *dispensationem ipsam Incarnationis a Patre et a seipso, secundum id quod Deus est, dispositam accepisse Filius hominis dicitur*: the Son of man is said to receive the very dispensation of the Incarnation, which the Father and he himself, according as he is God, had disposed. He is the Lamb in his humanity and the Father’s right hand in his deity (*Explanatio Apocalypsis* I, on 5:5–7). The one who gives and the one who receives are the same Son, in his two natures. On the greeting of chapter 1, Bede reads “the first begotten of the dead” as the one who for the suffering of death was crowned with glory and honour (Heb 2:9; Phil 2:9), and on the clause of Apoc 1:6 that the Introit leaves out, “hath made us a kingdom, and priests,” he gives the consequence for the redeemed: because the King of kings and heavenly Priest united us to his body by offering himself for us, no saint lacks a share in the priesthood (see the Preface, above).

Victorinus of Pettau had read the sealed book in the same direction. To open it is to overcome death for man; because the Lamb overcame death as testator, it was just that he be appointed heir and possess the human members (on Apoc 5:2–5). The Lamb receives an inheritance by dying, and the inheritance is mankind.

### The objection: he received nothing in addition {#sec:lamb-objection}

The strongest objection comes from St John Chrysostom, and it comes from this Mass’s own Gospel. On “For this was I born,” Chrysostom writes: “If then He was born a king, all His other attributes are by Generation, and He has nothing which He received in addition.” He draws at once a rule for every text that speaks of giving to the Son: “So that when you hear that, As the Father has life in Himself, so has He given to the Son also to have life, deem of nothing else but His generation, and so of the rest” (*Homilies on John* 83.4; Jn 5:26). St Cyril of Alexandria says of the same verse that Christ’s sovereignty is “not … added unto it from without, but … essentially and naturally inherent” (*Commentary on John* XII). A reading that made Christ king only from Easter, or that let the Introit’s *accipere divinitatem* mean that the Lamb becomes divine, would contradict them and the faith they defend.

The answer that Augustine’s division gives grants Chrysostom’s premise. The kingship by generation is the decree of verse 7, and it is not received; the dominion asked for in verse 8 is the same dominion held now in the humanity and won by the passion. But the answer does not meet Chrysostom on his own ground. His rule refers a text of giving to the eternal generation; the rule Cassiodorus states, and Theodoret applies, refers the asking and receiving to the humanity. Chrysostom does not expound Ps 2:8 at this place, and his rule is not a reading of it. The witnesses agree that Christ is King by nature, and they read the texts of giving and asking addressed to the Son by two different rules, and on that narrower question they disagree. Hilary’s reading of verse 7 of the resurrection is compatible with the answer only because he does not make the resurrection the beginning of the Sonship: what is given is *ut esset, quod fuerat ante*. Bellarmine’s first generation is the eternal one.

The instituting act of the feast holds both titles together. *Quas primas* quotes St Cyril for a dominion that is Christ’s by essence and by nature, grounded in the hypostatic union, so that to him as man angels and men are subject, and then names a second title, acquired by redemption, citing 1 Pet 1:18–19 on the price of his precious blood (n. 13).

### How the whole Mass receives the kingdom {#sec:lamb-elements}

Read in this way, every element of the formulary takes a place in one movement, from the eternal right to the received dominion and its exercise at the altar.

The **Introit** is the reading’s text: the Lamb *qui occisus est* is worthy to receive because he was slain, and *Ipsi gloria et imperium* is what he receives. Its *accipere … divinitatem*, read with Hilary’s servant raised into the name of God and Bede’s Son of man who receives what he disposed as God, means on this reading that the slain Lamb receives, in the nature he took, the name and glory of the God he always was. He does not become what he was not; as man he is given to be what he had been before. Schuster’s note on the antiphon, that the exaltation is in proportion to the humiliation and that John names the Passion first, states the same order for the Introit alone (p. 201). Its verse, *Deus, iudicium tuum Regi da*, is itself a giving to the King and the King’s Son, which Augustine reads of the Father giving all judgment to the Son (Jn 5:22) and Theodoret of the authority he has as God and receives as man. The **Collect** confesses that the Father willed, *voluisti*, to restore all things in his Son, and asks that the nations be subjected to him: the inheritance asked for in the psalm, still being given. The **Epistle** supplies the means and the moment of the receiving: redemption “through his blood,” peace “through the blood of his cross,” the “firstborn from the dead” who is head of the Church according to the humanity in Theodoret’s reading and *secundum quod huiusmodi* in Aquinas’s.

The **Gradual** sings the inheritance in possession, the dominion from sea to sea and the homage of the kings. The **Alleluia** keeps only the power as possessed, *potestas aeterna*; the giving that Jerome expounds by Philippians stands in the half of the verse that is not sung. In the **Gospel** the King stands bound before the governor, the passion already begun. Chrysostom says he was made captive “by consenting, and giving Himself up” (*Homilies on John* 83.4): the kingship of verse 8 is being acquired in the very act of the trial, by the prisoner who could have had servants fight for him. The **Offertory** is the reading’s centre, the command to ask, sung as the gifts are brought to the altar; Schuster, on this antiphon, calls it the deed of investiture received from the Father (p. 203).

The **Secret** then names the asking that the Fathers found in verse 8. *Hostiam … humanae reconciliationis offerimus*: the sacrifice by which, in Augustine’s words, he “offered up Himself as a Sacrifice in the stead of all sacrifices” is offered now, *quem sacrificiis praesentibus immolamus*, and what is asked of him is the gift that Hilary’s inheritance consists of, the nations’ unity and peace. The **Preface** gives the reading its order. The Son is anointed *Sacerdotem aeternum et universorum Regem*; he offers himself *in ara Crucis* so as to accomplish the mysteries of man’s redemption; only then, *suo subiectis imperio omnibus creaturis*, does he hand over the eternal and universal kingdom. The **Communion** sings the session of the exalted King, *Sedebit Dominus Rex in aeternum*. And the **Postcommunion** asks for those who fight under his standards a share in his reign: Hilary’s co-heirs who must become kings, Bede’s members who share the priesthood of the King who offered himself. The standard they fight under is, in Schuster’s note on that prayer, the Cross (p. 204).

Literal.

: An apocalyptic hymn acclaims the slain Lamb as worthy of power; a royal psalm prays that God give judgment to the king and the king’s son, which Bellarmine reads literally of David’s prayer for Solomon and Augustine, Cassiodorus and Theodoret of Christ alone; another psalm has God promise the nations to his Son, who is to ask for them; Paul thanks the Father for redemption through the blood of the Son’s cross; Jesus, bound, acknowledges before the governor that he is a king.

Allegorical.

: Christ, God from eternity and King by that right, receives as man, through his passion and resurrection, dominion over the nations, and the nations so received are the Church gathered from them. The Lamb who dies and the Lion who conquers are one Lord, who as God disposes the economy he receives as man (Bede).

Moral.

: Kingship is received through obedience unto death, and God wills that even what is owed be asked (Aquinas). The faithful come into the kingdom in the same way: by asking, and by offering themselves with the Priest-King as members who share his priesthood (Bede); and they reign as co-heirs by overcoming sin in themselves (Hilary).

Anagogical.

: The firstborn from the dead is the first of the exalted. His members will be raised after him and will reign with him for ever in the heavenly seat for which the Postcommunion asks.

## Not from Hence, and Therefore Everywhere: The Kingdom of Truth and the Kings of the Earth {#lane-kingdom-not-from-hence}

Christ’s kingdom takes neither its origin nor its means from this world, and for that very reason it reaches everywhere in the world and claims everyone in it. Its subjects are those who hear the truth, gathered from every nation. The kings and nations whose subjection the Collect asks and whose service the Gradual sings are brought under a rule whose instrument is truth and grace. That rule takes no earthly throne for itself, and yet rulers owe it, as rulers, the service of their own office. It begins in free conversion now and is completed at the judgment.

### A prisoner who claims the nations {#sec:hence-question}

The Gospel and the chants around it seem to pull apart. In the governor’s hall, bound and handed over by his own nation, Jesus says three times in two sentences that his kingdom does not belong to the world around him: it is not *de hoc mundo*, it would be defended by his servants if it were *ex hoc mundo*, and it is not *hinc*, from here. Yet the Gradual before the Gospel gives him dominion “from sea to sea, and from the river unto the ends of the earth” and sings that “all kings of the earth shall adore him: all nations shall serve him.” The Offertory after it has the Father promise him “the utmost parts of the earth for thy possession,” in a psalm whose next verses bid the kings “understand” and “Serve ye the Lord with fear” (Ps 2:10–11). The Collect asks that all the families of the nations be subjected, *subdantur*, to his rule. And the Postcommunion has the faithful *militare*, fight as soldiers, under his standards, where the Gospel’s King says that his servants do not fight. How can a kingdom that is not of this world, and not from hence, claim the whole earth, the nations as inheritance and the service of every king?

The question has two wrong answers, and the instituting act of the feast shows why both tempt. *Quas primas* calls it a grave error to say that Christ has no authority whatever in civil affairs, and draws from the feast a public duty of rulers towards him (nn. 17–19, 32). Read alone, the Gospel’s denial might seem to empty the formulary’s subjection of all public consequence; read alone, the subjection might seem to make Christ a rival of Caesar for his throne. The witnesses of this Mass’s texts refuse both.

### St Augustine: not of this world, but in it {#sec:hence-augustine}

St Augustine begins from Jesus’ counter-question, “Sayest thou this thing of thyself?” It was put, he says, so that the vain notion men had formed of his kingdom, Gentiles and Jews alike, might be shown up (*Tractates on the Gospel of John* 115.1). Then he gives the answer its widest audience: “Hear then, ye Jews and Gentiles; hear, O circumcision; hear, O uncircumcision; hear, all ye kingdoms of the earth: I interfere not with your government in this world” (115.2). The reassurance rests on two exact distinctions in the Lord’s words. He did not say that his kingdom is not *in* this world, but that it is not *of* this world; and he did not say that it is not *here*, but that it is not *from hence*. “For His kingdom is here until the end of the world, having tares intermingled therewith until the harvest,” and yet it is not from here, “for it only sojourns as a stranger in the world.” What is the kingdom? “Those who believe in Him,” who were of the world while they belonged to its prince and have been made a kingdom no longer of the world by being reborn in Christ. Augustine gives the Epistle’s verse as the account of that change: “For so did God rescue us from the power of darkness, and translate us into the kingdom of the Son of His love” (Col 1:13), and he hears the king set on Sion in the second psalm speaking of the same hill, which “is not of this world” (Ps 2:6).

“Thou sayest that I am a king” is balanced, he goes on, so that Jesus neither denies that he is a king nor confesses himself such a king as Pilate imagines: “Carnal thyself, thou sayest it carnally” (115.3). And the last words of the pericope define the subjects of this kingdom. “Every one that is of the truth heareth my voice”: hearing is obeying and believing, and in this word Christ “commendeth the grace whereby He calleth according to His own purpose” (115.4). The kingdom gathers its people by truth heard and by grace, not by the strength of servants.

### St John Chrysostom and St Thomas: an empire from above {#sec:hence-chrysostom}

St John Chrysostom reads the same sentence as a statement about the kingdom’s power rather than its people. Is his kingdom then not of this world also? “Certainly it is. How then says He it ‘is not’? Not because He does not rule here, but because He has his empire from above, and because it is not human, but far greater than this and more splendid.” The proof is the servants who do not fight: “Here He shows the weakness of kingship among us, that its strength lies in servants; but that which is above is sufficient for itself, needing nothing.” He says his kingdom is not from hence “not depriving the world of His providence and superintendence, but showing … that His power was not human or perishable” (*Homilies on John* 83.4). His paraphrase of the whole reply is “I am indeed a King, yet not such an one as you suspect, but far more glorious” (84.1). St Cyril of Alexandria makes the same reading a reassurance to Pilate: Christ did not deny that he was King, but showed that he was no enemy to Caesar, for he reigns as God over heaven and earth, “as God He is King, whether man so will, or no” (*Commentary on John* XII).

St Thomas Aquinas sets the two readings side by side. *Regnum* in the Gospel may mean the people who are ruled, as Augustine takes it, or the royal power itself, as Chrysostom does, whose origin is not from worldly causes; and he gives the Alleluia’s verse for that power, an everlasting power that shall not be taken away. Then he draws the conclusion that resolves the apparent contradiction of the Mass: *non est hinc … est tamen hic, quia ubique est*, it is not from here, and yet it is here, because it is everywhere; and he cites for it Wisdom’s reaching “from end to end” (Wis 8:1), the Offertory’s verse, and the first half of Daniel’s (*Super Io.* 18 lect. 6). A kingdom that takes its origin from one place in the world is bounded by that place. A kingdom that takes its origin from above is bounded by no place, and so can claim the whole earth without competing with any power in it.

### The kings of the Gradual and of the second psalm {#sec:hence-kings}

That answer explains how the kingdom can be everywhere. It does not yet say what the kings and nations of the Gradual and the Offertory are to do. Here the same Fathers speak more concretely than a reader of the Gospel alone would expect.

St Augustine reads the Gradual’s psalm of the Church spread through the whole world, and when he comes to the gifts the kings of Tharsis and Saba “shall lead,” he asks why the verb is *lead*, a word for things that walk. The gifts “seem to me to signify men, whom into the fellowship of the Church of Christ the authority of kings doth lead: although even persecuting kings have led gifts, knowing not what they did, in sacrificing the holy Martyrs”; and at once he quotes the Gradual’s verse, “And there shall adore Him all kings of the earth, all nations shall serve Him” (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 71.13). The reason for that homage is that he delivered the needy from the mighty, and “this needy and poor man is the people of men believing in Him. In this people are also kings adoring Him,” who do not disdain to be poor, humbly confessing their sins (71.14). So Augustine gives the kings two places: they belong to the poor people who confess their sins, and their authority has an office in the Church’s spread. On the second psalm he speaks to the kings directly: “now that I am set as King, be ye not sad, kings of the earth, as if your excellency were taken from you, but rather understand and be instructed. For it is expedient for you, that ye should be under Him” (2.9).

In his *Letter* 185, written to justify the imperial laws against the Donatists, Augustine joins the Offertory’s psalm to the Gradual’s verse himself. In the apostles’ time no emperor yet served Christ by laws, because the raging of the kings in Ps 2:1–2 was being fulfilled and not yet the command of verses 10–11. “How then are kings to serve the Lord with fear, except by preventing and chastising with religious severity all those acts which are done in opposition to the commandments of the Lord? For a man serves God in one way in that he is man, in another way in that he is also king”; and “so soon as the fulfillment began of what is written in a later psalm, ‘All kings shall fall down before Him; all nations shall serve Him,’ ” no sober man could tell kings that the Church was no concern of theirs (*Letter* 185.19–20). The distinction Augustine draws there, in defence of the laws against the Donatist schism, is that the king serves God as a man by living faithfully, and as a king in the exercise of his office. St Thomas reports the same distinction on Ps 2:10–11, *secundum Augustinum … rex servit Deo inquantum homo, in se juste vivendo, sed inquantum rex, leges ferendo contra ea quae sunt contra Dei justitiam*, and sees in the psalm a figure of the Church’s history: at first the kings of the earth made laws against Christ and Christians, and afterwards laws for Christ (*Postilla super Psalmos* 2, n. 9).

St Robert Bellarmine reads the kings’ part as conversion. On the second psalm the prophet “exhorts the kings of this world … to freely subject themselves to him, the true and supreme king of all kings,” correcting first their judgment and then their affections, and bids them serve the Lord as a son serves his father (*Explanatio*, Ps 2). On the Gradual’s verse he distinguishes three fulfilments. The adoration of all kings is fulfilled “to a certain extent” in that “a great many princes and nations will be converted to the service and worship of Christ”; it is fulfilled “to the letter” at the day of judgment, when every knee shall bend; and it is fulfilled “always” if it is referred to “the actual power that Christ has over all princes and all nations, so that, with or without their knowledge, with or without their consent, he may deal as he pleases with them” (Mt 28:18; Apoc 1:5; 19:16). They serve him, he adds, because “through him they will be delivered from the power of the devil … and will be introduced to his own most peaceful kingdom” (*Explanatio*, Ps 71).

Theodoret divides the same verse in time: some kings adore willingly in the present life, all after the resurrection, since “now we see not as yet all things subject to him” (Heb 2:8), but then every knee shall bow (Phil 2:10); and by his laws Christ restrains the powerful and makes them merciful to the poor (*Interpretatio in Psalmos* 71). Cassiodorus reads “all kings” as all nations, lest the verse be thought to speak of kings only, and sharpens the paradox: *reges adorabunt, qui putantur adorandi; gentes autem servire, quae terrenos dominos habere noscuntur*, the kings will adore who are thought to be adored, and the nations serve who are known to have earthly lords (*Expositio Psalmorum* 71). Blessed Ildefonso Schuster’s note on the Gradual gives the state of things between the two fulfilments: the kingdom is “complete and absolute de jure, but … opposed and incomplete de facto” (p. 202). On the Gospel he writes that the kingdom is not *hinc* “but it comprehends the world,” and that “Jesus does not come to dethrone the sovereigns of the earth,” but gives to rulers and subjects the laws of truth and justice (p. 203).

### Neither empty nor rival {#sec:hence-difficulty}

The strongest difficulty is now plain. Augustine’s “I interfere not with your government” could be pressed to mean that the kingdom has no public consequence at all, and his *Letter* 185 could be pressed the other way, to make the Gospel a programme of rule. Both statements are Augustine’s. The denial before Pilate is a denial of a worldly origin and of worldly means, not of authority over the world: Chrysostom’s empire from above, which does not deprive the world of his providence. The subjection the formulary asks is real, and it has three moments. It is rendered freely, in the conversion of kings and nations who come into the poor people that confesses its sins (Augustine, *Enarrationes in Psalmos* 71.14; Bellarmine; Theodoret’s “willingly”). It is rendered by rulers as rulers, in the exercise of their own office (Augustine, *Letter* 185.19; Aquinas). And it is completed at the judgment, when every knee bends (Bellarmine, Theodoret, Schuster). Beneath all three lies a dominion that depends on no one’s consent, Bellarmine’s “actual power” and Cyril’s King “whether man so will, or no.” None of these makes Christ a competitor for Caesar’s throne. *Quas primas* holds the two halves in the same way: Christ during his earthly life refrained from exercising such authority and does not now interfere with those who possess earthly goods, and yet individuals, families and states are under his rule, and rulers who know they rule by his mandate owe him public honour and obedience (nn. 17–19, 32). For the witnesses and the encyclical alike, the kingdom of the Gospel seeks no earthly throne, and the service of kings is owed to it.

A second, narrower difference runs inside the reading. For Augustine the kingdom of Jn 18:36 is the people ruled; for Chrysostom it is the royal power. Aquinas keeps both, and the formulary uses both: the Collect asks for a people gathered, and the Alleluia sings a power that lasts. The same two witnesses divide again on the next verse. Augustine reads “For this was I born” of the birth from the Virgin, Chrysostom of the eternal generation (see the Gospel, above). This reading rests on the origin of the kingdom in verse 36 and on the hearers of the truth in verse 37, and either birth serves it: born of the Virgin to bear witness, or born King from eternity, he is the King whose kingdom is not from hence.

### How the whole Mass claims the nations {#sec:hence-elements}

The **Gospel** is the reading’s text: the bound prisoner, the exact *de* and *hinc*, the testimony to the truth, and the hearers of his voice. The **Gradual** sets out the extent of the kingdom that the Gospel declares to be from above. Its “sea to sea” is the Church through the world, and its river is the Jordan, from which, in Augustine, Cassiodorus and Theodoret, the Gospel and baptism went out to all nations; its kings are those whose authority leads men into the Church and who serve as kings, partly now and wholly at the judgment. The **Offertory** gives the nations as inheritance in a psalm whose next verses bid the kings understand and serve: the free subjection Bellarmine reads there, and the service as kings that Augustine and Aquinas read there.

The **Introit**’s verse asks that God give the King his judgment and his justice: a king’s office is judgment and justice to the poor, Augustine’s reading of the psalm. Bellarmine’s literal sense of the same verse, that “kings were created for the people, not the people for kings,” names the standard by which the kings of the Gradual are measured. And the throne of the Introit’s antiphon belongs to the slain Lamb, not to a conqueror. The **Collect** names the nations’ condition as a wound, not a border: *peccati vulnere disgregatae*, scattered by sin, “a disruptive force” in Schuster’s note. The rule it asks them to be subjected to is *suavissimum*, most gentle, a rule whose instrument is its sweetness. On Augustine’s sense of *regnum* the petition is for the nations’ gathering into the Church; on Chrysostom’s it is for their subjection to a power that no earthly kingdom rivals.

The **Epistle** says how one enters such a kingdom: by being *translatus*, carried over out of the power of darkness. Augustine uses the verse to explain the Gospel, and Aquinas glosses it *ut essemus regnum Dei* with the same Gospel verse beside it. The **Alleluia** sings the power whose origin Aquinas, with Chrysostom, places above the world, and in Schuster’s double chant it adds to the Gradual’s universality of place and subjects the universality of the ages. The **Secret** asks for all nations the gifts of unity and peace: the nations’ gathering is a gift given at the altar, not a conquest. The **Preface** names the goods of the kingdom, and the first of them, *regnum veritatis*, is the truth to which the Gospel’s King bears witness. The **Communion**’s blessing in peace is, for Augustine, not peace in this world, which the Lord “hath not promised,” but peace in himself (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 28.11); Theodoret reads it of the peace given to the churches after the war the martyrs won.

The **Postcommunion** makes the faithful soldiers, the one place in the Mass that does so, *sub Christi Regis vexillis militare*, against the Gospel’s servants who do not fight. Chrysostom’s reasoning on Jn 18:36 shows what the figure cannot mean: earthly kingship depends on servants who fight, and Christ’s does not, so the faithful’s warfare is not his defence. Hilary’s reading of the second psalm shows what it can mean: the kingdom of God is where sin is overcome and death destroyed (*Tractatus super Psalmos* 2.42), so that the war is their share in his victory over sin. The standard itself, in Schuster’s note on the prayer, is the Cross (p. 204).

Literal.

: Before the Roman governor Jesus denies that his kingdom has a worldly origin or worldly defenders, and declares that he was born to bear witness to the truth. Royal psalms foresee a king whose dominion reaches the ends of the earth, whom all kings adore and all nations serve, and they warn the kings of the earth to serve the Lord.

Allegorical.

: The kingdom is the Church, here and not from here, gathered from all nations into one by the truth and spread from the Jordan through the world (Augustine). Kings enter it as members of the poor people who confess their sins, and their authority leads others in (Augustine, *Enarrationes in Psalmos* 71.13–14).

Moral.

: To be his subject is to be of the truth and to hear his voice. Every station owes him service in its own office: every man by living faithfully, the ruler also by ruling justly (Augustine, *Letter* 185.19; *Quas primas* nn. 18–19). That service seeks no rival throne; it is a warfare against sin rather than with swords, fought under the standard of the Cross.

Anagogical.

: At the harvest the angels will gather out of his kingdom all that offends (Mt 13:41, in Augustine’s reading). What kings and nations now give willingly, all will give after the resurrection, when every knee bends (Theodoret, Bellarmine). The mixed kingdom of this world becomes the manifest one, and the peace given in him becomes peace without storm.

## The Kingdom Handed Over That Has No End {#lane-kingdom-handed-over}

The end of Christ’s kingship is the Father. The eternal kingdom that the Preface says he hands over is his redeemed people, whom he brings to the vision of God, so that handing over the kingdom and reigning for ever are one consummation and not an abdication. The session for ever that the Communion sings begins already in the souls he has cleansed and is completed after the judgment. The reading begins from the Preface, which ends the Mass’s thanksgiving with the kingdom’s final destination, and lets the Alleluia and the Communion say what kind of end that is.

### A kingdom delivered up that shall not be taken away {#sec:handed-question}

The Preface follows St Paul’s order in 1 Cor 15. The Son is anointed priest and king; he offers himself on the cross; all creatures are subjected to his rule; and then he hands over *aeternum et universale regnum* to the Father’s majesty. The Apostle’s own words stand behind the verb: “Afterwards the end: when he shall have delivered up the kingdom to God and the Father: when he shall have brought to nought all principality and power and virtue. For he must reign, until he hath put all his enemies under his feet”; and “when all things shall be subdued unto him, then the Son also himself shall be subject unto him that put all things under him, that God may be all in all” (1 Cor 15:24–25, 28). Against this the Alleluia sings that his power is *potestas aeterna, quae non auferetur*, an everlasting power that shall not be taken away, and the Communion that *Sedebit Dominus Rex in aeternum*, the Lord shall sit King for ever. Gabriel had said the same to Mary: “of his kingdom there shall be no end” (Lk 1:33). If the Son delivers the kingdom up, does he cease to reign? If he reigns “until” his enemies are under his feet, does his reign stop when they are?

In St Cyril of Jerusalem’s day some answered yes, and he warns his catechumens: “shouldest thou ever hear any say that the kingdom of Christ shall have an end, abhor the heresy; it is another head of the dragon, lately sprung up.” He does not name its author; the English edition’s note identifies the teaching with Marcellus of Ancyra and his disciple Photinus. Against it Cyril sets Gabriel’s word and Daniel’s vision of the Son of man, whose power is everlasting (Dan 7:13–14), and he reads the Apostle’s “until” as Scripture reads the word elsewhere, as setting no term: death reigned from Adam “until” Moses (Rom 5:14), and the veil lies on the reader of Moses “until this day” (2 Cor 3:15), without either ending there. The Son’s subjection, he adds, is no new obedience, but “a self-chosen accordance; for He is not a servant, that He should be subjected by force, but a Son” (*Catechetical Lectures* 15.27–32). St Jerome had made the same point from Daniel: no man could be the one whose power is eternal, *quae nullo fine claudatur*, which no end encloses (*In Dan.* II, on 7:14).

### St Augustine: the faithful brought to contemplation {#sec:handed-augustine}

St Augustine answers the question by asking what the kingdom is. “The words, When He shall have delivered up the kingdom to God, even the Father, are as much as to say, When He shall have brought believers to the contemplation of God, even the Father” (*De Trinitate* I.8.16). The Mediator now reigns over the just, who live by faith; at the end he brings them to sight, “face to face,” and in bringing them there he is said to deliver up the kingdom. “For the Father will be manifested to us, and that will suffice for us,” as Philip asked, “Show us the Father, and it suffices us”; and the heart is made ready for that sight now, for “Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God” (I.8.17). The handing over is therefore not a loss to the Son, because what he hands over is not a possession separate from himself. “His faithful ones, whom He has redeemed with His blood, are called His kingdom, for whom He now intercedes; but then, making them to abide in Himself there, where He is equal to the Father, He will no longer pray the Father for them” (I.10.21).

The subjection of the Son belongs to the same economy. When the kingdom is delivered up, Christ will no longer make intercession for us “as our Mediator and Priest,” and “in so far as He is a Priest that has taken the form of a servant for us, shall be put under Him who has put all things under Him”; in so far as he is God, he with the Father will have put us under him (I.10.20). Augustine reports, without adopting it, the view of those who think the human nature in Christ will be changed into the substance of the Godhead, and answers it by keeping the form of the servant, the Priest, as what is subject (I.8.15; I.10.20).

On the Communion’s psalm Augustine gives the session the same two times. “The Lord inhabiteth the deluge”: “The Lord therefore first inhabiteth the deluge of this world in His Saints, kept safely in the Church, as in the ark. ‘And the Lord shall sit a King for ever.’ And afterward He will sit reigning in them for ever” (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 28.10). The strength of the next verse is given to his people fighting the storms of this world, “for peace in this world He hath not promised them”; the blessing in peace is “affording them peace in Himself” (28.11). The King sits in his saints now, in the flood, and afterwards for ever; his reign in them does not stop when they are delivered up, because he delivers them up by bringing them where he is.

### St Hilary: not his power, but us {#sec:handed-hilary}

St Hilary of Poitiers reaches the same answer by the grammar of the verse. “He shall deliver the Kingdom to God the Father, not in the sense that He resigns His power by the delivering, but that we, being conformed to the glory of His body, shall form the Kingdom of God. It is not said, He shall deliver up His Kingdom, but, He shall deliver up the Kingdom, that is, deliver up to God us who have been made the Kingdom by the glorifying of His body” (*De Trinitate* XI.39). Those he delivers are those he has called into his kingdom, “to whom also He has promised the blessedness of this Mystery, Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God.” Augustine and Hilary, then, both make the handing over the vision of God, and both cite the same beatitude for the purity it requires.

On the subjection that follows, Hilary speaks in terms Augustine does not use. The end of the subjection is “that God may be all in all, that no trace of the nature of His earthly body may remain in Him. Although before this time the two were combined within Him, He must now become God only; not, however, by casting off the body, but by translating it through subjection; not by losing it through dissolutions, but by transfiguring it in glory” (XI.40). Hilary insists that the body is translated and not cast off, while his phrase “God only” goes further than Augustine’s keeping of the servant’s form as what is subject. The Preface uses neither formulation: it says only that the kingdom is handed over and that it is eternal.

Hilary also says, expounding the Offertory’s psalm, that the nations given to the Son as inheritance are the eternal inheritance of the firstborn from the dead (*Tractatus super Psalmos* 2.31); in the *De Trinitate* he says that the kingdom the Son delivers to the Father is “us who have been made the Kingdom” (XI.39). This reading puts the two statements together: what is given to the Son is what the Son gives to the Father, and the Offertory’s inheritance is the kingdom that the Preface has him hand over.

### The Communion: a throne in the cleansed soul, and a throne after the judgment {#sec:handed-communion}

The Communion’s psalm, which the Missal cuts down to the session and the blessing, carries in its full verse the flood that the witnesses read in different ways, and their readings place the session for ever at different moments.

St Basil the Great reads it of baptism. The flood, *kataklysmos*, is a water that effaces all beneath it and cleanses all that was defiled, and it is the grace of baptism that the psalm calls a deluge, so that the soul washed of its sins and purged of the old man is made fit to be God’s dwelling in the Spirit. On “And the Lord shall sit king for ever” he says that God, settled in the soul that shines from that deluge, makes it, as it were, a throne for himself; and the peace of the last verse, the stability of the soul’s governing part, seems to him the most perfect of blessings, the peace the Lord gave his disciples (*Homilia in Psalmum* 28.8; Jn 14:27). Cassiodorus hears the threefold *Dominus* of the two verses as the Trinity dwelling in the waters of baptism, and the *sedebit* as the Son’s (*Expositio Psalmorum* 28). St Thomas Aquinas, among his mystical readings, gives the world and its carnal men brought into God’s temple by conversion, after which *sedebit rex in aeternum*; strength is given for progress and the blessing in peace belongs to the end (*Postilla super Psalmos* 28; Isa 32:18). Theodoret sees the world drowned by the torrent of lawlessness rebuilt as a new creation, in which the Lord will give impiety no place again and will reign for ever.

St Robert Bellarmine, on his second interpretation, places the session after the judgment: when the Lord on that day has discovered the thick woods, “then he will ‘make a flood to dwell,’ inundating the wicked with all manner of evils; and thus, all resistance being broken down, the whole power of demons, bad men, and all power in general being swept away, ‘the Lord shall sit King forever.’ ” The blessing in peace of the last verse is everlasting life (*Explanatio*, Ps 28). His sweeping away of “all power in general” matches in thought the Preface’s *suo subiectis imperio omnibus creaturis* and the Apostle’s bringing to nought “all principality and power,” though Bellarmine does not cite that verse here.

Blessed Ildefonso Schuster, commenting on the antiphon as it stands in this Mass, calls it “a prelude to the final effect of the eucharistic Sacrifice which ends with the divine blessing”; at the moment of Holy Communion “Jesus is enthroned in the faithful heart,” and he is the Blessed through whom, by the promise to Abraham, all nations are blessed (Gen 22:18; p. 204).

The witnesses therefore give the Communion two moments. The session has a beginning now: in the soul cleansed by baptism and made his throne (Basil), in the communicant (Schuster), in the saints amid the flood of this world (Augustine). It has a completion after the judgment, Augustine’s “afterward” and Bellarmine’s sweeping away of every power. Augustine’s session runs continuously from the one into the other; Bellarmine’s begins when the judgment is done.

### How the whole Mass moves toward the Father {#sec:handed-elements}

The **Preface** is the reading’s text: the anointed Priest and King subjects all creatures and hands over *aeternum et universale regnum*, and the kingdom is named by goods that belong to persons, not territory: truth and life, holiness and grace, justice, love and peace. Schuster says that its conception of the Pontiff-King who restores the realm of creation to the Father “that God might eternally be all in all” is “borrowed from St Paul” (p. 204). The **Alleluia**’s *potestas aeterna, quae non auferetur* is the text Cyril of Jerusalem sets against those who said the reign would end. The **Communion** sings the session for ever, begun in the cleansed and fed soul and completed after the judgment, and its blessing in peace is the peace given in himself (Augustine), the blessing of the end (Aquinas), everlasting life (Bellarmine).

The **Epistle** gives the head who goes first and brings the body after: *primogenitus ex mortuis*, through whom the Father reconciles all things “both as to the things that are on earth and the things that are in heaven.” Chrysostom says the things in heaven are reconciled because Christ brought man up to the angels (*Homilies on Colossians* 3), and Theodoret that through his sacrifice he joined the heavenly to the earthly. The **Collect**’s *omnia instaurare* is the summing up of all things in Christ of Eph 1:10, which Chrysostom joins to Col 1:20 and Theodoret to Col 1:16; the Collect asks for its fulfilment among the nations, the work whose completion is the kingdom handed over. The **Introit**’s *in saecula saeculorum* closes the doxology of John’s greeting (Apoc 1:6), which the antiphon sets after the angels’ acclamation of the Lamb; and Schuster reads its verse as the Messianic office conferred on Jesus, who governs “until … he shall have conquered the enemy” and then lays “the sceptre and the crown” at his Father’s feet (pp. 201–202).

The **Gradual**’s homage of all kings and all nations is the universal subjection that the Apostle’s “when all things shall be subdued unto him” completes, given by some now and by all after the resurrection (Theodoret). The **Gospel**’s *regnum meum non est hinc* names a kingdom whose origin is the Father’s, and its end is the Father’s too; the King bears witness to the truth, and the vision of that truth is what Augustine and Hilary make the handing over. The **Offertory**’s inheritance is, for Hilary, the eternal inheritance of the firstborn from the dead; on this reading it is given to the Son to be given by him to the Father. The **Secret** shows the Priest still offering and still giving unity and peace now; Augustine says that when the kingdom is delivered up he will no longer intercede for us as Priest (*De Trinitate* I.10.20). And the **Postcommunion** asks for the faithful, after the food of immortality, a share in the reign that does not end, *in caelesti sede iugiter regnare*.

Literal.

: Daniel sees one like a son of man given an everlasting kingdom; Paul says that the Son, firstborn from the dead, reconciles all things, and elsewhere that he will deliver up the kingdom to the Father; the psalm says that the Lord makes the flood to dwell and sits King for ever, which the witnesses read of God’s judgments from the beginning (Aquinas’s plain sense) or, mystically, of the world, of the Church in the ark, and of baptism.

Allegorical.

: Christ the head brings his body, the Church, to the Father, and the Church is the kingdom handed over (Augustine, Hilary). The flood is baptism, out of which the soul becomes his throne (Basil).

Moral.

: The present life is the purification of heart that the vision requires, “Blessed are the pure in heart,” cited by both Augustine and Hilary; it is lived in the peace he gives in himself rather than the world’s, and the soul cleansed in baptism and fed at the altar is made his throne (Basil; Schuster).

Anagogical.

: God all in all, and the vision face to face: an endless reign of the redeemed with Christ, who is not diminished by giving them to the Father, after the judgment has swept away every power that resisted him (Bellarmine).

## One Kingship Read Three Ways {#sec:comparison}

The three readings answer three questions that the same texts raise, and each lets a different element lead. The first answers from the Son’s humanity: how he who is King by nature receives the kingdom as man. The second answers from the kingdom’s origin: how a kingdom not from here can be everywhere and claim every ruler. The third answers from its end: how a kingdom handed over to the Father can last for ever.

|  | **The Lamb receives the kingdom** | **Not from hence, and therefore everywhere** | **The kingdom handed over** |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Question | How does the Lamb receive what the Son always had? | Where is a kingdom not of this world, and how does it claim kings and nations? | How can a kingdom handed over have no end? |
| Leading texts | Introit, Offertory | Gospel, Gradual, Collect | Preface, Alleluia, Communion |
| Answer | King by nature as God, he receives as man, through the cross, the same dominion: one dominion by two rights | Its origin and means are from above, so it is here in the faithful of every nation; kings serve it freely, in their own office, and all at the judgment | The kingdom handed over is the redeemed, brought to the vision of God; the session begins in the cleansed soul and is completed after the judgment |
| Principal witnesses | Hilary, Aquinas, Bede | Augustine, Chrysostom, Bellarmine | Augustine, Hilary |
| What it asks of the faithful | To ask, and to share the King’s self-offering | To hear the truth and serve him in one’s own office; to fight sin, not with swords | Purity of heart for the vision |
| Strongest objection | Chrysostom and Cyril of Alexandria: nothing received in addition; for Chrysostom every text of giving refers to the generation | That the Gospel’s denial leaves the subjection no public consequence, or that the subjection makes Christ a rival of Caesar | That the reign ends when the kingdom is handed over: the teaching Cyril of Jerusalem opposes, and Jerome answers from Dan 7:14 |
| Differences among its witnesses | On Ps 2:7: Hilary reads the resurrection, Augustine the eternal generation; Theodoret applies the verse to the humanity | On Jn 18:36, the people ruled (Augustine) or the royal power (Chrysostom); on Jn 18:37, the birth from the Virgin (Augustine) or the eternal generation (Chrysostom) | Hilary and Augustine on the Son’s subjection; Augustine and Bellarmine on when he sits |

All three identify the kingdom with the redeemed people, the Church, and none of them lets it become a territory. The cross has a place in each, and a different one. In the first reading it is the means by which the kingdom is received: the asking of the second psalm is the passion, and the Secret’s victim is the same sacrifice offered now. The second reading’s centre is the denial before Pilate and the service of kings; the cross enters it as the standard under which the faithful fight, in Schuster’s note on the Postcommunion, and their warfare is against sin. In the third it is the priestly offering that the Preface sets before the handing over. St Augustine contributes to all three readings: the Psalm 2 exposition to the first, on the decree and the asking, and to the second, on the kings; the tractate on John, the Psalm 71 exposition and *Letter* 185 to the second; the *De Trinitate* and the Psalm 28 exposition to the third. St Hilary also contributes to all three: his tractate on Psalm 2 to the first, on the asking and the inheritance, and to the second, on the kingdom where sin is overcome; his *De Trinitate*, with the same tractate, to the third. St Thomas and St Robert Bellarmine contribute to all three.

The answers differ, and each would mislead on its own. The first reading, taken alone, would mislead if it let the kingship begin at Easter. The second, taken alone, could leave the Preface’s handing over and the Communion’s eternal session as ornaments of a kingdom wholly present in history. The third, taken alone, could pass over the Gospel’s present, mixed kingdom, with its tares, and the service that kings owe now.

The first and second readings differ most over the Gospel, which both read of the same prisoner. For the first, the bound King is acquiring the dominion of the second psalm in the trial itself: he is captive, in Chrysostom’s words, “by consenting, and giving Himself up,” and the kingship of Ps 2:8 is won by the passion that has begun. For the second, the same prisoner declares a kingdom whose power is from above and is “sufficient for itself, needing nothing,” as Chrysostom says, a King as God “whether man so will, or no,” as Cyril of Alexandria says. The first reading speaks of a dominion received, the second of a dominion that needs nothing given. Jn 18:37 is where the two meet. “For this was I born” divides the second reading’s own principal witnesses: Augustine reads it of the birth from the Virgin, Chrysostom of the eternal generation, and Aquinas gives both. The second reading rests on verse 36 and on the hearers of the truth in verse 37, and either birth serves it. For the first reading, Chrysostom’s answer is its strongest objection: born a king, the Son “has nothing which He received in addition.” The first reading grants that premise and refers the receiving to the humanity. Taken with Chrysostom, then, the verse states the first reading’s premise and its objection at once; taken with Augustine, it speaks of the birth in which, on the first reading, the kingship is received. Augustine reads Ps 2:7 of the eternal generation and Jn 18:37 of the birth from the Virgin: the eternal birth grounds the decree of the psalm, and the temporal birth the testimony before Pilate.

Read together, the three readings say what the feast’s Preface says in one sentence: that the anointed Son reigns by right, reigns as the crucified Priest, and reigns towards the Father, in whom his kingdom of truth and life, holiness and grace, justice, love and peace has no end.

### Psalms 2, 28 (29) and 71 (72): the Introit verse, Gradual, Offertory and Communion {#sec:date-psalms}

The four chants drawn from the psalms share two rows. The “Historical setting” row records that no occasion is dated for any of them. Their titles name a king and a moment without placing either in time: Psalm 71 is *in Salomonem*, “on Solomon,” and its last verse closes “the praises of David, the son of Jesse”; Psalm 28 is “for David, at the finishing of the tabernacle”; Psalm 2 opens without a title, and Acts 4:25 ascribes it to David. These attributions belong to the texts, and the dated reign of no king is attached to any of the three psalms here. Their witnesses also read the titles differently: for Bellarmine Psalm 71 is literally David’s prayer for Solomon, while Augustine, Cassiodorus and Theodoret hold that it fits Solomon in no way and speaks of Christ.

The “Composition” row gives the bound that the introduction to the Psalms in the *New American Bible, Revised Edition* sets for the whole Psalter, in the same sentence in which it holds that no individual psalm can be dated securely. It dates the book of Psalms as a whole and is not a date for any of these psalms. The same row names the Tract’s Psalm 88, titled for Ethan the Ezrahite, because the chronology record carries the Tract; the Tract is printed for votive Masses after Septuagesima and is never said on this feast.

The Tract’s own “Historical setting” row dates the destruction of Jerusalem under Sedecias, the setting the record gives to the psalm’s lament for the anointed rejected and the throne cast down (Ps 88:39–45). Its preferred figure is the one Haydock’s Douay commentary prints in a chronological paragraph attributed to Ussher; the alternatives are those of the *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s articles “Captivities of the Israelites” (Reid) and “Temple of Jerusalem” (Meistermann), “Third and Fourth Books of Kings” (Schets), and “Chronology of the Kings” (Sloet, reporting Petavius). They date a setting of a chant that this feast does not sing.

### Daniel 7:14: the Alleluia {#sec:date-daniel}

Daniel dates the vision itself to the first year of Baltasar, king of Babylon (7:1), but the chronology record holds no row for that year. The “Historical setting” row gives instead the seventy years of servitude to Babylon that Jeremias foretold (Jer 25:11–12), as a duration and not as a year. The book presents its author as Daniel, one of the youths of Juda carried to Babylon and taught the learning of the Chaldeans (Dan 1:1–6).

The “Composition” row gives the date that Francis Gigot’s “Book of Daniel” in the *Catholic Encyclopedia* (vol. 4) calls the time-honoured position. Gigot sets beside it what he calls a comparatively recent theory, widely accepted by contemporary scholars, which dates the book in the time of Antiochus IV Epiphanes, and he concludes that Catholic scholars generally abide by the traditional view, although they are not bound to it by any decision of the Church. The traditional answer the row displays excludes the Antiochene date, and the critical answer the record holds offers no dated alternative for the book.

The paschal Alleluia printed for Paschaltide opens with this same verse of Daniel and adds Apoc 19:16, “KING OF KINGS AND LORD OF LORDS.” It is never said on this feast, and the chronology record has no element for it. The Apocalypse composition dates that the Introit’s verses receive below are not extended by the record to Apoc 19:16.

### John 18:33–37: the Gospel {#sec:date-john}

The “Event” row has no date. The chronology record dates the crucifixion and binds that date to the passion narrative from Jn 19:18, but no event or binding reaches the questioning before Pilate in Jn 18:33–37, and none is supplied here. John places the scene in the governor’s hall at Jerusalem, early in the morning, on the day before the Pasch (18:28). The composition dates shown are those of Alfred Durand’s “The New Testament” (vol. 14) and Leopold Fonck’s “Gospel of St. John” (vol. 8) in the *Catholic Encyclopedia*; both are marked disputed. Fonck reports St Irenaeus (*Adversus Haereses* III.1.2) for the tradition that John wrote his Gospel at Ephesus. The dates are dates of the book, not of the trial.

### Colossians 1:12–20: the Epistle {#sec:date-colossians}

The two composition dates are those of Ferdinand Prat’s “St. Paul” (vol. 11), which gives one year to the letters of the captivity, and Cornelius Aherne’s “Epistle to the Colossians” (vol. 4), which gives a range; both are marked disputed. Aherne reports the common view that the captivity letters were written at Rome. The letter itself says that Paul is bound (4:3, 18) and is addressed to the saints at Colossa (1:2): the writer’s place and the recipients’ place are not the same. The passage narrates no event.

### Apocalypse 1:6 and 5:12: the Introit {#sec:date-apocalypse}

The two composition dates are those of Christian Van den Biesen’s “Apocalypse” (vol. 1), which places the book in the reign of Domitian, and of Durand’s “The New Testament”; both are marked disputed. John writes that he was “in the island which is called Patmos” when he saw the visions (1:9), in the same chapter as the Introit’s verse 6. The throne vision of chapters 4–5 is a vision and carries no date of its own.

The sweep is bounded by the corpora named there and is not complete. The Collect, Secret, Preface and Postcommunion were composed for the feast in 1925, and no patristic witness comments on them as texts; their verbal sources are named where they bear on a claim. Blessed Ildefonso Schuster’s *The Sacramentary* V (English, 1930) is the one commentary read on this Mass as a Mass; he comments on its elements one by one, and the one join between two elements he makes himself is the Gradual with the Alleluia. Every other connection drawn between elements of the formulary belongs to the study’s readings unless a witness is named as making it. Bellarmine is read in O’Sullivan’s abridged English of 1866, checked against its facsimile; Cassiodorus and Aquinas in modern web transcriptions of Migne’s, Parma’s and Marietti’s texts; Theodoret in the PG printings, partly on their text layers; Augustine’s *Letter* 185 and Chrysostom’s homilies on John in web transcriptions of the NPNF English. Cassiodorus and Theodoret of Cyrus are ecclesiastical writers, and St Cyril of Alexandria, St Cyril of Jerusalem, the Daniel scholia under Hippolytus’s name and Victorinus of Pettau support particular claims; none of them carries a reading. *Quas primas* is cited as the instituting act of the feast, by number, in the Holy See’s English.

**Chronology.** Every biblical date in the Scriptural Date and Location appendix is generated from the repository’s Scripture chronology corpus, with its sources named there. The corpus reads the formulary from the 1962 calendar finding aid, which stores the votive Tract as an ordinary chant of the day and does not carry the paschal Alleluia; the Tract’s rows are printed with the rubric that limits it, and Apoc 19:16 has no row. The record holds no date for the questioning before Pilate or for the first year of Baltasar.
