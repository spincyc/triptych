## Whose Image Is This? {#sec:opening}

The Gospel of this Sunday is a trap and its undoing. Jesus is teaching in the temple at Jerusalem, and the men he has been answering go away to plan. “Then the Pharisees going, consulted among themselves how to insnare him in his speech. And they sent to him their disciples with the Herodians, saying: Master, we know that thou art a true speaker and teachest the way of God in truth. Neither carest thou for any man: for thou dost not regard the person of men. Tell us therefore what dost thou think? Is it lawful to give tribute to Caesar, or not?” Jesus, “knowing their wickedness”, calls them hypocrites and asks for the coin. “Shew me the coin of the tribute. And they offered him a penny. And Jesus saith to them: Whose image and inscription is this? They say to him: Caesar’s. Then he saith to them: Render therefore to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s; and to God, the things that are God’s” (Mt 22:15–21).

Scripture is quoted throughout in the Douay–Rheims version as revised by Bishop Challoner, a public-domain study translation made from the Latin Vulgate. It is not the text proclaimed at Mass: the English heard in the celebration is that of the *Lectionary for Mass for Use in the Dioceses of the United States of America*, which alone is the approved wording. Psalms are numbered as the Lectionary numbers them, with the Vulgate number, which the Douay–Rheims and the Latin books use, in parentheses; where a psalm verse is quoted from the Douay–Rheims, its Vulgate number is given.

The answer is famous and brief, and it leaves a great deal open. The coin belongs to Caesar because his image is on it; that much the questioners say themselves. But Jesus does not say what “the things that are God’s” are, nor how far Caesar’s claim runs, nor why men who had praised him truthfully should be called hypocrites. The rest of the Mass surrounds the scene with texts that press on those questions from different sides.

The first reading is an oracle addressed to a foreign king. “Thus saith the Lord to my anointed Cyrus, whose right hand I have taken hold of, to subdue nations before his face, and to turn the backs of kings.” The Lord has called the Persian by name for the sake of Israel, “and thou hast not known me”; he has girded him, so that from the rising of the sun to the west all may know “that there is none besides me” (Is 45:1, 4–6). The psalm answers by summoning the nations to bring the Lord the glory due to him, declaring that the gods of the gentiles are nothing beside the Lord who made the heavens, and announcing among them that the Lord reigns and will judge the peoples with justice (Ps 96 (95)). The second reading is the opening of Paul’s first letter to the church of the Thessalonians, a church “in God the Father and in the Lord Jesus Christ”, whose work of faith, labour of charity and enduring hope Paul remembers before God, and to whom the gospel came “not … in word only, but in power also” (1 Thes 1:1–5b). The Alleluia verse, taken from Paul’s letter to the Philippians, speaks of those who shine as lights in the world, holding forth the word of life (Phil 2:15d, 16a).

The Missal’s own texts, which serve this Sunday in all three years of the Lectionary’s cycle, frame the readings with three antiphons and three prayers. The Entrance Antiphon is the cry of one who calls on God because God has heard, and asks to be kept as the apple of the eye and sheltered under the shadow of his wings. The Collect asks that the assembly always hold its will devoted to God and serve his majesty with an undivided heart. The Prayer over the Offerings asks for a free mind in the service of God’s own gifts, and the Prayer after Communion that frequent sharing in heavenly things may help the assembly forward, with temporal benefits as its aid and eternal things as its instruction. At Communion the Missal offers either the psalm verse on the eyes of the Lord that rest on those who fear him and hope in his mercy, or the saying that the Son of Man came to give his life as a ransom for many.

The *Ordo lectionum Missae*, on which the Lectionary rests, marks what it singles out in each reading by the title it sets over it. Over the first reading stands *Apprehendi dexteram Cyri ut subiciam ante faciem eius gentes*, from Isaiah 45:1; over the Gospel, *Reddite quae sunt Caesaris Caesari, et quae sunt Dei Deo*, from Matthew 22:21; over the second reading, *Memores fidei vestrae et caritatis et spei*, from 1 Thessalonians 1:3. The introduction to the *Ordo* explains that the relation between the readings of one Mass is shown by the careful choice of these titles, and that in Ordinary Time the Old Testament reading is chosen for its relation to the Gospel while the Apostle and the Gospel are each read in their own course (*Praenotanda* 105–107). The two titles that belong together each name a gentile ruler: Cyrus, whose right hand the Lord grasps, and Caesar, who is to be given what is his. The second reading opens the Lectionary’s course through the First Letter to the Thessalonians in Year A, and the Gospel continues its course through Matthew.

The Sunday’s question, then, is what is owed, and to whom, and by what kind of giver. The Fathers and Doctors, each expounding his own passage, answer it along three lines, and each line, carried through every text of the Mass, yields an interpretation of the whole.

The first interpretation asks what “the things that are God’s” are. It follows an inference from the coin’s image to the human person, made in the image of God. Augustine introduces that inference as what “some understand skilfully” and then makes it his own; Ambrose, on Luke’s form of the saying, says that Christ bears no image of Caesar because he is the image of God; Hilary of Poitiers reaches the debt of the whole self by another premise, that body, soul and will are owed to God because they come from him; Thomas Aquinas gives a literal sense and keeps the image reading as the mystical one. Jerome reads the second half of the saying literally, of tithes and offerings. The second asks how God’s people can owe anything to a ruler who does not know God, and how far the debt runs. It reads the Gospel beside the oracle to Cyrus, whom Jerome and Aquinas explain as a gentile king called the Lord’s anointed for his royal dignity; it takes from John Chrysostom that tribute is rendered as a debt and ends where godliness would be harmed, and from Augustine the soldiers who served an unbelieving emperor and refused him only in the cause of Christ. Hilary and Ambrose differ from them, grounding the debt in the possession of the world’s goods. The third asks why true words earn the name of hypocrisy. Jerome, Chrysostom, Aquinas and Augustine each locate the fault in the distance between the questioners’ words and their hearts, by different reasoning; Chrysostom, Hilary and Aquinas make Christ’s knowledge of the heart a sign of who he is, while Jerome states the same discernment as a rule for anyone who answers. Each interpretation gives every appointed element a place and closes with four senses of its own, literal, allegorical, moral and anagogical.

## The Appointed Texts {#sec:appointed-texts}

The Missal’s texts for this Sunday are those of *Dominica XXIX “per annum”* in the *Missale Romanum*, editio typica tertia (2002), which the *Roman Missal*, Third Edition, renders for the dioceses of the United States; the readings, psalm and Alleluia verse are those of no. 145 of the *Lectionary for Mass*, which follows the *Ordo lectionum Missae* of 1981. The Latin of the Missal, the approved English of the Missal and the approved English of the Lectionary are all under copyright, and none of them is reproduced here. Each biblical element is given at its canonical verses in the Douay–Rheims version as revised by Bishop Challoner, which translates the Clementine Vulgate and numbers the psalms as the Vulgate does. That English is a study text and is not the wording proclaimed at Mass. Words printed in brackets and italics belong to the verse but not to the appointed text. Each oration is identified by the opening words of its Latin and described, and no English is supplied for it. ### Entrance Antiphon {#proper-entrance-antiphon}

*Ego clamavi, quoniam exaudisti me, Deus*. The Missal cites Psalm 16:6 and 8 in the Vulgate numbering, which is Psalm 17:6 and 8 in the Hebrew, and prints *Cf.*

A prayer in two movements. The speaker has called on God because God hears him, and asks him to bend his ear and hear his words; then he asks to be guarded like the pupil of the eye and protected under the shadow of God’s wings. The antiphon takes v. 6 without the Clementine Vulgate’s *mihi*, passes over v. 7, and gives v. 8 without its opening phrase, *A resistentibus dexterae tuae*, adding the vocative *Domine* after *Custodi me*. It follows the Clementine wording of v. 6, *Ego clamavi, quoniam exaudisti me*, rather than the *Nova Vulgata*’s, and its added vocative stands in neither Latin psalter. No English is printed for the antiphon. The verses it draws on follow.

### Collect {#proper-collect}

*Omnipotens sempiterne Deus, fac nos tibi semper*. Long conclusion.

Addressed to the almighty and eternal God, a petition with two members: that he make the assembly always (*semper*) hold its will devoted to him, and serve his majesty (*maiestati tuae … servire*) with an undivided heart (*sincero corde*). No English is printed.

### Prayer over the Offerings {#proper-prayer-over-offerings}

*Tribue nos, Domine, quaesumus, donis tuis*. Short conclusion.

A petition about the manner of service at the altar: that those who offer may serve the Lord’s own gifts with a free mind (*libera mente servire*), so that his grace, purifying them, may make them clean through the same mysteries in which they minister (*iisdem quibus famulamur mysteriis emundemur*). No English is printed.

## Each Element in Its Setting {#sec:each-element}

Before the interpretations, each text needs its own setting: where it stands in its book, what its words are in the Latin and Greek behind the study translation, and how the liturgical books relate it to the others. The books state four relations. The first reading is chosen for its relation to the Gospel, and the titles over the two show it (*Praenotanda* 106); the psalm responds to the first reading (*General Instruction* 61); the Alleluia verse greets the Gospel (*General Instruction* 62); and the second reading and the Gospel each belong to a course read semi-continuously, independent of the other. The Missal’s antiphons and orations belong to the Twenty-ninth Week in Ordinary Time and serve all three years of the Lectionary’s cycle.

Some words recur across these texts. The prayers ask to serve God and his gifts; the Gospel commands rendering; the psalm bids the nations bring glory and sacrifices. The coin has an image and an inscription, and in the Vulgate of Isaiah the Lord has made a likeness of Cyrus and called him by name. Nations and their rulers appear in the oracle to Cyrus, in the gods of the nations and the Lord who reigns among them, in Caesar, in the crooked generation among whom the faithful shine, and, in the verses around the two Communion antiphons, in the king not saved by his army and the rulers of the gentiles. Cyrus has not known the Lord; the questioners say “we know”; Jesus knows their wickedness; the Lord acts so that all may know. The Collect asks for a sincere heart, the Entrance Antiphon’s psalm speaks of lips without deceit, and the Gospel names hypocrites.

### The Gospel: a question with two horns

Matthew sets the scene in the temple, where the chief priests and the ancients of the people had challenged Jesus’ authority (21:23) and he had answered with parables. After the second, the husbandmen of the vineyard, the chief priests and Pharisees “knew that he spoke of them” and, seeking to lay hands on him, feared the multitudes (21:45–46); the parable of the wedding feast followed (22:1–14). The Pharisees now go aside and take counsel how to catch him in his speech (22:15). They do not come themselves. They send their disciples, and with them the Herodians, whom Jerome takes to be Herod’s soldiers or else those whom the Pharisees mocked for paying the tribute, and whom Chrysostom calls “Herod’s soldiers”.

The question is built to wound on either answer. Aquinas sets out the two opinions behind it: some held that a people dedicated to God ought not to be tributary to a man, others that all owed Caesar tribute because he fought for the peace of all; and so “the question had two members: not to pay pertained to the honour of God, to pay to the favour of men” (*Quaestio habebat duo membra: scilicet quod non solverent, ad honorem Dei pertinebat; quod solverent, ad favorem hominum*; *Super Matthaeum* c. 22, Venice ed., p. 283). Augustine calls it a “double noose”: a yes would make Jesus a maligner of the nation, a no a rebel against Caesar (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 57.8). Chrysostom says the plotters were “digging, as they thought, a precipice on either side” (*Homilies on Matthew* 70.1). The praise that opens the question serves the trap. They call him Master, truthful, a teacher of God’s way, one who regards no man’s person; and in the last two phrases, Chrysostom says, “they were hinting at Herod and Caesar”, urging him toward an answer that would set him against the rulers (70.1).

Jesus calls them hypocrites and asks for “the coin of the tribute”. The Greek names it *to nomisma tou kēnsou*, the coin of the census, and they bring a denarius. He asks whose image and inscription it carries, and they answer, “Caesar’s”. Jerome names the emperor: the Caesar of the saying is Tiberius. The answer turns on a verb. The questioners had asked whether it is lawful to *give* (*dare*) the census; Jesus commands them to *render* (*reddite*, Greek *apodote*), to give back what is owed. Chrysostom builds on the change of verb (70.2), and the second interpretation below follows him. Then comes the second clause, “and to God, the things that are God’s”, which the questioners had not asked about and which the saying does not explain. The reading ends there. The next verse, in which the questioners wonder and leave him, is not read.

The text of these verses is steady. The SBL Greek apparatus records only a variant form of one participle and an omitted pronoun; the Clementine Vulgate and the *Nova Vulgata* differ in a mood, a spelling and the naming of Jesus in v. 20. Nothing in the argument below depends on a variant.

### The first reading: an oracle to a foreign king

Isaiah 45 stands among the chapters of the book that speak to Israel of its release from Babylon (48:20). The verses just before the reading present the Lord as the redeemer who made all things, who confounds diviners and confirms the word of his servants, “Who say to Cyrus: Thou art my shepherd, and thou shalt perform all my pleasure. Who say to Jerusalem: Thou shalt be built: and to the temple: Thy foundations shall be laid” (44:24–28). Chapter 45 opens a new oracle addressed to Cyrus himself. The Lord holds his right hand, subdues nations before him and opens gates (v. 1). The verses the Lectionary leaves out promise that the Lord will go before him, break the gates of brass and give him hidden treasures, “that thou mayest know that I am the Lord who call thee by thy name, the God of Israel” (vv. 2–3). The reading resumes with the reason: for the sake of Jacob his servant the Lord has called Cyrus by name, though Cyrus has not known him (v. 4); there is no God besides the Lord, who girded Cyrus though Cyrus did not know him (v. 5); and the purpose is that all, from the rising of the sun to its setting, may know that there is none besides him (v. 6). The verses that follow, unread, go further: the Lord forms light and creates darkness, makes peace and creates evil (v. 7); he has raised Cyrus up “to justice”, and Cyrus “shall build my city, and let go my captives, not for ransom, nor for presents” (v. 13).

Two words of v. 1 and v. 4 have a history. In v. 1 the Hebrew calls Cyrus the Lord’s anointed. The Clementine Vulgate renders the title with *christo*, a transliteration of the Greek, and the *Ordo*’s Latin with *uncto*; the sense is the same. An early reading, preserved in Latin and Greek testimonies, took the Greek name of Cyrus (*Kyrō*) as the word for “Lord” (*Kyriō*) and read the verse as said “to Christ my Lord”; the second interpretation below weighs it. In v. 4 the Clementine’s *assimilavi te*, “I have made a likeness of thee”, is not the Hebrew’s sense, which the King James Version gives as “I have surnamed thee”; Brenton’s English of the Septuagint reads “and accept thee”. The Lectionary’s English follows the Hebrew. A Father who reads *assimilavi* is reading the Vulgate’s word.

The *Ordo*’s title for the reading, *Apprehendi dexteram Cyri ut subiciam ante faciem eius gentes*, condenses v. 1, putting the name of Cyrus where the verse has a relative pronoun: what the Lectionary singles out is the Lord’s grasp of a gentile ruler’s hand.

### The Responsorial Psalm: the nations brought to the Lord

Psalm 96 (95) summons all the earth to a new song. It moves from the command to sing and to declare the Lord’s glory among the nations (vv. 1–3), through the reason, that the Lord is great and to be feared above all gods, because the gods of the nations are nothing and the Lord made the heavens (vv. 4–5), to the summons of the families of the peoples to bring him glory and sacrifices and to enter his courts (vv. 7–8), and to the proclamation among the nations that the Lord reigns and will judge the peoples with justice (vv. 9–10). Its close, not sung, calls heaven, earth, sea and trees to rejoice before the Lord who comes to judge the world (vv. 11–13). Under the *General Instruction* the psalm responds to the first reading; read beside it, it sets the nations’ recognition that their gods are nothing after the Lord’s “there is none besides me” (Is 45:5–6).

The Vulgate and Greek title, which the Hebrew psalm lacks, sets it “when the house was built after the captivity”, the rebuilding that Isaiah 44:28 and 45:13 promise through Cyrus. Augustine reads the house as the whole earth built up of the nations: “all the earth is the house of God”, and “the very act of singing is building” (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 95.1–2). In v. 10 Augustine’s psalter read “the Lord hath reigned from the wood”, an Old Latin addition that neither the Clementine nor the Lectionary’s Latin reads, so that what he says of it concerns his own text and not the appointed verse. In v. 5 the Latin and Greek call the gods of the nations demons, as the Douay–Rheims does, where the Hebrew calls them idols; Augustine and Bellarmine expound the first form.

### The Second Reading: a church in God

The narrative of Acts 17:1–10 sets the scene of the church Paul is addressing. Paul had reasoned from the scriptures in the synagogue of Thessalonica on three sabbath days; some believed and joined Paul and Silas, “and of those that served God and of the Gentiles a great multitude”; a tumult was raised, and Jason and certain brethren were drawn before the city’s rulers with the charge that “these all do contrary to the decrees of Caesar, saying that there is another king, Jesus” (Acts 17:7), and Paul and Silas were sent away by night. Acts is not appointed on this Sunday; it is the narrative behind the letter.

The reading is the letter’s greeting and opening thanksgiving. Paul, with Silvanus and Timothy, writes “to the church of the Thessalonians: in God the Father and in the Lord Jesus Christ”. Chrysostom notes that Paul gives himself no title here because his readers were newly instructed, and he reads “in God” as a demand: “If any one walks not according to God, he cannot be said to be ‘in God’ ” (*Homilies on First Thessalonians* 1). Aquinas reads the same phrase as the faith of the Trinity, *in fide Trinitatis*. Paul remembers their work of faith, labour of charity and enduring hope; the *Ordo*’s title condenses this to *Memores fidei vestrae et caritatis et spei*. Aquinas says that faith “is not sufficient unless it has work and labour” (*non est sufficiens, nisi habeat operationem et laborem*) and that this hope is “before God, not before the eyes of men” (*Haec spes est ante Deum, non ante oculos hominum*). Chrysostom draws the labour of charity from the city’s persecution: “Merely to love is no labor at all. But to love genuinely is great labor”. Paul knows their election because the gospel came to them “not … in word only, but in power also: and in the Holy Ghost and in much fulness”. Aquinas explains how he knows: God gave him a great sign in the preaching, when he gives hearers the grace to hear the word with fruit, and the preaching itself “was not in loftiness of speech, but in power” (*non fuit in sublimitate sermonis, sed in virtute*; *Super I ad Thessalonicenses* c. 1, lect. 1).

The reading ends with the fullness of the gospel’s coming. The clause that follows, on what manner of men the missionaries were, is not read. The letter is read on this Sunday because its course begins here; the Gospel’s course follows Matthew, and the two are independent.

### The Alleluia Verse: lights in a crooked generation

The verse comes from Paul’s exhortation in Philippians 2:12–18. The Philippians are to work out their salvation with fear and trembling, since God works in them both to will and to accomplish (vv. 12–13); to do all things without murmuring and hesitation (v. 14), “That you may be blameless and sincere children of God, without reproof, in the midst of a crooked and perverse generation: among whom you shine as lights in the world. Holding forth the word of life” (vv. 15–16). The Latin word behind “sincere” is *simplices*, single. The verse the Lectionary sets before the Gospel takes only the shining and the word of life.

Chrysostom hears the image as a comparison with the stars: “For the stars too give light in the night, they shine in the dark, and receive no blemish to their own beauty, yea they even shine the brighter … Thus thou too dost appear with the greater lustre, whilst thou holdest straight in the midst of the crooked” (*Homilies on Philippians* 8). Aquinas observes that however the world changes, the heavenly lights stay bright, and that the faithful are light not by their own essence, for only God is light in that way, but “inasmuch as they have something of the light of him who was the light of men” (*sunt lux inquantum habent aliquid lucis illius qui erat lux hominum*; *Super ad Philippenses* c. 2, lect. 4, Dessain ed., p. 383). Under the *General Instruction* the verse greets the Gospel; what it says of the Gospel is drawn by the interpretations below.

### The Entrance Antiphon: the apple of the eye

Psalm 17 (16), titled in the Latin “The prayer of David”, is the plea of a man who asks God to hear a just cause, “my prayer, which proceedeth not from deceitful lips” (v. 1), and who says, “Thou hast proved my heart, and visited it by night” (v. 3). The antiphon begins only at v. 6, with the cry of one whom God has heard, and takes from v. 8 the request to be kept as the apple of the eye and sheltered under God’s wings. It passes over v. 7 and the opening words of v. 8, which ask protection “From them that resist thy right hand”.

Augustine assigns the prayer “to the Person of the Lord, with the addition of the Church, which is His body” (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 16.1). On v. 6 he hears the confidence of one already answered: “With a free and strong effort have I directed My prayers unto Thee: for that I might have this power, Thou hast heard Me when praying more weakly”. The apple of the eye, he says, “seems very little and minute: yet by it is the sight of the eye directed, whereby the light is distinguished from the darkness”, and the wings are “the defence of Thy love and mercy” (16.6, 8). Aquinas explains that the pupil is guarded with diligence because nothing that could harm it is allowed near, and reports the Gloss, which calls the pupil Christ, who discerns the faithful from the unfaithful (*Postilla super Psalmos* 16). Bellarmine calls the apple of the eye “a most delicate, though valuable article”, and those who pray “frail and delicate”, needing the same care. The antiphon is a prayer of trust in God’s protection; its psalm’s surrounding verses, unsung, concern sincerity of lips and a heart that God has tried.

### The Collect

The Collect is a single petition in two members, the will and the service. It asks that the assembly always hold its will devoted to God, and that it serve his majesty with a sincere heart. Its Latin stands word for word in the Old Gelasian sacramentary as H. A. Wilson edited it in 1894, where it is the first prayer of the Mass headed *Quinta Dominica post clausum Paschae*. It recurs in the third book of the same sacramentary as the second prayer of an *Item alia Missa* (III.ix), word for word in Wilson’s text; his apparatus records that the Vatican manuscript reads *maiestatem tuam* there.

### The Prayer over the Offerings

The Prayer over the Offerings asks that those who offer serve God’s own gifts with a free mind, and that his grace, purifying them, make them clean through the mysteries they serve. The gifts are called God’s (*donis tuis*) before they are offered. The prayer stands in the Verona sacramentary as C. L. Feltoe edited it in 1896, among its Masses for April, with a single difference of word order in the final clause (*purificante nos gratia tua* for the Missal’s *tua purificante nos gratia*).

### The Communion Antiphons

The first antiphon takes two verses from the middle of Psalm 33 (32). Before them, the Lord looks from heaven on all the sons of men, “He who hath made the hearts of every one of them: who understandeth all their works” (vv. 13–15), and “The king is not saved by a great army: nor shall the giant be saved by his own great strength. Vain is the horse for safety” (vv. 16–17). Then: the eyes of the Lord are on those who fear him and hope in his mercy, to deliver them from death and feed them in famine (vv. 18–19). Augustine reads the king not saved by strength as anyone “who ruleth his own flesh, if he presume much upon his own strength”, and the eyes of the Lord as his love: “if thou seek salvation, behold, the love of the Lord is upon them that fear Him”; the food in famine is “the nourishment of the Word, and of Everlasting Truth” (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 32.16, 18–19). Bellarmine joins the two dispositions the verse names: “For fear, without hope, is servile fear; hope, without fear, is presumption. Fear, combined with hope, is the mark of real love.” Aquinas says the same in a sentence, *timor sine spe desperat, et spes sine timore praesumit* (*Postilla* 32).

The second antiphon is the last clause of Mark 10:45. Its context is the request of James and John for the places at Jesus’ right and left, the indignation of the ten, and his answer: “You know that they who seem to rule over the Gentiles lord it over them: and their princes have power over them. But it is not so among you” (10:42–43); whoever would be first must be the servant of all, “For the Son of man also is not come to be ministered unto: but to minister and to give his life a redemption for many” (10:44–45). The antiphon keeps only the giving of the life. Bede reads the passage as the example of a humble and gentle teacher, and the verse of the one who ministered and gave his life as a ransom, “when he took the form of a servant, that he might pour out his blood for the world” (*quando formam servi accepit, ut pro mundo sanguinem funderet*); he restricts “many” to “those who willed to believe” (*pro eis qui credere voluerunt*; *In Marci evangelium expositio* III, PL 92, col. 236). Chrysostom, on Matthew’s form of the same saying, says that the ransom was given “for enemies” (*Homilies on Matthew* 65.4). Bede speaks of those who receive the ransom by faith, Chrysostom of what they were when it was paid.

The Missal’s rubric for Ordinary Time lets either antiphon be chosen and prefers one that agrees with the Gospel of the Mass. Neither is drawn from Matthew 22:15–21, and the books do not say whether either agrees with it.

### The Prayer after Communion

The Prayer after Communion asks that frequent sharing in heavenly things make the assembly advance, aided by temporal benefits and instructed by eternal ones. Its second half, from *caelestium rerum frequentatione* to the eternal things, stands in a prayer of the Verona sacramentary in the third person, for a people that asks to rejoice in God’s blessing; its opening words are not there.

## Render to God the One Who Bears His Image: The Coin, Its Image and the Self Owed to God {#lane-image-and-inscription}

<span id="sec:image-and-inscription" data-label="sec:image-and-inscription"></span>

Read from its last sentence, the Gospel asks a question it does not answer aloud. Jesus makes his questioners show the coin and name the image on it, and from their answer he concludes that the coin is Caesar’s. He then says that God too is to be given what is his, and stops. This interpretation follows the inference several Fathers draw from that silence: as the coin bears Caesar’s image and so belongs to Caesar, the human person bears God’s image and so belongs to God. Augustine draws the inference from the coin to the soul made in God’s image; Ambrose, on Luke’s form of the saying, says that Christ bears no image of Caesar because he is the image of God; Hilary of Poitiers argues from a different premise, that body, soul and will are owed to God because they come from him; Aquinas keeps a literal reading and gives the image reading as the mystical sense. Carried through the whole Mass, the inference makes “the things that are God’s” not a second tax set beside Caesar’s but the self, and the Mass’s prayers ask God to receive it as a devoted will and an undivided heart.

### What the coin settles, and what it leaves open

The questioners asked about one debt, the census owed to Caesar, and the answer speaks of two. The first is settled by the coin itself. Jesus does not tell them whose the denarius is; he asks, and they say. The coin carries an image and an inscription, and both name its owner. Whatever Jesus may think of the census, the questioners already carry Caesar’s coin and use it, and the coin declares whose it is.

The second debt is not settled by anything in the scene. “To God, the things that are God’s” names no object. A reader who wants to know what is owed to God must supply it, and here is the difficulty on which this interpretation turns: Jesus never says “the image of God”. Nothing in Matthew’s words moves from the face on the coin to the human face. The step is an inference, and it depends on another text, the account of man’s creation: “Let us make man to our image and likeness” (Gn 1:26). The question for this interpretation is whether that inference is warranted, and what follows if it is.

### Augustine: God looks for his image in man

Augustine takes the step in at least five places and states its logic each time. In his exposition of Psalm 4 he introduces it carefully. The psalm says that the light of God’s countenance is “stamped on us”, and Augustine explains, “as a penny is stamped with the king’s image. For man was made after the image and likeness of God, which he defaced by sin: therefore it is his true and eternal good, if by a new birth he be stamped.” Then he adds: “And I believe this to be the bearing of that which some understand skilfully; I mean, what the Lord said on seeing Caesar’s tribute money … As if He had said, In like manner as Caesar exacts from you the impression of his image, so also does God: that as the tribute money is rendered to him, so should the soul to God, illumined and stamped with the light of His countenance” (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 4.8). Three things are visible in that passage. The inference is not presented as his discovery; “some” understand it so, and he endorses them. It rests on Genesis, on man’s making after God’s image. And it does not stop at the image as given: the image was defaced by sin, and the good of man is to be stamped again “by a new birth”.

In his tractates on John the same inference becomes a sentence: “For as Caesar looks for his own image on the coin, so God looks for His in man” (*In Iohannis evangelium tractatus* 41.2). In the tractate before it he develops the defacement into a parable of his own. “We are God’s money: we have wandered away as coin from the treasury. The impression that was stamped upon us has been rubbed out by our wandering. He has come to refashion, for He it was that fashioned us at first; and He is Himself asking for His money, as Caesar for his. Therefore He says, ‘Render unto Caesar the things that are Caesar’s, and unto God the things that are God’s:’ to Caesar his money, to God yourselves. And then shall the truth be reproduced in us” (40.9). Here the coin has a history. It was minted in God’s treasury, it strayed, its stamp wore smooth, and the one who struck it has come to strike it again. What God asks is the coin itself, “yourselves”, and he asks it as the one who can restore what he asks for.

Twice more Augustine turns the inference into a command. In the exposition of Psalm 57: “Caesar seeketh his image; render it: God seeketh His image; render it. Let not Caesar lose from you his coin: let not God lose in you His coin” (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 57.8). And on the psalm verse “I will pay my vows to the Lord”, he asks what vows a man should pay, and answers: “let him vow himself, let him pay himself as a vow: this is exacted, this is due. On looking at the coin, the Lord saith, ‘Render unto Caesar the things which are Caesar’s, and unto God the things which are God’s:’ his own image is rendered unto Caesar: let His image be rendered unto God” (115.13). The person is not asked to give God something of his own; he is asked to give himself back, as a debt.

### Ambrose: the image Christ does not bear

Ambrose reads the saying in Luke’s Gospel, where it stands in the same exchange, and gives it a centre that Augustine’s passages imply but do not state. Asked for the coin, he says, the Lord asks about the image, “for the image of God is one thing, the image of the world another” (*alia enim imago Dei, alia imago mundi*), and he cites Paul: “as we have borne the image of the earthly, let us bear also the image of the heavenly” (1 Cor 15:49). Then he applies it to Christ himself: *Imaginem Caesaris non habet Christus, quia imago est Dei*, “Christ does not have Caesar’s image, because he is the image of God”; nor does Peter, who left all to follow him (*Expositio evangelii secundum Lucam* IX.34–35). Why, then, did Christ pay the census? *Non de suo dedit, sed reddidit mundo quod erat mundi*: “he did not give of his own, but returned to the world what was the world’s”.

Ambrose’s argument moves the image inference from the believer to its source. Christ is the image of God in person, and so he owes Caesar nothing of his own; the believer bears the image of the heavenly to the extent that he has left what is the world’s. Ambrose ends the passage with the believer’s renunciation. “It is well that he decrees first that the things that are Caesar’s be rendered to Caesar: for no one can be the Lord’s unless he has first renounced the world” (*neque enim potest esse quis Domini, nisi prius renuntiaverit mundo*). All renounce it in words when they receive the sacraments, he says, but not all in affection, and then: *Maior est contractus fidei, quam pecuniae*, “the contract of faith is greater than the contract of money” (IX.36). What is owed to God is a promise made at the font, to be paid “while you are in this body” (*Redde promissum, dum in hoc corpore es*).

### Hilary: owed to the one from whom it comes

Hilary of Poitiers, commenting on Matthew, reaches the same conclusion by another road. He marvels at the answer: *O plenam miraculi responsionem*, “O answer full of wonder”. It so balances the contempt of the world against any offence to Caesar that it frees minds devoted to God (*devotas Deo mentes*) from all human cares while decreeing that Caesar be given what is his. And what is God’s own? *Deo autem quae eius sunt propria reddere nos oportere, corpus, animam, voluntatem*: we must render to God what is properly his, “body, soul, will”. His reason is not the image: *Ab eo enim haec profecta atque aucta retinemus: et proinde condignum est, ut ei se totum reddant, cui debere se recolunt et originem et profectum*, “for we hold these as having come from him and been increased by him; and so it is fitting that men render themselves whole to him to whom they recall that they owe both their origin and their growth” (*Commentarius in Matthaeum* 23.2, PL 9, col. 1045).

Hilary’s premise is origin and increase; Augustine’s is the image stamped and restamped. The two arguments arrive at the same object, the whole self, from different starting points. For Augustine the coin’s image is the hinge; for Hilary the coin is Caesar’s because it is in use among those who handle Caesar’s goods, and the self is God’s because it came from God. His list also names something Augustine’s sentences do not: the will.

### Aquinas: the literal and the mystical sense

Aquinas, commenting on Matthew, gives the saying two senses and holds them together. Literally, he says, men have natural riches from God, such as bread and wine, and of these they should give to God; they have artificial things, such as denarii, from Caesar, and these they should render to Caesar (*Habetis divitias naturales a Deo, scilicet panem, et vinum; et de his date Deo. Habetis ista artificialia, ut denarios, a Caesare; et haec Caesari reddite*). Then: *Mystice sic. Nos habemus animam quae est ad imaginem Dei, ideo eam Deo reddere debemus*, “Mystically thus: we have a soul which is to the image of God, and therefore we owe it to God” (*Super Matthaeum* c. 22, Venice ed., p. 284). In Aquinas the two readings do not compete. The literal sense gives God natural goods; the mystical sense gives him the soul that bears his image.

### The strongest alternative: Jerome’s tithes and offerings

Jerome reads the second half of the saying literally. “Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s, that is, coin, tribute and money; and to God the things that are God’s: let us understand tithes, first-fruits, oblations and victims” (*Reddite quae sunt Caesaris Caesari, id est, nummum tributum, et pecuniam: et quae sunt Dei Deo, decimas primitias, et oblationes, ac victimas sentiamus*). He adds that Christ himself did both: he paid the tribute for himself and for Peter, and he rendered to God the things that are God’s “doing the will of the Father” (*Patris faciens voluntatem*; *Commentarii in Matthaeum* III, PL 26, col. 163).

On Jerome’s reading the image belongs to the coin alone. The saying sets the dues of the temple beside the dues of the empire, and it is a ruling on two obligations, each with its own object. That is the plain sense of the words. The image reading is the Fathers’ spiritual reading of the coin, resting on Genesis and not on anything Jesus says, and Augustine himself credits it to “some”. Aquinas, as above, keeps a literal sense of his own beneath it.

Jerome’s last clause, however, comes close to the others by another route. Christ rendered to God “doing the will of the Father”; Hilary names the will among the things owed to God. On that point the literal reader and the reader of origins meet without the image: what God is owed from Christ, and from those who follow him, includes the will. Cyril of Alexandria, preaching on Luke’s form of the saying, says something similar: those who govern impose a tribute of money on their subjects, “but God requires of us of things corruptible and transitory even nothing, but rather willing obedience and submission; faith and love; and the sweet savour of good works” (*Commentary on Luke*, Sermon 135).

### The Missal’s texts in this reading

Read beside the Gospel, the Collect asks for exactly the will that Hilary names among the things owed to God, and that Jerome says Christ rendered in doing the Father’s will. It asks that the assembly always hold its will devoted to God and serve his majesty *sincero corde*. On this reading the Gospel’s last words become the measure of the Collect’s petition.

The Entrance Antiphon gives the self that is rendered the posture of trust. The one who prays asks to be kept “as the apple of thy eye”, the small part by which, in Augustine’s explanation, the eye tells light from darkness (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 16.8); Aquinas reports the Gloss that reads the pupil as Christ, who discerns the faithful from the unfaithful. On this reading the person who is God’s coin asks to be guarded as God guards what is most his own.

The first reading contributes least. In the Clementine Vulgate the Lord says to Cyrus, *vocavi te nomine tuo: assimilavi te*, “I have called thee by thy name: I have made a likeness of thee”. Jerome comments that Cyrus was called by name lest he be thought to be the Christ himself, “to whom you were likened, and of whom you went before as a type and image” (*cui assimilatus es, et in cuius typum et imaginem praecessisti*; *Commentarii in Isaiam* XII, PL 24, col. 457). A likeness given by God and a name given by God would frame the Gospel’s image and inscription. But the frame rests on the Vulgate’s word and on Jerome alone. The Lectionary’s English, following the Hebrew, does not speak of a likeness, and Aquinas reads *assimilavi* of the other saints whose names were foretold, without a typology.

The psalm says what the self owes when it enters God’s courts. “Bring ye to the Lord, O ye kindreds of the Gentiles, bring ye to the Lord glory and honour” (Ps 95:7). Augustine says: “Ascribe them not unto yourselves: because they also who have declared it unto you, have not declared their own, but His honour.” On the courts of v. 8: “Confession is a present unto God. O heathen, if ye will enter into His courts, enter not empty … Enter with an humble heart into the house of God, and thou hast entered with a present” (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 95.8–9). The gift the nations bring is themselves, humbled.

The second reading describes what belonging to God looks like when it is lived. The church of the Thessalonians is “in God the Father and in the Lord Jesus Christ”, a phrase Chrysostom takes as a description of how one walks (*Homilies on First Thessalonians* 1). In this reading the church’s work of faith, labour of charity and enduring hope are the self rendered in deeds.

The Alleluia verse adds a second figure of borrowed likeness. The faithful shine as lights, and Aquinas says that they are light only by having something of the light of the one who was the light of men (*Super ad Philippenses* c. 2, lect. 4). Set beside the coin, that light behaves like an image: what shines in them is another’s.

The Prayer over the Offerings calls the gifts on the altar God’s own (*donis tuis*) before they are offered, and asks that they be served with a free mind. Beside it, Jerome’s oblations and Aquinas’s bread and wine given to God become the literal counterpart of this reading: what is rendered at the altar was God’s before it was offered, as the self is.

The first Communion antiphon says that the eyes of the Lord rest on those who fear him. Augustine’s God looks for his image in man (*Tractatus* 41.2); the antiphon names the God who looks. Where the second antiphon is sung, it names the one who rendered himself entirely: the Son of Man came to give his life as a ransom. Ambrose’s Christ owes Caesar nothing of his own because he is the image of God; in this antiphon the same Christ gives his life. Bede reads the verse of the one who took the form of a servant to pour out his blood for the world.

The Prayer after Communion asks that the assembly be aided by temporal benefits and instructed by eternal ones. Read in this interpretation, the coin belongs to the first kind and the self to the second: temporal goods help, but the person is formed by what is eternal. Aquinas’s two senses fall on either side of that line, natural goods given to God and the soul that bears his image.

### Where this interpretation strains

The interpretation is a spiritual reading, and its strain is the distance between the saying and the inference. Jesus speaks of a coin and of God; he does not speak of man as God’s coin. The Fathers who draw the inference supply Genesis, and Augustine supplies a history of defacement and restamping that Matthew does not narrate. A reader persuaded by Jerome will say that the saying concerns two sets of dues and nothing more. The answer is not to deny the literal sense, which Aquinas holds beside the mystical one, but to observe that the literal sense leaves “the things that are God’s” open, and that the Fathers who fill the phrase with the self have a reason in Scripture for doing so.

### The four senses of this interpretation

Literal.

: Asked whether tribute is lawful, Jesus makes his questioners show the census coin and name the image and inscription it bears, and commands that Caesar be repaid what is Caesar’s and God what is God’s (Mt 22:19–21). On the literal reading the Fathers differ about the second object: tithes, first-fruits, oblations and victims (Jerome), or body, soul and will (Hilary).

Allegorical.

: Christ is the image of God, who bears no image of Caesar (Ambrose), come to restamp the coin that wandering had worn smooth (Augustine, *Tractatus* 40.9); where the second Communion antiphon is sung, he is the Son of Man who gives his life as a ransom.

Moral.

: Render yourself: not a portion beside the tax, but the will devoted and the heart undivided (the Collect), the body, soul and will owed to their origin (Hilary), the person paying himself as a vow (Augustine on Psalm 115), entering God’s courts with a humble heart (Augustine on Psalm 95).

Anagogical.

: The image restored is seen at the end. Augustine joins the saying to the promise that “we shall be like Him … for we shall see Him as He is” (1 Jn 3:2) and says that “then shall the truth be reproduced in us” (*Tractatus* 40.9); the soul is rendered to God “stamped with the light of His countenance” (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 4.8).

## The Lord Who Grasps the Hand of Kings: Cyrus, Caesar and the Limit of the Debt {#lane-cyrus-and-caesar}

<span id="sec:cyrus-and-caesar" data-label="sec:cyrus-and-caesar"></span>

The *Ordo* sets two titles side by side: the Lord grasps the right hand of Cyrus to subdue nations before him, and Caesar is to be given what is Caesar’s. This interpretation reads the Gospel through that pairing, the one relation between the readings that the liturgical books themselves declare. Jerome and Aquinas explain why a gentile king who does not know the Lord is called his anointed, and what the Lord required of his power; John Chrysostom says that tribute is rendered as a debt and that the debt ends where godliness would be harmed; Augustine shows Christian soldiers who served an unbelieving emperor and refused him only in the cause of Christ; Aquinas says that the hearers are at once God’s and Caesar’s. Read together under the Lectionary’s pairing, the two passages show one sovereignty: the Lord who took Cyrus by the hand, though Cyrus did not know him, is the God in whose world Caesar’s coin circulates; tribute is therefore a real debt, and the same sovereignty bounds it.

### A gentile king called the Lord’s anointed

The first reading’s opening words raise the difficulty. The Lord speaks “to my anointed Cyrus”, and twice in three verses he adds, “thou hast not known me”. The title that Israel gave its kings is given to a Persian who worships other gods. How can a man be the Lord’s anointed and not know the Lord?

Jerome answers from what the title meant. *Iste appellatus est christus, id est, unctus Domini, quod erat insigne apud Hebraeos regiae potestatis, ut quomodo apud nos diadema et purpura solis imperatoribus datur: sic apud Hebraeos regnaturi perfundebantur unguento*: “this man is called *christus*, that is, anointed of the Lord, which was the mark of royal power among the Hebrews; as among us the diadem and the purple are given to emperors alone, so among the Hebrews those who were to reign were poured over with ointment” (*Commentarii in Isaiam* XII, PL 24, col. 457). Saul too was called the Lord’s anointed. The title names an office, not a faith. Aquinas says the same in fewer words: *Christo, idest regi … propter regiam dignitatem*, “to Christ, that is, to the king … because of his royal dignity” (*Expositio super Isaiam* c. 45, Parma ed., p. 539).

Both then ask what the Lord wanted of this king’s power. Jerome reads the omitted v. 3, the hidden treasures, as given “that you who formerly worshipped idols might perceive the one God through his benefits” (*ut qui prius colebas idola, beneficiis unum sentires Deum*); “thou hast not known me” means *simulacra coluisti, non Deum*, “you worshipped images, not God”. He reports Josephus’s account that Cyrus read the prophecy written about him, and on v. 6 he says that Cyrus wrote to all the nations that there is no God but the God of Israel, or else that the captives’ release made God known to all (cols. 457–458). Aquinas states the purpose as a fruit the Lord required: the fruit of the gift of power in Cyrus himself was the knowledge of God, *suae cognitionis*; the fruit in others, that all from east to west should know there is no God besides him, *Quod completum fuit in hoc quod Cyrus epistolis suis laudem Dei scripsit ubique; vel quia omnes Deum collaudaverunt videntes Iudaeorum liberationem*, “which was fulfilled in that Cyrus wrote the praise of God everywhere in his letters, or because all praised God seeing the liberation of the Jews” (p. 539). John Chrysostom, explaining how the Magi were moved to seek the child, adds a qualification about how God moves such a king. God moved their soul, “which same kind of thing He did also in the case of Cyrus, disposing him to let the Jews go. He did not however so do this as to destroy their free will” (*Homilies on Matthew* 6).

What the first reading shows, on these readings, is a God who uses the power of those who do not know him. He grasps the king’s hand, gives him victories and treasures, and does it for the sake of Jacob his servant and so that the nations may know that there is no other God. The king’s power is real and given, and its purpose is not the king’s.

### “To Christ my Lord”: a reading Jerome rejects

The verse has a second history. Some early Latin and Greek readers took it as spoken to the Son. Cyprian’s collection of testimonies cites it, among the proofs that the gentiles would believe in Christ, as “Thus saith the Lord God to Christ my Lord, whose right hand I hold, that the nations may hear Him” (*Ad Quirinum* I.21); Novatian cites “Thus saith the Lord to Christ my Lord” among proofs that the Son is a second person (*De Trinitate* 26); and the *Epistle of Barnabas* reads “The Lord said to Christ, my Lord, whose right hand I have holden” (12). The editor of Novatian’s English notes the source of the reading: a copyist wrote the Greek word for “Lord”, *Kyriō*, where the verse had the name of Cyrus, *Kyrō*.

Jerome names the error and refuses it. *Scio in hoc capitulo non solum Latinorum, sed Graecorum plurimos vehementer errare, existimantium scriptum esse: Sic dicit Dominus Christo meo, Domino*: “I know that in this chapter very many, not only of the Latins but of the Greeks, err greatly, thinking it written, Thus saith the Lord to my Christ, the Lord”. It is said not *Kyriō* but to Cyrus, king of the Persians. And he will not let “thou hast not known me” be said of Christ: *In quo loco satis mirari nequeo, quae stultitia sit legentium, ut haec ad Christum referant, per quem mundus reconciliatus est Deo*, “I cannot wonder enough at the folly of readers who refer these things to Christ, through whom the world was reconciled to God” (col. 457). The Lectionary’s Latin, *de uncto meo Cyro*, names Cyrus and excludes the variant.

A narrower difference remains. Jerome, having refused the variant, still reads the Vulgate’s *assimilavi te* of Cyrus as going before “as a type and image” of the Christ to whom he was likened. Aquinas reads the same word of the other saints whose names the Lord foretold (*assimilavi, hoc in aliis sanctis meis, quorum nomina prius a Domino praedicta sunt*) and sees no type. The Lectionary’s English, following the Hebrew, has no likeness at all. On the Lectionary’s words Cyrus is the Lord’s instrument; the figure of Christ is Jerome’s, drawn from the Vulgate’s word.

### Render, not give

If the Lord’s sovereignty reaches a pagan king, what follows for the coin in the questioners’ hands? Chrysostom answers from the verb. The questioners had asked whether it was lawful to *give* tribute; Jesus told them to *render* it. “For this is not to give but to render, and this He shows both by the image, and by the superscription” (*Homilies on Matthew* 70.2). The coin bears Caesar’s image and name; it is already his, and paying it is returning what is owed, not making a gift. Chrysostom then reads the second clause as protecting the first from misunderstanding: “Then that they might not say, Thou art subjecting us to men, He added, ‘And unto God the things that are God’s.’ For it is possible both to fulfill to men their claims and to give unto God the things that are due to God from us.” He cites Paul, from a chapter this Mass does not read: “Render therefore to all men their dues. Tribute, to whom tribute is due” (Rom 13:7).

In his homily on that chapter of Romans Chrysostom makes the same point from the other side: Paul wrote “to show that it was not for the subversion of the commonwealth that Christ introduced His laws, but for the better ordering of it”, and “this subjection is not subversive of religion” (*Homilies on Romans* 23). Justin Martyr had told the emperors that Christians pay “the taxes both ordinary and extraordinary, as we have been taught by Him”, quoting this saying, and then: “Whence to God alone we render worship, but in other things we gladly serve you” (*First Apology* 17).

Aquinas gives the double belonging its shortest form. Explaining the answer, he paraphrases it: *quasi dicat: Vos estis Dei, et Caesaris, et habetis in usu vestro et quae Dei sunt, et quae Caesaris*, “as if he said: You are God’s, and Caesar’s, and you have in your use both the things that are God’s and the things that are Caesar’s”. What we have from the world binds us, he says, to keep peace with the world, and he cites the first chapter of Baruch, where the exiles are bidden to pray for the life of the king of Babylon (*Super Matthaeum* c. 22, Venice ed., p. 284).

### Where the debt ends

The same sovereignty that makes the debt real sets its limit. Chrysostom says so in the next breath: “But thou, when thou hearest, ‘Render unto Caesar the things which are Caesar’s,’ know that He is speaking only of those things, which are no detriment to godliness; since if it be any such thing as this, such a thing is no longer Caesar’s tribute, but the devil’s” (70.2). The second clause of the saying does not merely add a second obligation; it measures the first. Whatever Caesar claims that belongs to God is no longer Caesar’s to claim.

Augustine gives the limit a case. Expounding a psalm on the rod of the wicked over the lot of the just, he says that a Christian servant should serve even a bad master, and that what holds of master and slave holds “also of powers and kings”. Then: “Julian was an infidel Emperor, an apostate, a wicked man, an idolater; Christian soldiers served an infidel Emperor; when they came to the cause of Christ, they acknowledged Him only who was in heaven. If he called upon them at any time to worship idols, to offer incense; they preferred God to him: but whenever he commanded them to deploy into line, to march against this or that nation, they at once obeyed. They distinguished their everlasting from their temporal master; and yet they were, for the sake of their everlasting Master, submissive to their temporal master” (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 124.7). The soldiers’ obedience and their refusal come from the same source: they obeyed the emperor for the sake of the master in heaven, and for his sake refused the incense. Elsewhere Augustine asks what harm Christians had done to the kingdoms of the earth: “Did their King forbid His soldiers to pay and to render due service to the kings of the earth?”, and he answers with this saying and with the tribute paid from the fish’s mouth (118, under the letter Schin).

On this reading the saying is neither a charter for two independent realms nor a refusal of civil duty. Caesar’s claim is real because it lies within a world where the Lord alone is God, and for the same reason it is bounded.

### A disagreement about the ground of the debt

Not every witness grounds the debt in the same way. Hilary of Poitiers makes the obligation depend on holding what is Caesar’s: *Si enim nihil eius penes nos resederit, conditione reddendi ei quae sua sunt non tenebimur*, “for if nothing of his remains with us, we shall not be held by the condition of rendering to him what is his”. But if we rest on his goods and use the right of his power, then it is beyond complaint that Caesar be given back what is Caesar’s (*Commentarius in Matthaeum* 23.2). Ambrose, on Luke, draws the consequence for the disciple: *Et tu si vis non esse obnoxius Caesari, noli habere quae mundi sunt: sed si habes divitias, obnoxius es Caesari. Si vis nihil regi debere terreno, relinque omnia tua, et sequere Christum*, “If you do not wish to be bound to Caesar, do not have the things of the world; but if you have riches, you are bound to Caesar. If you wish to owe nothing to an earthly king, leave all that is yours and follow Christ” (*Expositio evangelii secundum Lucam* IX.35).

For Hilary and Ambrose, then, the debt follows possession: whoever keeps the world’s goods owes the world’s ruler, and the one who has left everything owes him nothing. Chrysostom and Augustine say otherwise, each at a passage already quoted. Chrysostom says that Paul opens his command with “let every soul be subject unto the higher powers” “to show that these regulations are for all, even for priests, and monks, and not for men of secular occupations only”: the subjection holds “if thou be an Apostle even, or an Evangelist, or a Prophet, or anything whatsoever” (*Homilies on Romans* 23). The monk and the Apostle, who have done what Ambrose bids the disciple do, are named among those who owe. Augustine, in the exposition that gives the soldiers under Julian, grounds the honour paid to rulers in God’s ordering of his Church: “thus hath God ordained His Church, that every power ordained in the world may have honour, and sometimes from those who are better than those in power” (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 124.7). For them the debt does not wait on what a Christian holds of Caesar’s; it binds the one who has left everything as well, and its limit is fixed not by renunciation but by what belongs to God. The two positions agree that the tribute is lawful and that God’s claim comes first. They disagree about whether one who keeps nothing of the world’s goods still owes Caesar, and so about what the debt rests on.

### The Lord reigns among the nations

The psalm turns the first reading’s “there is none besides me” into a summons to the nations. The Lord is to be feared above all gods, “For all the gods of the Gentiles are devils: but the Lord made the heavens” (Ps 95:5). The Hebrew calls them idols; the Greek and Latin, which Augustine and Bellarmine expound, call them demons. Bellarmine: the gods of the gentiles “are not true gods, but demons”. Augustine reads the psalm’s whole movement as the Redeemer’s freeing of the nations from captivity: “All nations then had devils for their gods” (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 95.5).

“Say ye among the Gentiles, the Lord hath reigned” (v. 10). Bellarmine explains the kingship: “God always reigns in heaven, and he reigns on earth through his power and majesty; but he began to reign, through faith, among the gentiles, from the coming of the Messias, where the devil previously reigned”. Augustine’s Old Latin psalter read the verse with an addition, “the Lord hath reigned from the wood”, which the appointed Latin does not have; on that text, not the verse sung at this Mass, he says that “Christ already possesseth regions where the Roman empire hath never yet reached; what is as yet closed to those who fight with the sword, is not closed to Him who fighteth with wood” (95.2). For one Father who read the psalm within the Roman empire, the Lord’s reign did not wait on Caesar’s. The strophe ends with the judgment, “he will judge the people with justice” (10c), and Bellarmine, at the psalm’s close, describes the Judge as one who will “have no regard of persons, and … judge in all justice”. The questioners had praised Jesus for regarding no man’s person (Mt 22:16); Bellarmine gives the same quality to the Lord who judges the nations.

### The Missal’s texts in this reading

The Entrance Antiphon asks for shelter under the shadow of God’s wings, which Augustine reads as “the defence of Thy love and mercy”. The protection asked for is God’s and not a ruler’s. In the psalm the same verse opens by asking protection from those who resist God’s right hand, words the antiphon leaves out; in the first reading it is the Lord who grasps the right hand of a king.

The Collect asks to serve God’s majesty, *maiestati tuae … servire*, with a sincere heart. The Latin word belonged to gods and rulers alike: Lewis and Short give *maiestas* first “of the gods”, then of men in high station and of the sovereignty of the Roman people, and also “as a title of honor of the Roman emperors” (*Latin Dictionary*, s.v. *majestas*, p. 1101). In this interpretation the Collect’s word, said to God, stands beside the Gospel’s Caesar: the majesty served first is God’s, and every other service, Caesar’s included, is ordered under it.

The second reading greets a church that had learned what it meant to live under Caesar’s decrees and confess another king. Acts, which is not read at this Mass, tells how Jason and certain brethren of Thessalonica were dragged before the city’s rulers with the charge that “these all do contrary to the decrees of Caesar, saying that there is another king, Jesus” (Acts 17:7). Chrysostom, expounding the church’s “labour of charity”, recalls that scene: “Did not they that warred against the Preaching … drag Jason before the rulers of the city?” Acts reports the charge as the cry of those who raised the tumult; whatever its intent, the church’s faith and charity had to endure the suspicion that it owed Caesar nothing.

The Alleluia verse places the faithful in the midst of a crooked and perverse generation, shining as lights. Chrysostom’s stars, which shine the brighter in the night (*Homilies on Philippians* 8), describe a church living among powers it does not control.

The Prayer over the Offerings asks to serve God’s gifts with a free mind. In this interpretation the freedom is that of those whose first service is God’s: they pay Caesar what is his without belonging to him.

The first Communion antiphon takes the eyes of the Lord upon those who fear him from a psalm whose preceding verse says, “The king is not saved by a great army” (Ps 32:16), a verse not sung. Aquinas, commenting on it, says that no temporal power can bring men to the salvation of the just (*nulla potestas temporalis potest homines ad salutem iustorum perducere*), and cites “Put not your trust in princes” (Ps 145:2; *Postilla super Psalmos* 32). The salvation the antiphon promises comes from the Lord’s regard, not from the strength of kings.

The second Communion antiphon, where it is sung, comes from a passage that sets the Son of Man against the rulers of the gentiles: “they who seem to rule over the Gentiles lord it over them … But it is not so among you” (Mk 10:42–43). Bede reads the passage as teaching that one comes to the height of the virtues “not by power, but by humility” (*cum ad summitatem virtutum non potentia, sed humilitate veniatur*; *In Marci evangelium expositio* III, PL 92, col. 236). Chrysostom, on Matthew’s form of the saying, gives the Son of Man’s own reason: “For being King of the powers above, I was willing to become man … even my life did I give a ransom; and for whom? For enemies” (*Homilies on Matthew* 65.4). The antiphon itself keeps only the ransom; the contrast with the rulers lies in its unsung context. Where it is sung, that context makes the King who rules by service the measure of every authority, Caesar’s included.

The Prayer after Communion asks that the assembly be aided by temporal benefits and instructed by eternal ones. That is the order of goods within which Caesar’s coin has its place: it helps, and it does not teach. Aquinas’s “You are God’s, and Caesar’s” names the double belonging that the prayer puts in order.

### Where this interpretation strains

The interpretation stands on the strongest relation the books declare, and its difficulty lies in that relation. The Fathers speak to each half separately: Jerome and Aquinas to the Lord’s hand on Cyrus, Chrysostom and Augustine to the coin and its limit. The two halves meet in the *Ordo*’s titles, and the sovereignty that joins them is this reading’s inference from the pairing. The Fathers’ cases, moreover, are emperors and kings, tribute, incense and the deployment of soldiers; what the soldiers under Julian teach is the order of two masters, not a form of government, and the reading reaches no further.

### The four senses of this interpretation

Literal.

: The Lord raises Cyrus, a king who does not know him, to subdue nations and free Israel, so that all may know there is no other God (Is 45:1, 4–6); Jesus declares the census coin owed to Caesar and God’s due owed to God (Mt 22:21). Tribute is a debt rendered and not a gift (Chrysostom).

Allegorical.

: Christ is the King who reigns over the nations, by faith among the gentiles (Bellarmine on Ps 95) and, in Augustine’s Old Latin psalter, “from the wood”; where the second Communion antiphon is sung, he is the Son of Man who gives his life as a ransom, whom the antiphon’s unsung context sets against the rulers of the gentiles (Bede; Chrysostom on the parallel). Cyrus figures Christ only in Jerome, on the Vulgate’s *assimilavi*.

Moral.

: Render civil dues as justice requires and keep peace with the world from which you hold its goods (Aquinas), but refuse whatever is against God: the soldiers who served Julian and would not offer incense (Augustine); the limit at godliness (Chrysostom).

Anagogical.

: The Lord “will judge the people with justice” (Ps 95:10c), without regard of persons (Bellarmine), and the rod of the wicked over the just is felt “for a season” and “will not be there for ever” (Augustine, *Enarrationes in Psalmos* 124.8).

## True Words from a Divided Heart: The Hypocrites’ Question and the One Who Reads Hearts {#lane-sincere-heart}

<span id="sec:sincere-heart" data-label="sec:sincere-heart"></span>

Read from its middle, the Gospel is a scene about the one who renders before it is a ruling about what is rendered. Men come to Jesus with a question and open it with praise, and every word of the praise is true. He answers by calling them hypocrites. This interpretation asks why, and follows the witnesses who locate the fault not in the words but in the heart behind them. Jerome defines the hypocrite as one who is one thing and pretends another; John Chrysostom says that they spoke the truth “not with an upright mind”; Aquinas says that they had one thing in the mouth and another in the heart; Augustine says that they spoke one way and judged another. Chrysostom, Hilary of Poitiers and Aquinas add that Christ’s reading of their heart showed who he is; Jerome states the same discernment as the first virtue of anyone who answers. Carried through the whole Mass, that contrast shows the Mass asking for the opposite of the questioners’ duplicity, a will devoted and a heart sincere, and setting the Church in a crooked generation as lights whose words and lives agree.

### Why true words are called hypocrisy

The difficulty is in the Gospel’s own sequence. The questioners say, “Master, we know that thou art a true speaker and teachest the way of God in truth. Neither carest thou for any man: for thou dost not regard the person of men” (Mt 22:16). Every clause is accurate; Matthew’s readers know that Jesus is truthful and teaches God’s way, and the rest of the scene shows that he regards no one’s person. Yet two verses later Jesus calls them hypocrites (22:18). If the charge does not concern the truth of what they said, what does it concern?

Jerome answers by defining the word from the scene. *Hypocrita ergo appellatur, qui aliud est, et aliud simulat, id est, aliud opere agit, et aliud voce praetendit*: “he is called a hypocrite, then, who is one thing and feigns another, that is, does one thing in deed and puts forward another with his voice” (*Commentarii in Matthaeum* III, PL 26, col. 163). On the praise itself he says, *Blanda et fraudulenta interrogatio*, “a flattering and deceitful question”, which draws the one who answers toward fearing God rather than Caesar, so that he may say tribute should not be paid (cols. 162–163). The words are true; their purpose is a snare.

John Chrysostom makes the same distinction with more patience. “And see their flattery, and their hidden craft.” They call him truthful, but “How said ye then, ‘He is a deceiver’ ”? They “testify the truth unto Him, confessing what was really so, nevertheless, not with an upright mind, nor willingly” (*Homilies on Matthew* 70.1). Truth spoken without an upright mind is still truth; but it is not testimony, because the speaker does not mean it. Chrysostom also notices the turn of their question: “They say not, Tell us what is good, what is expedient, what is lawful? but, ‘What thinkest Thou?’ ” They do not want to learn; they want something to use.

Aquinas gives the anatomy of the act. *Homines mali ab adulatione incipiunt*, “evil men begin from flattery”. He explains why the praise is nonetheless true of Christ: *Verax est qui veritatem loquitur; et hoc est proprium Dei, et eius qui Deo coniunctus est … Christus autem est Deo coniunctus per unionem, et ideo verax est*, “truthful is he who speaks the truth; and this is proper to God and to one joined to God … but Christ is joined to God by union, and therefore he is truthful”. The questioners’ lie lay elsewhere: they spoke against their own heart, since they held him not a master but a deceiver. And so: *hypocritae sunt proprie qui aliud in ore habent, aliud in corde*, “hypocrites, properly speaking, are those who have one thing in the mouth, another in the heart” (*Super Matthaeum* c. 22, Venice ed., p. 283). Augustine, expounding a psalm that bids men speak justice and judge rightly, puts the same charge in the questioners’ own terms: “Of paying tribute do ye think? To do justice are ye willing? … But now because in one way ye speak, in another way judge, hypocrites ye are” (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 57.8).

The four witnesses agree on the diagnosis by distinct reasoning. Jerome sets deed against voice, Chrysostom mind against testimony, Aquinas heart against mouth, Augustine judgment against speech. None of them makes the fault a matter of the words’ content; all of them place it in the distance between the words and the one who speaks them. The vice they name belongs to no people and no party. The Gospel names those who came, disciples of the Pharisees sent with the Herodians, and the vice can belong to anyone who praises in order to use.

### He answers the heart before the words

Matthew says that Jesus answered “knowing their wickedness” (22:18; in the Clementine *Cognita autem Jesus nequitia eorum*, as Jerome’s lemma also reads, where Aquinas’s printed lemma has *malitia*). Under that verse the witnesses ask what kind of knowing it was. Hilary of Poitiers says that Christ, *interna cogitationum secreta cognoscens (Deus enim nihil non eorum quae intra homines sunt absconsa speculatur)*, “knowing the inner secrets of their thoughts, for God sees everything hidden within men”, asked for the denarius (*Commentarius in Matthaeum* 23.1, PL 9, col. 1044). Aquinas divides the answer in two: *Et primo respondet ad mentem eorum; secundo ad verba … Quia hominis est respondere ad verba, Dei autem respondere ad mentem; ideo quia Christus erat Deus, et homo, ideo ad utrumque respondet*, “and first he answers their mind, then their words … because it belongs to man to answer words, but to God to answer the mind; and therefore, because Christ was God and man, he answers both” (p. 283). The rebuke “ye hypocrites” is the answer to their mind; the coin is the answer to their words.

Here the interpretation meets its difficulty. Is the reading of their heart a sign of who Jesus is, or only the discernment that any wise person should have when flattered? The objection can draw on Jerome, who states the same discernment as a rule for every answerer: *Prima virtus est respondentis, interrogantium mentem cognoscere, et non discipulos, sed tentatores vocare*, “the first virtue of one who answers is to know the mind of those who ask, and to call them not disciples but tempters” (col. 163). Jerome draws no conclusion about Christ from this; he gives a rule. The objection might add that the snare was laid before a crowd, and that a shrewd hearer could have guessed it. Matthew, however, says only that the Pharisees took counsel how to ensnare him (22:15) and that Jesus knew their wickedness (22:18); he does not say whether anyone else knew their purpose beforehand.

Chrysostom’s own reasoning answers the objection from the scene itself. The questioners’ words gave nothing away: “And yet their words were full of much respect, for they both called Him Master, and bore witness to His truth, and that He was no respecter of persons; but being God, He was deceived by none of these things. Wherefore they also ought to have conjectured, that the rebuke was not the result of conjecture, but a sign of His knowing their secret thoughts” (70.1). Because the intent was hidden behind true and respectful words, the rebuke could not have been a guess. And when the scene is over he draws the conclusion: “He gave them proof of His Godhead, by revealing the secrets of their hearts, and with gentleness did He silence them” (70.2).

The christological claim is Chrysostom’s, Hilary’s and Aquinas’s, and Chrysostom grounds it in the concealment. Jerome’s sentence is a rule of prudence for any answerer, a sign of no one’s Godhead, and it belongs with the moral lesson Aquinas draws from the same verse: *datur nobis exemplum, quod non debemus adulatoribus credere*, “an example is given us, that we ought not to believe flatterers” (p. 283).

### The counter-question: the verdict from their own mouths

Jesus does not answer the question as asked. He asks for the coin and asks whose image it bears. Augustine, Chrysostom, Jerome and Aquinas agree that the counter-question is not an evasion but the means of the answer, and that the answer comes out of the questioners’ own mouths. They give different reasons.

Augustine explains it as a rule of disputation. Sometimes, he says, a questioner should be asked something else, “by telling which he would answer himself”; those who asked about tribute “were asked another question, viz. whose image the money bore which was brought forward by themselves; and because they told what they had been asked … they gave a kind of answer to themselves in reference to the question they had asked the Lord: and accordingly from their answer He drew this inference, ‘Render therefore unto Caesar …’ ” He sets this case apart from the question about Jesus’ authority, where the questioners refused to answer and Jesus said, “Neither tell I you” (*De sermone Domini in monte* II.20.70). In his exposition of Psalm 57 he hears the psalm’s teeth broken in their own mouth: Jesus “saith not, it is Caesar’s: but asketh Whose it is? in order that their teeth in their own mouth might be utterly broken … Now ye have made answer” (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 57.8).

Chrysostom: “by their tongue He brings out the decision, and causes them to decide, that it is lawful; which was a clear and plain victory. So that, when He asks, not from ignorance doth He ask, but because it is His will to cause them to be bound by their own answers” (70.2). Jerome: *Sapientia semper sapienter agit, ut suis potissimum tentatores sermonibus confutentur*, “wisdom always acts wisely, so that tempters are refuted above all by their own words”; and Jesus asks whose the image is not in ignorance but *ut ad sermonem eorum competenter respondeat*, “that he may answer fittingly to their word” (col. 163). Aquinas: *non potest melius confutare opponentem, quam secundum verba sua. Unde primo ponit quaestionem; secundo ex responsione elicit veritatem*, “he cannot better refute an opponent than by his own words; so first he puts the question, then from the answer he draws out the truth”, asking *ex dispensatione*, by design and not from ignorance (pp. 283–284).

The reasons differ: the questioner made to answer himself, the teeth broken in their own mouth, the binding by their own answer, wisdom refuting by the tempters’ words. They converge on one point: the one who reads the heart also makes the hypocrite’s own mouth give the verdict. The divided speaker is answered by the very words he had used to hide himself.

### The remedy: a single intention

If hypocrisy is a divided heart, its contrary is a heart that is one. The acclamation’s verse comes from a sentence that names that contrary. Paul wants the Philippians to be “blameless and sincere children of God”, in the Latin *simplices*, before they shine as lights. Aquinas comments: *Filius enim est similis patri, Deus autem simplex est: unde simplices simus sicut filii Dei; quod est, quando intentio est ad unum*, “for a son is like his father, and God is simple; so let us be simple as sons of God, which is when the intention is toward one thing”; and he cites James: “A double minded man is inconstant in all his ways” (Jas 1:8) (*Super ad Philippenses* c. 2, lect. 4). The word “sincere” stands in an earlier clause of v. 15, outside the acclamation’s words, which take only the shining and the word of life. But the light Paul describes is the light of children whose intention is single.

The second reading shows the same agreement of word and power in the preaching that founded the Thessalonian church. The gospel came “not … in word only, but in power also: and in the Holy Ghost and in much fulness”, and Aquinas sets that power against loftiness of speech (*Super I ad Thessalonicenses* c. 1, lect. 1). Chrysostom adds that God permitted persecution from the first “that no one might say, that the Preaching was established lightly or by flattery … that it was not human persuasion, but the power of God, that persuaded the souls of the believers” (*Homilies on First Thessalonians* 1). The questioners in the temple used true words as flattery; the gospel reached Thessalonica without flattery, and its words and power agreed.

Augustine, expounding the psalm of this Mass, gives a name to the divided heart: “What is the whited wall, save hypocrisy, that is, pretence? Without it is bright, within it is dirt” (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 95.3).

### The Missal’s texts in this reading

The Collect asks that the assembly serve God’s majesty *sincero corde*. In this interpretation those words name the precise contrary of what the Gospel exposes: a heart not divided between what it says and what it intends. The Collect also asks for a will devoted to God *semper*, always, not when it is useful.

The Entrance Antiphon is the prayer of one who calls on God because God hears. Its psalm begins, in verses not sung, with a prayer “which proceedeth not from deceitful lips” and a heart God has proved (Ps 16:1, 3). Augustine, reading the psalm as the voice of Christ with his Church, glosses the first verse: “not going forth to Thee in deceitful lips … that I may not in judging utter aught else than I understand in Thee” (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 16.2). Bellarmine, on the verse about the proved heart, says that “there are many wicked persons, to all appearance with a fair exterior”. The antiphon’s own words are a cry of trust; the sincerity belongs to the psalm around them.

The first reading contributes only by contrast, and less than any other text. Cyrus does not know the Lord and yet serves his purpose; Jerome glosses “thou hast not known me” as “you worshipped images, not God”. The questioners say “we know” and yet tempt. Aquinas says that the fruit God required of Cyrus’s power was the knowledge of God. Set beside the Gospel, the two show that knowing God appears in what is done and not in what is said. Cyrus is no model of sincerity; he serves a purpose he does not know.

The psalm bids the nations bring the Lord glory and enter his courts, and Augustine’s present for those courts is a humble heart (95.9). The gift the psalm asks for is a heart, and it is judged by the Lord who will “judge the people with justice” (v. 10c).

The Prayer over the Offerings asks to serve God’s gifts *libera mente*, with a free mind, and to be cleansed by the mysteries served. A mind free of self-interest, purified at the altar, is the opposite of the questioners’ mind, which served its own purpose under a show of respect.

The first Communion antiphon names the eyes of the Lord upon those who fear him and hope in his mercy: the God who sees. Bellarmine says that fear joined to hope “is the mark of real love”. The questioners feigned respect; the antiphon describes reverence that is real. Where the second antiphon is sung, it names the Son of Man who gives his life as a ransom. The truthful teacher whom the questioners praised in order to trap gives his life for many; Bede reads the verse of one who took the form of a servant.

The Prayer after Communion asks to be instructed, *erudiamur*, by eternal things: a formation of the heart, which the questioners did not seek. They came, as Chrysostom observes of their “What thinkest Thou?”, for an opinion they could use, not for teaching.

### Where this interpretation strains

The interpretation reads every text through a contrast with the questioners, and that is its difficulty. A contrast drawn against a vice is easily turned against people, and some of the Fathers who expound this scene pass, on the same pages, from those who came to Jesus to a whole people. The vice the Gospel exposes belongs to anyone who praises in order to use, and the Mass’s petitions for a devoted will and a sincere heart are the assembly’s prayer about itself.

### The four senses of this interpretation

Literal.

: The disciples of the Pharisees and the Herodians flatter Jesus with true words in order to trap him; knowing their wickedness, he calls them hypocrites, and he answers through the coin, drawing the verdict from their own mouths (Mt 22:15–21). Hypocrisy is the distance between mouth and heart (Jerome, Chrysostom, Aquinas, Augustine).

Allegorical.

: Christ is the Truth, truthful by his union with God (Aquinas), who reads hearts as only God does (Hilary, Chrysostom); the Church, to whom the gospel came in power and not in word only, shines with a borrowed light in a crooked generation (Aquinas on Philippians).

Moral.

: Be single, “when the intention is toward one thing” (Aquinas): a sincere heart and a will devoted always (the Collect); no flattery given or believed (Aquinas); deeds that match words (Jerome); discernment of those who praise in order to use (Jerome).

Anagogical.

: The Lord comes to judge the peoples with justice (Ps 95:10c) and, at the psalm’s close, the world with justice and the peoples with his truth (95:13, not sung), when nothing hidden within men will be hidden from the God who, Hilary says, sees all that lies concealed within them.

## The Three Interpretations Compared {#sec:comparison}

The three interpretations answer three different questions raised by the same sentence. The first asks what is owed to God and answers with the self, which bears his image. The second asks how the debt to Caesar stands to God’s sovereignty and answers that it is real and bounded. The third asks what is wrong with the questioners and answers that their hearts and words are divided. The table sets out where each turns.

### What they share

All three read the saying as setting Caesar’s claim beneath God’s, and none reads it as a division of life into two equal realms, one political and one religious. In the first, Caesar’s claim reaches the coin and God’s reaches the one who carries it; in the second, Caesar’s claim is real because God rules the world in which it is made, and it ends where God’s begins; in the third, the debt to God includes the heart of the one who pays. All three rest on Matthew 22:15–21 read as a whole scene, from the plot to the verdict. And all three use the Collect’s petition, for a will devoted to God and service of his majesty with a sincere heart, but each for a different reason: the will as the self that is owed (the first), the majesty served first (the second), the heart undivided (the third).

All three also meet the counter-question in the same way. Augustine, Chrysostom, Jerome and Aquinas agree that Jesus’ question about the image is not an evasion but the means of his answer, which he draws from the questioners’ own mouths. That agreement is developed in the third interpretation, where it serves the argument most directly.

### Where they differ

The interpretations differ in what they ask of a hearer. The first would be satisfied by one who pays every tax and gives God himself. The second asks, in addition, where the tax must be refused, and finds the answer in a soldier’s refusal to offer incense. The third asks whether the giver means what he says, and would find wanting even a correct answer given with a divided heart.

They also differ in what they need from the rest of the Mass. Only the second needs the first reading as an argument, and it alone stands on a relation that the liturgical books declare. The first uses Isaiah only through Jerome’s reading of the Vulgate, and the third only by contrast. Conversely, the first and the third depend most on the Missal’s prayers, whose words about the will and the heart they take up, while the second uses the prayers chiefly to place Caesar’s coin within an order of goods.

The first and the third can be preached together without strain: render yourself, with an undivided heart. The second adds a public dimension that the other two leave implicit, and with it a disagreement among the Fathers, about whether one who keeps nothing of the world’s goods still owes Caesar, that the other two do not have to settle. Each has its own principal difficulty: for the first, that the image of God is an inference the saying does not state; for the second, that the Fathers speak to each of the two readings the Lectionary pairs and not to the pair; for the third, that one of its own witnesses, Jerome, treats the reading of hearts as a rule for any respondent rather than a sign of Godhead.

### Psalms 17 (16), 33 (32) and 96 (95): the antiphons and the psalm {#sec:date-psalms}

None of the three psalms names an occasion in a way the chronology record can date, except for Psalm 96 (95), whose Latin and Greek title does. The “Historical setting” row says so for the Entrance Antiphon’s psalm and the first Communion antiphon’s: the Vulgate titles them “The prayer of David” and “A psalm for David” without naming an event or a place. The composition date shown for all three is the bound that the introduction to the Psalms in the *New American Bible, Revised Edition* sets for the whole Psalter, within which, it holds, no individual psalm can be dated securely. It is not a date for any of these three.

Psalm 33 (32) alone carries an attribution answer. Its title, *Psalmus David* in the Clementine and *Tō Daueid* in the Greek, ascribes it to David, and the record dates David’s reign in what the *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s “David” (Corbett, vol. 4) calls “the usual chronology”, marked disputed because the article reports later datings beside it. The same article places David’s kingship first at Hebron and then at Jerusalem. The reign is the reference point of the attribution and not a date of the psalm. Psalm 17 (16) is also titled for David in the Vulgate, but the record holds no attribution answer for it, and none is given.

Psalm 96 (95) is the responsorial psalm. Its title in the Clementine and in Swete’s Septuagint sets it “when the house was built after the captivity”; the Hebrew psalm and the *Nova Vulgata* carry no title, and the title is not sung. The superscription row dates the setting the title names, the raising of the Temple at Jerusalem after the return, on the figure the *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s “Temple of Jerusalem” (Meistermann, vol. 14) gives. It dates the title’s setting, not the writing of the psalm. The prophetic-referent row records the same encyclopedia’s statement, in its “Psalms” (Drum, vol. 12), that the coming of the Messiah is foretold in this group of psalms, and the chronology record’s own step from that coming to the Nativity; the two dates shown are those its articles “General Chronology” (Gerard) and “Biblical Chronology” (Howlett, both vol. 3) report, and the record marks them disputed. They date the event the psalm was read as foretelling, not the psalm.

### Isaiah 45:1, 4–6: the first reading {#sec:date-isaiah}

The first reading has two answers, and they are not two dates for one thing. The traditional attribution is to Isaias, the son of Amos (Is 1:1), whose ministry the *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s “Isaias” (Souvay, vol. 8) describes as lasting from the closing year of King Ozias to the latest prophecies it admits; the article calls him a citizen, perhaps a native, of Jerusalem, and the same paragraph reports that commentators differ on when he was called. The row dates the prophet’s ministry in that account and not the oracle to Cyrus. The second answer is the critical placement of the oracles of chapters 40–55: the introduction to Isaiah in the *New American Bible, Revised Edition* attributes them to an anonymous prophet who spoke toward the end of the Babylonian exile. That introduction gives the prophet no place; the oracles themselves bid the exiles “Come forth out of Babylon” (48:20). Neither answer is a date for the composition of 45:1–6, for which the record holds none.

### Matthew 22:15–21: the Gospel {#sec:date-matthew}

The “Event” row has no date: the chronology corpus holds none for the question about tribute, and none is supplied here. Matthew sets it in the temple at Jerusalem, where Jesus is teaching (21:23) after his entry into the city (21:10), and before he leaves the temple (24:1); the *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s “Gospel of St. Matthew” (Jacquier, vol. 10) groups these chapters under the heading “In Jerusalem”, among them the questions “concerning the tribute”. The composition dates shown are those the same article reports from the ancient writers, who “are at variance”, together with the figures it gives where opinion is divided, and one from the encyclopedia’s “The New Testament” (Durand, vol. 14); all are marked disputed. The article says that Matthew wrote for the Jews, and reports Eusebius’s account that he wrote in Hebrew when he was leaving Palestine; the place of writing is not fixed. These are dates of the book, not of the saying.

### Mark 10:45: the second Communion antiphon {#sec:date-mark}

The antiphon’s source is dated only as part of Mark’s Gospel. The figures shown are those the *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s “Gospel of Saint Mark” (MacRory, vol. 9) reports from a manuscript subscription, the Paschal Chronicle and Eusebius’s Chronicle, together with the article’s own conclusion, a figure from its “The New Testament” (Durand), and the answer of the Pontifical Biblical Commission of 1912 placing the Gospel after Matthew and before the destruction of Jerusalem; all are marked disputed. MacRory holds that the Gospel was written at Rome for Roman Christians, and reports, to reject it, Chrysostom’s statement that Mark wrote in Egypt.

### Philippians 2:15d, 16a: the Alleluia verse {#sec:date-philippians}

The verse is dated as part of the letter. The first figure is the year the *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s “St. Paul” (Prat, vol. 11) gives the letters of the captivity; the second is the range its “Epistle to the Philippians” (Vander Heeren, vol. 12) gives for Paul’s captivity at Rome. Both are marked disputed. Vander Heeren reports that some have held the letter to have been written at Caesarea, and holds that it was written at Rome; the letter itself names Paul’s bonds (1:13) and greets the Philippians from “Caesar’s household” (4:22). The verse narrates no event.

### 1 Thessalonians 1:1–5b: the second reading {#sec:date-thessalonians}

The figures shown are those of the *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s “St. Paul” (Prat) and “Epistles to the Thessalonians” (Drum, vol. 14), the second of which gives its years “according to the commonly received scheme of Pauline chronology”; both are marked disputed. Drum reports that the received Greek text and several uncials subscribe the letters “written from Athens”, and holds from the letter itself (3:6) and from Acts (18:5) that they were written during Paul’s first stay in Corinth. The letter is addressed “to the church of the Thessalonians” (1:1), whose founding Acts narrates (17:1–10).

## Appendix: Liturgical Resolution {#sec:liturgical-resolution}

**Celebration.** The Twenty-ninth Sunday in Ordinary Time, a Sunday in Ordinary Time at place 6 of the table of liturgical days; green; Gloria and Creed. The formula is PC-S55-A, the Year A target of the parent PC-S55; it has the single Sunday form. A Mass anticipated on Saturday evening uses the same formulary and readings, and there is no Vigil form.

**Books, calendar and territory.** The *Roman Missal*, Third Edition, for use in the dioceses of the United States of America (2011), whose Latin base is the *Missale Romanum*, editio typica tertia (2002), as reprinted with emendations in 2008; the *Lectionary for Mass for Use in the Dioceses of the United States of America*, second typical edition, volume I, no. 145, which follows the *Ordo lectionum Missae*, editio typica altera (1981); the *General Instruction of the Roman Missal* in the United States edition of 2011 with the emendations of 2021; the *Universal Norms on the Liturgical Year and the Calendar* and the General Roman Calendar, in the Latin printed in the 2002 Missal; and the *Liturgical Calendar for the Dioceses of the United States of America* for 2026. Only the national calendar is applied. No diocesan, religious, parish, titular, dedication or patronal calendar was supplied, and none is assumed.

**Cycle and occurrence.** Year A of the Sunday cycle runs from 30 November 2025 to 22 November 2026, and the national calendar enters Sunday 18 October 2026 as the Twenty-ninth Sunday in Ordinary Time, green, with the readings of Lectionary no. 145 and Psalter Week I. The General Roman Calendar inscribes the Feast of Saint Luke, Evangelist, on 18 October. A feast of a saint of the General Calendar ranks below a Sunday in Ordinary Time, and under *Universal Norms* 60 it is omitted in this year: it is neither transferred nor commemorated, and it supplies no text to this Mass. The weekday cycle governs no Sunday.

**World Mission Day.** The Holy See’s message for the hundredth World Mission Day dates it to Sunday 18 October 2026, and the Pontifical Mission Societies in the United States keep it on that day with the World Mission Sunday collection. It is a day of prayer and support for the missions. No witness gives it a rank, colour, formulary or text, the national calendar prints nothing for it, and it does not displace the Sunday. Its one bearing on the Mass is the conditional branch for the Mass for the Evangelization of Peoples below.

**The Missal formulary and the three cycles.** The antiphons and orations belong to the Twenty-ninth Week in Ordinary Time and are common to Years A, B and C; their text is recorded once, in the collection’s record for that week. The readings, psalm and Alleluia verse are those of Year A alone. The second reading begins the Lectionary’s course through the First Letter to the Thessalonians in Year A, and the Gospel belongs to its semi-continuous reading of Matthew.

**Branches.** No selection among the following was supplied, and none is inferred. Both Communion antiphons are studied in full; a claim resting on one of them holds only where it is chosen, and a claim resting on the wording of an antiphon does not hold where another approved chant replaces it. ## Appendix: Scope and Qualifications {#sec:scope}

**Edition and formulary.** The study treats the Mass of the Twenty-ninth Sunday in Ordinary Time, Year A, as the *Roman Missal*, Third Edition, and the *Lectionary for Mass* give it for the dioceses of the United States, in the national calendar alone, for the occurrence of 18 October 2026. Identity, occurrence and branches are set out in the preceding appendix and recorded in `instance/manifest.md` and `research/context.md`. Nothing in the study holds for a celebration that replaces this formulary with another Mass.

**Text control.** The Missal formulary was read in the Latin of the *Missale Romanum*, editio typica tertia (2002), in a digitally typeset reproduction, and compared with the list of changes for the 2008 reprint in *Notitiae* 44 (2008), which lists none for this week, and with the *Antiphonary* excerpted from the English Missal (2010). No page of the 2008 reprint and no United States altar book was inspected, so the approved English orations were not read in any witness, and no chant book was collated. The Lectionary’s boundaries rest on three witnesses that agree in every figure: the national calendar for 2026, the bishops’ conference’s dated page of readings, and no. 145 of the Latin *Ordo lectionum Missae* (1981). The page of readings refused direct requests on 16 October 2026 and was read in the Internet Archive’s capture of it made on 15 March 2026; the psalm’s United States locator and the division of its strophes rest on that capture, and the printed United States Lectionary was not inspected. The *Nova Vulgata* was read in the Holy See’s web publication, in deliveries that are not retained; the readings it alone supports (at Isaiah 45:4, Psalm 96 (95):5 and Philippians 2:16, and in the antiphons’ comparison) are reported as its readings. Four questions about the text remain open: how the printed Lectionary gives the psalm response; whether the *Nova Vulgata* the *Ordo* used read *meo* or *suo* at Isaiah 45:1; the Hebrew and Greek of Isaiah 45, which were not read in the original languages; and manuscript evidence for Matthew 22:15–21 beyond what the SBL apparatus reports. The branch rows rest on the United States *General Instruction*: chapter II as read in its official chapter page on 16 October 2026, and chapters VII and VIII as read in Internet Archive captures of their official pages made on 13 and 18 June 2026, because the publisher’s site refused direct requests for them that day. Nothing is drawn from those two chapters beyond nos. 364–365, 372 and 374, which the branch rows cite.

**English.** All Scripture is printed in the Douay–Rheims version as revised by Bishop Challoner, a public-domain study translation of the Clementine Vulgate. It is not the text proclaimed in the celebration; the controlling English is that of the *Lectionary for Mass for Use in the Dioceses of the United States of America*, and for the antiphons and orations that of the *Roman Missal*, Third Edition, neither of which is reproduced. The orations and the antiphons are identified by Latin incipit and described; the descriptions are not translations and may not be quoted as the texts’ words. Where the Lectionary appoints part of a verse, the rest is marked beside it. English glosses of Latin passages are the editor’s renderings of the Latin printed beside them. In quotations the Latin is normalized only by writing *i* for consonantal *j* and writing out the ligatures, and the ligature of “Caesar” in the nineteenth-century English translations is written out.

**Reception.** Each appointed Scripture passage has at least one ancient and one medieval or later witness read at an exact locus; for the second Communion antiphon the only ancient witness is Chrysostom on Matthew’s parallel. The witnesses are those named in the References, and the search behind them is bounded and recorded in `research/scope.md`. Augustine, Chrysostom, Justin Martyr, Cyprian, Novatian and the *Epistle of Barnabas* were read in the English of the Nicene and Ante-Nicene Fathers; Cyril of Alexandria in Payne Smith’s English; Bellarmine in O’Sullivan’s abridged English of 1866, the Latin not compared. Jerome on Matthew and Isaiah, Hilary on Matthew, Bede on Mark, and Aquinas on Matthew, Isaiah and Philippians were read on page images of the printings named; Ambrose in an electronic Latin text of the Migne printing; Aquinas on First Thessalonians and on the Psalms in the electronic Latin of the Corpus Thomisticum. No witness was collated with a modern critical edition. Not opened, among others: Origen, Rabanus Maurus and the *Catena aurea* on Matthew; Tertullian; Eusebius, Cyril of Alexandria and Theodoret on Isaiah; Theodoret and Theophylact on First Thessalonians; Basil on Psalm 33; Cassiodorus beyond the title of Psalm 96. Nothing is said here about who first proposed any reading, about any reading being the Fathers’ common view, or about the order in time or the mutual dependence of the witnesses; Augustine’s own remark that “some” read the coin of the soul shows only that the reading was known to him. No commentary on any of the three orations was found. Cyril of Alexandria, Justin Martyr and Cyprian have no row in the registry of authors’ standing used for this study and are cited only in support; Cyprian, Novatian and *Barnabas* are cited only as witnesses to a variant reading. The liturgical commentaries of Schuster and Fromage, which treat this Gospel and Psalm 17 (16):6 within a Mass of another order, were read on page images of their printings and are not cited.

**Relations among the elements.** Four relations are stated by the liturgical books: the first reading is chosen in relation to the Gospel, as the *Ordo*’s titles show (*Praenotanda* 106); the psalm responds to the first reading (*General Instruction* 61); the acclamation greets the Gospel (*General Instruction* 62); and the second reading and the Gospel each belong to a semi-continuous course. The Missal’s antiphons and orations serve all three years. The recurrence of the words for serving and rendering, image and likeness, nations and rulers, knowing, seeing and sincerity across the texts is an observation about the texts. Among the witnesses cited, Augustine joins the saying on tribute to Psalm 4 and to 1 John 3:2, Chrysostom and Augustine join it to Romans 13, Chrysostom joins 1 Thessalonians 1:3 to Acts 17, Aquinas joins the saying to the first chapter of Baruch, and Bede and Chrysostom read Mark 10:45 against the rulers of the gentiles in its own context. Every other connection drawn between one element and another is the editor’s synthesis, grounded in a witness’s statement about his own passage where one is cited and otherwise an editorial proposal resting on the wording of the texts. No witness cited comments on this Mass, and none joins Isaiah 45 to Matthew 22.

**The questioners.** Several Fathers read for this study pass, in sentences near those quoted, from the men who questioned Jesus to the Jewish people as a whole. None of those sentences is used. The study keeps to the agents the Gospel names, the disciples of the Pharisees and the Herodians, treats hypocrisy as a vice that belongs to anyone, and follows the Second Vatican Council’s teaching that the Jews are not to be presented as rejected or accursed by God as if this followed from the Scriptures (*Nostra aetate* 4).

**The whited wall.** Augustine’s definition of the whited wall as hypocrisy (*Enarrationes in Psalmos* 95.3) is used as a definition only. In its section he applies it to the Donatist party, and that application is not used.

**Chronology.** The Date cells print the shared chronology corpus’s answers with their relations and alternatives unchanged, and the place phrases beside them rest on the sources recorded in `research/scope.md`. The first reading’s answers date the prophet’s ministry in one author’s account and the critical placement of chapters 40–55, not the oracle. The Psalter’s common composition bound is not a date for any of the three psalms; it was read in a delivery of 16 October 2026 of the introduction it cites, not in the earlier state the corpus cites. The responsorial psalm’s superscription answer dates the setting its Greek and Latin title names, and its referent answers date the event the psalm was read as foretelling. The Gospel’s event has no date in the corpus. The composition answers of the Gospels and epistles are dates of the books. Writing sites, first readers and narrative settings are kept apart.

**Rights.** The Douay–Rheims, the Clementine Vulgate, the King James Version, Brenton’s Septuagint, Swete’s Septuagint, the Nicene and Post-Nicene and Ante-Nicene Fathers, Payne Smith’s Cyril, O’Sullivan’s Bellarmine, the volumes of Migne, the Venice, Parma and Liège printings of Aquinas, Wilson’s and Feltoe’s editions, the Harper issue of Lewis and Short’s dictionary and the *Catholic Encyclopedia* are in the public domain. The Corpus Thomisticum texts are quoted in short phrases. The Latin and English of the Missal, the *Ordo lectionum Missae*, the Lectionary, the *General Instruction*, the national calendar, the *Nova Vulgata*, *Nostra aetate*, the World Mission Day message and the page of the Pontifical Mission Societies, and the introductions of the *New American Bible, Revised Edition* are protected; they are cited, and only incipits, the three titles of the *Ordo*, its Latin response and verse, short Latin phrases of the orations needed by the argument, and short phrases of the Council’s declaration and of the introductions are given.

**Records.** The composition of the target is audited in `propers/verified.md`, and the Missal formulary in the collection’s record for the Twenty-ninth Week; the reception search, loci, disagreements and negative results in `research/scope.md`; and the reasoning behind the three interpretations, with the class of every cross-element link, in `research/interpretations.md`.
