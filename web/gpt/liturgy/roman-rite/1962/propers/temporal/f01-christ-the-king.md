# Our Lord Jesus Christ the King: Hearing the King and Receiving His Peace

*An expansive study of the 1962 Roman Missal formulary for 25 October 2026*

**Our Lord Jesus Christ the King**

Hearing the King and Receiving His Peace

1962 Roman Missal — Expansive study

Last Sunday of October: 25 October 2026

## A King Who Gives His Life

Christ stands before a governor who can order his death, and the Mass calls him King of the universe. That encounter is the feast’s sharpest question. How can the prisoner who declines armed rescue be the Lord to whom every ruler owes allegiance? The answer unfolds through a sacrifice that makes a people, a truth that gives them ears to hear, and a peace that reaches beyond the boundaries they have made for themselves. The King’s apparent helplessness in the Gospel belongs beside the Epistle’s account of his creative power and its proclamation of reconciliation through the Cross.

The Introit has already joined royal acclaim to the Lamb’s death. The assembly therefore approaches the trial with a different understanding of victory from Pilate’s. It does not have to choose between a successful king and a suffering victim. Colossians identifies the same Son as the origin and sustainer of creation, the head of the Church, and the mediator of reconciliation. The proper Preface gathers priesthood and kingship around his self-offering. The Mass’s prayers ask that this victory become effective in divided human families and in the fidelity of those receiving the sacrament.

Two readings develop that movement. In the first, the King makes a people capable of hearing him. Augustine explains the kingdom’s different origin through regeneration and identifies hearing with obedient belief; Jerome explains the Son of Man’s received dominion through the Incarnation and the saints’ enduring inheritance. In the second, the Creator restores communion by giving himself. Chrysostom follows the Epistle from the Son’s creative primacy to reconciliation through his blood; Bellarmine explains the Psalter’s universal royal claims through justice, conversion, and final judgment. Each reading accounts for the whole formulary, but their questions differ: how does a person become a willing subject of this King, and what does his universal rule accomplish among the creatures he reconciles?

The feast celebrated here is *D. N. Iesu Christi Regis*, I class, in the Sanctorale of the 1962 *Missale Romanum*. Its appointed place is the last Sunday of October, which falls on 25 October in 2026. The Gloria, Creed and proper Preface belong to this occurrence. The otherwise occurring Sunday supplies no additional prayers. The eleven active elements appear below in their liturgical order. Psalm numbers follow the Missal’s Vulgate system; the usual Hebrew number appears in parentheses when different.[^1]

## The Propers: Detailed Commentary

### Introit: the victory retains the wound

The opening antiphon draws together Apocalypse 5:12 and 1:6. In the fifth chapter, John sees the Lamb standing as slain within the heavenly court. The slaughter is not an unfortunate prelude now forgotten by victorious heaven: it identifies the one who receives worship. The chapter’s movement from the closed book to the Lamb’s ability to open it locates royal worthiness in redemptive victory. Apocalypse 1 places kingdom and priesthood within an account of Christ’s faithful witness and saving blood. The composite chant brings those claims into one act of praise.

The received commentary of Victorinus on Apocalypse 5 connects the opening of the testament with the Lamb’s overcoming death and bringing humanity into life. It illuminates the chapter’s redemptive movement rather than explaining every term of the chant’s selected verse. The ANF delivery carries attribution and interpolation cautions; its contextual argument is useful without making that received text the sole authority for the interpretation.[^2]

The term *divinitatem* in the Latin acclamation must be heard with the Son’s identity in Colossians, not as an acquisition of divine nature after death. The acclamation celebrates the Lamb; the Epistle identifies him as the one through whom all creatures exist. The psalm verse then asks for judgment and justice. It makes right rule, rather than power considered alone, part of the assembly’s first approach to the King.

### Collect: the wound beneath division

The Collect diagnoses the scattering of peoples through sin. The movement from a universal horizon to the concrete wound of human division gives restoration a personal and social content. People cannot be healed simply by enlarging the territory under one administration. The prayer asks that their relation to Christ change, and describes his dominion by its gentleness. Its royal vocabulary therefore concerns the manner of rule as well as its extent.

The contrast becomes more exact in the Gospel: a king whose servants do not fight to prevent his delivery is heard, believed and obeyed. The Collect’s request prepares for that account of allegiance. Augustine’s explanation of regeneration and Chrysostom’s distinction between rescue and transfer show why the prayer needs a divine gift. A new allegiance is not produced merely by a better label for an unchanged captivity.[^3]

### Epistle: rescue, creation and reconciliation

The thanksgiving of Colossians 1:12–20 begins with the recipients. They have been made fit for an inheritance and transferred from darkness into the beloved Son’s kingdom. It then turns toward the identity of that Son. The sequence matters: the person who saves them is not one more member of the invisible world through whom they must climb to God. Visible and invisible creatures alike depend on him. The inventory of heavenly powers removes the possibility of reserving an exempt spiritual territory beyond his lordship.

The passage’s two uses of *firstborn* organize its account of primacy: the Son’s relation to creation and his relation to the dead. Creation, the Church and resurrection converge upon him. The latter part then names the Cross as the means of peace. The God who sustains beings does not reconcile them by withdrawing from their condition. Chrysostom emphasizes both the Creator’s continuing preservation of creatures and the extraordinary condescension of his joining himself to the Church as its head.[^4]

The next verses, Colossians 1:21–23, identify the recipients as former enemies and call them to perseverance. They prevent a careless inference from cosmic language to automatic salvation irrespective of response. The passage reaches all creation in its account of the Son’s authority; personal reconciliation remains a gift to be received and lived. The second interpretation develops Chrysostom’s answer to the most demanding phrase: what can reconciliation of things in heaven mean if holy angels have not sinned?

### Gradual: the reach of justice

Psalm 71:8 and 11 are two selected verses, not a continuous reading of verses 8–11. They extend dominion across seas and toward every nation’s rulers. The intervening and surrounding psalm describes homage and deliverance; verses 12–14 especially give the royal work its character in the rescue of the needy. Worldwide reach and care for the helpless belong to the same portrait.

Augustine begins his exposition with Christ as the true Solomon, the peacemaker whose kingdom exceeds that of the historical king. Bellarmine permits an immediate petition by David for Solomon’s just government before unfolding its fulfillment in Christ. The difference affects how the opening petition is heard: one begins directly with the fulfillment, the other gives the historical royal prayer its own place within it. Both understand the reach and endurance of the psalm to surpass Solomon.[^5]

### Alleluia: a kingdom that does not pass away

Daniel 7 first displays beastly kingdoms, then the heavenly court and the human figure who receives dominion. The selected concluding clauses of verse 14 celebrate that dominion’s endurance. The longer verse and the chapter explain whose dominion it is and how the saints belong to its future. The Mass does not sing the entire vision, but its unending kingdom has its meaning within that contrast between what rises from the earth and what God gives.

Jerome takes the figure’s humanity seriously. Received power does not make the eternal Son a lesser divinity; it is spoken of him according to the human nature he assumed and the humiliation described in Philippians 2. The same commentary denies that the saints’ inheritance is exhausted by a temporary earthly victory. Its endurance gives the Postcommunion’s hope of shared reign a horizon larger than this life’s political fortunes.[^6]

### Gospel: the prisoner questions the judge

The appointed scene is inside the governor’s hall. Pilate asks whether Jesus is King of the Jews; Jesus asks where that description comes from. The exchange discloses the accusation and exposes the questioner’s assumptions. Pilate is investigating a threat to authority. Jesus answers by identifying the source of his kingdom and the purpose of his coming into the world.

John 18 has already included Peter’s sword and Jesus’ refusal of it. In the trial, the absence of a fight to prevent his delivery becomes evidence for the kingdom’s different origin. Jesus then speaks of testimony and hearing. The appointment ends there, at verse 37. Pilate’s subsequent question about truth and his departure belong to the surrounding narrative, not to the appointed Gospel’s final sentence. The assembly is left with a call to hear. The narrative identifies particular speakers and authorities; its conflicts do not assign guilt to Jewish people across generations.[^7]

### Offertory: intercession for the nations

Psalm 2’s promise of the nations follows rebellion against the Lord and his anointed. At verse 8 the Son asks and the Father grants. Augustine relates this asking to the Incarnation, sacrifice and intercession. The Son’s eternal dignity and his temporal work for humanity belong to one person. At the offering, the nations are consequently the horizon of priestly petition, not a possession the congregation wins for itself.[^8]

Verse 9’s rod belongs to the psalm’s context, not the Offertory’s sung words. Bellarmine’s account includes correction of sinful habits and final judgment. The passage demands accountability before the Son; it supplies no commission for worshippers to impose conversion by violence.

### Secret: the sacrifice gives what division lacks

The Secret names human reconciliation and asks the Father that Christ, the Son offered in sacrifice, himself give unity and peace among the nations. Its logic joins the altar to the need named in the Collect. A divided human family cannot make its unity merely by declaring itself united. Addressed to the Father, the petition places its hope in the personal agency of the Son.

The relation to the Epistle is especially close: peace through the blood of the Cross becomes a sacrificial petition. Chrysostom’s insistence on the manner of reconciliation makes that connection intelligible. The giver does not remain outside the cost of the gift. The community asks for a peace purchased through his self-giving, which places its own enmities under judgment.

### Preface: the royal priest

The Preface names the eternal Priest and universal King together and places his self-offering on the Cross at the center of their unity. Its account of the kingdom proceeds through truth and life, holiness and grace, justice, love and peace. These qualities identify the life of the kingdom, rather than a second kingdom added to a purely political one. The royal offering reaches the Father and draws the assembly into thanksgiving with the heavenly host.

The sequence deepens the Epistle’s movement from cosmic authority to the Church and reconciliation. The Son who sustains creation is the priest who offers himself. His subjection of creatures and the kingdom’s presentation to the Father belong within this priestly work. Pius XI’s feast teaching likewise joins kingship to redemption and priesthood, while extending its moral claims to public life.[^9]

### Communion and Postcommunion: peace that perseveres

The Communion takes selected clauses from Psalm 28:10–11. The psalm’s storm, powerful divine voice and disrupted landscape precede its conclusion in royal permanence and peace. Augustine reads the Church as exposed to the world’s flood while receiving peace in Christ. The antiphon therefore promises something stronger than an agreeable interval between troubles: a blessing grounded in the King’s enduring rule. Bellarmine preserves both natural and ecclesial readings of the psalm and relates present strength to final blessing.[^10]

The Postcommunion brings sacramental nourishment, active service and heavenly reign together. Its military image is governed by the King’s conduct in the Gospel and by the peace requested at the altar. Fidelity includes struggle, but the battle that would prevent his self-offering has already been refused. The final petition looks toward lasting fellowship with him. Receiving the sacrament opens into persevering service; service reaches its end in the gift of shared reign.

## The King Makes a People Capable of Hearing Him {#lane-hearing-king}

The kingdom Jesus describes to Pilate is present among human beings, yet its life comes from another source. Its subjects are made anew. The Mass expresses this change as rescue from darkness, healing of division, obedient hearing and perseverance toward an enduring inheritance. Augustine’s exposition of John explains entry into this kingdom; Jerome’s exposition of Daniel explains the lasting life its members receive in the incarnate Son.

### In the world, but with another origin

Pilate hears a claim to kingship within the practical categories of his office. A king has subjects, interests and means of resistance. A claimant delivered by his own authorities and lacking an armed rescue appears either defeated or dangerous. Jesus’ answer changes the question from the kingdom’s military capacity to its origin. Augustine attends closely to the difference between a kingdom that is not *in* the world and one that is not *of* it. Jesus says the latter. The distinction allows Augustine to identify the kingdom with believers who remain in the world, while refusing to derive their new life from the world.[^11]

This answer is concrete. A people who remain here still have neighbors, work, households, rulers and responsibilities. Their baptism does not remove them from human history. Augustine recalls Christ’s prayer that his disciples be kept from evil while remaining in the world. He also recalls the mixture of wheat and tares until the harvest. The kingdom’s present existence does not imply the present purification of every member or the disappearance of all evil within its visible reach. Its origin and its final perfection answer different questions.

The contrast between worldly origin and presence in the world is especially useful beside the Gradual. Its worldwide reach could otherwise be mistaken for a map of successful Christian administration. Augustine instead asks what makes the people a kingdom at all. They were under the prince of this world and become Christ’s people through regeneration. At precisely this point he cites Colossians 1:13, the transfer into the beloved Son’s kingdom. The connection between the Gospel and Epistle is thus already present in his actual argument: changed citizenship names a saving change in persons, not a relocation to another part of the earth.

The Collect gives that argument a communal voice. Sin is a wound that disperses human families. The petition for Christ’s gentle dominion concerns the cure of that wound. A society can share an external authority while remaining divided by envy, fear and hatred. Augustine’s account explains why the gathering asked for in the prayer reaches more deeply than an administrative settlement. The King creates willing allegiance through grace. Its gentleness does not make the claim less demanding; it identifies the healer whose demand restores the persons who obey him.

The Gospel’s absence of armed rescue now has positive content. The disciples cannot bring this kingdom into existence by fighting to prevent Jesus’ delivery. Such a rescue would oppose the very self-giving through which the Lamb makes a people. The Introit therefore supplies the proper beginning for this interpretation. The assembly praises someone who has been slain, and approaches him as the source of its life. Its first act is thanksgiving for a victory it did not manufacture.

### Why hearing is more than receiving information

At the end of the appointed Gospel, Jesus connects belonging to truth with hearing his voice. The order of the statement troubles an easy account of conversion. One might suppose that a hearer independently recognizes a true proposition and thereby earns the status of belonging to truth. Augustine reads the sentence in the opposite direction. A person hears because the truth has given him a share in itself. Hearing is the fruit of grace, not a prior achievement that obliges grace to follow.[^12]

He reaches this conclusion by distinguishing two ways of being related to truth. Every human being is a creature made through the divine Word. That universal dependence cannot by itself explain the hearing named here, because not everyone believes. Jesus is speaking of a further gift. Augustine describes its effect as the inward hearing of the person who obeys and believes. The hearer has not merely received sound or understood the grammar of an assertion. The heart is turned toward the speaker as trustworthy Lord.

The distinction protects both grace and responsibility. If hearing meant only exposure to words, Pilate’s conversation would already constitute full obedience. If it meant an independent achievement preceding grace, salvation would become the reward of a merit the sinner supplied unaided. Augustine’s account instead identifies an enabled response. The listener really believes and obeys, but that response begins in Christ’s gracious calling. Thanksgiving, which opens the Epistle, is consequently the fitting speech of someone who has become a hearer.

Chrysostom’s reading of Colossians 1:12 makes the same gift vivid from another angle: the Father has not simply offered an inheritance but made the recipients fit to share it. A gift can stand before someone who has no capacity to use it; divine generosity also changes the recipient. The transfer from darkness is therefore richer than release from a prison. It brings the rescued into a royal relationship.[^13]

This is why the Preface’s truth, life, holiness and grace belong together. Truth is not detached information about a remote ruler. It is the truth of the Son who gives life and sanctifies those he gathers. Holiness describes the new relation’s character; grace identifies its gratuitous source. The qualities of the kingdom answer the Gospel’s question about the sort of King standing before Pilate, while the Gospel gives those qualities a personal center.

A concrete moral consequence follows. To say that one belongs to Christ while refusing every correction is to divide allegiance from hearing. To treat another person’s conversion as a trophy of one’s own cleverness is to forget the prior gift. The Mass trains a different response: gratitude for having been made fit, patient witness to others, and willingness to have one’s own habits corrected. These are consequences of Augustine’s account of hearing, not conditions by which a sinner purchases the first grace.

### How the eternal Son receives dominion

Daniel’s vision presents another difficulty. The human figure receives power and a kingdom before the Ancient of Days. If Christ is eternally divine, why is dominion given to him? Jerome addresses the problem by attending to the figure’s humanity. The Son of Man signifies the Son of God’s assumption of human flesh. The giving of power, honor and kingdom is read through the Incarnation and the self-emptying described by Paul in Philippians 2.[^14]

The divine Son has not advanced from the condition of a creature into divinity. He has truly assumed the condition in which obedience, humiliation and exaltation can be predicated of him. Jerome’s reasoning therefore refuses to solve the difficulty by making the humanity unreal. The Son’s received glory belongs to the saving economy of the one who became man. The very humanity through which he suffers is not abandoned when he reigns.

This distinction matters for the people Augustine describes. Their hope is not merely to watch a divine power remain secure above their vulnerability. The incarnate King brings human nature into the enduring kingdom. Daniel 7:18 and 27 associate the saints with its possession. Jerome contrasts their heavenly inheritance with kingdoms that arise from earth and return to it. He rejects an account that exhausts the promise in Maccabean victory, because the promised universality and permanence exceed that event; he also rejects an earthly millennial kingdom as the final form of the saints’ inheritance.[^15]

Augustine and Jerome thus answer different parts of one question. Augustine explains how former subjects of darkness become Christ’s people. Jerome explains why their kingdom can be received through a genuinely human King without making his divine nature inferior or their final inheritance another temporary empire. The Alleluia’s endurance gives their changed membership a future. The Postcommunion can ask for shared heavenly reign because the Son has made humanity his own and the saints belong to his kingdom.

The Introit’s acclaim of the Lamb can be heard within the same doctrinal relation. The slain one receives honor; the Epistle identifies him as uncreated source of creatures. His redemptive victory makes divine glory manifest in the humanity he has assumed. Receiving royal acclaim and possessing eternal divinity are not competing descriptions of two different Christs. The priestly King of the Preface is one person, and his people live from his one saving work.

### Intercession gathers what sin has scattered

The Offertory turns from the assembly’s hearing to the Son’s asking. The Father promises the nations as his inheritance. Augustine reads Psalm 2:8 within the temporal economy of incarnate sacrifice and intercession. The Son asks for the salvation of those who will be his possession. His people are not assembled by the self-assertion of one nation against another, but through the priestly agency of their King.[^16]

This makes the Offertory a fitting passage from proclamation into sacrifice. The peoples named by the Collect and Gradual enter the horizon of petition. Their number and distance do not make them strangers to the Son’s concern. Nor can the faithful congratulate themselves as though they already represented a humanity fully reconciled. The Secret asks the Father that the Son give unity and peace precisely because the gifts remain necessary. The one offered is himself the giver who can gather them.

Bellarmine’s treatment of Psalm 2 helps locate the struggle involved. The Lord’s authority can break the habits that enslave a person. Correction is mercy when it destroys a sinful attachment and frees the person to obey; judgment also remains real for resistance that refuses the King. The image cannot be transferred into a permission for the congregation to break other people. The Gospel has supplied the decisive image of royal action: Christ gives himself, and his servants hear.[^17]

### The peace of pilgrims

The Communion’s blessing could be misunderstood as a promise that obedience will produce an untroubled life. Psalm 28 has prepared a stronger account. The voice of the Lord resounds through disruption, and the King remains. Augustine reads the concluding verses as the Church’s preservation amid the world’s flood and its future reign with Christ. Peace in Christ is compatible with adversity in the world.[^18]

The difference changes how perseverance is understood. A difficult week does not prove that the blessing failed. It may be precisely the setting in which the received gift must be lived. Bellarmine likewise distinguishes the strength needed on the journey from the final blessing toward which it leads. The Postcommunion places nourishment before continued service and shared reign. Those who have heard the King’s voice need continuing sustenance; their first response does not make further dependence unnecessary.

The movement of this reading is therefore from redemption to regenerated hearing, from hearing to intercession for all peoples, and from sacramental nourishment to faithful perseverance. Every royal claim concerns a King who makes his subjects capable of life with him. Universal reach keeps the argument from shrinking into private feeling; the heavenly inheritance keeps it from identifying salvation with a successful political settlement.

Literal.

: Jesus answers a royal accusation before Pilate; Colossians gives thanks for deliverance and transfer; Daniel presents an enduring kingdom; the prayers ask for healed allegiance, peace and perseverance.

Allegorical.

: The incarnate Son gathers the Church through redemption and regeneration. His received human glory opens the enduring kingdom to the saints whose life comes from him.

Moral.

: Hearing becomes obedient faith. Gratitude displaces self-sufficiency, intercession reaches other peoples, and correction of sinful habits serves faithful life together.

Anagogical.

: The pilgrim Church receives peace amid present conflict and hopes for lasting reign with Christ. Its final inheritance surpasses every temporary earthly kingdom.

## The Creator Restores Communion by Giving Himself {#lane-reconciled-creation}

The authority celebrated by the Mass is the authority of the one through whom the world exists. Yet the Epistle directs that cosmic claim toward the blood of the Cross. The Creator restores communion by entering the cost of reconciliation himself. Chrysostom explains the identity and action of this reconciling Son; Bellarmine explains how the universal royal claims of the Psalter concern justice, conversion and a recognition still awaiting completion. Together their arguments make peace the content of royal victory, rather than a pleasant consequence added after it.

### The firstborn and the origin of every power

Colossians names the Son the firstborn of all creation. The phrase can appear to place him at the head of a sequence of creatures. Chrysostom refuses that inference by following the clauses that explain it: all things were created through him, and all continue to depend on him. The Son cannot be assigned to the class whose existence he causes. The vocabulary of birth must be read within the passage’s account of creative agency.[^19]

Chrysostom’s exposition develops the point through the distinction between visible and invisible creatures. Invisible powers might seem to offer a higher route to God than the human Christ. Paul names them within creation precisely to exclude that exemption. Thrones, dominations, principalities and powers are not rival sources of being. Their greatness remains received. The Son’s primacy is therefore more radical than superiority in rank within a common order.

Nor does his work stop with an initial act of creation. Chrysostom explains that creatures would cease to stand if separated from the Son’s sustaining providence. Their present existence is dependent. The ruler who resists him, the angel who serves him and the ordinary believer who prays to him share this creaturely dependence, though their moral relations to him differ. This distinction will matter when Bellarmine distinguishes universal authority from willing obedience.

The Epistle next names the Son head of the Church. Chrysostom hears the astonishing generosity of the transition: the one above all joins himself to those below. His account of divine dignity becomes an account of love. The firstborn from the dead gives resurrection the same personal center as creation. The Creator does not discard the world that needs reconciliation; the risen head establishes a new life for his members.

The Preface places that identity within priestly thanksgiving. The universal King offers himself on the Cross. His dominion is not secured by obtaining something outside him that could supply a power he lacks. The giver of being gives himself for those whose enmity cannot be cured by the fact of existence alone. Creation explains why all are his; redemption explains how enemies can come to live with him in peace.

### Why the blood of the Cross matters to the argument

It is possible to acknowledge that reconciliation happened and pass too quickly over its manner. Chrysostom makes the manner decisive. He distinguishes the greatness of reconciliation itself from the further wonder that God accomplishes it through the Son himself, through death, and through the Cross. The one who makes peace bears its cost personally. The Cross is not an independent instrument with a saving power detached from the one who gives himself upon it.[^20]

This is the point at which the feast’s royal claims become most demanding. A king might pacify a territory by suppressing everyone who resists. Christ reconciles the estranged through his own self-giving. His conduct before Pilate belongs to that work. The absence of a fight to prevent delivery is not evidence that his reconciling mission has failed. It permits the Passion through which the Epistle says peace is made. The Gospel’s truth and the Epistle’s Cross belong to the life of the same person.

The Introit has made the relation audible from the first moment. Heavenly worship recognizes the slain Lamb. Victory has not erased his sacrificial identity. The Collect then names the wounded human families who need restoration. The Secret brings both sides into one petition: the Father is asked that the offered Christ give the peoples unity and peace. The prayer does not treat unity as something the congregation produces by assembling in one place. The sacrificial giver is its source.

Such peace has a moral shape. It does not require pretending that sin caused no wound or that hostility is a trivial misunderstanding. The Epistle names reconciliation because enmity is real. Its generosity is measured by the actual breach it heals. A community that refuses to name wrongdoing cannot recognize the need for reconciliation; one that treats wrongdoers as beyond every possibility of conversion denies the purpose of the King’s self-giving. The proper prayers hold the wound and the hoped-for healing together.

### What is reconciled in heaven?

The last phrase of the Epistle raises a problem deeper than the scale of earthly conflict. Things in heaven also belong to reconciliation. Chrysostom asks how there can be war among heavenly beings when heaven is the model of obedience invoked in the Lord’s Prayer. His answer does not make the holy angels sinners. The breach lies between heaven and sinful humanity: the angels oppose the dishonor humanity has done to their Lord.[^21]

The solution is correspondingly relational. Christ brings humanity into heavenly company and removes the estrangement. Chrysostom points to the angels’ joyful appearance among human beings and to humanity’s reception in heaven. Earth is reconciled both internally and with heaven; heaven receives the human beings formerly alienated from it. The result is communion across a divide that no created intermediary could heal by independent power.

This explains why Chrysostom turns against reliance upon angels as substitutes for Christ. Their ministry is real, but they are themselves servants within the order the Son reconciles. To make them alternative saviors would mistake the beneficiaries and witnesses of his peace for its independent source. The Son’s creative primacy and reconciling action thus answer one connected concern: no invisible power can offer a saving route that bypasses him.

The interpretation also gives the Preface’s heavenly company particular force. The assembly’s thanksgiving joins angelic worship after proclaiming the priestly King’s self-offering. Human beings do not enter that praise by claiming they have become divine or ceased to be creatures. They enter through the reconciliation accomplished by the Son. The Introit’s heavenly court and the Communion’s blessing of peace are two moments within this shared life.

The universality of the claim must retain the Epistle’s immediate context. Colossians 1:21–23 turns from the cosmic horizon to former enemies and their perseverance in faith. Chrysostom’s explanation of heavenly reconciliation concerns holy angels and humanity, not universal pardon for every rebellious spiritual being. Nor does the scale of Christ’s work imply that every human person already enjoys saving friendship with him. The kingdom’s reach and the response of its subjects remain distinct.

### All kings, and the recognition still to come

The Gradual declares a dominion embracing all nations and rulers. Bellarmine asks how such language can be true when willing acknowledgment is visibly incomplete. His commentary on Psalm 71:11 presents three ways the universal claim bears upon Christ. It can describe widespread conversion in the scriptural manner of speaking generally while allowing exceptions; it can look toward final judgment, when acknowledgment will be complete; and it can name the actual authority Christ already has over all, with or without their consent.[^22]

The alternatives are not interchangeable. Conversion describes a change in the subject. Final acknowledgment describes an encounter with judgment that cannot be evaded. Present authority describes the King’s right and power even where the subject resists. Confusing these claims produces two opposite errors: announcing that everyone already obeys willingly, or deciding that resistance proves Christ lacks universal authority. Bellarmine avoids both by explaining what each sense asserts.

His reading of the psalm’s beginning is equally important. The prayer for the king’s judgment may concern David and Solomon in its immediate historical reference. Solomon can receive a responsibility to govern justly; the request is intelligible within Israel’s royal life. Yet the psalm’s endurance and scope carry the interpretation beyond him. Bellarmine moves from the historical king to Christ rather than forcing every promise to be exhausted by Solomon’s reign. Augustine begins instead from Christ as true Solomon and peacemaker. Their disagreement concerns the immediate scope of the opening, while both find its fulfillment in Christ.[^23]

The verses following universal homage explain why people turn to this King. He rescues the helpless. Bellarmine develops that rescue as liberation from the devil’s captivity through forgiveness and restoration of grace. Human poverty is not a reason for the King to despise his subjects; it is the condition in which they discover their need of him. Bellarmine’s exposition therefore joins the Psalm’s royal scope to the Epistle’s transfer from darkness. He supplies more than another assertion that Christ is powerful: he explains the saving attraction and justice of his rule.[^24]

At the offering, Psalm 2:8 extends the same horizon to the ends of the earth. Augustine relates the Son’s asking to priestly intercession; Bellarmine relates the inheritance to the Son’s identity and rightful possession. The nations are not marginal to Christian prayer. Neither are they already indistinguishable from a fully obedient people. The Collect and Secret keep asking for a healing that the universal King can give and that divided humanity continues to need.

### The Church’s peace and the feud at its door

Chrysostom does not allow reconciliation to remain a grand picture remote from ordinary conduct. His homily turns to the Church’s repeated exchange of peace. The greeting is directed to all. A person who maintains peace with one neighbor and a feud with another cannot treat partial agreement as the health of the whole body. Chrysostom compares such selective peace to a body in which some members are at rest while others remain disordered. The body is not healthy merely because the disorder is localized.[^25]

The comparison changes the meaning of the liturgical greeting. It is not a decorative word laid over unchanged relationships. The Church asks for a gift whose demands reach the person one would prefer to exclude. Chrysostom’s conclusion grows directly from his exegesis: the Son came to reconcile heaven and earth, so a recipient of that peace contradicts the gift by deliberately preserving enmity. The universality that appeared immense in the Gradual becomes exacting in one ordinary relationship.

The Communion and Postcommunion place this challenge within grace. Peace is the King’s blessing, and nourishment sustains the service that follows. A Christian is not asked to invent reconciliation out of personal resources, but to act from a gift received. Augustine’s reading of Psalm 28 also keeps peace from being confused with immunity to opposition. Perseverance can require truthfulness, patient correction and resistance to wrongdoing; it cannot make hatred the principle of Christian fidelity.

The Alleluia finally protects this work from despair. The kingdom outlasts the unstable powers of Daniel’s vision. Present failures of willing acknowledgment do not determine its final horizon. Jerome’s account of the Son’s received human dominion and the saints’ heavenly inheritance agrees with the hope of the Postcommunion. What the Creator begins through the Cross leads toward communion that death and political change cannot dissolve.

Literal.

: Colossians joins creation, headship and the Cross; the royal Psalms join dominion to justice; Jesus’ trial discloses a kingship whose witness proceeds through his delivery; the prayers seek reconciliation and peace.

Allegorical.

: The one Son is Creator, incarnate head, priestly victim and King. His self-giving restores humanity to communion with God and heavenly worship, and gathers his Church from the nations.

Moral.

: Those who receive peace must oppose their own feuds and care for the helpless. Royal justice requires conversion and public responsibility; voluntary allegiance is more than unavoidable subjection to authority.

Anagogical.

: Universal authority will receive complete acknowledgment at judgment. Saving communion reaches the eternal kingdom, while present resistance and imperfect recognition neither exhaust nor annul Christ’s reign.

## One Kingship, Two Questions

The first reading asks how a person becomes a subject who hears. Augustine’s answer begins with grace: the kingdom’s origin is not the world, and inward hearing follows the gift of belonging to truth. Jerome supplies the enduring human inheritance opened by the incarnate Son. The principal movement is from rescue to obedient faith and perseverance. Its distinctive temptation is self-sufficiency, whether expressed as confidence in one’s own merit or in force that claims to manufacture the kingdom.

The second reading asks what universal lordship does for estranged creatures. Chrysostom’s answer begins with the Creator who sustains all and gives himself to reconcile them; Bellarmine distinguishes rightful authority, willing conversion and the acknowledgment of judgment. The principal movement is from creative primacy through sacrifice into shared peace. Its distinctive temptation is to confess a universal kingdom while preserving private enmity or overlooking the helpless whom royal justice protects.

Their agreement does not erase their different starting points. Both locate the kingdom within the world without deriving it from worldly force. Both depend upon grace, sacrifice and a future not reducible to political success. Yet the first exposes a mistaken account of entry, while the second exposes a mistaken account of communion. The believer who listens humbly still has to learn what reconciliation demands; the believer who works for peace still receives the capacity to hear as a gift.

The genuine difference between Augustine and Bellarmine on Psalm 71 remains useful within that agreement. Augustine introduces Christ as the true Solomon; Bellarmine allows the opening prayer its immediate royal setting before tracing the fulfillment beyond it. The former brings the peacemaker directly into view; the latter shows how historical justice and Christological fulfillment belong together. Neither licenses the conclusion that the psalm’s universal homage is already the willing obedience of every ruler.

The feast ends by asking for lasting reign with the King whose life has been given for his people. Hearing and peace converge there. A kingdom whose truth never reaches obedience has been reduced to information; a peace that leaves every chosen feud intact has been reduced to a greeting. The Mass gives both a deeper reality: persons made fit for the Son’s inheritance, and a people learning to live from the reconciliation his Cross accomplishes.

### H1. The Psalms: attribution, occasion and writing {#history-psalms}

The Davidic reference era comes from John Corbett’s *King David* in the *Catholic Encyclopedia*, IV, opening paragraph. Corbett calls it the usual chronology and immediately records proposals that place the reign thirty to fifty years later. The displayed range dates the attributed figure. It neither locates every poem within his reign nor supplies the date of a particular performance.

The three Psalms reach David through different evidence. Psalm 28’s title names him. Psalm 2 has no such name in the inspected title, but Acts 4:24–28 gives its Davidic attribution in apostolic reception. Psalm 71’s title concerns Solomon; Bellarmine understands its opening as David’s prayer for the newly anointed king. That is his interpretation of the speaker, not the wording of a title that explicitly names David as author. Augustine begins directly from Christ, the true Solomon. The difference belongs to the reception of the psalm and does not establish a new composition date.

The historical occasions remain uncertain. Psalm 28’s tabernacle-completion title does not identify a securely dated building project; its Libanus and Cades belong to the psalm’s storm world. Psalm 2 names Sion and the nations without dating the appointment of its royal speaker. Bellarmine’s accession setting for Psalm 71 is an exegetical identification. No shared precise place of writing follows from these different settings.

The composition boundary comes from the USCCB NABRE introduction to Psalms, in its discussion of superscriptions and dating. It describes a pre-Maccabean limit alongside the inability to date individual psalms securely. Thus “before” remains part of the answer. The boundary is not an estimate that these poems were written near its endpoint. The complete contemporary introduction was independently checked in the reviewed research; its historical judgment is distinct from Corbett’s conventional chronology and the received attributions.[^26]

### H2. Daniel: Babylon and the heavenly court {#history-daniel}

Daniel 7:1 places the vision in the first year of Baltasar, king of Babylon. The heavenly court is the visionary scene, not a terrestrial place of composition. The table’s seventy years describes Babylonian servitude in the received historical frame, supported by Jeremiah 25:11–12. A duration cannot be used as a calendar year for the Son of Man’s reception of dominion.

Francis Gigot’s *Book of Daniel*, CE IV, authorship discussion, reports the displayed traditional composition range and the competing theory locating the book’s formation in the Maccabean crisis under Antiochus IV. Those are different accounts of the book’s history. Jerome’s rejection of a fulfillment exhausted by the Maccabees concerns the meaning and endurance of the promised kingdom; it is not a modern critical judgment about the date of composition. The interpretation of the prophecy and the circumstances of the book’s formation remain distinguishable questions.[^27]

### H3. John: the trial and the Gospel’s formation {#history-john}

The narrated scene is at Jerusalem, in the governor’s hall, in the morning of the Passion sequence. John 18:28 and 33 locate the interrogation; John 19:14–18 carries the narrative toward sentence and execution. A chronological binding for the Crucifixion cannot simply be attached to this distinct scene. The table therefore retains an unresolved date for the interrogation. Howlett’s *Biblical Chronology*, CE III, Passion discussion, preserves several alternative years and expressly declines certainty; its disagreement supplies context rather than a selected date for this row.

The received attribution names John son of Zebedee, and Ephesus is the traditional writing place. The *Catholic Encyclopedia* articles *New Testament*, XIV, dating discussion, and *Gospel of St John*, VIII, circumstances of composition, supply the two displayed late composition ranges. The latter acknowledges the lack of certain historical information and proposes a year within the last decade rather than an exact completion date established by contemporary documentation.

The USCCB NABRE introduction to John distinguishes the eyewitness testimony underlying the tradition from authorship of the finished Gospel. It reports that most modern scholars do not confidently identify that witness with Zebedee’s son. Its account of successive editing points to chapter 21, the adapted prologue hymn and discontinuities in the farewell discourse. Neither the number nor identities of all contributors are established. Alongside Ephesus it reports proposals for Syria, possibly Antioch, and Alexandria. These concern the writing and formation of the Gospel; the trial remains at Jerusalem. The displayed composition ranges are not reassigned as exact dates for particular editorial layers.[^28]

### H4. Colossians: the prison and the recipients {#history-colossians}

Pauline authorship and Roman captivity form the received setting used by the displayed chronology. The *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s *St Paul*, XI, chronology list, supplies the single-year answer; *Epistle to the Colossians*, IV, opening and captivity discussion, supplies the wider range. Different precision need not imply a direct contradiction. The figures date the letter’s writing, not the creation or reconciliation it proclaims. Colossae is the recipients’ city and cannot be substituted for the prison’s location.

The USCCB NABRE introduction notes that the letter does not identify the prison and that Paul’s several imprisonments complicate dating. It reports Roman, Caesarean and Ephesian proposals, and also the possibility of a pupil or follower writing in Paul’s name. It does not decide between that account and direct Pauline authorship or supply the follower with a definite city and date. The traditional Roman chronology therefore remains identified as such, beside the distinct uncertainties of authorship and imprisonment.[^29]

### H5. Apocalypse: the seer’s place and the vision {#history-apocalypse}

The two Apocalypse passages in the Introit share a book-composition answer. The Psalm verse does not share that answer. The *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s *Apocalypse*, I, time-and-place section, presents Patmos and testimony connecting the work with the end of Domitian’s reign. The whole reign provides a broad bound, not a statement that each year is equally probable. The *New Testament* article supplies the alternative approximate late range and acknowledges an earlier Neronian proposal. The selected late chronology does not exhaust the history of scholarly positions.

Patmos belongs to the received seer’s setting; the heavenly throne room belongs to the vision. Neither should be mistaken for the other. Apocalypse 1 situates the witness amid suffering and patient endurance, while chapter 5 displays worship around the Lamb. That literary relation supports the study’s interpretation without assigning a historical date to the eternal kingship being acclaimed.[^30]

## Appendix: Scope and Qualifications

This is a study of the universal 1962 Roman Missal formulary for Our Lord Jesus Christ the King on 25 October 2026. It covers all eleven active proper elements and retains the two inactive seasonal votive witnesses in the preceding appendix. Unspecified local calendars and privileged local occurrences are outside its scope. It is a study aid, not an official liturgical book or a substitute for the governing rubrics.

The full Latin follows the independently collated 1962 controller, printed pp. 697–698 and 291. Its preexisting proper spans were compared with *Acta Apostolicae Sedis* XVII (1925), pp. 666–668; the common Latin framing has an independently checked 1862 Pustet antecedent. The English reproduces the continuous Lasance–Walsh witness, revised edition of 1945, in the registered later printing, pp. 1257–1260 and 813–814, with common framing from pp. 757, 772 and 819. It is historical lay-book English, not an approved English translation for celebration. The historical proper Latin and specified English body pages have separately recorded United States public-domain bases; that finding does not clear entire facsimiles or excluded later inserts. Third-party wording is not offered under the project’s license.

The source record preserves the English witness’s printed *familes*, repeated *the* in the Epistle heading, *The the Gentiles* in the Offertory, and punctuation peculiarities. Lasance’s Latin reporting verb at Pilate’s second question differs from the 1962 controller; the latter governs the Latin printed here. Its older Sunday-commemoration instruction is excluded. The Introit’s psalm petition is numbered 71:1 by the Missal but falls at 71:2 in the inspected American-1899 Douay delivery, whose title occupies verse 1. The Communion selects clauses from both Psalm 28:10 and 11. The Secret’s final dialogue and Preface’s opening represent one shared ceremonial transition, displayed at both textual boundaries, not repeated in celebration.

The full inspection outcomes, textual expansions and evidence ownership are in `propers/verified.md` and `research/scope.md`.

The two whole-formulary interpretations are editorial syntheses grounded in the checked arguments of the named Fathers and saints. Their sources expound particular biblical texts, not this feast Mass as a whole. The reception inquiry checked the major accessible witnesses recorded in the research matrix; it did not establish exhaustive coverage. No direct later saintly exposition of Apocalypse 5:12 was verified. The received Victorinus text has attribution and interpolation qualifications and is contextual support only. The NPNF rendition of Augustine’s Psalm 71 contains printed omissions; it is not represented as a complete examination of the Latin exposition. Modern editorial notes in NPNF remain distinct from the Fathers’ arguments. Pius XI supplies the institution and doctrinal context of the feast, not an eligible saint counted among the interpretations’ carriers.

The historical appendix preserves the canonical chronology’s alternatives and unresolved states, alongside the reviewed modern Catholic scholarly qualifications for John and Colossians. Chronology dates its stated subjects; it does not date creation, divine kingship or the eternal kingdom. The study carries no claim of ecclesiastical approval or human specialist review.
