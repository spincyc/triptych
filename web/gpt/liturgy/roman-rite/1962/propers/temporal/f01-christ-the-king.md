# Our Lord Jesus Christ the King: Hearing the King and Receiving His Peace

*An expansive study of the 1962 Roman Missal texts for 25 October 2026*

**Our Lord Jesus Christ the King**

Hearing the King and Receiving His Peace

1962 Roman Missal — Expansive study

Last Sunday of October: 25 October 2026

## A King Who Gives His Life

Christ stands before a governor who can order his death, and the Mass calls him King of the universe. How can the prisoner who declines armed rescue be the Lord to whom every ruler owes allegiance? Colossians identifies this same Son as the one through whom all things were created and through whose blood peace is made. His refusal of rescue allows the Passion to proceed; his death reconciles the people over whom he reigns.

The Introit acclaims the Lamb as slain, and the proper Preface names Christ as Priest and King in its account of his self-offering. The Mass’s prayers ask for the reconciliation of divided human families and for shared heavenly reign with Christ. The Postcommunion is prayed by those already nourished by the sacrament who glory in serving him. Their present service can be understood as perseverance toward the reign they ask to share.

The first interpretation concerns how a person becomes a willing subject of this King. Augustine explains the kingdom’s different origin through regeneration and identifies hearing with obedient belief; Jerome explains the Son of Man’s received dominion through the Incarnation and the saints’ enduring inheritance. The second concerns what the King’s universal rule accomplishes among estranged creatures. Chrysostom follows the Epistle from the Son’s creative primacy to reconciliation through his blood; Bellarmine explains the Psalter’s universal royal claims through justice, conversion, and final judgment.

The feast celebrated here is *D. N. Iesu Christi Regis*, I class, in the Sanctorale of the 1962 *Missale Romanum*. Its appointed place is the last Sunday of October, which falls on 25 October in 2026. The Gloria, Creed and proper Preface belong to this occurrence. The otherwise occurring Sunday supplies no additional prayers. The eleven texts used at this Mass appear below in their liturgical order. Psalm numbers follow the Missal’s Vulgate system; the usual Hebrew number appears in parentheses when different.[^1]

## The Propers: Detailed Commentary

### Introit: the Lamb slain and acclaimed

The opening antiphon draws together Apocalypse 5:12 and 1:6. In the fifth chapter, John sees the Lamb standing as slain within the heavenly court. The Lamb is worthy to open the closed book because he was slain and redeemed a people. The heavenly worshippers acclaim him in the words selected for the Introit. Apocalypse 1 likewise names kingdom and priesthood in its account of Christ’s faithful witness and saving blood.

The received commentary of Victorinus on Apocalypse 5 connects the opening of the testament with the Lamb’s overcoming death and bringing humanity into life. It expounds the chapter as a whole, without explaining every term of the chant’s selected verse. The ANF edition raises attribution and interpolation questions about this received commentary.[^2]

The Latin acclamation includes *divinitatem*, divinity, among the things the Lamb is worthy to receive. The Epistle identifies him as the one through whom all creatures exist. Read together, these passages concern the acclaim of the divine Son, not his acquisition of a divine nature after death. The Introit’s psalm verse asks for judgment and justice, so that the assembly’s praise includes a petition for just rule.

### Collect: sin and divided peoples

The Collect asks for the restoration of all things in the beloved Son, then names the peoples scattered by the wound of sin. The division for which it seeks healing is therefore both a relation to Christ and a relation among human families. It asks that they be gathered under his dominion and describes that dominion as gentle.

The Gospel describes allegiance to Christ as hearing his voice. Augustine explains how regeneration makes that hearing possible; Chrysostom distinguishes rescue from darkness from the further gift of transfer into the Son’s kingdom. Their accounts help explain the change for which the Collect asks: people estranged by sin need to be made capable of life under Christ’s rule.[^3]

### Epistle: rescue, creation and reconciliation

The thanksgiving of Colossians 1:12–20 begins with the recipients. They have been made fit for an inheritance and transferred from darkness into the beloved Son’s kingdom. It then identifies the Son as the one through whom all creatures exist. The person who saves them is therefore not one of the invisible creatures through whom they must climb to God. Visible and invisible creatures alike depend on him, including every heavenly power Paul names.

The passage uses *firstborn* twice: of the Son’s relation to creation and of his relation to the dead. The one through whom creatures exist is also the risen head of the Church. The latter part then names the Cross as the means of peace. Chrysostom emphasizes both the Creator’s continuing preservation of creatures and the extraordinary condescension of his joining himself to the Church as its head.[^4]

The next verses, Colossians 1:21–23, identify the recipients as former enemies and call them to perseverance. The Son’s authority over all creation is distinct from the personal reconciliation they have received and must live. Universal authority does not imply automatic salvation irrespective of response. The second interpretation develops Chrysostom’s answer to the phrase about things in heaven: what can their reconciliation mean if holy angels have not sinned?

### Gradual: the reach of justice

Psalm 71:8 and 11 are two selected verses, not a continuous reading of verses 8–11. They describe dominion across seas and over every nation’s rulers. The intervening and surrounding psalm describes homage and deliverance; verses 12–14 recount the king’s rescue of the needy. The king who receives universal homage cares for the helpless.

Augustine begins his exposition with Christ as the true Solomon, the peacemaker whose kingdom exceeds that of the historical king. Bellarmine permits an immediate petition by David for Solomon’s just government before unfolding its fulfillment in Christ. The difference affects how the opening petition is heard: one begins directly with the fulfillment, the other gives the historical royal prayer its own place within it. Both understand the reach and endurance of the psalm to surpass Solomon.[^5]

### Alleluia: a kingdom that does not pass away

Daniel 7 first displays beastly kingdoms, then the heavenly court and the human figure who receives dominion. The selected concluding clauses of verse 14 celebrate that dominion’s endurance. The longer verse and the chapter explain whose dominion it is and how the saints belong to its future. The Mass does not sing the entire vision, but its unending kingdom has its meaning within that contrast between what rises from the earth and what God gives.

Jerome explains received power according to the human nature the eternal Son assumed and the humiliation described in Philippians 2. It does not imply an inferior divine nature. The same commentary denies that the saints’ inheritance is exhausted by a temporary earthly victory. His account of their enduring kingdom helps explain the hope of shared heavenly reign in the Postcommunion.[^6]

### Gospel: the prisoner questions the judge

The appointed scene is inside the governor’s hall. Pilate asks whether Jesus is King of the Jews; Jesus asks where that description comes from. The exchange discloses the accusation and exposes the questioner’s assumptions. Pilate is investigating a threat to authority. Jesus answers by identifying the source of his kingdom and the purpose of his coming into the world.

John 18 has already included Peter’s sword and Jesus’ refusal of it. In the trial, the absence of a fight to prevent his delivery becomes evidence for the kingdom’s different origin. Jesus then speaks of testimony and hearing. The appointment ends there, at verse 37. Pilate’s subsequent question about truth and his departure belong to the surrounding narrative, not to the appointed Gospel’s final sentence. The assembly is left with a call to hear. The narrative identifies particular speakers and authorities; its conflicts do not assign guilt to Jewish people across generations.[^7]

### Offertory: intercession for the nations

Psalm 2’s promise of the nations follows rebellion against the Lord and his anointed. At verse 8 the Father commands the Son to ask and promises to grant the nations as his inheritance. Augustine relates this asking to the Incarnation, sacrifice and intercession. The Son’s eternal dignity and his temporal work for humanity belong to one person. Understood through that intercession, the Offertory concerns the salvation of the nations the Son receives.[^8]

Verse 9’s rod belongs to the psalm’s context, not the Offertory’s sung words. Bellarmine’s account includes correction of sinful habits and final judgment. The passage demands accountability before the Son; it supplies no commission for worshippers to impose conversion by violence.

### Secret: the offered Son gives peace

The Secret names human reconciliation and asks the Father that Christ, the Son offered in sacrifice, himself give unity and peace among the nations. The peoples whose division the Collect described are the recipients of this petition. Although the prayer is addressed to the Father, it identifies the Son as both the one offered and the giver of peace.

The Epistle identifies the blood of the Cross as the means of peace; the Secret asks for peace through the Son offered in sacrifice. Chrysostom’s explanation of Colossians concerns precisely this means: reconciliation through Christ himself, his death and his Cross. On that understanding, the community asks to receive among the nations the peace his self-giving accomplishes.

### Preface: the royal priest

The Preface names Christ eternal Priest and universal King and recounts his self-offering on the Cross. It describes the kingdom presented to the Father through truth and life, holiness and grace, justice, love and peace. These qualities concern both the life received from God and the relations of those who serve him. After recounting Christ’s offering and kingdom, the assembly gives thanks with the heavenly host.

The Son whom the Epistle identifies as the sustainer of creation is here named as the priest who offers himself. The Preface recounts the subjection of creatures to his rule before the presentation of the kingdom to the Father. Pius XI’s feast teaching likewise relates kingship to redemption and priesthood, while extending its moral claims to public life.[^9]

### Communion and Postcommunion: peace, service and shared reign

The Communion takes selected clauses from Psalm 28:10–11. The psalm’s storm, powerful divine voice and disrupted landscape precede its conclusion in royal permanence and peace. Augustine reads the Church as exposed to the world’s flood while receiving peace in Christ. In his interpretation, the blessing can be received amid continuing adversity. Bellarmine preserves both natural and ecclesial readings of the psalm and relates present strength to final blessing.[^10]

The Postcommunion names those already nourished by the sacrament who glory in serving under Christ’s standard; it asks that they reign with him in heaven. Read beside the Gospel, its military image describes fidelity to the King whose servants do not fight to prevent his self-offering. Such service is also consistent with the peace requested at the altar. The nourishment already received can sustain their perseverance toward the lasting reign for which they pray.

## The King Makes a People Capable of Hearing Him {#lane-hearing-king}

The kingdom Jesus describes to Pilate is present among human beings, yet its life comes from another source. Its subjects belong to it through a new birth. Augustine explains this regeneration through Christ’s words about the kingdom and obedient hearing. Jerome explains how the incarnate Son receives the enduring kingdom that the saints will share. Together these accounts distinguish the gift by which people become Christ’s subjects from the inheritance toward which they persevere.

### In the world, but with another origin

Pilate hears a claim to kingship within the practical categories of his office. A king has subjects, interests and means of resistance. A claimant delivered by his own authorities and lacking an armed rescue appears either defeated or dangerous. Jesus’ answer changes the question from the kingdom’s military capacity to its origin. Augustine attends closely to the difference between a kingdom that is not *in* the world and one that is not *of* it. Jesus says the latter. The distinction allows Augustine to identify the kingdom with believers who remain in the world, while refusing to derive their new life from the world.[^11]

Augustine recalls Christ’s prayer that his disciples be kept from evil while remaining in the world. He also recalls the mixture of wheat and tares until the harvest. His account of the kingdom’s heavenly origin therefore includes its present mixture: believers still live amid evil, awaiting the harvest. Regeneration begins their life in the kingdom; the final separation remains to come.

Augustine says that people formerly under the prince of this world become Christ’s people through regeneration. He cites Colossians 1:13, the transfer into the beloved Son’s kingdom, to explain Christ’s answer to Pilate. That transfer changes the condition of people who continue to live in the world. The Gradual’s universal dominion can thus be understood as reaching people of every nation who need this new birth.

The Collect describes sin as a wound that disperses human families and asks that they be gathered under Christ’s gentle dominion. Augustine’s account of regeneration helps explain the healing this requires. The peoples need a change in the persons whose sin divides them. Christ’s grace makes willing allegiance possible, and that allegiance includes obedience to the one who heals them.

Jesus cites his servants’ refusal to fight for his release as evidence of the kingdom’s different origin. Preventing his delivery would also prevent the Passion through which he redeems his people. Read beside the Introit’s praise of the slain Lamb, the refusal of rescue concerns the sacrifice on which their life depends. The assembly praises him for that redemption before hearing the account of his trial.

### Belonging to truth and hearing Christ

At the end of the appointed Gospel, Jesus connects belonging to truth with hearing his voice. The order of the statement troubles an easy account of conversion. One might suppose that a hearer independently recognizes a true proposition and thereby earns the status of belonging to truth. Augustine reads the sentence in the opposite direction. A person hears because the truth has given him a share in itself. Hearing is the fruit of grace, not a prior achievement that obliges grace to follow.[^12]

He reaches this conclusion by distinguishing two ways of being related to truth. Every human being is a creature made through the divine Word. That universal dependence cannot by itself explain the hearing named here, because not everyone believes. Jesus is speaking of a further gift. Augustine describes its effect as the inward hearing of the person who obeys and believes. The hearer has not merely received sound or understood the grammar of an assertion. The heart is turned toward the speaker as trustworthy Lord.

Pilate has heard Jesus speak, but Augustine’s inward hearing requires belief and obedience. The listener really responds, and the capacity to respond begins in Christ’s gracious calling. To reverse that order would make salvation the reward of a merit the sinner supplied unaided. A hearer whose response begins with this gift has reason to give thanks, as the recipients of the Epistle do.

Chrysostom distinguishes two gifts in Colossians 1:12–13. The Father makes the recipients fit to share the inheritance he gives them; he also transfers those rescued from darkness into the Son’s kingdom. Rescue releases them from their former captivity, and transfer admits them to life with the Son. Divine generosity changes the recipients as well as their condition.[^13]

The Preface describes the kingdom through truth, life, holiness and grace. In light of Augustine’s account, its truth concerns the Son whom his subjects hear and obey. Holiness describes the life to which they are called, and grace the gift that makes it possible. These descriptions apply to the people who acknowledge the King standing before Pilate.

This account of hearing has consequences for the believer’s conduct. Willingness to be corrected belongs to an allegiance expressed in obedience. Gratitude for grace also changes how one regards another person’s conversion: the response of faith depends on God’s gift, even when someone else’s testimony helps the hearer. Patient testimony and correction of one’s own habits follow from that gift; they do not earn its beginning.

### How the eternal Son receives dominion

Daniel’s vision presents another difficulty. The human figure receives power and a kingdom before the Ancient of Days. If Christ is eternally divine, why is dominion given to him? Jerome addresses the problem by attending to the figure’s humanity. The Son of Man signifies the Son of God’s assumption of human flesh. The giving of power, honor and kingdom is read through the Incarnation and the self-emptying described by Paul in Philippians 2.[^14]

The divine Son has not advanced from the condition of a creature into divinity. He has truly assumed the condition in which obedience, humiliation and exaltation can be predicated of him. The explanation depends on that humanity being real. The Son’s received glory belongs to the saving economy of the one who became man. The very humanity through which he suffers is not abandoned when he reigns.

The saints’ hope depends on this received dominion. Daniel 7:18 and 27 associate them with possession of the kingdom. Jerome contrasts their heavenly inheritance with kingdoms that arise from earth and return to it. He rejects an account that exhausts the promise in Maccabean victory, because the promised universality and permanence exceed that event; he also rejects an earthly millennial kingdom as the final form of the saints’ inheritance.[^15]

Augustine explains how former subjects of darkness become Christ’s people. Jerome explains the reception of dominion by the Son made man and the saints’ possession of an enduring kingdom. The Alleluia sings that kingdom’s permanence. The hope of shared heavenly reign in the Postcommunion can be understood through Jerome’s account: the incarnate Son receives the kingdom, and the saints will share its life.

Jerome’s distinction also helps explain the Introit’s acclaim. The slain one receives honor; the Epistle identifies him as the uncreated source of creatures. Applying the distinction to these passages, the honor he receives concerns the redemptive victory of the Son made man. The one who possesses eternal divinity has truly suffered in his humanity. The Preface names this same Christ as Priest and King.

### Intercession gathers what sin has scattered

The Offertory quotes the Father’s promise to give the Son the nations as his inheritance. Augustine reads Psalm 2:8 in relation to the humanity Christ assumed, his sacrifice and his intercession. The Son asks for the salvation of those who will be his possession. In this interpretation, the peoples’ gathering under Christ’s rule is a gift for which their King himself asks.[^16]

The Offertory is sung as the offerings are prepared. With Augustine’s account of the Son’s asking, the promised inheritance of the nations can be understood beside the Secret’s request for their unity and peace. Those for whom the Son intercedes still need reconciliation, including the faithful who make this offering. The Secret identifies the offered Son as the giver of the peace they seek.

Bellarmine’s treatment of Psalm 2 helps locate the struggle involved. The Lord’s authority can break the habits that enslave a person. Correction is mercy when it destroys a sinful attachment and frees the person to obey; judgment also remains real for resistance that refuses the King. The image cannot be transferred into a permission for the congregation to break other people. In the Gospel, Christ refuses a battle to prevent his delivery and describes his subjects as those who hear his voice.[^17]

### The peace of pilgrims

The psalm from which the Communion is taken describes the Lord’s voice resounding through a storm before naming his enduring kingship and his blessing of peace. Augustine reads the concluding verses as the Church’s preservation amid the world’s flood and its future reign with Christ. In his account, peace in Christ is received during adversity in the world.[^18]

Bellarmine likewise distinguishes the strength needed on the journey from the final blessing toward which it leads. That distinction helps explain the present service and future hope of those praying the Postcommunion. Already nourished by the sacrament and glorying in service under Christ’s standard, they ask to reign with him in heaven. Their present service can be understood as perseverance, sustained by the nourishment they have received, through the adversity Augustine describes toward the enduring inheritance Jerome explains.

Literal.

: Jesus answers a royal accusation before Pilate; Colossians gives thanks for deliverance and transfer; Daniel presents an enduring kingdom; the prayers ask for healed allegiance, peace and shared reign.

Allegorical.

: The incarnate Son gathers the Church through redemption and regeneration. His received human glory opens the enduring kingdom to the saints whose life comes from him.

Moral.

: Hearing becomes obedient faith. Gratitude displaces self-sufficiency, intercession reaches other peoples, and correction of sinful habits serves faithful life together.

Anagogical.

: The pilgrim Church receives peace amid present conflict and hopes for lasting reign with Christ. Its final inheritance surpasses every temporary earthly kingdom.

## The Creator Restores Communion by Giving Himself {#lane-reconciled-creation}

The King acclaimed in the Mass created the beings over whom he rules. Colossians identifies him as their sustainer and as the one through whose blood they are reconciled. Chrysostom explains why the Creator himself accomplishes this reconciliation through the Cross. Bellarmine distinguishes Christ’s authority over all from the willing obedience of those who acknowledge him. This interpretation asks how creatures estranged from God come to share the peace of his kingdom.

### The firstborn and the origin of every power

Colossians names the Son the firstborn of all creation. The phrase can appear to place him at the head of a sequence of creatures. Chrysostom refuses that inference by following the clauses that explain it: all things were created through him, and all continue to depend on him. The Son cannot be assigned to the class whose existence he causes. The vocabulary of birth must be read within the passage’s account of creative agency.[^19]

Chrysostom’s exposition develops the point through the distinction between visible and invisible creatures. Invisible powers might seem to offer a higher route to God than the human Christ. Paul names them within creation precisely to exclude that exemption. Thrones, dominations, principalities and powers are not rival sources of being. Their greatness remains received. The Son’s primacy is therefore more radical than superiority in rank within a common order.

Nor does his work stop with an initial act of creation. Chrysostom explains that creatures would cease to stand if separated from the Son’s sustaining providence. Their present existence is dependent. The ruler who resists him, the angel who serves him and the ordinary believer who prays to him share this creaturely dependence, though their moral relations to him differ. This distinction will matter when Bellarmine distinguishes universal authority from willing obedience.

The Epistle next names the Son head of the Church. Chrysostom emphasizes his condescension in becoming the head of those whose existence depends on him. The one called firstborn of creation is also called firstborn from the dead. The Church’s members thus receive their new life from the risen Son through whom they were created.

The Preface names the universal King as the priest who offers himself on the Cross. This sacrifice concerns the enmity of creatures who depend on him for their existence. The distinction between creation and reconciliation matters here: they already belong to him as creatures, and through his death they can come to live with him in peace.

### Why the blood of the Cross matters to the argument

Chrysostom distinguishes the greatness of reconciliation itself from the manner in which God accomplishes it. Estranged humanity is reconciled to God; the Son himself does this, through his death and through the Cross. The Creator of those estranged from him submits to death for their reconciliation.[^20]

Jesus’ conduct before Pilate can be understood in relation to this reconciliation. His servants do not fight to prevent his delivery, and the Passion proceeds. The Gospel recounts the trial of the one whose death the Epistle identifies as the means of peace. His refusal of rescue allows that self-offering to be accomplished.

The Introit’s heavenly worshippers acclaim the Lamb as slain. The Collect asks for the restoration of human families wounded by sin. In the Secret, the Father is asked that the offered Christ give those peoples unity and peace. The same Christ is acclaimed for his sacrifice and offered for the reconciliation of those who still need its fruits.

The Epistle describes the recipients as former enemies at Colossians 1:21, immediately after the appointed passage. Their reconciliation requires a change in their relation to God. The prayer for divided human families asks for such a change among them as well. A community receiving this peace must acknowledge the wrongdoing that divides it and seek the conversion of those responsible, including its own members.

### What is reconciled in heaven?

The Epistle’s phrase about things in heaven requires explanation. Chrysostom asks how there can be war among heavenly beings when heaven is the model of obedience invoked in the Lord’s Prayer. His answer does not make the holy angels sinners. The breach lies between heaven and sinful humanity: the angels oppose the dishonor humanity has done to their Lord.[^21]

Christ brings humanity into heavenly company and removes the estrangement. Chrysostom points to the angels’ joyful appearance among human beings and to humanity’s reception in heaven. Earth is reconciled both internally and with heaven; heaven receives the human beings formerly alienated from it. The angels’ joy accompanies the peace the Son has made.

This explains why Chrysostom turns against reliance upon angels as substitutes for Christ. Their ministry is real, but they are themselves servants within the order the Son reconciles. To make them alternative saviors would mistake the beneficiaries and witnesses of his peace for its independent source. The Son’s creative primacy and reconciling action thus answer one connected concern: no invisible power can offer a saving route that bypasses him.

The Preface recounts the priestly King’s self-offering before the assembly gives thanks with the angels. Chrysostom’s explanation of heavenly reconciliation helps explain this shared worship. Human beings estranged from heaven are received into its company through the Son’s work. In this interpretation, the assembly’s praise with the angels expresses that restored communion.

The universality of the claim must retain the Epistle’s immediate context. Colossians 1:21–23 turns from the cosmic horizon to former enemies and their perseverance in faith. Chrysostom’s explanation of heavenly reconciliation concerns holy angels and humanity, not universal pardon for every rebellious spiritual being. Nor does the scale of Christ’s work imply that every human person already enjoys saving friendship with him. The kingdom’s reach and the response of its subjects remain distinct.

### All kings, and the recognition still to come

The Gradual declares a dominion embracing all nations and rulers. Bellarmine asks how such language can be true when willing acknowledgment is visibly incomplete. His commentary on Psalm 71:11 presents three ways the universal claim bears upon Christ. It can describe widespread conversion in the scriptural manner of speaking generally while allowing exceptions; it can look toward final judgment, when acknowledgment will be complete; and it can name the actual authority Christ already has over all, with or without their consent.[^22]

The alternatives are not interchangeable. Conversion describes a change in the subject. Final acknowledgment describes an encounter with judgment that cannot be evaded. Present authority describes the King’s right and power even where the subject resists. Confusing these claims produces two opposite errors: announcing that everyone already obeys willingly, or deciding that resistance proves Christ lacks universal authority. Bellarmine avoids both by explaining what each sense asserts.

Bellarmine also allows the prayer for the king’s judgment to concern David and Solomon in its immediate historical reference. Solomon can receive a responsibility to govern justly; the request is intelligible within Israel’s royal life. Bellarmine then interprets the promises of endurance and universal dominion as fulfilled in Christ, since they exceed Solomon’s reign. Augustine begins instead from Christ as true Solomon and peacemaker. Their disagreement concerns the immediate scope of the opening, while both find its fulfillment in Christ.[^23]

The verses following universal homage explain why people turn to this King. He rescues the helpless. Bellarmine develops that rescue as liberation from the devil’s captivity through forgiveness and restoration of grace. Human poverty is not a reason for the King to despise his subjects; it is the condition in which they discover their need of him. His account of deliverance can be read beside the Epistle’s transfer from darkness: the Son’s rule frees those held captive and restores their life with God.[^24]

At the offering, Psalm 2:8 names the ends of the earth as the extent of the Son’s inheritance. Augustine relates the Son’s asking to priestly intercession; Bellarmine relates the inheritance to the Son’s identity and rightful possession. His authority extends to peoples who still need conversion. The petitions for their healing in the Collect and Secret therefore remain necessary even as the Mass acclaims his universal rule.

### The Church’s peace and the feud at its door

Chrysostom does not allow reconciliation to remain a grand picture remote from ordinary conduct. His homily turns to the Church’s repeated exchange of peace. The greeting is directed to all. A person who maintains peace with one neighbor and a feud with another cannot treat partial agreement as the health of the whole body. Chrysostom compares such selective peace to a body in which some members are at rest while others remain disordered. The body is not healthy merely because the disorder is localized.[^25]

Chrysostom applies the comparison to the person who receives the Church’s greeting of peace while deliberately preserving a feud. The Son came to reconcile heaven and earth, and the recipient refuses reconciliation with a fellow member of the Church. For Chrysostom, this conduct contradicts the peace being exchanged. His application makes the healing of a particular relationship part of the response to Christ’s reconciling work.

The Communion names peace as the King’s blessing. The Postcommunion asks that those already nourished by the sacrament and glorying in service under Christ’s standard may reign with him in heaven. In this interpretation, their present service includes reconciliation with others in response to the peace they receive. Augustine’s exposition of Psalm 28 describes peace in Christ amid continuing opposition. Perseverance in that peace can require truthfulness, patient correction and resistance to wrongdoing, while excluding hatred of the person who resists.

The Alleluia sings of the dominion that outlasts the unstable powers of Daniel’s vision. Jerome explains its reception by the Son made man and its enduring possession by the saints. The Postcommunion asks for a share in that heavenly reign. The peace received and practiced amid present conflict reaches its fulfillment in lasting life with Christ.

Literal.

: Colossians joins creation, headship and the Cross; the royal Psalms join dominion to justice; Jesus’ trial discloses a kingship whose witness proceeds through his delivery; the prayers seek reconciliation and peace.

Allegorical.

: The one Son is Creator, incarnate head, priestly victim and King. His self-giving restores humanity to communion with God and heavenly worship, and gathers his Church from the nations.

Moral.

: Those who receive peace must oppose their own feuds and care for the helpless. Royal justice requires conversion and public responsibility; voluntary allegiance is more than unavoidable subjection to authority.

Anagogical.

: Universal authority will receive complete acknowledgment at judgment. Saving communion reaches the eternal kingdom, while present resistance and imperfect recognition neither exhaust nor annul Christ’s reign.

## One Kingship, Two Questions

The first interpretation distinguishes the grace by which a person enters the kingdom from the inheritance toward which that person perseveres. The second distinguishes the Son’s authority over all creatures from their willing obedience and their reconciliation with God and one another. The first explains why belief and obedience begin with a gift; the second explains why the recipient of peace must seek reconciliation with others. Gratitude for grace and the refusal of a feud are different consequences of belonging to the same King.

Their different starting points also explain how they approach the kingdom’s universality. The account of regenerated hearing concerns the new life of people who remain in the world and can be gathered from every nation. The account of creative lordship concerns Christ’s authority even over those who resist him. Augustine’s distinction between origin and presence leaves room for the public responsibilities Bellarmine describes; Bellarmine’s distinction between authority and conversion leaves the need for grace intact. Both anticipate a fulfillment beyond present political fortunes, but one emphasizes the saints’ inheritance and the other the complete acknowledgment of Christ at judgment.

Their agreement includes a narrower exegetical difference. Augustine introduces Christ as the true Solomon in Psalm 71; Bellarmine allows the opening prayer its immediate Davidic petition for Solomon’s just rule before tracing its fulfillment beyond him. Augustine begins with the promised peacemaker, while Bellarmine explains how a prayer within Israel’s royal life exceeds its historical king. This difference concerns the opening petition. On universal homage, Bellarmine’s three alternatives still distinguish present conversion, final acknowledgment and the authority Christ already possesses over unwilling subjects.

### H1. The Psalms: attribution, occasion and writing {#history-psalms}

The Davidic reference era comes from John Corbett’s *King David* in the *Catholic Encyclopedia*, IV, opening paragraph. Corbett calls it the usual chronology and immediately records proposals that place the reign thirty to fifty years later. The displayed range dates the attributed figure. It neither locates every poem within his reign nor supplies the date of a particular performance.

The three Psalms reach David through different evidence. Psalm 28’s title names him. Psalm 2 has no such name in the inspected title, but Acts 4:24–28 gives its Davidic attribution in apostolic reception. Psalm 71’s title concerns Solomon; Bellarmine understands its opening as David’s prayer for the newly anointed king. That is his interpretation of the speaker, not the wording of a title that explicitly names David as author. Augustine begins directly from Christ, the true Solomon. The difference belongs to the reception of the psalm and does not establish a new composition date.

The historical occasions remain uncertain. Psalm 28’s tabernacle-completion title does not identify a securely dated building project; its Libanus and Cades belong to the psalm’s storm world. Psalm 2 names Sion and the nations without dating the appointment of its royal speaker. Bellarmine’s accession setting for Psalm 71 is an exegetical identification. No shared precise place of writing follows from these different settings.

The composition boundary comes from the USCCB NABRE introduction to Psalms, in its discussion of superscriptions and dating. It describes a pre-Maccabean limit alongside the inability to date individual psalms securely. Thus “before” remains part of the answer. The boundary is not an estimate that these poems were written near its endpoint. The complete contemporary introduction was independently checked in the reviewed research; its historical judgment is distinct from Corbett’s conventional chronology and the received attributions.[^26]

### H2. Daniel: Babylon and the heavenly court {#history-daniel}

Daniel 7:1 places the vision in the first year of Baltasar, king of Babylon. The heavenly court is the visionary scene, not a terrestrial place of composition. The table’s seventy years describes Babylonian servitude in the received historical frame, supported by Jeremiah 25:11–12. A duration cannot be used as a calendar year for the Son of Man’s reception of dominion.

Francis Gigot’s *Book of Daniel*, CE IV, authorship discussion, reports the displayed traditional composition range and the competing theory locating the book’s formation in the Maccabean crisis under Antiochus IV. Those are different accounts of the book’s history. Jerome’s rejection of a fulfillment exhausted by the Maccabees concerns the meaning and endurance of the promised kingdom; it is not a modern critical judgment about the date of composition. The interpretation of the prophecy and the circumstances of the book’s formation remain distinguishable questions.[^27]

### H3. John: the trial and the Gospel’s formation {#history-john}

The narrated scene is at Jerusalem, in the governor’s hall, in the morning of the Passion sequence. John 18:28 and 33 locate the interrogation; John 19:14–18 carries the narrative toward sentence and execution. A chronological binding for the Crucifixion cannot simply be attached to this distinct scene. The table therefore retains an unresolved date for the interrogation. Howlett’s *Biblical Chronology*, CE III, Passion discussion, preserves several alternative years and expressly declines certainty; its disagreement supplies context rather than a selected date for this row.

The received attribution names John son of Zebedee, and Ephesus is the traditional writing place. The *Catholic Encyclopedia* articles *New Testament*, XIV, dating discussion, and *Gospel of St John*, VIII, circumstances of composition, supply the two displayed late composition ranges. The latter acknowledges the lack of certain historical information and proposes a year within the last decade rather than an exact completion date established by contemporary documentation.

The USCCB NABRE introduction to John distinguishes the eyewitness testimony underlying the tradition from authorship of the finished Gospel. It reports that most modern scholars do not confidently identify that witness with Zebedee’s son. Its account of successive editing points to chapter 21, the adapted prologue hymn and discontinuities in the farewell discourse. Neither the number nor identities of all contributors are established. Alongside Ephesus it reports proposals for Syria, possibly Antioch, and Alexandria. These concern the writing and formation of the Gospel; the trial remains at Jerusalem. The displayed composition ranges are not reassigned as exact dates for particular editorial layers.[^28]

### H4. Colossians: the prison and the recipients {#history-colossians}

Pauline authorship and Roman captivity form the received setting used by the displayed chronology. The *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s *St Paul*, XI, chronology list, supplies the single-year answer; *Epistle to the Colossians*, IV, opening and captivity discussion, supplies the wider range. Different precision need not imply a direct contradiction. The figures date the letter’s writing, not the creation or reconciliation it proclaims. Colossae is the recipients’ city and cannot be substituted for the prison’s location.

The USCCB NABRE introduction notes that the letter does not identify the prison and that Paul’s several imprisonments complicate dating. It reports Roman, Caesarean and Ephesian proposals, and also the possibility of a pupil or follower writing in Paul’s name. It does not decide between that account and direct Pauline authorship or supply the follower with a definite city and date. The traditional Roman chronology therefore remains identified as such, beside the distinct uncertainties of authorship and imprisonment.[^29]

### H5. Apocalypse: the seer’s place and the vision {#history-apocalypse}

The two Apocalypse passages in the Introit share a book-composition answer. The Psalm verse does not share that answer. The *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s *Apocalypse*, I, time-and-place section, presents Patmos and testimony connecting the work with the end of Domitian’s reign. The whole reign provides a broad bound, not a statement that each year is equally probable. The *New Testament* article supplies the alternative approximate late range and acknowledges an earlier Neronian proposal. The selected late chronology does not exhaust the history of scholarly positions.

Patmos belongs to the received seer’s setting; the heavenly throne room belongs to the vision. Neither should be mistaken for the other. Apocalypse 1 situates the witness amid suffering and patient endurance, while chapter 5 displays worship around the Lamb. That literary relation supports the study’s interpretation without assigning a historical date to the eternal kingship being acclaimed.[^30]

Modern editorial notes in NPNF remain distinct from the Fathers’ arguments. The historical appendix preserves the canonical chronology’s alternatives and unresolved states, alongside the reviewed modern Catholic scholarly qualifications for John and Colossians. Chronology dates its stated subjects; it does not date creation, divine kingship or the eternal kingdom. The study carries no claim of ecclesiastical approval or human specialist review.
