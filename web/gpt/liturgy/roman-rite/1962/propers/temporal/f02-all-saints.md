# All Saints: Communion, Conversion, and the Promised Kingdom

*An expansive study of the 1962 Roman Missal propers for 1 November 2026*

**All Saints**

Communion, Conversion, and the Promised Kingdom

1962 Roman Missal

1 November 2026; with Sunday commemoration

The saints whom the Church celebrates together have reached the happiness Christ promises on the mountain. The people celebrating them still ask for mercy, release from sin, and protection. All Saints brings those two conditions into the same act of worship. The Gospel names the purity and peace of God’s children, including their fidelity under persecution. The prayers ask God to give the worshippers the saving gifts they need, with the saints praying for them. The distinction between the saints’ happiness and the worshippers’ need explains why a feast of so much joy contains so much petition.

John’s vision also requires patient attention. Before the multitude from every nation appears, John hears an exact number and a list of twelve tribes. Bede explains how such a definite count can signify the Church in its completeness. Augustine explains why death does not remove Christ’s faithful members from his Church. Together their arguments help us understand the celebration of one people whose members praise God in different conditions. A second interpretation concerns the life Christ teaches those people to live. Chrysostom connects the refreshment promised to the burdened with Christ’s humility; Augustine explains the Beatitudes as the formation of desire, purity, and peace. Their accounts address the apparent difficulty of finding rest while hungering for justice and enduring persecution.

On Sunday, 1 November 2026, the first-class feast of All Saints is celebrated with a commemoration of the Twenty-third Sunday after Pentecost. The Sunday adds a second Collect, Secret, and Postcommunion. The feast’s Lesson, Gospel, and chants remain those of All Saints; the Common Preface is said. The Creed is expressly appointed.[^1] Those additional prayers make the congregation’s present need unusually explicit: sins have entangled God’s people through their frailty; a gift already received needs completion; participation in divine things calls for protection amid human dangers.

## The Propers: Detailed Commentary

### Introit: rejoicing with the angels

The entrance antiphon calls everyone to rejoice in the Lord. Its celebration honors all the saints, while its last words name the Son of God as the object of the angels’ praise. Human veneration and angelic praise therefore meet in Christ. Schuster explains the feast’s breadth by naming apostles and martyrs, clergy and laity, laborers and slaves: the Holy Spirit sanctifies members of the whole body of Christ. His explanation concerns holiness in every condition of life, rather than distinctions of prestige.[^2]

The following Psalm verse, Ps. 32:1, gives the human singers a qualification: praise befits the upright. Augustine’s first exposition identifies uprightness with submission to God. The contrast is with the person whose will is bent away from him. Rejoicing in the Lord therefore involves accepting the one being praised, rather than measuring him by the singer’s preference. Augustine also expressly directs joy toward God rather than oneself. This gives the feast’s opening an account of the act of praise: the just rejoice because they belong to the Lord whose goodness they acknowledge. The Psalm citation belongs to this verse; the preceding antiphon is a composed liturgical text.[^3]

### The Collect and the Sunday commemoration

The feast’s Collect addresses almighty and eternal God as the giver of the celebration itself. He has granted that the merits of all his saints should be venerated on one day. The request then concerns the living: the mercy they desire, sought amid a multitude of intercessors. Merits, intercession, and mercy are related without being identified. The saints’ holiness is honored; their prayers assist the petitioners; the gift requested comes from God. Schuster’s exposition retains both the one festival and the many pleading for the faithful.[^4]

The second Collect names the congregation as God’s people and asks release from the bonds of sin. The cause confessed is human frailty; the means sought is God’s kindness. Schuster emphasizes the prayer’s universal humility. No member of the people can claim to have no need of pardon. Read beside the feast’s Collect, this prayer distinguishes the veneration of holiness from the present condition of those offering it. The assembly can celebrate the saints truthfully while asking God to free it from sins it has itself contracted.[^5]

### The Lesson: sealing, enumeration, and worship

Apocalypse 7 interrupts the sequence of threatened judgment. Chapter 6 ends with the question of who can stand before divine wrath. In chapter 7 an angel orders the delay of injury to land, sea, and trees until God’s servants have been sealed on their foreheads. The action identifies them as belonging to God before the vision describes their praise. The appointed Lesson begins at 7:2; the preceding restraint of the winds is part of its literary context.

John first hears the number of the sealed: 144,000. The repeated twelve thousand from each tribe occupies a substantial part of the proclamation. Bede understands the definite total as the completeness of the Church, descended from the patriarchs either naturally or by imitation of faith. He appeals to Galatians 3:29: belonging to Christ makes one Abraham’s seed. His arithmetic develops that interpretation: twelve multiplied by twelve, then expressed in thousands, signifies completeness; the apostolic twelve likewise preach throughout the world. This is Bede’s explanation of the vision’s number, not a prediction of a maximum count of the saved. [^6]

The next sight exceeds human counting and names nations, tribes, peoples, and languages. Bede explains the passage as a return to the victory of those who overcome persecution, following a recapitulation. He also allows the sequence to distinguish Israel, to whom the Gospel was first preached, from the salvation of the Gentiles. That alternative keeps a distinction within the vision which his interpretation of the whole numbered Church need not make in the same way. Neither explanation erases the text’s repeated enumeration. [^7]

The multitude stands before the throne and the Lamb, dressed in white and holding palms. Bede identifies the robes with baptism and the palms with the victory of the Cross, through which Christ’s people overcome the world. He also gives a further explanation of the robes in terms of glory bestowed by the Spirit. The act of praise confesses salvation to God and the Lamb; on verse 12 Bede says that the Church acknowledges having received its gifts from God. The praise of the angels permits two explanations in his commentary: they can represent the worshipping people, or they can be the heavenly spirits rejoicing at the salvation of the Gentiles. The latter provides a particularly direct comparison with the Introit’s angels.[^8]

The Lesson ends with the final Amen of verse 12. The elder’s explanation of the white robes and the great tribulation follows in verses 13–17. Those verses help a reader understand the scene, but their explanatory dialogue is not proclaimed within this Lesson. The distinction matters especially when the feast is described as showing the fruits of Christ’s Passion: the appointed palms and the Lamb already permit that discussion, while the explicit washing of robes in the Lamb’s blood belongs to the surrounding chapter.[^9]

### Gradual: the good promised to those who seek God

Psalm 33 addresses the saints themselves with a command to fear the Lord. The title is compatible with continued reverence and dependence. The chant then promises that those who fear him lack nothing and that seekers of the Lord will not be deprived of good. It selects verse 10 and the second part of verse 11. The verse’s first statement about the rich lacking and hungering belongs to the biblical context, not the chant as sung here.

Augustine directly confronts the apparent contradiction. A wicked rich man can grow old and die wealthy, with an expensive funeral, while a worshipper who seeks God remains poor. If good means possessions, the promise appears false. Augustine names justice, truth, charity, faith, and endurance as riches of the heart. He then identifies Christ as the living bread and expressly cites the Beatitude of hunger and thirst for justice. His explanation of the Psalm consequently bears on this Mass’s Gospel through a connection he actually makes.[^10]

Aquinas preserves several explanations of the same difficulty. Spiritual treasure, contentment with what suffices, God’s providential help, and a physician’s beneficial withholding of food each explain how lack can coexist with divine care. The physician comparison concerns an apparent deprivation which serves the patient. It cannot mean that every absence is already experienced as relief, or that prayer assures immediate material abundance. The several explanations concern the good God gives, and the way its gift may differ from what a person initially seeks.[^11]

### Alleluia: the invitation to the burdened

The Alleluia appoints Matthew 11:28. In the chapter, the invitation follows Christ’s thanksgiving for revelation to little ones and his words about the Father and the Son. It is followed by his invitation to take his yoke and learn his meekness and humility. Chrysostom includes people burdened by anxiety, sorrow, and sins. He reads the promise of rest through that following account of discipleship.[^12]

Chrysostom also asks how an easy yoke agrees with a narrow way and a command to take up the cross. His answer has several parts: humility already frees the soul from pride’s anxiety; virtue releases it from the heavier bondage of sin; Christ gives his own example; future glory exceeds present suffering. He expressly recalls that Christ began the Beatitudes with humility. His argument thus explains a specific relationship between the Alleluia and Gospel without making either text promise freedom from suffering. [^13]

### Gospel: the kingdom promised in the Beatitudes

Jesus sits on the mountain, his disciples draw near, and he teaches. The Vulgate order proclaimed here places the meek before those who mourn. The repeated blessings name dispositions and actions, alongside present losses and future gifts. Poverty of spirit begins the sequence; the kingdom is promised again to those persecuted for justice. The final address blesses disciples reviled falsely for Christ and promises a great heavenly reward. The Missal ends before the subsequent comparison with the persecuted prophets.

Augustine understands the first blessing as humility and fear of God. The following Beatitudes describe a connected education: receptiveness to God’s teaching, grief over bondage to lesser things, effort to desire justice, mercy toward those needing help, purification of the heart, and ordered peace. The kingdom at the beginning and at the persecution blessing encloses this formation. He sees the eighth blessing as showing the completeness of what has been formed, tested by opposition from outside.[^14]

Chrysostom stresses the freedom with which humility is chosen: social lowliness imposed by circumstance does not by itself describe the will. He also insists that mercy concerns actions as well as money, purity cannot be replaced by generous giving, and peacemaking requires reconciling other people. The persecution that follows qualifies the praise of peace: agreement in evil is not the peace Christ blesses. The final blessing similarly requires both false accusation and fidelity to Christ.[^15]

### Offertory: the just in God’s hand

Wisdom 2 presents people who persecute the just man and test his claim to be God’s child. Wisdom 3 answers their judgment of his death. The Offertory selects and adapts clauses from 3:1–3. Its wording concerns the torment of malice; the ordinary biblical wording at this point concerns death. The intervening biblical clauses are not all retained. What the chant sings is the security and peace of the just whom the unwise suppose to have perished.[^16]

In his exposition of Psalm 87, Augustine directly invokes Wisdom 3:1 while distinguishing Christ’s resurrection from the resurrection still awaited by his members. The righteous dead remain in God’s hand while their bodies await restoration. Christ freely lays down his life and takes it up; his members receive at the end what preceded them in their Head. Schuster’s explanation of the chant likewise denies the persecutor’s supposed possession of the martyr: the martyr belongs to God. [^17]

### The Secrets and the Common Preface

The feast’s Secret concerns both honor paid to all the just and salvation needed by those making the offering. God’s mercy is expressly requested for that saving benefit. The commemorated Sunday’s Secret adds the completion of an unmerited gift and growth in service. Schuster interprets this service as the ministry of the altar and the sacrifice of praise through Christ. The language of offering therefore remains the language of dependence: the offerers ask God to complete what he has granted.[^18]

The Common Preface gives thanks to the Father through Christ. It names angels, dominations, powers, heavenly virtues, and seraphim, then asks that human voices be admitted with them. The assembly’s praise has the same Christological direction as the entrance chant. Its request for admission also distinguishes the earthly singers’ petition from John’s description of the worshippers before the throne. The Preface appointed in this dated Mass is the Common Preface; the historical Preface printed in Schuster’s feast commentary is a different text.[^19]

### Communion and the two Postcommunions

The Communion repeats three blessings from the Gospel: purity of heart, peacemaking, and persecution for justice. The selection places the promise of seeing God beside divine sonship and the kingdom. For Augustine, the purified heart is single, able to see by the faculty suited to God; peace requires the person’s desires to be ordered under reason and reason under the Son. Chrysostom adds the practical task of reconciling others. The communicants hear a description of sanctity that concerns both inward conversion and the treatment of other people.[^20]

The feast’s Postcommunion asks for enduring joy in veneration of all the saints and protection through their continual supplication. The Sunday’s prayer asks that those granted divine participation should not succumb to human dangers. Schuster explains the latter as a request for strengthening within the battle, rather than removal from every trial. Participation and danger coexist in the same petition. The feast thus ends with the living still seeking help, assisted by the saints they have honored.[^21]

## One people praises God across nations and death {#lane-communion-of-saints}

### The number John hears and the multitude he sees

John’s exact number can seem to restrict the hope proclaimed at All Saints. He hears 144,000, then hears twelve thousand repeated for each named tribe. The subsequent multitude exceeds counting and comes from every nation. Bede addresses the relation between those descriptions at the definite number itself. The number signifies the entire Church, whose members descend from the patriarchs by birth or by imitation of faith. He supports the inclusion of the latter with Paul’s statement that those who belong to Christ are Abraham’s seed. His explanation concerns the way a people is identified in the vision.[^22]

The arithmetic is part of his argument. Twelve is already associated with the apostles sent to preach the faith throughout the world. Its multiplication by twelve and by thousands expresses the complete people gathered through that mission. A precise number can therefore represent a multitude without setting a statistical ceiling on it. Bede’s explanation depends on the symbolic force of the number and on membership by faith; it would lose its warrant if the tribal list were treated merely as an unnecessary delay before the multinational scene.

The tribal names receive extended attention in his exposition. Beginning with Juda, which he associates with confession, Bede interprets the ordered names through a sequence of virtues and their consummation in inheritance. This is his spiritual exposition of the names. It does not require a reader to accept the list as an independent lesson in Hebrew derivation. Within his account, the named people are also a people formed in holiness. The Gospel’s descriptions of the blessed can consequently be compared with the moral formation Bede finds in the enumeration, while each remains a distinct biblical text.[^23]

Bede’s comment on verse 9 allows another account of the transition. The enumeration can describe Israel, to whom the Gospel was first preached, followed by the salvation of the Gentiles. This retains a difference between the two groups at that point in the vision. It is an alternative to making each scene describe precisely the same subjects in precisely the same way. Both explanations admit the Gentiles to salvation; they locate the distinction between Israel and the nations differently. The feast’s universal rejoicing can be understood through either, without deciding that Bede supplied only one explanation.[^24]

### Belonging to the Lamb and confessing his gifts

The seal identifies servants of the living God. The multitude’s robes and palms then describe people who stand in Christ’s victory. Bede’s baptismal interpretation of the robes concerns their admission; his interpretation of the palms concerns victory through the Cross. Salvation is acclaimed to God and the Lamb, rather than claimed by the multitude as its own achievement. Bede’s comment on the sevenfold praise says that the Church confesses, in each member, gifts received from God.[^25]

This account helps explain the Introit’s veneration of the saints alongside its praise of the Son. The saints’ holiness is a reason to praise the giver of holiness. Schuster develops that relationship through the mystical body: the Spirit’s sanctification reaches its members in every condition. His examples include people without social distinction as well as those whose offices are conspicuous. The multitude’s different languages can accordingly remain visible. Their common confession of salvation does not require that their earthly conditions or histories were identical. [^26]

The angels’ relation to this people also needs care. Bede permits them to represent the worshipping multitude, but he also permits actual angelic spirits rejoicing at the salvation of the Gentiles. The Introit explicitly speaks of angels rejoicing and praising the Son. The Common Preface similarly names heavenly powers and asks admission for human voices. Comparing these prayers with Bede’s latter explanation gives a straightforward account of human and angelic praise together. His symbolic alternative remains an explanation of the Apocalypse, without requiring that the Introit’s angels should mean human singers.[^27]

The Preface’s request is particularly exact: it asks that our voices be admitted among theirs. Those praying are already giving thanks through Christ, yet they ask to share in the heavenly acclamation. Their present worship and the worship in John’s vision are related through Christ and directed toward God. Their conditions differ. That difference permits the Mass to celebrate the saints’ joy while retaining the congregation’s dependence on mercy.

### Death and continued membership in Christ

Death raises a further question about this common people. The Offertory sings of righteous souls whom the unwise suppose to have died, but who remain in God’s hand and peace. Augustine explains the same Wisdom phrase while expounding Psalm 87. His difficulty there concerns the dead whom God has not yet remembered by raising them. Righteous people are among the dead, so their awaited resurrection cannot mean that they have been cut off from God’s protection. Wisdom 3:1 supplies his explicit reason: their souls are in God’s hands.[^28]

Augustine distinguishes the Head from the members. Christ’s death is freely embraced; he has power to lay down his life and take it up. What has already happened in his resurrection is reserved for his body at the end. The righteous dead’s continued security therefore concerns souls awaiting resurrection. To say that they are at peace neither denies their bodily death nor treats the final resurrection as already accomplished. This makes the Offertory’s apparent paradox intelligible: the persecutor can kill the body without obtaining possession of the person as one separated from God.

In *The City of God* XX.9, Augustine gives a further account of the departed faithful’s membership. His immediate subject is Apocalypse 20, where souls reign with Christ. He appeals to remembrance of the departed at the altar when the body of Christ is received. Such practice, he argues, presupposes that the faithful dead remain Christ’s members. Their souls reign with him while they await their bodies. His argument concerns an ecclesial relation that persists through death.[^29]

The feast’s prayers can be understood in that relation. Those celebrating honor all the just, ask mercy through their intercession, and offer gifts whose saving benefit they themselves still need. The saints’ intercession in the Collect and Postcommunion is expressly stated in those prayers. Augustine’s account of membership explains how death need not exclude the departed from the Church’s present communion; it does not supply his own commentary on these particular orations. The connection with their intercession follows from reading their actual petitions beside his account of Christ’s body.

### The present Church and the promised kingdom

Augustine’s account has an important distinction within it. The Church is already Christ’s kingdom, but its present condition includes wheat and tares. The kingdom at the end excludes evil. The people gathered to celebrate All Saints therefore cannot infer that everyone presently within the assembly has already reached the holiness being celebrated. The same chapter describes the saints’ present reign through adherence to Christ, while distinguishing it from the kingdom’s final condition. [^30]

The commemoration Collect makes this present need audible. The people confess sins contracted through their frailty and ask release. The feast’s request for abundant mercy is thus joined to an account of why mercy is needed. The Sunday Secret similarly asks God to complete an unmerited gift and increase service. In this interpretation, the congregation’s membership becomes a reason for asking and serving. The saints’ communion encourages petition; it supplies no exemption from conversion.

The Gradual and Gospel describe the life of those whom the Mass calls saints. Fear of God, seeking him, mercy, purity, peace, and fidelity under persecution are concrete relations and actions. Augustine’s explanation of upright praise already required submission to God. His explanation of the Gradual names goods which do not depend on prosperity. The Alleluia invites the burdened to Christ, so people who need pardon are addressed within the celebration of those who have received his gifts. The invitation concerns the present hearers and their need for the Lord whom the saints praise.[^31]

Augustine’s explanation of the Beatitudes describes their several rewards as names of the one kingdom. Purity’s sight of God, peace’s divine sonship, and the kingdom promised amid persecution are therefore compatible ends of the same sanctified life. The Communion repeats precisely those three promises. Those receiving hear them as promises still requiring fidelity, while honoring saints whose happiness displays their fulfillment. The Postcommunions continue that distinction: the faithful ask enduring joy, the saints’ prayers, and protection amid danger.[^32]

### Four senses

Literal.

: John hears the enumeration of sealed Israel and sees a multinational multitude praising God and the Lamb. The Mass honors all the saints while its living worshippers ask mercy and protection.

Allegorical.

: Bede explains membership in Abraham’s seed through belonging to Christ. Augustine explains the continued membership of the faithful dead in Christ’s body. The Church gives thanks through Christ with the heavenly company, while awaiting the body’s resurrection.

Moral.

: Upright praise submits to God. Those celebrating the saints confess frailty, seek God’s good, practice mercy and peace, and remain faithful under opposition. Present membership calls for the holiness described in the Gospel.

Anagogical.

: The multitude’s praise, the righteous dead’s peace, and the promised sight of God concern the completion sought by the pilgrim Church. The saints’ souls remain secure while the final resurrection and the kingdom without evil are still awaited.

## Christ gives rest through humility, purity, and peace {#lane-purification-and-rest}

### Refreshment and the cross

Christ invites all who labor and are burdened to come to him for refreshment. In the Gospel he blesses mourners, people hungering for justice, and those persecuted for it. The Offertory adds righteous people whose bodies have been killed. Refreshment understood as exemption from these afflictions would contradict the other texts heard in the same Mass. Chrysostom confronts that difficulty within Matthew 11 itself, where the invitation is followed by the easy yoke and the light burden.

The following verses explain what disciples are to learn: Christ’s meekness and humility of heart. Chrysostom explicitly connects that humility with the beginning of the Beatitudes. He also asks how the light burden agrees with the narrow gate and the cross. His answer includes Christ’s example, the reward promised, and a comparison between the burden of obedience and the burdens of sin. These contextual verses explain the single appointed Alleluia verse; they are not additions to its words.[^33]

The comparison concerns experience as well as future reward. Chrysostom describes the anxious effort to preserve possessions and the anger that keeps a quarrel alive. Refusal to retaliate can end a contest which retaliation would prolong. Humility removes the pride which continually demands satisfaction. He thus gives a reason why obedience can afford present relief, even when it requires restraint or loss. The rest he describes is related to the changed will of the disciple.

He also invokes the suffering of apostles and martyrs and the greater glory to come. His explanation retains actual toil. Both the yoke and its ease are spoken of, he says, so that disciples will neither flee it as unbearable nor despise it as requiring no effort. A person can therefore be refreshed by Christ while still struggling to act justly. The Sunday Collect’s confessed entanglement in sins can be compared with the bondage Chrysostom says Christ removes. Its request for release also makes moral formation a petition for divine help.

### How humility changes what a person desires

Augustine begins his exposition of the Beatitudes with poverty of spirit as humility and fear of God. Pride is swollen self-importance; the humble person submits to divine authority and can learn. This is why humility belongs at the beginning of his account. The person who refuses instruction cannot be corrected by the later teaching. The Introit’s upright praise, understood by Augustine as submission to God, describes a compatible disposition at the opening of the Mass.[^34]

In Augustine’s sequence, meekness includes receptiveness to Scripture, especially when a passage is not yet understood. Mourning then follows the recognition of attachments which held the soul in bondage. His account concerns the sorrow of conversion: former delights have been lost, while love of eternal goods is still being formed. Hunger and thirst for justice name the effort to desire the good strongly enough to leave the old attachments. The pain of relinquishing a familiar pleasure explains why the sequence contains both sorrow and hope. [^35]

Mercy toward others is the next act in this formation. A person seeking help from one stronger should help another who is weaker. Augustine’s reasoning puts dependence and generosity together: the need to receive assistance is compatible with the duty to give it. The feast’s Collect asks mercy amid many intercessors. Although Augustine does not expound that prayer, his account of mercy explains a fitting disposition for those who ask it: willingness to relieve another’s misery. [^36]

In the Lesson, the servants of God receive the seal before John sees the multitude praising God and the Lamb. Those standing before the throne wear white robes and hold palms. Bede understands the robes as baptism and the palms as the triumph of the Cross: they have overcome the world in Christ. At the praise which concludes the appointed passage, he explains that the Church acknowledges in each member what it has received from God.[^37]

Read beside Christ’s invitation to the burdened, the baptismal robes suggest that the labor of conversion begins with a gift from God. Relinquishing sinful attachments is a response to holiness received. The disciple needs Christ’s help both to begin and to persevere in the meekness and mercy Augustine describes.

The Sunday Secret asks for growth in service and for God to complete what he granted without merit on the recipients’ part. Schuster interprets the requested service through ministry at the altar. An increase of service can accordingly be understood as part of the formation being asked for. The feast’s Secret likewise asks that the offerings be saving for those who make them, through God’s mercy. Offering and continued need remain present in both prayers.[^38]

### The good which hunger does not take away

The Gradual’s promise of no want is harder to understand when heard beside the blessing of hunger. Augustine faces the devout poor person’s objection directly. A rich wrongdoer may die surrounded by costly goods, while the person attending church and seeking God remains without them. The difference is observable; an explanation must say what good the promise concerns.

Augustine describes a heart rich in justice, truth, charity, faith, and endurance. These are goods the rich sinner can lack while possessing an ivory bed. He then names Christ as living bread and cites the Beatitude promising fullness to those who hunger and thirst for justice. The apparent contradiction is answered by the object of desire. The hungering disciple seeks a good whose possession differs from being materially prosperous. This is an explicit connection between the Gradual’s Psalm and the Gospel in Augustine’s exposition. [^39]

Aquinas gives several accounts rather than reducing every deprivation to one explanation. A person can possess spiritual treasure, be content with enough, receive providential assistance, or be treated like a patient from whom a physician withholds a desired food. The last comparison explains why a request’s fulfillment can depend on what benefits the recipient. These alternatives prevent the Gradual from being heard as a promise that a holy person will always obtain the material thing desired. They also leave room to ask God for actual help in bodily need.[^40]

Chrysostom offers a different emphasis in his explanation of the meek inheriting the earth. Augustine understands an enduring inheritance, the land of the living where the saints rest. Chrysostom argues for a sensible present reward: meekness can preserve possessions which anger and rashness would destroy. He also retains future gifts. [^41]

### A pure heart and active peacemaking

For Augustine, the pure heart is single. The comparison with sight is precise: bodily light requires healthy eyes, and the sight of God requires the inward faculty appropriate to him to be purified. Singleness concerns the will and attention directed toward God. His explanation follows the earlier relinquishment of contrary attachments; it gives a reason why desire and vision belong within the same account of conversion.[^42]

Chrysostom’s exposition specifies a further practical difficulty. Generous almsgiving does not make impurity irrelevant. His example is the person who gives freely and avoids greed but still sins against chastity. The blessing of purity asks for a life whose different actions are answerable to God. Read beside Augustine’s singleness, this excludes treating one conspicuous virtue as permission to retain an incompatible habit.[^43]

Augustine next explains peace as an order within the person. Desires are subject to reason, and reason is subject to the truth, the only-begotten Son of God. The highest human faculty itself needs submission; its rule of what is lower depends on obedience to what is higher. This explains how the peacemaker resembles God and why peace can survive external opposition. A hostile act need not make the disciple’s desires rebel against the Son. [^44]

Chrysostom gives peacemaking an outward task: bringing others who are divided into reconciliation. He connects the title of God’s children with the work of the only-begotten Son in uniting the alienated. He then reads the persecution blessing as a necessary qualification. An agreement which requires wrongdoing is not blessed peace. The two expositions thus give distinct, compatible accounts of peace: order under Christ within the person, and reconciliation in the person’s dealings with others. [^45]

The Communion repeats purity, peacemaking, and persecution for justice in that order. Its short selection returns the hearers to the promised sight of God, likeness to the Father, and kingdom under opposition. In this interpretation, reception of the sacrament is accompanied by the continued conversion these promises describe. The Postcommunion requests for protection are fitting because moral formation remains exposed to danger.

### Persecution, peace, and the body’s resurrection

The Gospel qualifies persecution: it is for justice. Reviling is qualified further by falsehood and its being for Christ. Chrysostom expressly rejects the supposition that being accused by itself makes a person blessed. This keeps fidelity from being confused with any quarrel in which a Christian happens to be involved. Reconciliation remains a duty, and agreement in evil remains excluded.[^46]

Augustine explains the return of the kingdom promise in the eighth Beatitude as the showing of a completed formation. Persecution tests what has been established within. His claim concerns the strength of the person’s adherence to God, not the harmlessness of the persecutor’s acts. The Offertory explicitly acknowledges bodily death while proclaiming peace in God’s hand. Its words permit a distinction between what violence does to the body and whether the righteous person is abandoned by God. [^47]

Gregory develops an additional consequence of the righteous person’s suffering while explaining Job’s image of a tree which can grow again after being cut down. In *Moralia* XII.5.6 he directly quotes Wisdom 3:2: the unwise judge the righteous person’s death to be misery. He interprets the despised preaching and killed body as the apparently dried root and trunk. Through the Spirit, the example awakens virtue in other people. His account concerns fruit in the living, arising from the sufferer’s fidelity. [^48]

Gregory’s following paragraph gives a distinct interpretation of the tree’s renewed growth as bodily resurrection. These are two explanations, with different subjects of renewal: those influenced by the just person’s example, and the just person’s own body. Both preserve what the unwise fail to perceive when they suppose death has ended the matter. His commentary is on Job and reuses Wisdom; its application to the Offertory follows that actual reuse.[^49]

The Common Preface describes the life toward which Christ’s refreshment leads as thanksgiving with the heavenly company. The saints’ rest includes praise. The feast’s Postcommunion asks that veneration continue in joy and that the saints continually pray for the faithful. The Sunday’s petition then names the continuing danger of those who have shared in divine gifts. Schuster explains protection as grace to stand within trial. This makes the closing request compatible with the Gospel’s blessed perseverance and Chrysostom’s rest under the cross. [^50]

### Four senses

Literal.

: Jesus invites the burdened and blesses humility, mercy, purity, peace, and fidelity under righteous persecution. The prayers ask pardon, fruitful offerings, continued service, and protection; the Offertory proclaims the peace of the just.

Allegorical.

: The meek Son gives rest and reconciles those divided. Through him, God’s children give thanks with the heavenly worshippers. His victory permits suffering and death to be understood in relation to life with him.

Moral.

: Humility makes a person teachable; desire is redirected toward justice; mercy assists the weak; purity requires singleness and chastity; peace requires ordered desires and practical reconciliation. Opposition tests fidelity under the Gospel’s own qualifications.

Anagogical.

: Present goods anticipate the promised sight of God and the kingdom. The righteous dead’s peace and the resurrection still awaited distinguish present refreshment from the final transformation which Augustine says exceeds words.

## Communion and conversion

The first interpretation concerns belonging: how the numbered people and the multinational multitude can describe the fullness of God’s people, and how Christ’s members remain united across death. Bede’s explanation of Abraham’s seed and Augustine’s account of the departed at the altar give distinct reasons for understanding the saints as members of one Church. The prayers express the relationship between the saints honored and the living who ask their intercession.

The second concerns the kind of life Christ gives: how his refreshment agrees with costly obedience, hunger for justice, and persecution. Chrysostom reasons from Christ’s humility and from the bondage of sin; Augustine explains the formation of desire, purity, and peace. The Gradual’s difficult promise and the Communion’s repeated Beatitudes receive more sustained attention in that account.

Both interpretations distinguish present participation from final completion. Augustine’s account of the Church includes faithful members still needing conversion and departed members still awaiting resurrection. His account of the Beatitudes admits present fulfillment while preserving a future transformation beyond expression. The differences among the sources remain particular: Bede permits alternative identifications of the numbered company; Augustine and Chrysostom give different referents to the inherited earth; inward order and outward reconciliation are compatible emphases within their explanations of peace.

The feast’s final petitions allow both subjects to remain concrete. The people who have honored the saints ask to rejoice in them always, to be helped by their prayers, and to withstand human dangers. They seek a holiness already displayed in Christ’s members and still needing to be formed in their own lives.

### Psalms: attribution, flight, and composition {#hist-psalms}

Both Psalms have Davidic titles in the Douay–Rheims/Challoner Psalter. Psalm 32 supplies no particular occasion; Psalm 33 names the dismissal associated with David’s altered behavior. The reign shown above follows Corbett’s “usual chronology” in the historical *Catholic Encyclopedia*. Corbett immediately records alternatives placing the reign thirty to fifty years later. A royal era attached to an attributed author does not date each attributed poem.[^51]

The flight to Achis, king of Geth, is before accession and is separate from that royal era. The received Psalm title names Achimelech; the narrative in 1 Kings 21 names Achis. The Haydock edition’s chronological marker is inherited from the surrounding narrative, rather than freshly printed beside the flight itself. Its attribution to Ussher is retained in the table. The related Haydock note explicitly mentioning Usher is at Ps. 70:1; that separate note identifies the chronology’s source, without supplying the occasion of Psalm 33.[^52]

The USCCB Psalms introduction supplies the displayed collection-wide upper composition boundary and states the difficulty of securely dating an individual psalm. It neither fixes one occasion for these two poems nor establishes a shared place of composition. The traditional titles, the flight narrative, and this composition judgment answer different questions.[^53]

### Wisdom: the author’s city and the persecution in view {#hist-wisdom}

Gigot distinguishes the book’s Solomonic literary voice from its actual unknown authorship. His argument for Alexandria appeals to Greek language and thought, Egyptian circumstances, and the intended Jewish audience amid surrounding idolatry. Alexandria is therefore his proposed place of composition, rather than the named site of an individual death in Wisdom 3.[^54]

The two reigns in the table identify alternative periods of persecution which Gigot considers to be in the author’s view. He expressly permits publication after those rulers’ deaths. The visible “in or after” qualification retains that possibility: neither reign’s end is a latest possible date of writing. The alternatives are the historical source’s proposals, not a claim that every later scholar accepts either one.

### Matthew: the unnamed mountain and the written Gospel {#hist-matthew}

Matthew 4:23–5:1 places Jesus’ teaching after activity in Galilee. The mountain is not named. The Alleluia’s invitation belongs to the wider Galilean narrative, without a named site for its delivery. The table’s unresolved event date is independent of the dates proposed for writing the Gospel.

Jacquier reports several estimates in the historical *Catholic Encyclopedia*’s account of Matthew’s composition. His discussion distinguishes proposed early dates for an original Hebrew or Aramaic form from questions about the extant Greek Gospel. Durand similarly proposes an Aramaic original and calls the time of its rendering into Greek indefinite. The several estimates above retain their distinct qualifications; averaging them would supply a date neither source asserts.[^55]

### Apocalypse: Patmos and the heavenly vision {#hist-apocalypse}

Apocalypse 1:9 identifies John’s setting as Patmos; 1:4 addresses churches in Asia. The throne and worshippers in chapter 7 belong to the vision, rather than to a second earthly address. Van den Biesen reports the tradition placing John’s exile near the end of Domitian’s reign. The table’s broader reign span is a reference period, not a claim that the book took that entire period to write. Durand gives the other approximate range displayed above and acknowledges a different earlier proposal among critics of his period. The generated orientation preserves the returned alternatives without inventing a new event date for the heavenly worship. [^56]
