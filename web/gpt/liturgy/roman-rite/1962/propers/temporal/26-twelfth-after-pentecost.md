## The Propers: Themes and Movement {#triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

> **Governing thesis**
>
> Everything needed for Christian movement comes first as gift: help for the imperilled, sufficiency for ministers, the law read by Christ, covenant mercy for sinners, the fruits of creation, and life through the holy mystery. The gift reaches its liturgical test when the hearer stops defining the neighbour and becomes one.

### 1. Need confessed; sufficiency received <span class="nodecor">(*Int., Coll., Ep.*)</span>

The Introit supplies the grammar of dependence: *inténde* and *festína* ask help now. The Collect makes a still stronger claim. Even worthy service proceeds *de cuius múnere*; the petition to run without stumbling therefore asks grace to carry forward a motion grace has already begun. Paul gives the doctrinal center: confidence is *through Christ toward God*, while sufficiency is not *quasi ex nobis* but *ex Deo*. These elements do not cultivate religious passivity. They relocate agency so that service can be real without becoming self-authored.

The point is already audible in the Introit’s opening petition. Its first movement is not a report about danger but an address: God is named and help is asked before the enemies are described. The Collect then turns that emergency grammar into a rule for the whole Christian course. Grace is not merely a remedy applied after a failed attempt; it is the gift from which worthy service comes and the aid by which the runner advances.

### 2. The written command reaches its living end <span class="nodecor">(*Ep., Gosp.*)</span>

Paul’s “letter kills” is a covenantal contrast, not contempt for Scripture or law. Jesus proves the point by making the lawyer read: “What is written? How readest thou?” The answer from Deuteronomy and Leviticus is correct, yet knowledge reaches life only in *doing*. The lawyer asks for a class of persons; Jesus answers with a sequence of verbs—seeing, approaching, binding, carrying, paying, promising to return—and reverses the question: which traveller *became* neighbour? Law is neither abolished nor left on stone; it is fulfilled as Spirit-enabled mercy.

This also fixes the limits of Paul’s antithesis. The “letter” is not the literal sense of Scripture, and the ministry associated with Moses remains glorious. What kills is command encountered externally by the sinner without the inward gift that forgives and forms charity. The Gospel accordingly does not replace Torah with sentiment. It places Deut. 6:5 and Lev. 19:18 on the lawyer’s lips, accepts the answer, and makes its truth visible in conduct.

### 3. Praise and lament share one faithful address <span class="nodecor">(*Grad., All.*)</span>

The Gradual promises blessing “at all times.” The Alleluia immediately tests that universality by taking its verse from Psalm 87, whose prayer descends into almost unrelieved darkness. The singer nevertheless says “God of my salvation” and prays by day and night. The juxtaposition refuses two easy reductions: praise is not denial of suffering, and lament is not the loss of faith. In both, the mouth remains turned toward God; the meek can rejoice not because pain is imaginary but because dependence has found an addressee.

The received commentaries preserve more than one historical and spiritual horizon. Theodoret reads Psalm 87 through Israel’s calamity and fallen human suffering, while Augustine reads it through Christ’s Passion and through the Church’s prayer in prosperity and adversity. Their disagreement should not be flattened. Their convergence is narrower and firmer: day and night, the singer continues to address the God called salvation.

### 4. Promise is pleaded; pardon honors God’s name <span class="nodecor">(*Coll., Off., Sec.*)</span>

The Collect names God’s promises as the runner’s destination. At the Offertory Moses pleads a prior promise for people who cannot make innocence their argument: Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob are remembered while judgment remains real. Theodoret’s reading of Exodus keeps both sides together. Mercy preserves the people and the covenantal future, but it neither calls the sin good nor removes all discipline and accountability.

The Secret translates that posture into the Church’s offering. Its grammar does not make the gifts a price that purchases forgiveness. The petition asks God to regard the offerings favorably, so that by obtaining pardon for the offerers they may give honor to his name. Promise, intercession, and pardon therefore exclude boasting without making prayer or offering unreal.

The Offertory’s received wording sharpens the same restraint. It is not a continuous excerpt from the Clementine Vulgate: it incorporates matter associated with verse 12, says Jacob where the Vulgate says Israel, replaces the stars-and-land promise with the land flowing with milk and honey, and ends in an Old-Latin construction independently witnessed by Augustine. Those differences belong to the chant and may not be repaired by silently importing the Douay or Clementine wording. At the same time, Augustine reads the threatened “evil” as punishment, not moral evil in God, and Aquinas places intercession within immutable providence rather than against it.

The oration history adds a complementary limit. Shorter ancestors occur separately in the Veronese collection; the Old Gelasian gathers the three prayers with variants; the Hadrianum witnesses their later lexical shape together. This proves a received prayer-family and real development. It proves neither the name of an author nor that one compiler designed the complete chant-and-lesson argument reconstructed on these pages.

### 5. Providence remains visible at sacramental Communion <span class="nodecor">(*Comm., Postcomm.*)</span>

Communion sings bread, wine, and oil within Psalm 103’s praise of providence: earth is filled from God’s works before sacramental reception is considered. Theodoret stays at that created horizon; Eusebius develops spiritual readings without making them plainly Eucharistic; Augustine and Cassiodorus supply an explicitly sacramental reception. The tradition is richest when these levels are distinguished rather than collapsed.

The Postcommunion then states what the Church actually asks from the holy mystery: that participation vivify, and that it grant expiation and protection. The verbs name requested fruits, not a timetable or an automatic effect. Their doctrinal horizon includes both the objective gift and the recipient’s disposition. The rite closes with communicants still dependent on God for the life and defense needed to obey what they have heard.

The Communion antiphon is itself a disciplined act of selection. It begins at the second half of Psalm 103:13, reorders the clause, inserts *Dómine*, omits the first half of verse 14, and then sings bread, wine, and oil from verse 15. The historical English therefore prints the continuous psalm verses and marks the gap instead of manufacturing a hybrid translation. The liturgical use intensifies a sacramental hearing, but the underlying creation hymn continues to confess rain, cultivation, food, gladness, and human creatureliness.

The Postcommunion also has a history worth preserving. An early Veronese form has *participatio tui sancta mysterii*; later witnesses transmit the prayer-family that reaches the 1962 *huius*. Change of wording does not make the petition unstable. Across the witnesses participation remains a gift whose fruits are asked from God. Ignatius’ “medicine of immortality” and Aquinas’s treatment of Eucharistic effects illuminate that petition while also guarding the need for worthy reception.

> **Movement in four stages.** Cry and receive; read and enact; praise through the night; plead, offer, and partake. Every appointed element belongs to one or more of these stages. The movement is a source-grounded account of the received juxtaposition, not evidence that one historical compiler designed all ten texts as a single argument.

### The complete appointed text

The preceding formulary is part of this canonical full edition. Its Latin is the image-collated text rather than normalized Bible Latin: the Introit retains *inimíci mei* and *cógitant*; the Alleluia retains the comma after *Dómine*; the Gospel retains the comma after *iumentum suum*; and the Offertory and Communion preserve their received adaptations. The English witnesses are printed without silently repairing those differences.

### Help as a lifelong posture <span class="nodecor">(*Int.*)</span>

Psalm 69 is short but not contextless. Its cry is substantially the close of Psalm 39, now presented under a title of remembrance. The antiphon selects the request for immediate help, the enemies who seek the singer’s life, and their reversal. It is therefore neither a free-standing magical formula nor a warrant to assign biblical enemy language to contemporary rivals. Its literal force is the threatened worshipper’s appeal to the God on whom life depends.

The reception makes that appeal durable. Cassian, *Conference* 10.10, gives *Deus, in adiutorium* to the monk as a formula able to meet every condition: danger, distraction, gratitude, and perseverance. The Rule of St Benedict places it at the opening of the Hours (chs. 17–18) and three times on the lips of the incoming kitchen server (ch. 35); urgent prayer becomes both common worship and humble work. Augustine, *Enarratio in Psalmum* 69.1–4, hears the voice of Christ’s members and makes perpetual need, not occasional failure, the reason for perpetual help. Theodoret (PG 80, 1415–1418) instead keeps the Davidic crisis prominent: calamities surround the speaker, whose own past sin sharpens the distress. Bellarmine’s commentary on Psalm 69 notes both the Psalm 39 doublet and the verse’s daily liturgical use. These are compatible extensions, not proof that the Mass compiler intended monastic custom or a single Davidic episode.

Greek reception is broader than Theodoret’s historical reading. Eusebius, *Commentary on the Psalms* at Psalm 69 (PG 23, 767–772), treats the cry as a formula kept ready on the lips, then moves among David, Christ, the Church, and the Passion–resurrection mystery. The rapid shift of personae is a feature of his ecclesial exegesis, not a reconstruction of the psalm’s date.

### Grace precedes the run <span class="nodecor">(*Coll.*)</span>

The Collect’s syntax protects Catholic teaching from two opposite errors. Worthy and praiseworthy service is genuinely the faithful people’s act, yet the prayer locates even its coming-to-be in God’s gift. The ensuing imperative, *tríbue*, asks not to replace human running but to make it reach the promises *sine offensióne*, without stumbling. Grace precedes, accompanies, and brings the act toward its promised end.

This is the order taught at Trent: preparation for justification is itself impossible without prevenient grace, while those moved and assisted freely assent and cooperate (Session VI, chs. 5–6); growth in the received life remains God’s gift and the justified person’s action (ch. 16). The *Catechism* 2008–2011 likewise describes merit as arising from God’s initiative and the believer’s free collaboration. These sources illuminate the prayer’s grammar; they do not document why this Collect was joined to this Sunday. Hilary, *Tractatus in Psalmum 118*, Daleth 11–12, and Augustine, *Enarratio in Psalmum 118*, sermo 11.1–5, place progress in the path under divine teaching and help; Augustine, *De gratia et libero arbitrio* 16.32–17.33, and Aquinas, *ST* I–II, q. 109, give the grace-and-cooperation distinction its developed form. Benedict’s Prologue makes running toward the heavenly homeland a monastic reception rather than a history of this prayer. In the Old Gelasian, all three orations occur together in Book III, no. VIII (Wilson, pp. 228–229); the Hadrianum Gregorian family places them together under its thirteenth Sunday after Pentecost (Wilson, no. LXXXI, p. 173). The history establishes a received oration-set, not a personal authorship claim.

### Letter and Spirit within one divine economy <span class="nodecor">(*Ep.*)</span>

The pericope begins after Paul has called the Corinthians a letter of Christ, written not in ink or on stone but by the living God’s Spirit on hearts (2 Cor. 3:3). It ends before his comparison of transient and surpassing glory has finished (vv. 10–18). Within that frame, *líttera enim occídit* cannot mean that literal sense, written Scripture, Israel, or moral command is evil. Paul calls the stone-lettered ministry glorious. The contrast concerns what external command does when it encounters sin without the inward gift that forgives and enables obedience; compare Jer. 31:31–34, Ezek. 36:25–27, and Rom. 7–8.

| **Witness** | **Exact locus** | **Interpretive move** |
|:--------------|:--------------------|:------------------------------|
| St John Chrysostom | *Homilies on 2 Corinthians* 6.2; 7.1, 5 | The Corinthians themselves are Spirit-written evidence. The minister’s confidence does not arise from self-praise, and the old ministry was truly glorious even as the new surpasses it. |
| Theodoret of Cyrus | *Interpretatio in II Corinthios*, PG 82, 391–394 | The ministers’ fitness comes from God; “letter” names the law without the enlivening gift, while the Spirit supplies power for obedience. |
| Ambrosiaster | *Commentarius in II Corinthios* 3:4–9, PL 17, 176–177 | Moses’ glory is honored, but the Spirit’s ministry gives the forgiveness and righteousness the condemning letter could not confer. |
| St Augustine | *De spiritu et littera* 4.6; 14.23–25; 19.34; 26.42 | Even a good command remains “letter” if inward grace is absent; the Spirit writes charity in the heart. His earlier *De doctrina christiana* 3.5.9 also warns against a carnal captivity to signs, but that hermeneutical use is an application, not Paul’s primary covenantal contrast. |
| St Thomas Aquinas | *Super II ad Corinthios* 3, lect. 1–2; *ST* I–II, q. 106, aa. 1–2 | New law is principally the grace of the Holy Spirit; even Gospel writing without inward grace would remain an external letter. Human ministry remains real because God makes ministers fit. |

Catholic reading therefore rejects both self-sufficient activism and a law-versus-grace caricature directed against the Jewish people. *Dei Verbum* 14–16 and *Nostra aetate* 4 require the old covenant and Israel’s received gifts to be honored within salvation history. Paul contrasts modes and ministries under sin and grace; he does not assign ethnic worth. The Pontifical Biblical Commission’s *The Jewish People and Their Sacred Scriptures in the Christian Bible* 41 further warns that 2 Cor. 3 must be read within Paul’s positive recognition of the old dispensation’s permanent value as revelation.

### Praise tested by adversity <span class="nodecor">(*Grad.*)</span>

Psalm 33 is alphabetic wisdom praise whose title connects it with David’s escape before a Philistine ruler. Basil (*Homilia in Psalmum 33* 1, PG 29), Theodoret (PG 80, 1101–1109), and Augustine (*Enarratio in Psalmum* 33, sermones 1–2) preserve different solutions to the title’s Abimelech/Achish problem; none converts a difficult superscription into a secure modern date. On the appointed verses their convergence matters more: “always” embraces prosperity and affliction, and praise in the Lord makes the meek fellow participants rather than spectators.

Augustine’s first sermon reads an Old-Latin title addition, “he was carried in his own hands,” Eucharistically: Christ entrusted the sacrament of his body while carrying that body. The addition is absent from the Clementine title and from the Douay printed here. It is genuine reception of Augustine’s psalter, not an extra appointed verse. Bellarmine, commenting on vv. 2–3, takes “always” as praise at fitting times throughout life and in the Church’s continuing worship. In the Gradual this becomes enacted: the congregation has heard that sufficiency is from God and answers by making its boast in him.

### An Alleluia from the night <span class="nodecor">(*All.*)</span>

Psalm 87 is remarkable for ending without an explicit turn to consolation. The selected verse nevertheless contains its theological fulcrum: *Deus salútis meæ*. Day and night are not two rival spiritual states but the entire duration of an appeal addressed to the God named as salvation.

Augustine, *Enarratio in Psalmum* 87.1–2, hears Christ’s Passion and reads day and night as prosperity and adversity; Cassiodorus follows that Christological line, and Bellarmine concretizes it in Gethsemane and Good Friday. Theodoret (PG 80, 1567–1573) supplies a real counter-reading: Israel’s calamities and, with them, fallen humanity’s pains. The Alleluia does not force one historical hypothesis. Its liturgical work is to confess salvation from within lament immediately before eyes are blessed for seeing Christ.

Eusebius (PG 23, 1052–1056) reads Psalm 86’s birth language followed by Psalm 87’s death language as an incarnational sequence and takes day-and-night prayer as Christ’s continuous oblation. His construction differs both from Augustine’s prosperity/adversity pairing and Theodoret’s exilic-human horizon; their convergence lies in sustained address to God, not in a shared account of composition.

### From correct reading to enacted neighbour-love <span class="nodecor">(*Gosp.*)</span>

Luke first blesses the disciples’ eyes and ears, then stages a reader of the law. Jesus neither mocks Torah nor answers in place of the lawyer: he asks what is written and how it is read. The lawyer joins Deut. 6:5 and Lev. 19:18 correctly. The problem emerges in *volens iustificáre seípsum*. His question seeks a boundary around obligation; Jesus asks which of three men *became* neighbour through mercy. The command *Vade, et tu fac simíliter* prevents admiration from substituting for action.

The road descends sharply through desert terrain from Jerusalem toward Jericho, so the narrated danger is credible; yet it remains parable, not a report locating Jesus on that road. Priest and Levite are not proofs about Judaism as such. The story’s shock depends on the Samaritan being the socially and religiously distrusted outsider who performs the law’s demand.

| **Witness** | **Exact locus** | **Interpretive move** |
|:--------------|:--------------------|:------------------------------|
| Origen | *Homiliae in Lucam* 34 | Preserves the ancient Christological reading: humanity descends and is wounded; Christ draws near; the inn receives the patient. He attributes the eschatological reading of the promised return to an elder rather than presenting it as his own invention. |
| Clement of Alexandria | *Quis dives salvetur?* 28–29 | Makes the neighbour one who shares in love and receives love, with Christ as the supreme neighbour; this is moral-Christological reuse rather than continuous exegesis. |
| St Irenaeus | *Against Heresies* 3.17.3 | Reuses the Samaritan’s oil as an image of the Holy Spirit’s healing gift; this is a bounded theological application rather than a continuous exposition of Luke 10. |
| St Cyril of Alexandria | *Commentary on Luke*, Sermons 67–68 | Insists that love of God and neighbour fulfills the law and develops Christ’s pedagogical reversal of the lawyer’s boundary-making question. |

| St Augustine | *Quaestiones Evangeliorum* 2.19 | Gives the famous compact allegorical table from the fall and incarnation to the Church, charity, apostolic ministry, and return. It does not explicitly map the remedies to sacraments and does not claim the evangelist’s historical intent. |

The later Catholic line remains practical. St John Paul II, *Dives in misericordia* 3, calls the parable a revelation of merciful love through action; Pope Francis, *Fratelli tutti* 56–86, reads every day as a fresh decision whether to be passer-by or neighbour. These applications are not substitutes for Christology. They show why Bede’s both-senses rule remains important: Christ heals, and the healed are commanded to act.

### Moses between judgment and the people <span class="nodecor">(*Off.*)</span>

The Offertory is not a simple excerpt from the Clementine Vulgate. It condenses Exod. 32:11–14, carries matter from unprinted v. 12, names Jacob where the Vulgate has Israel, substitutes the land flowing with milk and honey for the stars-and-land formulation, and closes in an Old-Latin shape. Augustine’s lemma in *Quaestiones in Heptateuchum* II.143—*propitiatus est Dominus de malitia quam dixit facere populo suo*—independently witnesses the same construction. The text’s received difference is therefore part of the chant, not a corruption to repair from the Douay.

Augustine’s questions II.143, 147, and 149–151 hold together divine constancy and real intercession. “Evil” here names threatened punishment, not moral evil in God; Moses’ love for the people is itself within divine providence. In *City of God* 15.25 Augustine adds the doctrinal guardrail that divine anger is not a perturbation but just judgment. Gregory the Great, *Moralia* 9.16.23, presents God’s “let me alone” as giving Moses boldness to supplicate; *Moralia* 20.5.14 reads the servant’s willingness to die for the people as self-offering. Tertullian, *Adversus Marcionem* 2.26, explicitly calls Moses’ deprecation a type of Christ; Chrysostom, *Homily 16 on Romans*, uses Moses as the intercessor daring all for God’s glory.

The Greek and medieval controls add needed limits. Theodoret, *Questions on the Octateuch* 67–68, distinguishes the narrative’s language from changeable passion in God. Question 68 also preserves the sequence in which mercy postpones and preserves without removing discipline or judgment. First Clement 53 and Chrysostom, *On the Priesthood* IV.1, receive Moses’ willingness to be blotted out as pastoral intercession. Aquinas, *ST* I, q. 19, a. 7, ad 1 and II–II, q. 83, a. 2, explains that an immutable providential will includes secondary causes and prayer. The *Catechism* 2574–2577 presents Moses as the intercessor educated in fidelity and mercy. None of this revokes Israel’s covenantal gifts or competes with Christ, the unique mediator.

At the Offertory that history becomes present action: the Church approaches the altar without claiming the congregation deserves immunity. It pleads the promise and places a received figure of Christ’s intercession beside the gifts. Typology clarifies the liturgical act; it must not turn biblical Israel into a mere negative foil or imply mutability in God.

### Pardon as the offering’s honor <span class="nodecor">(*Sec.*)</span>

The Secret’s main request is God’s favorable regard upon the offerings. Its purpose clause is striking: the grammatical subject of *dent* remains the offerings, and *nobis indulgéntiam largiéndo* describes the way they give honor to God’s name—by granting pardon to us. Honor is not an achievement presented for divine approval. The 1861 English reverses the Latin clause order and recasts the offerings’ grammatical agency, although it preserves the relation between pardon and honor.

The prayer belongs to the ancient three-oration set found together in the Old Gelasian and Hadrianum witnesses. The Old Gelasian has a *largiaris*/*largiendo* variation in the transmission; the 1962 typical reads *largiéndo*. Schuster, *The Sacramentary* III, pp. 128–131, makes pardon the favor to which the other benefits are ordered. Trent, Session XXII, ch. 2, supplies the doctrinal distinction: the Mass is the sacramental representation of Christ’s one sacrifice and is truly propitiatory; the prayer does not say that human gifts purchase forgiveness. Justin, *Dialogue with Trypho* 116–117; Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* IV.17.5–18.6; Cyril of Jerusalem, *Mystagogical Catechesis* V.8–10; and Augustine, *City of God* X.5–6, 20, provide complementary ancient controls: the Church offers Eucharistic praise and created gifts, while Christ and the communion of his body give the offering its acceptable form.

### Providence and sacramental reception <span class="nodecor">(*Comm.*)</span>

In Psalm 103 the literal sequence is creation’s providence: water from God’s upper chambers makes earth fruitful, and cultivation yields bread, wine, and oil. Theodoret (PG 80, 1693–1707) stays at this horizon: bread nourishes, wine gladdens, and oil feeds and brightens. Bellarmine adds gratitude, generosity, and frugality. Eusebius (PG 23, 1273–1280) begins from providence but also develops spiritual senses: wine evokes the true Vine, bread the mystical Logos, and oil light and healing. This is Greek Christian spiritualization of the created triad, but it is not plainly a Eucharistic exposition. Together these witnesses prevent an exclusively allegorical reading of created goods.

Augustine, *Enarratio in Psalmum* 103, sermo 3.12–14, receives the same gifts through Christ and his body: bread from the earth becomes Christ preached through apostolic earth, the cup gives sober inebriation, and oil evokes the anointing of the whole Christ. Cassiodorus, *Expositio Psalmorum* 103.20, is more explicit: wine consecrated as Christ’s blood and royal chrism belong to the verse’s ecclesial reception. That development is especially apt when the 1962 Missal appoints the text at Communion. It remains reception, not proof of the psalmist’s intention.

### Participation that vivifies, expiates, and guards <span class="nodecor">(*Postcomm.*)</span>

The Postcommunion asks one holy participation to give three related fruits: life, expiation, and protection. *Páriter* keeps the last two together; the prayer neither reduces Communion to interior encouragement nor treats the sacrament as an automatic charm. The communicants ask for what the mystery effects in rightly disposed recipients.

The Verona sacramentary prints an early form with *participatio tui sancta mysterii* (Feltoe, p. 60); the Old Gelasian and the Hadrianum transmit the prayer within the same oration-family used here. The *Catechism* 1391–1395 gives a doctrinal vocabulary for its petitions: Communion deepens union with Christ, preserves and renews the life of grace, separates from sin, and strengthens charity. Historical transmission does not establish the prayer’s author or a single date, and the doctrinal parallel does not establish formulary intent.

Ignatius, *Letter to the Ephesians* 20, receives Eucharistic communion as medicine of immortality; Aquinas, *ST* III, q. 79, distinguishes its life-giving, sin-remitting, and protective effects while preserving the need for worthy reception and the obstacle posed by mortal sin. These are doctrinal illuminations of the oration’s petitions, not translations of *expiatiónem* or evidence for its original date.

## Source-Grounded Synthesis

### Gift does not abolish action; it makes action Christian

The Collect and Epistle locate the beginning in God: service is his gift and sufficiency is from him. The Gospel then refuses to let received grace become an alibi for non-action. Its imperatives are exact: do this and live; go and do likewise. Augustine’s grace-and-charity account, Aquinas’s new-law account, and Bede’s refusal to let allegory dissolve mutual duty all support the same distinction. Catholic cooperation is neither self-salvation nor inertia; it is action made possible and ordered by grace.

### The opposition is not law versus mercy but killing isolation versus vivifying fulfilment

Paul honors the old ministry’s glory while identifying its inability, as external letter, to overcome sin. Jesus sends the lawyer to the written law, accepts his answer, and exposes what self-justifying reading withholds. Moses then speaks from within covenant and the Secret asks pardon. Across these texts, mercy does not cancel command or judgment. It forgives, writes charity within, and makes the command become neighborly deed.

### Seeing becomes morally significant only when it becomes approach

The disciples’ eyes are blessed; priest, Levite, and Samaritan all see. Sight alone therefore cannot be the criterion. The narrative differentiates vision by the movement that follows it: passing by or drawing near. The Gradual’s meek hearers and the Alleluia’s day-and-night speaker widen this into a liturgical anthropology: eyes, ears, mouth, memory, and body are trained for mercy. No located source makes this exact ten-proper argument, but the Gospel’s grammar and the checked reception ground the central distinction.

### Intercession and pardon do not erase judgment

Moses remains before God for a guilty people and invokes a covenant promise that precedes their desert. Augustine and Aquinas protect divine immutability; Gregory receives the prayer as self-offering; Theodoret preserves the narrative sequence in which mercy leaves discipline and accountability real. The Secret likewise asks pardon without treating the offerings as a price. Intercession is a graced secondary cause within providence, not a correction of God.

### Providence remains visible in sacramental reception

Psalm 103 praises the Creator’s bread, wine, and oil before later reception reads the verses sacramentally. Theodoret guards the providential literal sense; Augustine and Cassiodorus document that later sacramental horizon. The Postcommunion asks participation to vivify and to grant expiation and protection. Neither verb promises automatic fruit or encodes continuation. Created provision and sacramental gift can therefore be confessed together without being made interchangeable.

## The Propers: Notable and Quotable

These four afterlives do more than repeat a biblical phrase: each moves it into a new register and changes what the wording is made to do.

> **The neighbour becomes a legal duty — *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932)**
>
> **Appointed wording.** The lawyer asks, “And who is my neighbour?” (Luke 10:29).
>
> **Later use and locus.** Lord Atkin’s speech in *Donoghue v Stevenson*, \[1932\] A.C. 562 at 580, moves from the command to love one’s neighbour to a restricted legal duty of care and asks: “Who then, in law, is my neighbour?”
>
> **The turn and its limit.** Jesus refuses the lawyer’s attempt to delimit a class and asks who became neighbour by mercy. Tort law must do the thing the parable resists: specify foreseeability and legal proximity. Atkin turns a self-justifying question into a rule for liability; the civil rule is not presented as the Gospel’s whole moral demand.

> **The Jerusalem–Jericho road becomes an experiment — Darley and Batson (1973)**
>
> **Appointed wording.** A man goes “from Jerusalem to Jericho”; priest and Levite see him and pass by (Luke 10:30–32).
>
> **Later use and locus.** John M. Darley and C. Daniel Batson, “From Jerusalem to Jericho: A Study of Situational and Dispositional Variables in Helping Behavior,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 27.1 (1973), 100–108, sent seminarians past a slumped confederate while varying hurry and talk topic. The discussion reports that some “literally stepped over the victim” while going to speak on the parable (p. 107).
>
> **The turn and its limit.** A narrative about seeing and passing becomes an experimental protocol. The assigned talk topic was not a significant predictor of helping, while time pressure had a marked effect. The staged emergency and small seminary sample do not exhaust mercy or prove the motives of the parable’s priest and Levite.

> **The killing letter exposes a marriage contract — Thomas Hardy (1896)**
>
> **Appointed wording.** “For the letter killeth: but the spirit quickeneth” (2 Cor. 3:6).
>
> **Later use and locus.** Thomas Hardy places 2 Cor. 3:6 on the title page of the 1896 book edition of *Jude the Obscure*. In Part VI, ch. VIII (ch. 50 in the linked edition), Jude tells Sue inside a church that their return to socially sanctioned marriages is acting by the letter, and repeats “the letter killeth!”
>
> **The turn and its limit.** Paul’s contrast between covenant ministries becomes an indictment of civil and ecclesial marriage convention; the epigraph also makes the phrase a key for the novel’s conflict between legal form and lived attachment. Jude is a conflicted fictional speaker, not Hardy’s unqualified doctrinal voice, and the novel’s use is not Paul’s exegetical meaning.

> **Paul becomes a canon of statutory construction — Justice Frankfurter (1950)**
>
> **Appointed wording.** “The letter killeth” (2 Cor. 3:6).
>
> **Later use and locus.** Justice Felix Frankfurter’s dissent in *United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy*, 338 U.S. 537, 548, 550 (1950), applies the phrase to Acts of Congress and invokes it while construing the War Brides Act in the case of an excluded soldier’s wife.
>
> **The turn and its limit.** A Pauline claim about letter and Spirit is naturalized as an interpretive maxim: statutory purpose should control a harsh literal application. The dissent does not cite the verse and supplies no covenant exegesis. Its force depends on an English idiom descended from Paul, not on identity between apostolic ministry and judicial method.

## The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities

*The following are exploratory editorial or AI proposals. They are not attributed teaching, historical intent, or evidence about the formulary’s compiler. Each states its anchors, mechanism, fruit, nearest checked analogue, and controlling limit.*

> **Exploratory proposal — Ministry begins where the traveller leaves**
>
> **Anchors:** Epistle, Gospel, Postcommunion.
>
> **Mechanism:** Paul is made a fit minister; the Samaritan entrusts the wounded man to an innkeeper for continuing care; the Postcommunion asks holy participation to vivify and to grant expiation and protection to its recipients.
>
> **Fruit:** Mercy needs durable ministries, not only a first responder. The Church receives care from Christ in order to continue it responsibly.
>
> **Precedent result:** **Near analogue located.** Ambrose 7.82–84, Augustine *Questions on the Gospels* 2.19, and Bede identify the inn and its keeper ecclesially; none joins Paul’s sufficiency or this Postcommunion.
>
> **Limit:** No minister or institution may identify itself simply with the Samaritan; ecclesial allegory remains subject to the literal command of mercy.

> **Exploratory proposal — Promise is destination, covenant ground, and guaranty**
>
> **Anchors:** Collect, Offertory, Gospel.
>
> **Mechanism:** The Church runs toward God’s promises; Moses invokes the oath to the patriarchs; the Samaritan promises to return and repay further expense.
>
> **Fruit:** Christian hope may be read not as escape from wounded history but as fidelity that sustains costly care within it.
>
> **Precedent result:** **Near analogue located.** Origen, *Homily on Luke* 34, reports an elder’s eschatological reading of the return; Augustine, *Questions on the Gospels* 2.19, also maps the return within his allegory. Checked witnesses do not join all three promise forms.
>
> **Limit:** The Samaritan’s fictional undertaking is not a covenant equal to God’s oath, and the proposal proves no compositional design.

> **Exploratory proposal — Mercy’s longer clock**
>
> **Anchors:** Gradual, Alleluia, Gospel.
>
> **Mechanism:** Praise is “always,” prayer continues day and night, care passes into the next day and awaits return.
>
> **Fruit:** Compassion becomes a temporal discipline: attention must survive the first emotion, the first expense, and the first liturgical moment.
>
> **Precedent result:** **Not located in the checked corpus.** Individual witnesses interpret each temporal phrase, but no checked source joins this three-part duration.
>
> **Limit:** The words arise in different literary units; they do not create a schedule, and urgency in the Introit remains as important as duration.

> **Exploratory proposal — A bounded question receives an open-ended answer**
>
> **Anchors:** Gospel and Secret.
>
> **Mechanism:** *Quis est meus proximus?* seeks the limit of the person owed love; *quodcúmque supererogáveris* opens expenditure beyond the initial sum; *indulgéntiam largiéndo* asks pardon to be lavished.
>
> **Fruit:** Mercy may resist the self-protecting move from “whom must I love?” to the smallest defensible obligation.
>
> **Precedent result:** **Near analogue located.** Gospel commentators stress universal neighbour-love and the innkeeper’s further expense, but no checked witness joins the Secret’s gerund.
>
> **Limit:** Christian generosity requires prudence, justice, vocation, and finite stewardship; *Fratelli tutti* 79–82 does not erase these goods.

> **Exploratory proposal — Compassion and appeasement name two directions of mercy**
>
> **Anchors:** Gospel, Offertory, Secret.
>
> **Mechanism:** The Samaritan is moved toward the sufferer; Moses speaks before God for the guilty; the Secret asks favorable regard and pardon at the altar.
>
> **Fruit:** Mercy can be both approach to the wounded and intercession for the culpable, holding care and judgment in one prayer.
>
> **Precedent result:** **Near analogue located.** Patristic witnesses read Christ in both Moses and the Samaritan, but no checked source constructs this directional pairing with the Secret.
>
> **Limit:** God undergoes no emotional change: Augustine, *City of God* 15.25, governs the language of anger and appeasement.

> **Exploratory proposal — The medicine is neither only natural nor only sacramental**
>
> **Anchors:** Gospel, Communion, Postcommunion.
>
> **Mechanism:** Oil and wine treat wounds; Psalm 103 praises bread, wine, and oil as providential gifts; holy participation gives life and protection.
>
> **Fruit:** Bodily works of mercy and sacramental communion can be held together without turning either into a decorative metaphor.
>
> **Precedent result:** **Near analogue located.** Ambrose and Augustine connect the Samaritan’s remedies to ecclesial healing; Augustine and Cassiodorus receive Psalm 103 sacramentally. The exact three-element synthesis is editorial.
>
> **Limit:** The psalm’s literal created goods and the parable’s practical care must not be dissolved into Eucharistic allegory.
