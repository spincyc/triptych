## Scriptural Date and Location

### Offertory

**Citation:** Ps. 39 (40):14–15, selected

**Location:** An endangered petitioner amid public thanksgiving.

**Date:** Composition: Before c. 165 B.C.

The whole psalm moves from rescue and obedience back to threatened life; no particular writing-place or enemy is identified. The USCCB Psalms introduction supplies a shared latest composition boundary, not a secure date for this psalm. <span class="nodecor">(Ps. 39:1–18; USCCB, Psalms introduction.)</span>

### Communion

**Citation:** Ps. 70 (71):16–18, selected

**Location:** An aging speaker; Davidic and captivity settings remain distinct.

**Date:** Composition: Before c. 165 B.C. Historical setting: When he fled from the face of his son Absalom. Superscription setting: Preferred In the third year of the reign of Joakim, king of Juda; alternatives A.M. 3398.

Youth, failing strength and future hearers frame the prayer. Bellarmine reads David’s testimony; no exact composition place is established. The common critical boundary does not date the individual psalm; the alternate A.M. figure belongs to the superscription discussion, not the preferred historical setting. <span class="nodecor">(Ps. 70:1–24; Bellarmine, pp. 212–213; chronology sources in References.)</span>

### Introit

**Citation:** Ps. 85 (86):3,5; v. 1

**Location:** Davidic title; a needy servant threatened by attackers.

**Date:** Composition: Before c. 165 B.C.

The title supplies the received attribution; the psalm names no particular Davidic crisis or writing-place. Its audience of prayer extends toward the nations’ worship. Individual composition remains insecure within the common critical boundary. <span class="nodecor">(Ps. 85:1–17; USCCB, Psalms introduction.)</span>

### Alleluia

**Citation:** Ps. 97 (98):1, opening

**Location:** Israel’s salvation before the nations and creation.

**Date:** Composition: Before c. 165 B.C. Prophetic referent – disputed: B.C. 3; c. B.C. 7.

No particular rescue or composition place is named. The critical boundary is common to the Psalter. The Nativity alternatives concern its prophetic referent; Gerard reports one as an opinion held by others. <span class="nodecor">(Ps. 97:1–9; USCCB, Psalms introduction; Gerard, “General Chronology,” “Christian era,” first paragraph; “Chronology of the Life of Jesus Christ.”)</span>

### Gradual

**Citation:** Ps. 101 (102):16–17

**Location:** Sion/Jerusalem restored; peoples gathered for worship.

**Date:** Composition: Before c. 165 B.C.

An afflicted speaker contrasts his frailty with God’s permanence. Jerusalem is the subject, not a proved writing-location. Neither the restoration nor the psalm has an individual date established here. <span class="nodecor">(Ps. 101:1–29; USCCB, Psalms introduction.)</span>

### Gospel

**Citation:** Luke 14:1–11

**Location:** St Luke’s Gospel; an unnamed leading Pharisee’s house.

**Date:** Composition – disputed: c. A.D. 70; Before the end of the Roman imprisonment, when the Acts was finished. Event – No narrated-event date in the chronology corpus.

*Event: Sabbath meal and cure in the journey-to-Jerusalem context (Luke 13:22; 14:1–11); town and host unnamed. The wedding is the seating parable’s setting.*

Traditional composition alternatives are those of the *Catholic Encyclopedia* and the Biblical Commission. Composition geography, detailed first-audience geography and a full modern authorship discussion are not established in the consulted material. The modern USCCB chapter note reports son/ox beside the ass/ox variant; the Missal retains ass/ox.

### Epistle

**Citation:** Eph. 3:13–21

**Location:** St Paul, imprisoned for his Gentile mission; saints sharing one inheritance.

**Date:** Composition – disputed: A.D. 58–63; A.D. 61.

The apostle’s affliction and the Gentile recipients frame his prayer (Eph. 2–4). The traditional alternatives come from the *Catholic Encyclopedia*. Prison city, destination geography and a full modern authorship account are not established in the consulted introductions; the USCCB chapter notes treat the prayer’s wording and interpretation.

## The Propers: Themes and Movement {#triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

God’s mercy sustains a life rich in charity, fruitful in good works and open to others, while freeing it from the desire to possess its own greatness. The fullness for which Paul prays belongs with the low place Jesus teaches, the purification sought at the altar and the aging voice that still asks for help.

### Grace gives strength for the work of charity

The Introit begins with a cry that lasts all day. The singer appeals to God’s sweetness and abundant mercy before naming his poverty in the psalm verse. Need therefore speaks with confidence: God is accessible to those who call upon him. Augustine hears Christ and his members praying together, and reads the continuing cry across the Church’s successive generations; the materially rich, too, can be God’s poor when they depend upon him. <span class="nodecor">(Augustine, *Enarrationes* 85, abridged NPNF “Psalm LXXXVI,” §§1–3,5,7.)</span>

The Collect gives this dependence an active purpose. Grace is asked to precede and follow so that good works receive constant attention. Paul’s prayer names the divine agency more fully: the Father grants strength through the Spirit, Christ dwells through faith, and charity roots and establishes the saints. He seeks this gift for people who might lose heart at his affliction on their behalf. The prayer’s abundance does not require an untroubled life; it strengthens a people within the demands of their common vocation. <span class="nodecor">(Collect; Eph. 3:13–21; Chrysostom, *Homilies on Ephesians* VII.)</span>

Mercy then receives concrete expression in the Gospel. Jesus takes hold of the sick man, heals him and sends him away before his observers have answered his question. He also corrects the guests’ choice of precedence. Both bodily relief and the reform of ambition concern the life the Collect asks grace to sustain. Cyril teaches that spiritual Sabbath rest includes holy works and the cessation of sin: mercy belongs to the law’s divine purpose. <span class="nodecor">(Luke 14:1–11; Cyril, *Commentary on Luke*, Sermon CI, pp. 471–475.)</span>

The Secret brings the same dependence into the offering. The Church asks the present sacrifice to cleanse her and divine mercy to make her fit for participation. Her good works remain real, but they do not render the giver of grace dispensable. Augustine’s exposition of the Communion psalm makes precisely this point: God does not merely show a road and then leave the traveler to complete it alone. <span class="nodecor">(Secret; Augustine, *Enarrationes* 70, abridged NPNF “Psalm LXXI,” §§18–21.)</span>

### Honor becomes a gift received without grasping

Jesus’ parable leaves room for honor: the host may invite a guest higher. Its rebuke falls on the guest who chooses the first place for himself. The difference concerns both action and desire. Francis de Sales exposes the false humility that takes a low place only to obtain a better one; his echo of the seating saying requires the heart to agree with its outward signs. The same chapter rejects a supposed humility that refuses God’s gifts or obstructs charity. <span class="nodecor">(Luke 14:7–11; Francis de Sales, *Introduction à la vie dévote* III.5.)</span>

Paul’s use of glory deepens this correction. He calls his afflictions the recipients’ glory, asks from the riches of God’s glory and ends by giving glory to God in the Church and Christ. Honor before dinner companions and doxological glory have different objects. Together they place human good within relations of gift: a host confers a place, an apostle suffers for others, and the Church praises the giver. Cyril likewise directs the guest beyond wealth and social display toward truthful self-knowledge and honor from God. <span class="nodecor">(Eph. 3:13,16,21; Cyril, Sermon CII, pp. 476–479.)</span>

The Offertory keeps this humility close to a threatened life. Its repeated appeal asks help and the shame of those seeking the singer’s destruction. Humility can seek protection. The Communion’s remembrance of God’s justice gives grateful dependence a clear object, without making the sufferer responsible for an enemy’s harm. <span class="nodecor">(Ps. 39:14–15 and 70:16–18, appointed selections; Aquinas, *Super Psalmo* 39 n. 7.)</span> <span id="triptych:brief-synthesis:end" data-label="triptych:brief-synthesis:end"></span>

### Christ’s inward dwelling gathers a people of praise

Paul asks that Christ dwell in hearts, but the prayer never closes the heart upon itself. Comprehension is with all the saints; the doxology is in the Church. The surrounding epistle names Gentile co-inheritance and the common dwelling of God in the Spirit, then turns toward humility, patience and unity. Rooted charity grows through a life shared with others. <span class="nodecor">(Eph. 2:18–22; 3:6,17–21; 4:1–6.)</span>

The Gradual’s Sion is God’s work of building, revealed before nations and kings. Augustine reads this as the Church gathered and built now before Christ’s glorious judgement. Theodoret begins with the restoration of the humbled city, then argues that the universal conversion promised in the psalm exceeds that return: the neighboring peoples’ wonder did not mean that all had believed. The restored city’s glory and the Church’s gathering thus remain distinct moments within his exposition. <span class="nodecor">(Augustine, *Enarrationes* 101, abridged NPNF “Psalm CII,” §§16–18; Theodoret, *Interpretatio*, PG 80, 1679B–1682B.)</span>

The Alleluia summons the new canticle because the Lord has done wonders. In the whole psalm, Israel’s salvation becomes visible to the nations and creation welcomes the Lord’s just judgement. Theodoret interprets the new song through the Savior’s new manner of worship and life; Bellarmine unfolds the wonders through Christ’s life and saving victory. Praise answers divine action and draws singers into its public acknowledgment. <span class="nodecor">(Ps. 97:1–9; Theodoret, PG 80, 1657C–1658D; Bellarmine, *Commentary*, p. 306.)</span>

The *Liturgical Year* continuation already joins the Epistle’s inward strengthening to Sion’s divine building and common praise. Its movement agrees with the prayer’s own breadth: charity enlarges the saints’ common life. Chrysostom interprets Paul’s dimensions as the immensity of Christ’s love. Augustine and Aquinas also unfold them through the Cross, where charity works, perseveres, hopes for heaven and receives its hidden beginning from God. Their spiritual readings teach a greatness whose source is gift. <span class="nodecor">(*Liturgical Year*, 1909 continuation, pp. 359,363; Chrysostom VII; Augustine, Letter 140, 25.62–26.64; Aquinas, *Super Ephesios* 3.5.)</span>

### The whole person continues to receive God’s help

The man before Jesus needs bodily healing. Paul asks for strength in the inner person. The Secret asks cleansing through sacrifice; the Postcommunion asks the purification and renewal of minds and help for bodies, now and hereafter. These are distinct gifts to embodied persons. The inward language of grace does not make bodily need irrelevant, and the bodily cure does not exhaust the fullness of charity for which the Church prays. <span class="nodecor">(Luke 14:2–4; Eph. 3:16–19; Secret; Postcommunion.)</span>

The sacramental prayers have a long association: Wilson’s Gelasian edition sets *Munda nos* and *Purifica* in their respective positions within the same Mass; the Gregorian supplement groups all three orations under Sunday XVII. The *Liturgical Year* continuation interprets the Secret in relation to ensuing Communion and the Postcommunion’s renewal as reaching bodily life and its future good. The healing in Luke remains a particular cure; the prayers after Communion promise no automatic medical recovery. <span class="nodecor">(Wilson, *Gelasian Sacramentary* III.12, p. 231; *Gregorian Sacramentary*, supplement XXXV, p. 174; *Liturgical Year*, pp. 370–371.)</span>

The Communion gives this continuing help a personal voice. God has taught the singer from youth; old age still needs his presence. Augustine reads both a person’s perseverance to the last breath and the Church’s endurance to the world’s end. Bellarmine hears David remembering divine power rather than trusting human strength. The remembered gift is a reason to ask again. The Introit’s all-day appeal, the Offertory’s repeated plea and the Postcommunion’s future hope therefore coexist with Paul’s confidence in abundant divine power: mercy sustains the life that praises it. <span class="nodecor">(Augustine, *Enarrationes* 70, abridged NPNF “Psalm LXXI,” §§18–21; Bellarmine, pp. 212–213; Eph. 3:20–21.)</span>

### Introit: mercy hears the poor

The chant places the plea of Psalm 85:3 first, follows it with the reason for confidence in verse 5, then sings the poverty of verse 1. The servant calls because God is merciful; his poverty describes the dependence of someone who expects to be heard. The whole psalm holds fidelity and need together. Its servant trusts God, praises him among the nations and still faces attackers seeking his life. The return to danger in verses 14–17 gives the opening plea urgency without canceling the confidence that carries it.

The abundant mercy of verse 5 belongs with the fuller divine-mercy formula in verse 15 and Exodus 34:6–9. In Exodus, God’s proclamation accompanies forgiveness, renewed accompaniment and covenant; judgement remains within that proclamation. The psalm’s mercy is God’s faithful action toward his servant, extending into the promised worship of the nations. The Introit’s Latin also has its own liturgical shape: *mihi* replaces the Clementine *mei*, the mildness clause is recast, and the psalm verse adds *mihi* after the divine ear. Challoner’s English remains the biblical wording beside these changes.

Augustine begins his exposition with Christ and the Church praying together. The cry of the whole day passes through the generations of Christ’s members. Poverty before God concerns humility: a rich person can be poor in this sense, whereas reliance on possessions can prevent recognition of need. Augustine also locates divine graciousness in God’s patience with the distracted petitioner; God’s willingness to hear does not promise the fulfillment of every desired object. <span class="nodecor">(*Enarrationes* 85, abridged NPNF “Psalm LXXXVI,” §§1–3,5,7.)</span>

Theodoret likewise sees humility compatible with abundance in righteousness. His attention to the Greek versions adds precision: Symmachus’s daily expression and Aquila and Theodotion’s placability describe repeated approach to a God willing to receive the petitioner. Bellarmine explicitly develops Augustine’s distinction between material poverty and freedom from trust in wealth. At the entrance to Mass, this prayer therefore asks the mercy needed for the Collect’s sustained good works and the Epistle’s strengthening. <span class="nodecor">(Theodoret, PG 80, 1553C–1556B; Bellarmine, pp. 259–260.)</span>

### Collect: grace before and after

The Collect asks that grace always precede and follow, with constant attention to good works as its intended fruit. Its verbs describe a dependence that surrounds action rather than suspending it. Cummiskey’s historical English uses “prevent” for the preceding gift and then asks zeal in practice. The older English must be read with that movement of the Latin, not as a request that grace hinder the person.

The prayer has more than one place in the Gregorian tradition. Wilson prints it among evening or morning prayers, as the fifth *Alia* on p. 135, and again in the Sunday supplement on p. 174. The apparatus records a word-order variant in the former use. In supplement XXXV, Sunday XVII after Pentecost, it accompanies *Munda nos* and *Purifica*. The supplement’s ordinal and the different post-Pentecost reckoning in Wilson’s manuscripts belong to those witnesses. <span class="nodecor">(Wilson, *Gregorian Sacramentary*, pp. 133–135,174; introduction pp. xviii,xli.)</span>

Paul’s prayer makes the Collect’s divine initiative concrete: the Father gives strength through the Spirit so that Christ may dwell through faith. Aquinas reads Paul’s knees as the bodily acknowledgment of smallness and weakness, and the perseverance requested as God’s gift. Augustine’s Communion-psalm exposition extends the same dependence through life: divine teaching initiates a way on which God remains necessary. These expositions illuminate the prayer’s preceding and following grace, while its own request remains the practical constancy of good works. <span class="nodecor">(Aquinas, *Super Ephesios* 3.4; Augustine, *Enarrationes* 70, abridged NPNF “Psalm LXXI,” §§18–21.)</span>

### Epistle: charity that cannot be contained

The reading opens with apostolic affliction. Ephesians 3:1–12 has explained Paul’s mission to the Gentiles; verse 13 asks its beneficiaries not to faint at the sufferings endured for them. The knees of verse 14 resume the prayer interrupted at the beginning of the chapter. Gentile co-inheritance, access through one Spirit to the Father and the common dwelling of chapter 2 give the prayer its communal horizon. The next chapter’s humility, patience and unity show what this strengthened life demands. <span class="nodecor">(Eph. 2:11–22; 3:1–21; 4:1–16.)</span>

The prayer is explicitly Trinitarian. The Father grants, the Spirit strengthens and Christ dwells in the hearts of believers through faith. Roots and foundations are two images of charity’s stability; comprehension happens with all the saints. The Latin *omnis patérnitas* and Challoner’s paternity retain one received wording. Aquinas distinguishes the order in which human beings learn the name of fatherhood from the priority of its reality in God. Chrysostom considers both derivative fatherhood and heavenly families or orders. The USCCB note on verses 14–15 explains the Father/family wordplay through alternatives concerning every family or God’s whole family. <span class="nodecor">(Aquinas, *Super Ephesios* 3.4; Chrysostom VII; USCCB, Ephesians 3 notes.)</span>

The four dimensions in verse 18 have no explicit object in that verse. Christ’s charity in verse 19 gives the immediate context for interpreting them; the USCCB note also records the universe as an alternative. Chrysostom explains the dimensions as immensity: the point is to recognize love’s transcendence, not to measure it. He also offers two accounts of fullness, through Trinitarian knowledge or the plenitude of virtue. The doxology then gives thanks for gifts exceeding what the recipients had requested or expected, while their faith and love remain necessary responses. <span class="nodecor">(Chrysostom, *Homilies on Ephesians* VII, on 3:13–21.)</span>

Augustine and Aquinas develop an explicit reading through the Cross. Augustine describes charity’s good works, perseverance, heavenly hope and hidden origin in grace; the unseen root of the Cross gives the fourth dimension its force. The gift’s beginning is unearned and cannot be mastered by an explanation of God’s hidden counsel. In the same letter, common life among the saints extends toward the one bread and body and toward love of enemies. <span class="nodecor">(Augustine, Letter 140 to Honoratus, 25.62–26.64, Latin digital edition.)</span>

Aquinas offers both the immeasurable perfections of God and the charity signified by the Cross. Comprehending God means attaining knowledge of his presence, not containing all that he is. Charity’s breadth, perseverance, heavenly aim and divine origin permit genuine understanding while maintaining the excess of the gift over the knower. Benedict XVI similarly reads the passage as loving contemplation of Christ’s inexhaustible mystery; his treatment concerns Colossians and Ephesians together. <span class="nodecor">(Aquinas, *Super Ephesios* 3.5; Benedict XVI, General Audience, 14 January 2009, mystery paragraph.)</span>

Colossians 1–2 strengthens these relations through apostolic affliction for the Church, fruitful conduct, roots and building, and fullness in Christ. John 14:15–26 joins love, obedience, divine indwelling and the Spirit’s teaching. In the Mass, the Epistle’s abundance belongs with the Collect’s fruitful grace and the Secret’s purification for participation. Paul’s opening affliction continues to qualify that abundance: divine power is already at work among those who still require courage.

### Gradual: the city built before the nations

Psalm 101 begins with an afflicted man’s frailty: bodily wasting, wakefulness, reproach and the shortness of life. Against this stands God’s enduring remembrance. Mercy for Sion and concern for its stones and dust lead into the appointed nations, kings, building and manifested glory. The next verses join the humble person’s prayer to prisoners, future generations and peoples assembled for worship. The closing contrast between creation’s change and God’s permanence sustains the children of his servants. <span class="nodecor">(Ps. 101:1–29.)</span>

The psalm’s restoration has a close counterpart in Isaias 60, where glory over Jerusalem draws nations and kings and accompanies rebuilding. Hebrews 1:10–12 applies Psalm 101:26–28 to the Son; those are the psalm’s concluding verses, not the Gradual’s selection. The chant itself changes the Clementine’s opening connective and renders the final glory with *in maiestáte sua*. Its liturgical words announce the divine builder’s majesty.

Augustine reads the nations’ gathering as the Church’s present construction, completed before Christ’s glorious judgement. Theodoret first explains the recovery of a city whose humiliation had seemed to imply divine weakness. Yet the response of neighboring peoples to that restoration did not fulfill universal conversion: some marveled and still attacked. He therefore directs the promise beyond the return toward the Incarnation and a fulfillment still unfolding. Bellarmine places Christ’s present Church-building beside the future appearance in glory after his first coming in humility. <span class="nodecor">(Augustine, *Enarrationes* 101, abridged NPNF “Psalm CII,” §§16–18; Theodoret, PG 80, 1679B–1682B; Bellarmine, p. 317, the Gentiles and built-Sion lemmas.)</span>

The Epistle has already asked for Christ’s dwelling in hearts and comprehension with all saints. The Gradual gives this communal divine work a city and a gathering: the *Liturgical Year* continuation joins the two in its account of inward strengthening, ecclesial unity and Sion’s praise. Schuster also identifies the chant’s use at the Third Sunday after Epiphany. <span class="nodecor">(*Liturgical Year*, pp. 359,363; Schuster III, pp. 143–144.)</span>

### Alleluia: the new life that sings

The chant sings the opening of Psalm 97:1, with the explicit final *Dóminus*; the saving right hand and holy arm belong to the verse’s continuation. The whole psalm makes Israel’s salvation visible before the nations, then calls singers, instruments, sea, rivers and mountains into praise before the Lord’s equitable judgement. The wonders summon a common response. Isaias 52:7–10 likewise joins salvation seen by the ends of the earth with the holy arm and the nations; Simeon’s canticle in Luke 2:28–32 gives salvation seen, Gentile revelation and Israel’s glory their Christological expression.

Augustine interprets new song as new life in Christ and the peace of the Church. He then distinguishes bodily recovery from inward healing for God while expounding the rest of verse 1. His immediate Gospel allusion is the raising at Nain in Luke 7, not this Sunday’s cure in Luke 14. The difference leaves his argument clear: wonderful bodily acts call for a life renewed in its relation to God. <span class="nodecor">(*Enarrationes* 97, abridged NPNF “Psalm XCVIII,” §1.)</span>

Theodoret connects the song’s newness with the Savior’s new manner of worship and life, and the wonders with acts beyond human expectation. Bellarmine draws those wonders through the Incarnation, healings, Resurrection, Ascension and mission. His treatment of the unappointed right hand and arm emphasizes victory through humility, obedience and the Cross. In the Mass, the acclamation precedes a Gospel where Christ heals and corrects ambition: both mercy and humility belong to the life that praises his works. <span class="nodecor">(Theodoret, PG 80, 1657C–1658D; Bellarmine, p. 306.)</span>

### Gospel: healing and the lower place

Jesus enters the house of an unnamed leading Pharisee for a Sabbath meal. Luke 13:22 places the surrounding narrative on the journey toward Jerusalem; the town is not given. The observers watch Jesus; he questions them about healing, receives silence, heals the man and dismisses him. His rescue question follows the cure. The man has no recorded speech or petition, and Luke gives no cause of his illness. The subsequent wedding scene is a parable addressed to the guests, not a description of the actual meal. <span class="nodecor">(Luke 13:22; 14:1–11.)</span>

The Missal and Clementine text use ass and ox. Cyril’s Payne Smith translation uses son and ox, so his appeal to parental affection rests upon a different textual witness. Cyril interprets Christ’s power as revealing the law’s merciful purpose and develops a spiritual Sabbath in which rest from sin accompanies holy action. Deuteronomy 22:4 commands aid to a fallen ass or ox; Exodus 23:4–5 includes an enemy’s animal and verse 12 extends Sabbath rest to servants and animals. Luke 13:10–17 contrasts animal watering with the freeing of a daughter of Abraham. These comparisons give the rescue question its concrete moral weight. <span class="nodecor">(Cyril, Sermon CI, pp. 471–475; USCCB, Luke 14:5 note.)</span>

The meal’s second scene concerns guests who choose the first places. Jesus allows the inviter to confer a higher place and honor before the company. Proverbs 25:6–7 gives a close wisdom parallel; Luke 18:9–14 repeats the final reversal in a parable directed against self-trust and contempt. Luke 22:24–30 places the disciples’ competition for greatness beside Christ’s table-service. These passages reveal a humility deeper than avoiding an embarrassing seating mistake.

Cyril grounds the low place in truthful self-knowledge. Wealth is transient, the body weak and mortal; the person who recognizes this can relinquish even honor he might claim without blame. Ambrose praises Christ’s gentle correction, in which persuasion reforms ambition. His preceding interpretation of dropsy as fleshly excess belongs to his spiritual exposition, not to a diagnosis of the man’s moral character. Ambrose then develops generosity to poor and disabled guests from verses 12–14, which lie beyond this Sunday’s Gospel. <span class="nodecor">(Cyril, Sermon CII, pp. 476–479; Ambrose, *Expositio in Lucam* VII.195, PL 15, 1752A–B, Latin web text.)</span>

Francis de Sales’s last-place echo exposes an inward contradiction: performing lowliness in order to secure greater honor keeps ambition alive. He also refuses a false humility that declines divine gifts or interrupts charity. Benedict XVI reads the overlapping postconciliar Gospel through humanity’s need for redemption and the humility of Christ’s Cross, interpreting exaltation as nearness to the Lord. The latter address concerns Luke 14:1,7–14, not the present formulary’s whole selection. <span class="nodecor">(Francis de Sales, III.5; Benedict XVI, Angelus, 29 August 2010.)</span>

The Gospel thus gives the Collect’s good works both mercy’s positive act and ambition’s correction. Received honor remains possible, as does the afflicted apostle’s glory in serving others. Neither the healed man’s seat nor the observers’ eventual inward conversion is narrated.

### Offertory: the threatened members call for help

The chant repeats its opening plea around a request that those seeking the singer’s life be confounded and ashamed. It selects from Psalm 39:14–15, omitting the first petition of verse 14, the word *simul* and the latter part of verse 15. The whole psalm has already remembered rescue, spoken of obedience and proclaimed justice in the assembly before returning to evils and iniquities surrounding the speaker. Prior mercy makes renewed need speak with hope. Psalm 69 repeats the closing petitions of Psalm 39; Hebrews 10:5–10 applies earlier verses 7–9 to Christ’s obedient offering, not to the appointed chant. Hebrews’ prepared body and the psalm’s pierced ears also differ within the same English Bible.

Augustine hears the suffering members appeal to their physician in the setting of Christ’s Passion. On the continuation about turning enemies backward, he develops conversion: those who tried to precede Christ can learn to follow him. That backward-turning clause is omitted by the chant. The chant still asks for the frustration and shame of a real destructive threat. <span class="nodecor">(*Enarrationes* 39, abridged NPNF “Psalm XL,” §§21–25.)</span>

Theodoret gives the Church one voice that includes strong and weak members, and emphasizes the thwarting of persecutors’ intended harm. Aquinas understands divine regard as help to escape evil and to perform good. His alternatives for the enemy’s shame include both penitence and punishment under God’s justice. These readings preserve the seriousness of harm while entrusting its defeat to God. Their distinct explanations also prevent the prayer from becoming either a general mood of peace or permission for personal vengeance. <span class="nodecor">(Theodoret, PG 80, 1159A–1160D; Aquinas, *Super Psalmo* 39 nn. 6–7.)</span>

The Offertory’s dependence belongs with the Introit and Communion, and its aid toward good with the Collect. Schuster calls its wording similar to the Offertory of Friday after the Second Sunday in Lent; his comparison is of similar text, not a demonstrated identity of every word. <span class="nodecor">(Schuster III, pp. 143–144.)</span>

### Secret: cleansed for participation

The Secret asks cleansing through the effect of the present sacrifice and merciful completion in the people so that they may participate. Sacrifice, mercy and fitness for participation belong together in the prayer. Cummiskey expresses its requested fruit as being made worthy through mercy. The communicant’s self-appraisal is not the source of that effect.

The Secret and Postcommunion already occupy their respective places together in Wilson’s Old Gelasian book III, section XII. Its two opening prayers differ from the current Collect. Wilson’s Gregorian supplement XXXV then gives the present triad, but under Sunday XVII. These historical associations distinguish the sacramental pair from the longer history of its present arrangement. <span class="nodecor">(Wilson, *Gelasian*, p. 231; *Gregorian*, p. 174.)</span>

The *Liturgical Year* continuation relates this Secret to the imminent consecration and ensuing Communion. Its interpretation develops the ritual position of a prayer whose Latin already attributes cleansing to the present sacrifice. The Epistle asks divine strength for Christ’s indwelling; the Secret seeks divinely effected fitness for participation. Both ask for transformation in the recipient, and the Postcommunion continues to ask renewal after reception. <span class="nodecor">(*Liturgical Year*, p. 370; Eph. 3:16–20.)</span>

### Communion: the justice remembered through life

The chant assembles three clauses: remembrance of God’s justice alone, instruction from youth and the plea not to be forsaken in old age. Psalm 70’s complete setting includes failing strength and enemies who claim that God has abandoned the petitioner. His answer is continued hope and praise. The chant stops before the purpose of proclaiming God’s arm to the coming generation; that continuation gives the whole psalm’s request a public fruit. Psalm 77:3–7 likewise joins remembered wonders, children and the transmission of hope.

Augustine interprets God’s justice alone as the renunciation of self-grounded righteousness. Youth means conversion received without prior deserving; old age means continued need for the grace that began the life. God remains indispensable after teaching the way. Augustine extends the ages both to the person persevering to the last breath and the Church enduring to the world’s end. <span class="nodecor">(*Enarrationes* 70, abridged NPNF “Psalm LXXI,” §§18–21.)</span>

Theodoret gives a different corporate subject to the ages: the people schooled through Moses and the oldness of the law, followed by testimony to the Church from the nations. His relation to Hebrews 8:13 belongs to that exposition. The PG edition brackets some additions; the Christian-grace comparison in its brackets cannot be treated as undifferentiated text. Bellarmine instead develops David’s personal remembrance of God’s help and testimony through the Psalms, with divine power taking precedence over human counsel and strength. <span class="nodecor">(Theodoret, PG 80, 1423B–1426C; Bellarmine, pp. 212–213.)</span>

The psalm narrates no Eucharistic meal. Its Communion placement makes remembrance and dependence the voice of those receiving the sacrament, and the *Liturgical Year* continuation reads its thanksgiving toward fruitfulness. Schuster identifies the same Communion verses on Thursday after the Fourth Sunday in Lent. The old-age prayer remains fitting after a divine gift: remembrance does not make the giver unnecessary. <span class="nodecor">(*Liturgical Year*, pp. 370–371; Schuster III, pp. 143–144.)</span>

### Postcommunion: renewal reaches embodied life

The Postcommunion asks God to purify minds kindly and renew them through heavenly mysteries, so that bodily help may follow, both now and hereafter. The Latin expressly names bodies. Cummiskey’s historical English speaks of purification of souls and help while in mortal bodies and hereafter; its wording carries that translation’s own articulation of the prayer.

The Gelasian pairing with the Secret and the Gregorian three-prayer group keep purification before and after participation within the same sacramental sequence. The *Liturgical Year* continuation develops the bodily good in this life and the next. The prayer’s interior renewal therefore includes embodied need, while its future horizon exceeds the particular cure described in Luke. <span class="nodecor">(Wilson, *Gelasian* III.12, p. 231; *Gregorian*, supplement XXXV, p. 174; *Liturgical Year*, pp. 370–371.)</span>

The Lord who helps the sick man and strengthens the saints is asked to continue his work in the worshippers. The Communion’s aging voice and the Postcommunion’s future help give received grace a continuing horizon. Neither the body’s mortality nor the need to pray again refutes the abundance Paul celebrates; their endurance is entrusted to the giver whose power works within the Church. <span class="nodecor">(Luke 14:4; Eph. 3:16–21; Ps. 70:16–18, selected.)</span>

## Source-Grounded Synthesis Across the Propers

### Fruitfulness remains dependent on its giver

The Collect asks grace to sustain good works, the Epistle seeks strength for indwelling charity, and the Secret asks purification for participation. Augustine’s exposition of the Communion psalm rejects a life that receives God’s teaching and then proceeds without him: the giver remains necessary along the way. Aquinas explains why the Epistle’s inward strength belongs with that continuing need. Paul’s bent knees acknowledge weakness, and the perseverance he requests requires God’s gift. Strength enables the faithful to act without making them independent of its source. The Collect’s fruitful works and the Secret’s worthy participation therefore remain reasons for gratitude and further prayer; success in charity never makes mercy dispensable. <span class="nodecor">(Collect; Eph. 3:14–20; Secret; Augustine, *Enarrationes* 70, abridged NPNF “Psalm LXXI,” §§18–21; Aquinas, *Super Ephesios* 3.4.)</span>

### Humility permits the reception of good

The Gospel’s inviter can confer honor, while its guests must relinquish their self-assigned precedence. Cyril grounds this relinquishment in truthful self-knowledge: wealth passes and bodily weakness remains, so even honor one could claim without blame need not be demanded. Francis de Sales carries the correction into desire itself. Taking the last place to secure promotion keeps ambition intact; refusing God’s gifts when charity needs them is another failure of humility. Together their reasoning distinguishes freedom from a demand for precedence from a refusal of received good. Paul’s glory can include affliction for others and end in praise of God; the Communion can remember divine justice without turning its benefits into a claim to superiority. Humility receives and uses the gift while leaving its honor to the giver. <span class="nodecor">(Luke 14:7–11; Eph. 3:13,21; Communion; Cyril, *Commentary on Luke*, Sermon CII, Payne Smith 1859, pp. 476–479; Francis de Sales, III.5.)</span>

### Interior growth bears a common form

Christ’s dwelling in hearts is joined in Ephesians to all the saints and the Church. The Gradual brings nations and kings before Sion’s divine builder, and the Alleluia turns wonders into public praise. The *Liturgical Year* continuation’s indwelling–Sion–praise relation thus develops a communal movement already present in the texts. Augustine’s present Church-building and future judgement and Theodoret’s restoration exceeding its first observers give that movement distinct theological articulations. Charity’s growth is shared without making the heart, city and Church interchangeable objects. <span class="nodecor">(*Liturgical Year*, pp. 359,363; Augustine, *Enarrationes* 101, abridged NPNF “Psalm CII,” §§16–18; Theodoret, PG 80, 1679B–1682B.)</span>

### Purification concerns embodied persons

Inward strengthening, a bodily cure, cleansing through sacrifice and renewal with bodily help are different works of divine aid within one formulary. The sacramental prayers’ ancient pairing and the continuation’s interpretation of bodily renewal give this relation historical depth. The person who receives interior grace remains a person with a body, and the future good sought for that body exceeds its immediate relief. The prayer for bodily help after Communion carries no guarantee of immediate medical cure. <span class="nodecor">(Eph. 3:16; Luke 14:4; Secret; Postcommunion; Wilson, *Gelasian* III.12, p. 231; *Liturgical Year*, pp. 370–371.)</span>

### Abundance sustains continuing need

Paul’s doxology is preceded by affliction; the Communion’s remembrance ends in a plea; the Offertory repeats its call for rescue. Received help and continuing vulnerability coexist. Augustine’s prayer of the members and Bellarmine’s lifelong testimony make the recurrence intelligible: the history of mercy gives reasons to turn again toward God. The Church can sing wonders, ask that a threat be defeated and pray for help hereafter within the same act of worship. <span class="nodecor">(Eph. 3:13,20–21; Augustine, *Enarrationes* 39, abridged NPNF “Psalm XL,” §§21–25; Bellarmine, pp. 212–213; Postcommunion.)</span>

## The Propers: Notable and Quotable

### An eternity spent waiting for a lover

The Epistle ends its doxology with “world without end” (Eph. 3:21, Challoner). Shakespeare’s Sonnet 57 turns the phrase into “Nor dare I chide the world-without-end hour” (line 5, Folger edition). The speaker presents himself as the beloved’s slave, unable to complain of absence while jealousy makes a single hour oppressive. Doxological eternity becomes the subjective duration of waiting. The hyphenated expression is a biblical and liturgical idiom echoed; dependence specifically on this Pauline verse or this Mass is not documented. <span class="nodecor">(Shakespeare, *Sonnets* 57, lines 1–6,9–14; Folger edition, Mowat and Werstine, with Poston and Niles, FDT 0.9.0.1.)</span>

### Humility accused of ambition

The Gospel promises that “he that humbleth himself shall be exalted” (Luke 14:11, Challoner). Nietzsche rewrites the saying: “He that humbleth himself wisheth to be exalted.” His heading is “Luke 18:14 Improved.” The explicit target is the parallel saying in Luke 18, not the appointed pericope. Alexander Harvey’s English makes the change from promised vindication to suspected desire unmistakable: humility itself is accused of concealing ambition. This is a hostile psychological reversal of wording heard in the Gospel. <span class="nodecor">(Nietzsche, *Human, All Too Human*, trans. Harvey, 1908, “History of the Moral Feelings,” aphorism 87, p. 109; Gutenberg eBook 38145.)</span>

### Paul’s dimensions in a human love poem

Paul asks comprehension of “the breadth and length and height and depth,” then names Christ’s charity (Eph. 3:18–19, Challoner). Elizabeth Barrett Browning writes: “I love thee to the depth and breadth and height / My soul can reach” (*Sonnets from the Portuguese* XLIII, lines 2–3). She reorders the dimensions and omits length. The verbal and contextual echo transfers immeasurable love toward a human beloved, while the sonnet retains soul, grace, faith, saints and God. The poem does not simply remove religion from the language, and no authorial acknowledgment of dependence on Ephesians was located. <span class="nodecor">(Caradoc Press printing, 1906; David Price transcription, Gutenberg eBook 2002.)</span>

### A dean who wants the higher place

The Gospel’s “Friend, go up higher” and final humiliation–exaltation saying become social satire in Percy Fitzgerald’s *Fatal Zero*. A fictional narrator remembers the dean preaching on “Go up higher,” then watches him seek a place beside a lord and interprets his humility as a hope for promotion. The scriptural language becomes an accusation against status-seeking, but the narrator also admits that his judgement may be unfair. These are a character’s judgements, not events established in a real dean’s biography. <span class="nodecor">(*All the Year Round*, new series I, 16 January 1869, p. 163, left column, paragraph beginning “Of course he had not heard of my fall in the world.”)</span>

The journal’s conductor was Charles Dickens; the story was Fitzgerald’s. Dickens himself identifies its author in his letter to Mrs James T. Fields of 16 December 1868. The attribution matters to the joke’s literary setting, rather than making the journal’s running head its author’s name. <span class="nodecor">(Dickens, *Letters*, ed. Mamie Dickens and Georgina Hogarth, III, 1882, that letter; Gutenberg eBook 25854.)</span>

### An ox the Senate should leave in the ditch

Jesus asks who would not rescue “an ox fall into a pit” even on the Sabbath (Luke 14:5, Challoner). During a natural-gas pricing debate, Senator Riegle declared: “Sometimes you have to leave the ox in the ditch, if that is where it belongs.” He opposed treating a substantive dispute and the risk of higher consumer costs as merely an obstruction to Senate business. Rescue ceased to be the automatic good: the legislative predicament might properly remain unresolved. <span class="nodecor">(*Congressional Record—Senate*, 4 October 1977, p. 32287, right column.)</span>

“Ditch” belongs to the later idiom; Challoner prints “pit.” Doug Batchelor explicitly links the idiom to Luke 14:5 in *How to Keep the Sabbath Holy*, p. 78 (2014). That modern witness establishes a consciously biblical use of the expression, not that Riegle had read Luke or intended an argument about Christ’s cure. The political reversal concerns legislative action.

## The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities

*These six connections are exploratory editorial and AI-assisted proposals, not sourced historical intent or teaching attributed to the witnesses. Their precedent results refer to the bounded comparison recorded in the research audit.*

### Distorted fullness and receptive plenitude

**Anchors.** Gospel, Luke 14:2–4: the dropsical man and his cure; Epistle, Eph. 3:19–20: divine fullness and abundant power; Secret: cleansing for participation.

**Mechanism.** Bodily swelling and being filled through a gift offer sharply different images of enlargement. Placed beside the Secret’s cleansing, their contrast can become an examination of desire: accumulation may enlarge a claim to importance, while charity enlarges a capacity to receive and give. Ambrose’s spiritual account of fleshly excess in Luke 14 supplies one received point of comparison; the three-element relation is the proposal. <span class="nodecor">(Ambrose, *Expositio in Lucam* VII.195.)</span>

**Fruit.** A worshipper may ask what kind of growth a desired good enables. Receiving charity gives a different account of plenitude from collecting reasons to occupy the first place.

**What the element-by-element reading misses.** The two images of fullness ordinarily remain in different passages; cleansing for participation makes their difference spiritually productive without turning them into synonyms.

**Precedent result.** Near analogue located: the Eleventh-Sunday study’s “From unvoid grace to heavenly fullness,” and Anthony’s *Dominica XVII post Pentecosten*, §§8–12, Basilica del Santo Latin web edition. Anthony pairs this Gospel with Ephesians 4, not the present Epistle.

**Strongest limit.** Bodily disease is no proof of personal sin. Divine plenitude is not a physiological quantity, and the analogy fails if it makes Luke’s patient morally guilty or his cure an increase in the quantity of divinity.

### Grace across daily, lifelong and final horizons

**Anchors.** Collect: grace preceding, following and always present; Communion, Ps. 70:17–18: youth and old age; Postcommunion: present and future help; Introit, Ps. 85:3: the all-day cry.

**Mechanism.** These spans can be heard together as simultaneous horizons of dependence. A day’s petition belongs within a remembered life; grace needed for an action belongs with grace still needed afterward; bodily help now stands beside help hereafter. The spans illuminate each other without becoming stages on a single calendar.

**Fruit.** Perseverance can include both today’s task and the tasks a changed life will bring. Memory becomes a resource for asking help in circumstances not yet encountered.

**What the element-by-element reading misses.** Separate exposition leaves daily prayer, aging and final hope in their own units. Their coexistence allows the person to understand one received life across different scales of need.

**Precedent result.** Near analogue located: the Fifteenth-Sunday study’s “The proper’s several clocks” and the Twelfth-Sunday study’s “Mercy’s longer clock.” Neither comparison coordinates these three principal prayers.

**Strongest limit.** No timetable of grace, guarantee of longevity or uninterrupted health follows. The words supply no chronological assignment or ritual-age numerology.

### Interior hospitality and room for another

**Anchors.** Epistle: Christ dwelling in hearts, rooted and founded in charity; Gradual: God building Sion; Gospel: yielding a place and reclining at the contested table.

**Mechanism.** The established relation between inward dwelling and common building can be extended into the use of shared space. Receiving a guest inwardly and being built into a people may change the will to control a seat outwardly. Hospitality then concerns a disposition as well as a room: a place ceases to be only a possession that fixes one’s rank.

**Fruit.** The invitation to receive Christ can test the habits by which others are granted room, attention and honor. A heart made hospitable has reason to loosen its grasp upon precedence.

**What the element-by-element reading misses.** Separate exposition does not develop the movement from the Pauline heart and the psalmic city into the Gospel’s social allocation of places.

**Precedent result.** Near analogue located for the fuller conjunction. The shorter indwelling–Sion–praise relation has precedent in the *Liturgical Year* continuation, pp. 359,363; the Fourteenth-Sunday study’s camp-and-courts proposal supplies a further dwelling analogue.

**Strongest limit.** Heart, Sion and dining room have different referents. The parable does not explicitly identify the inviter as Christ. The healed man is dismissed and is never narrated taking a seat; no architectural code or compiler’s programme follows.

### Lowliness compatible with enlargement

**Anchors.** Introit: the inclined divine ear and the needy petitioner; Epistle: bent knees, height and depth; Gospel: reclining in the low place and ascending at the inviter’s word.

**Mechanism.** Divine inclination, bodily kneeling, charity’s dimensions and honor received from another put lowliness beside enlargement. A lowered posture and a higher place can each express reception, while an insistence on taking height for oneself can obstruct it.

**Fruit.** Humility can be understood without contempt for one’s worth or resistance to God’s gifts. Kneeling and growth in charity need not compete, and accepting an honor can remain obedient to the giver rather than self-promoting.

**What the element-by-element reading misses.** The different spatial expressions cross chant, prayer and parable. Together they disclose how lowliness and enlargement can belong to the same disposition without becoming one literal movement.

**Precedent result.** Near analogue located: the Tenth-Sunday study’s “Downcast eyes can accompany a lifted soul” and the Fifteenth-Sunday study’s inclined ear and raised appeal.

**Strongest limit.** Posture alone proves no virtue. God’s ear is figurative, whereas kneeling is bodily prayer. Paul’s dimensions are not a map of ritual gestures, and the sequence licenses no numerological scheme.

### The petitioner also receives correction

**Anchors.** Offertory: shame requested against those seeking the singer’s life; Gospel: the shame of a guest told to yield a place he assumed for himself.

**Mechanism.** The prayer’s requested judgement about another can stand beside the guest’s possible experience of correction. A person who rightly seeks deliverance can also relinquish pride. The change of direction concerns willingness to be corrected, not the substitution of self-accusation for protection.

**Fruit.** Prayer against harm can remain truthful without becoming a certificate of personal superiority. The worshipper can ask that a threat fail while also accepting correction of an unrelated fault.

**What the element-by-element reading misses.** Independent accounts leave the endangered singer and the ambitious guest apart. Their juxtaposition asks whether the praying self is prepared to receive the kind of moral correction he readily desires for another.

**Precedent result.** Near analogue located: the Tenth-Sunday study’s “Divine leveling without competitive self-ranking” compares protection from adversaries with correction of self-exaltation.

**Strongest limit.** The threatened singer is not blamed for the threat; enemies are not identified with fellow guests or current opponents. A victim is never required to accept injury as deserved humiliation. Augustine’s conversion, Theodoret’s thwarted persecution and Aquinas’s penitence-or-punishment readings retain their distinct meanings.

### A new song in an aging voice

**Anchors.** Alleluia, Ps. 97:1: the new canticle; Communion, Ps. 70:17–18: instruction from youth and prayer into old age; Postcommunion: renewal through heavenly mysteries.

**Mechanism.** New praise and sacramental renewal can coexist with a long received history. Augustine’s separate readings of new life and persevering dependence support the two psalmic anchors; their conjunction allows memory to become a condition for fresh thanksgiving rather than an obstacle to it. <span class="nodecor">(*Enarrationes* 97, abridged NPNF “Psalm XCVIII,” §1; 70, “Psalm LXXI,” §§18–21.)</span>

**Fruit.** Renewal need not imitate chronological youth. An aging Christian can discover new reasons for praise through the very history of help that makes the present plea possible.

**What the element-by-element reading misses.** Separate exposition leaves new song, old-age prayer and sacramental renewal as parallel themes; the conjunction considers their coexistence in one present worshipper.

**Precedent result.** Not located in the checked corpus: the 328-file English and Latin proper-study snapshot, Anthony’s *Dominica XVI* and *XVII post Pentecosten*, Basilica del Santo Latin web edition, and the *Liturgical Year* continuation’s complete Sixteenth-Sunday chapter. The nearest age and song passages did not develop this combined interpretation; unsearched psalm homilies, poetry, music and sermon collections remain outside that result.

**Strongest limit.** The chants do not identify their historical speakers as one person. Spiritual renewal promises no reversal of biological aging, and the bounded precedent result supports no claim of universal novelty or priority.
