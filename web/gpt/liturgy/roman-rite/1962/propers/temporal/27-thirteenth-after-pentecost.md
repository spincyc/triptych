## The Propers: Themes and Movement {#triptych:brief-synthesis:start}

> **Governing thesis: God keeps covenant by giving in Christ, and the fitting human answer is obedient faith returned as thanksgiving and sustained as growth**
>
> The Mass begins with a people whose plea rests on God’s covenant. It asks for the increase of the theological virtues, hears that inheritance is promise and gift in Christ, watches mercy meet sufferers who obey before they see, and receives bread from heaven while asking to advance. Grace calls forth love, movement, thanksgiving, offering, and perseverance.

1.  **Promise precedes achievement:** covenant and inheritance are God’s acts before they become the worshipper’s hope.

2.  **Faith moves under a word:** the ten go before they see; cleansing is given to all, but Luke follows one into thanksgiving and saving faith.

3.  **Gift returns as thanksgiving:** the Samaritan returns, the Secret places people and gifts under mercy, and Wisdom’s manna becomes bread given “to us.”

4.  **Reception opens further growth:** the virtues’ increase at the beginning becomes progress toward eternal redemption at the end.

*How to read the movement.* These are converging themes, not a claim that a compiler designed a four-stage argument. A witness may explain one text, an older pairing, or a later reception; the detailed commentary marks the difference.

### Covenant, promise, and time

Psalm 73 speaks from catastrophe: the poor can ask God to remember his flock and look upon his covenant even when no human achievement carries their case. Cassiodorus makes that rhetoric explicit. Augustine and the medieval Gloss hear Jeremiah’s new covenant; Bellarmine hears the covenant with the fathers. The chant does not force their readings into one. Galatians then gives a defined covenantal field: the promise to Abraham and his seed, Christ, is not annulled by the later law. The law is good and provisional—exposing and restraining transgression—but cannot itself impart life. It serves promise; it does not compete with another god.

The Collect therefore asks for real growth without turning promise into an independent wage. Trent, Session VI, chapter 10, excerpts its petition for the increase of faith, hope, and charity; chapter 16 and canon 32 place Christian merit wholly within Christ’s preceding grace. The Alleluia widens the horizon to God’s refuge “from generation and progeny,” and the Offertory entrusts *témpora mea*—the worshipper’s changing times—to God’s hands. This does not excuse passivity; it locates action, suffering, and hope within a faithfulness larger than one lifetime.

### Obedience, cleansing, and thankful faith

Luke’s sequence must remain uneven. All ten call, obey Jesus’ command to show themselves to the priests, and are cleansed while going. One Samaritan sees, returns, glorifies God, falls at Jesus’ feet, and gives thanks; he alone hears the saving pronouncement. Tertullian emphasizes obedient movement and thanks offered to Christ; Augustine refuses self-credit; Bede distinguishes common cleansing from the faith whose living response receives salvation. The priests certify and restore social and cultic standing; they do not cause the healing.

The nine are not identified as Jews, and Luke does not state their fate. Ancient one-Gentile/nine-Jews typologies belong to reception history, not to the narrative’s asserted facts. *Nostra aetate* 4 excludes any account of the Jewish people as rejected or accursed. Likewise, biblical “leprosy” is not a warrant to diagnose or stigmatize people with Hansen’s disease, chronic illness, disability, visible difference, or contested belief. Moral readings must turn the examiner toward the self, never make another’s body a symbol on demand.

### Bread received, response offered, growth continued

The Communion adapts Wisdom’s third-person manna narrative into direct prayer: *Panem de caelo dedísti nobis, Dómine*. Christian reception had several registers. Rabanus and Bonaventure develop Eucharistic readings; John Paul II cites Wisdom 16:20–21 for the Eucharist as pilgrim food and messianic foretaste. Gregory the Great, directly quoting the same sweetness clause, instead sees divine speech accommodating itself to hearers. His non-Eucharistic reading disciplines the others: spiritual sweetness need not mean a sensible feeling, and later sacramental typology does not erase the verse’s original wilderness setting.

The Secret places people and gifts beneath God’s merciful *propitiáre*; thanksgiving and offering answer a gift they cannot buy. The Postcommunion then repeats the Collect’s *augméntum*: those who have received the heavenly sacraments still ask that *we* may advance toward eternal redemption. Sacramental grace is not defective, but its recipient remains a pilgrim. The proper ends neither in self-salvation nor in static possession, but in grace-enabled progress.

| **Movement** | **Positive form** | **Controlling limit** |
|:-------------|:------------------------|:----------------------------|
| **Lack to promise** | The poor plead covenant; inheritance remains gift in Christ. | The law is not evil, Israel is not erased, and merit remains grace-enabled. |
| **Word to movement** | Faith obeys before sight and discovers mercy on the way. | Obedience neither purchases healing nor blames the sick. |
| **Gift to thanks** | Cleansing returns as praise, offering, and Eucharistic reception. | Gratitude is response, not price; typology cannot become supersessionism. |
| **Reception to growth** | Sacrament and virtues carry the pilgrim toward redemption. | Growth is neither self-salvation nor sacramental deficiency. |

## The Propers: Detailed Commentary

## The Appointed Propers in Full

The Latin below is the complete recurring formulary between marginal nos. 1562 and 1571 in the 1962 Vatican typical Missal, printed pp. 393–394. It was visually checked against the identified CMAA facsimile; the surviving pre-1962 text is reproduced as received, without modernization. Scriptural English is the public-domain Douay–Rheims/Challoner; the Collect, Secret, and Postcommunion are the public-domain English of the Cummiskey hand missal (Philadelphia, 1861). Neither historical English witness is official 1962 liturgical text. Where a chant differs from the Clementine text translated by the Douay, the gap is disclosed rather than filled by an editorial translation.

### Introit (*Int.*)

> ***Psalmus 73:20, 19 et 23; 73:1*; no. 1562**
>
> *Réspice, Dómine, in testaméntum tuum, et ánimas páuperum tuórum ne derelínquas in finem: exsúrge, Dómine, et iúdica causam tuam, et ne obliviscáris voces quæréntium te. <span class="nodecor">Ps. ibid., 1</span> Ut quid, Deus, repulísti in finem: irátus est furor tuus super oves páscuæ tuæ? ℣. Glória Patri.*

> **Douay–Rheims/Challoner, Ps. 73:20, 19, 22–23 and 1**
>
> Have regard to thy covenant: for they that are the obscure of the earth have been filled with dwellings of iniquity. Deliver not up to beasts the souls that confess to thee: and forget not to the end the souls of thy poor. Arise, O God, judge thy own cause: remember thy reproaches with which the foolish man hath reproached thee all the day. Forget not the voices of thy enemies: the pride of them that hate thee ascendeth continually. O God, why hast thou cast us off unto the end: why is thy wrath enkindled against the sheep of thy pasture? ℣. *Glória Patri.*

**Witness boundary.** The chant has *Dómine* where the source verses do not, *derelínquas* where the Clementine has *obliviscáris*, and *voces quæréntium te* where it has the voices of enemies. The printed reference also omits v. 22 although the antiphon uses it. The complete Douay verses expose those differences; no composite English antiphon is supplied.

### Collect (*Coll.*)

> ***Oratio*; no. 1563**
>
> *Omnípotens sempitérne Deus, da nobis fidei, spei et caritátis augméntum: et, ut mereámur ássequi quod promíttis, fac nos amáre quod prǽcipis. Per Dóminum.*

> **Cummiskey hand missal (1861), Collect, p. 423**
>
> O Almighty and eternal God, grant us an increase of faith, hope, and charity; and, that we may deserve what thou promisest, make us love what thou commandest. Thro’.

**Witness boundary.** The English does not separately render *ássequi*, “to attain.” The commentary therefore reasons from the Latin when it distinguishes loving the command from attaining the promise.

### Epistle (*Ep.*)

> ***Ad Galatas 3:16–22*; no. 1564**
>
> *Léctio Epístolæ beáti Pauli Apóstoli ad Gálatas.*
>
> Fratres: Abrahæ dictæ sunt promissiónes, et sémini eius. Non dicit: Et semínibus, quasi in multis; sed quasi in uno: Et sémini tuo, qui est Christus. Hoc autem dico: testaméntum confirmátum a Deo, quæ post quadringéntos et trigínta annos facta est lex, non írritum facit ad evacuándam promissiónem. Nam si ex lege heréditas, iam non ex promissióne. Abrahæ autem per repromissiónem donávit Deus. Quid ígitur lex? Propter transgressiónes pósita est, donec veníret semen, cui promíserat, ordináta per Angelos in manu mediatóris. Mediátor autem uníus non est: Deus autem unus est. Lex ergo advérsus promíssa Dei? Absit. Si enim data esset lex, quæ posset vivificáre, vere ex lege esset iustítia. Sed conclúsit Scriptúra ómnia sub peccáto, ut promíssio ex fide Iesu Christi darétur credéntibus.

> **Douay–Rheims/Challoner, Gal. 3:16–22**
>
> To Abraham were the promises made and to his seed. He saith not: And to his seeds as of many. But as of one: And to thy seed, which is Christ. Now this I say: that the testament which was confirmed by God, the law which was made after four hundred and thirty years doth not disannul, to make the promise of no effect. For if the inheritance be of the law, it is no more of promise. But God gave it to Abraham by promise. Why then was the law? It was set because of transgressions, until the seed should come to whom he made the promise, being ordained by angels in the hand of a mediator. Now a mediator is not of one: but God is one. Was the law then against the promises of God: God forbid! For if there had been a law given which could give life, verily justice should have been by the law. But the scripture hath concluded all under sin, that the promise, by the faith of Jesus Christ, might be given to them that believe.

**Witness boundary.** The address *Fratres* is a liturgical incipit borrowed from v. 15 and is not part of the translated verses. The lesson stops at v. 22; Paul’s pedagogue image begins later and is context, not appointed text.

### Gradual (*Grad.*)

> ***Psalmus 73:20, 19 et 22*; no. 1565**

> *Réspice, Dómine, in testaméntum tuum: et ánimas páuperum tuórum ne obliviscáris in finem. ℣. Exsúrge, Dómine, et iúdica causam tuam: memor esto oppróbrii servórum tuórum.*

**Witness boundary.** The closing *memor esto oppróbrii servórum tuórum* is not the Clementine’s Ps. 73:22b; it corresponds verbally, apart from the vocative, to Ps. 88:51. The Missal nevertheless cites Ps. 73. The Douay is not altered to hide that boundary.

### Offertory (*Off.*)

> ***Psalmus 30:15–16*; no. 1568**
>
> *In te sperávi, Dómine; dixi: Tu es Deus meus, in mánibus tuis témpora mea.*

### Introit: the covenant is stronger than the petitioners’ case (*Int.*)

The antiphon is a liturgical construction from Psalm 73 rather than a continuous biblical verse. It begins with v. 20, returns to the poor of v. 19, inserts v. 22 although the Missal’s margin does not name it, and closes by changing v. 23’s “enemies” into those “seeking thee.” It also sings *derelínquas* where the Clementine has *obliviscáris*. The appended psalm verse, by contrast, is the lament’s first question exactly: why has God cast off the sheep of his pasture? The chant therefore places a carefully intensified plea over a biblical catastrophe.

The historical horizon is contested in Christian reception. Augustine sees the Roman standards of AD 70 and treats the ruin as accomplished history; Cassiodorus and Theodoret read the same Roman destruction as prophecy. Jerome briefly points instead to Babylon. Bellarmine argues for the persecution of Antiochus IV and even uses the Church’s liturgical singing as an argument against assigning the psalm to an unbelieving voice. Modern Catholic orientation associates the lament especially with the destruction of the Jerusalem temple in 587 BC. These judgments cannot all describe the first historical referent. Their disagreement is more instructive than a false consensus: the chant receives Israel’s lament as the Church’s prayer without settling the psalm’s compositional crisis.

The covenant clause likewise admits distinct readings. Augustine asks God to fulfil the new covenant of Jeremiah 31 and the promised kingdom; Cassiodorus follows the same line and gives its spiritual logic extraordinary precision: when the people’s merits cannot be recalled, the Lord’s promise can. The *Glossa ordinaria* makes that patristic reading the medieval schoolroom gloss. Bellarmine, however, hears the older covenant with the fathers and the land given to them. Guéranger, citing Rupert of Deutz, and Schuster connect the appeal more generally with the Abrahamic promise. The Sunday does not require the old and new covenants to be rivals. Galatians will insist that the later law does not annul the earlier promise, while Christian reception sees that promise fulfilled in Christ.

At v. 22, textual history changes the spiritual accent. Augustine’s lemma asks God to judge “my cause,” and he answers by the risen Christ who will judge. Cassiodorus has the chant’s “thy cause” and says God judges his own cause by converting those in error so that they confess what they denied. Theodoret expressly defends “thy” rather than “my”; Bellarmine notes Augustine’s variant and rejects it. Thus *causam tuam* need not become a warrant for religious combat. Its strongest received reading is missionary and penitential: God’s truth is vindicated when error yields to conversion.

The poor and their voices keep the plea ecclesial. Augustine interprets poverty as abandonment of self-made justice for grace; Cassiodorus names the faithful remnant and the riches God gives to those who become poor in spirit. Neither reading permits contempt for materially poor persons. Nor may the psalm’s enemy language be assigned to Jews, political opponents, sick persons, or any contemporary group. The worshipper enters the desolated community’s question and asks God to remember, hear, judge, and restore.

### Collect: grace increases what grace has begun (*Coll.*)

The prayer has two linked petitions. First, God is asked to give an increase of faith, hope, and charity. Second, in order that the petitioners may deserve to attain what God promises, he is asked to make them love what he commands. The syntax refuses to separate interior virtue from concrete obedience, or promise from a transformed will. Even love of the command is requested as divine action: *fac nos amáre*.

Its transmission is older than its current Sunday. The Verona sacramentary has the Collect in a July votive series without Sunday assignment. The Old Gelasian prints it among unnumbered Sunday Masses and again in prayers for charity. The Gregorian supplement places the prayer set on a differently numbered Sunday. Those witnesses establish movement through Roman and Frankish books, not authorship by Leo, Gelasius, Gregory, or Alcuin. The Sunday number drift is evidence against imagining that every surviving pairing arose at once.

Trent gives the Collect unusual doctrinal reception. Session VI, chapter 10, quotes its request for growth in faith, hope, and charity while teaching the increase of justification already received. This is not a claim that natural effort earns first grace. Chapter 16 grounds the merit of the justified in Christ’s grace, and canon 32 protects both that divine source and the genuine meritorious character of works performed in grace. The prayer is thus fully Catholic in both directions: the virtues really grow, love and obedience really matter, and every good movement remains gift.

Aquinas supplies a compatible account without directly quoting this Collect at the checked loci. Charity on pilgrimage can increase as a person draws nearer to God; acts of charity dispose toward further increase, and eternal life is the end toward which grace moves. Guéranger receives the Collect as the Church’s petition for the three foundational virtues and devotedness to the commandments. The repeated *augméntum* in the Postcommunion will show that this prayer has no isolated endpoint before glory.

### Epistle: one seed, a prior promise, and a law that cannot give life (*Ep.*)

Paul’s argument begins with Abraham’s “seed” in the singular and identifies that seed as Christ. Jerome makes an exhaustive philological claim that Scripture never uses a plural “seeds” in this promise; Theodoret distinguishes Christ as seed in the proper sense from Abraham’s descendants by nature. Augustine widens the singular christologically to Christ with the Christians who imitate Abraham’s faith: the unity is the unity of faith, not the exclusion of Christ’s members. Aquinas holds the center: Christ alone is the one in whom all nations can be blessed.

*Testaméntum* carries the argument between covenant and testamentary inheritance. Jerome observes that Hebrew *berith* means pact or covenant, while Aquinas explains why Paul can reason from a human will: the divine promise orders an inheritance and, once confirmed, cannot be displaced by a later disposition. The 430 years make posteriority visible. Jerome leaves the chronology unresolved; Aquinas counts from the promise in Genesis 12 to the Exodus and distinguishes the shorter count from Isaac. The theological point does not depend on adjudicating every ancient chronology: later law cannot turn prior gift into no gift.

Paul then asks the difficult question himself: why the law? Chrysostom calls it a bridle and a probe of wounds that makes the sick seek a physician. Augustine says it was given to a proud people so that transgression would humble them into seeking grace. Theodoret describes its care for the people from whom the seed would come. Aquinas gathers four ends: restraint, discipline, exposure of weakness, and figurative instruction toward future grace. The law is therefore neither a rival god’s work nor a useless mistake. Paul says explicitly that it is not against God’s promises. It lacks the power to give life, but it serves the promise by exposing the need that only gift can answer.

The mediator generated a preserved disagreement. Jerome and Chrysostom name Christ, the giver of the law; Augustine insists that Christ mediates according to his humanity; Ambrosiaster presents Christ as arbiter reconciling two peoples. Marius Victorinus likewise names Christ, though his comments on vv. 11–19 are lost. Theodoret names Moses. Aquinas bridges the traditions: the law is in Christ’s power, and Moses signifies the Mediator. This is a case where a patristic matrix is more Catholic than a flattened answer, because it holds the historical ministry of Moses and the saving mediation of Christ in their proper relation.

The lesson ends at v. 22, before the famous pedagogue image. Scripture has “concluded all under sin” so that promise may be given to believers. Jerome compares the closure to judicial custody awaiting pardon; Chrysostom and Theodoret interpret it as conviction; Aquinas calls knowledge of disease and impotence an inducement to seek Christ the physician. Universal conviction is ordered to universal need for grace, not to contempt for Israel. The appointed boundary leaves the hearer inside that need and directs attention to the covenant plea sung again in the Gradual.

### Gradual: God’s own cause remembered between promise and mercy (*Grad.*)

The Gradual repeats the Introit’s covenant plea but reverts from one of its psalter differences: here the poor are not to be *forgotten*, the Clementine’s verb, rather than not to be forsaken. Its final clause, *memor esto oppróbrii servórum tuórum*, is not the Vulgate’s Psalm 73:22b; it corresponds to Psalm 88:51 even though the Missal continues to cite Psalm 73. Fidelity to the liturgical source requires retaining both the received wording and the printed reference without silently rewriting one to match the other.

Guéranger explicitly joins this repeated *testaméntum* to the Epistle, reporting Rupert’s interpretation of the ancient people’s cry for fulfilment of the promise made to Abraham. The pairing is therefore documented liturgical reception, not merely a modern thematic guess. Yet Rupert’s precise older Sunday arrangement was not recovered here, and his witness cannot establish why the 1962 compiler retained the pair.

The repeated *causam tuam* also deserves discipline. Leo X used the same psalmic cry to open *Exsurge Domine*; the phrase consequently entered one of the defining conflicts of Western Christianity. In the Mass it remains a prayer addressed to God’s judgment, not a transfer of judgment to the assembly. Cassiodorus’s conversion-centered gloss is the safer spiritual measure: divine truth prevails by bringing persons to know and confess it.

### Alleluia: refuge before the journey (*All.*)

The Alleluia’s Vulgate number is Psalm 89; modern Bibles number it 90. Its title assigns the prayer to Moses, but Augustine and Bellarmine both deny that the title establishes Mosaic composition. Augustine still treats Moses as minister of the old covenant and prophet of the new, while Cassiodorus says the name directs the reader to the two dispensations the psalm joins. A title can govern canonical reception without functioning as modern authorship metadata.

The chant’s *a generatióne et progénie* is Cassiodorus’s lemma exactly. He takes the first noun from the beginning of the human race and the second through its successive renewal to the end. Augustine and Jerome instead read two generations as old and new covenantal peoples; Augustine also offers the plain sense of every generation. The refuge is therefore both diachronic and christological: the God who sheltered Moses’ people does not cease to be the refuge of those brought into Christ’s covenant.

Jerome provides a direct cross-chant relation. Explaining why refuge is needed, he quotes the threatened confessing soul of Psalm 73:19, the verse from which the Introit and Gradual take the poor. Augustine deepens the temporal contrast: immutable divine eternity becomes refuge from the mutability of time. That prepares the Offertory’s surrender of “my times” without dissolving the literal distress of the Gospel’s travelers.

### Gospel: mercy on the way and thanksgiving at Jesus’ feet (*Gosp.*)

Luke locates the encounter on Jesus’ journey toward Jerusalem, in the Samaria–Galilee region, at an unnamed village. The phrase does not permit an exact route reconstruction, village identification, month, or Passion year. The ten keep their distance in accordance with the social and ritual facts of their condition; they make a common appeal to Jesus as Master. Jesus neither touches them nor pronounces cleansing at once. He commands the action that would allow priests to examine restoration, and the cleansing occurs on the way.

Tertullian, in an anti-Marcionite argument, treats their going as obedience to Christ’s word, the priests as witnesses, and the Samaritan’s thanksgiving as an offering rendered to Christ the true priest and temple. Cyril says Jesus sends healing with those sent to the priests. Augustine reads the priestly examination as a figure of the Church’s ministry in doctrine and sacrament; Bede preserves this line. Bruno makes the juridical distinction most plainly: priests judge the person clean; they do not cause the cure. The later penitential tradition uses the cleansing on the road to defend both interior contrition before sacramental encounter and embodied confession to the priest. These are received sacramental applications, not Luke’s literal description of Christian absolution.

All ten receive cleansing; the narrative then narrows to one. Augustine’s *Quaestiones* makes the Samaritan, etymologized as “keeper,” the person who guards unity by giving glory back to God. In *Sermon 176* he applies the scene against self-attribution: do not claim amendment as one’s own lest one stand among the unthankful nine. Bede distinguishes cleansing from the salvation spoken to the returning man and associates the difference with faith in operation rather than bare recognition. Gregory the Great uses the cleansed lepers for reconciled heretics, while Bruno warns that outward cleansing alone does not guarantee final perseverance. Together they prevent the episode from becoming a simple transaction in which cure automatically equals mature discipleship.

The Samaritan’s sequence is bodily and theological: he sees, returns, glorifies God with a great voice, falls before Jesus, gives thanks, and is raised by Jesus’ word. Luke places glorifying God and prostration before Jesus in one act without pausing to explain their relation. Theophylact’s generous reception draws the moral conclusion that foreign origin is no barrier to pleasing God and sacred ancestry no ground for pride. St. Anthony of Padua, preaching this Gospel under a different medieval Sunday number, gives the return, prostration, thanksgiving, and saving faith an extended penitential and moral reading. St. John Henry Newman’s notes return to the themes of disease as an image of sin and gratitude as worship; St. Alphonsus develops the scene morally, but his inherited medical premises cannot be repeated as history or medicine.

Several safeguards are non-negotiable. Luke does not call the nine Jews; Alfred Plummer’s *International Critical Commentary* on Luke, pp. 403–405, explicitly notes that the inference does not follow. Augustine, Cyril, Guéranger, Schuster, and even Newman at one locus receive the contrast in forms that can become supersessionist. Their claims must remain attributed and critically bounded. The Church confesses Israel’s Scriptures as God’s word and, under *Nostra aetate* 4, may not portray the Jews as collectively rejected or accursed. Likewise biblical *lepra* must not be projected onto modern Hansen’s disease or used to shame visible difference, chronic illness, disability, or unbelief. The Gospel addresses every Christian first: mercy already received seeks the truthful return of thanksgiving.

### Offertory: changing times in faithful hands (*Off.*)

The Offertory is Vulgate Psalm 30:15–16: modern Psalm 31:15–16 when the title is numbered as verse 1, and 31:14–15 when it is not. Its final noun is not the Clementine *sortes*, “lots,” but *témpora*, “times.” This is no modern correction. Cassiodorus carries the complete chant wording and interprets the times of Christ’s assumed human life and death as held in the Father’s power. Jerome’s Psalter according to the Hebrew has *tempora*; the medieval Gloss records “the times of life” beside the lot reading; Bellarmine notes both Hebrew and ancient Latin support and specifically says the Mass frequently sings *tempora mea*.

Augustine’s rich exposition belongs instead to *sortes meæ*. He reads the unknown lot as grace and election without preceding merit, illustrated by the lot for Christ’s seamless garment and by Ephesians 2. Theodoret knows both Greek readings and treats them together as life’s changing circumstances. The two reception streams can be compared, but Augustine may not be presented as commenting on the word the Mass actually sings. In either case, the confession denies chance or persecutors final mastery of life.

The verse is not fatalism. “My times are in thy hands” does not identify every human act with God’s positive will, excuse injustice, or abolish prudence. At the Offertory it entrusts the worshipper’s contingent life while real gifts are prepared and freely offered. The same psalm earlier supplies the dying Christ’s commendation of his spirit; Cassiodorus consequently hears the whole psalm in Christ’s voice. That wider canonical context gives the short antiphon a paschal depth without pretending that v. 6 is appointed here.

### Secret: people and gifts beneath the same mercy (*Sec.*)

The prayer turns on a doubled imperative: be propitious to the people; be propitious to the gifts. Only then does it ask that God, placated by this oblation, grant pardon and the petitions sought. The syntax neither identifies the gifts with the people nor separates them. Offerers and offering stand together under mercy, and pardon precedes the granting of requests in the prayer’s order.

The Old Gelasian preserves both a single-imperative Vatican reading and the doubled form in Rheinau and St. Gall witnesses; the Gregorian supplement has the received double imperative. The ancient witnesses support textual transmission, not a reconstruction of the prayer’s author. A direct check also corrects a tempting larger claim: Wilson’s p. 172 note gives the Ottobonianus marginal incipits *Respice*, *Respice*, *In te speravi*, and *Panem de caelo* at Sunday XII, while the three 1962 orations appear at Sunday XIV with different cues on p. 173. These are incipits, not full chants, and no ninth-century co-location of the whole 1962 formulary has therefore been shown.

The Preface of the Most Holy Trinity follows. The Secret does not specify how the oblation placates God and should be read within the Roman Canon’s Christological sacrificial grammar, not as material gifts changing a hostile divinity. The people ask the Father through Christ to receive what divine gift first made possible.

### Communion: Israel’s manna received in Eucharistic thanksgiving (*Comm.*)

Wisdom 16 contrasts God’s nourishment of Israel with the punishment of oppressors and praises manna as fitted to every taste. The Missal freely adapts the verse: third person becomes *nobis*; *dedísti* recalls Psalm 77’s manna verb; laborless preparation disappears; the clauses about delight and sweetness are rearranged. The antiphon is thus a liturgical confession, not merely a biblical quotation.

Greek reception is broad but not yet explicitly sacramental. Origen, in a homily on Psalm 77, echoes Wisdom 16:20–21 almost verbatim and reads manna as the divine Word feeding souls according to their capacity. Gregory of Nyssa uses the same verses for the virginal birth and the Logos adapting himself to the soul. The text often printed under Chrysostom’s name in the *Synopsis Scripturae* is pseudo-Chrysostom and must not be cited as the saint’s. These witnesses establish christological and spiritual reception, not an assertion that Wisdom’s human author described the Eucharist directly.

The Latin medieval line becomes expressly Eucharistic. Rabanus moves from the literal manna to Christ’s declaration, “I am the bread of life,” and to eternal life through participation in that bread. Bonaventure’s Eucharistic use of the every-taste phrase presents sacramental sweetness as the abundance of the one Christ who answers varied spiritual need. Such language belongs to devotional and theological reception; it cannot make felt consolation the measure of sacramental reality. Gregory the Great quotes the same clause in *Moralia* VI.16.22 for a non-Eucharistic analogy: divine speech condescends to the quality of its hearers just as manna answered varied taste. His distinct use keeps sweetness from becoming a promised sensation. The Catechism’s canonical typology of manna and its Eucharistic teaching provide the doctrinal frame.

Albert the Great, commenting on Matthew 6:11, directly cites Wisdom’s every-taste manna in a Eucharistic exposition; Aquinas identifies manna as a figure of this sacrament at *Summa theologiae* III, q. 73, a. 6. Their claim concerns one objectively given Christ, not a substance that mutates to match appetite or a promise that every wish will be gratified. Aquinas’s treatment of sacramental fruit in q. 79 preserves the need for worthy reception, while q. 80, a. 2 distinguishes the angels’ spiritual feeding on Christ from sacramental reception proper to embodied human pilgrims.

St. John Paul II explicitly cites Wisdom 16:20–21 in his General Audience of 25 October 2000. Christ illuminates the Exodus gift; the Eucharist nourishes the pilgrim people and anticipates messianic fulfillment. This later saintly reception is especially apt for a Postcommunion that still asks progress: the bread is both present sustenance and pledge of an end not yet possessed.

The Gospel keeps this typology from becoming supersessionist. The antiphon’s “us” does not replace the biblical “them” but makes Israel’s remembered gift the Church’s thanksgiving through Christ. The returned Samaritan stands at Jesus’ feet not as evidence of Israel’s rejection, but as a stranger whose faith exposes the wideness of divine mercy and summons every recipient to gratitude.

### Postcommunion: recipients ask to advance (*Postcomm.*)

The prayer begins after reception: *Sumptis cæléstibus sacraméntis*. Its subject is the communicants themselves, *proficiámus*, not the sacraments: the prayer makes their progress answer redemption’s eternal *augméntum*. Cummiskey’s historical English changes the grammatical subject and omits the noun “increase”; the Latin must control the theological reading.

The Old Gelasian places this prayer with the Collect in one unnumbered Mass and preserves variation between *proficiant*, *proficiat*, and the received *proficiámus*; the Gregorian supplement has the latter. The first-person plural makes reception active without making it autonomous. Those who have received heavenly sacraments are precisely those who ask to advance.

The shared *augméntum* closes the formulary’s arc. Faith, hope, and charity grow toward what God promises; communicants grow toward redemption’s eternal fullness. The sacrament is not deficient, and the recipient does not complete Christ’s redemption. Rather, an objectively given mystery bears fruit through a pilgrim person’s continuing incorporation, conversion, and charity. The grateful Samaritan is therefore not the final image of a finished account: Jesus raises him and sends him onward.

### The Gospel’s penitential reception: real continuity, real boundaries

The movement from command to priestly showing became a major line in Western penitential theology. The anonymous *De vera et falsa poenitentia*, long attributed to Augustine, uses the episode for personal confession and for the priority of the contrite heart; it entered Gratian and Lombard. Bruno of Segni states that God may remit before the penitent reaches the priest, while the priest judges the cleansing. Albert the Great and Bonaventure continue the distinction: God cleanses, ordained ministry discerns and reconciles, and the intention of completing confession matters before the external act is finished. Their theology is received application. It should not be projected back as the sole literal meaning of the Levitical instruction.

The attribution record matters. *De vera et falsa poenitentia* is not Augustine. The sermon *De decem leprosis* at PL 175, col. 823 is Richard of St. Victor or pseudo-Hugh, not authentic Hugh of St. Victor. A responsible catena is improved by excluding false saints as much as by adding genuine ones.

### Modern papal reception: gift before response

Benedict XVI distinguishes bodily cleansing from definitive salvation and makes faith and gratitude the hinge; in a second Angelus he reads Naaman and the Samaritan as signs of a salvation universally offered through Israel and fulfilled in Christ. Francis repeatedly stresses that the ten obey before they are cured, that walking in faith and returning in gratitude belong together, and that Eucharist means thanksgiving rather than possession of a gift apart from the giver. Leo XIV, preaching the lection on 12 October 2025, emphasizes that Jesus heals freely without coercing thanks, that grace can touch a person without eliciting a mature response, and that outsiders must not be labeled “lepers.” These official receptions reinforce the strongest literal distinction: gift is prior and free; salvation draws the recipient into relation, worship, and gratitude.

### Bounded negatives and attribution controls

No genuine Chrysostom homily on the ten lepers was located, and Gregory’s forty Gospel homilies do not include the pericope. Ambrose offers only a transitional sentence. Cyril’s surviving matter is fragmentary because the corresponding Syriac sermons are lost. Marius Victorinus’s Galatians comments on 3:11–19 are lost. Jerome has no direct Psalm 30 exposition and only fragmentary comments on Psalm 73. These are evidence about the checked corpora, not universal absence claims.

The *Synopsis Scripturae* at PG 56 is pseudo-Chrysostom. The Pentateuch material at PL 91 and a Wisdom repetition at PL 92 are not Bede. The *Breviarium in Psalmos* at PL 26 is pseudo-Jerome. None is silently promoted into patristic authority. Protected modern translations were used, where necessary, only for orientation and were not reproduced.

## Source-Grounded Cross-Proper Synthesis

> This section retains only relations for which a named witness explicitly joins the texts, or for which separate direct witnesses establish the same bounded theological structure. It distinguishes historical pairing from proof of original design. The following section of exploratory proposals has a lower evidential status.

### The covenant chants and Paul’s promise (*Int., Ep., Grad.*)

This is the formulary’s most securely documented cross-proper relation. Guéranger explicitly says the Gradual repeats the Introit because the ancient people, in Rupert of Deutz’s account, calls for fulfilment of the covenant promised to Abraham’s faith. Independently, Augustine begins his exposition of Psalm 73 with Galatians 3:29 and the heirs of Abraham according to promise, while Jerome and Theodoret explain Paul’s *testaméntum* or *diathēkē* as the promise to Abraham. The sources do not agree whether Psalm 73:20 itself denotes the old covenant, new covenant, or Abrahamic promise. What is established is a received theological field in which the psalm’s covenant plea and Paul’s non-annullable promise illuminate one another.

### Moses, the two covenants, and the one refuge (*Ep., All.*)

Augustine, Cassiodorus, and Jerome read Psalm 89’s Mosaic title through the old and new covenants: Moses ministers the one and prophesies the other. Galatians names a mediated law while insisting that God is one. Aquinas’s Christ-as-Mediator with Moses as figure gives the two passages a controlled Christian relation: refuge across generations is the fidelity of the one God, fulfilled through Christ without denying Moses’ historical ministry. This is documented patristic and scholastic reception, not a claim that the Alleluia was chosen to settle the disputed referent of Galatians 3:19.

### Command, road, and continued advance (*Coll., Gosp., Postcomm.*)

The Collect and Postcommunion are historically paired in an Old Gelasian Mass and share *augméntum*; the first asks love of what God commands and the second asks that recipients advance. The Gospel places cleansing precisely *dum irent*, while Tertullian, Bede, Bruno, Albert, and Bonaventure receive the journey as obedient faith with penitential or sacramental application. The sources support a bounded structure: grace enables obedient movement, and sacramental reception continues rather than ends the pilgrim’s growth. They do not support a prosperity rule in which obedience guarantees physical cure.

### Gift received, thanks returned, oblation offered (*Gosp., Sec., Comm.*)

Tertullian explicitly describes the Samaritan’s thanksgiving as an offering to Christ; Augustine makes true thanksgiving the return to God of what was received. The Secret places people and gifts under propitiating mercy, and the Communion turns Wisdom’s third-person manna report into a first-person address to the Lord. Rabanus, Bonaventure, and John Paul II document Eucharistic reception of the manna verse. The integrated conclusion is therefore source-grounded: gratitude and Eucharist answer prior gift. It does not mean that the one grateful man merited his earlier cleansing or that Christian reception cancels Israel’s place in the manna narrative.

### The virtues increase toward redemption (*Coll., Postcomm.*)

The exact repeated noun *augméntum* supplies the textual link. Trent quotes the Collect’s virtue-increase petition in its chapter on increased justification; Aquinas explains charity’s increase during pilgrimage. The Postcommunion makes communicants the subject of continued progress toward eternal redemption. Together the two prayers give a fully Catholic account of sacramental fruit: divine grace initiates and sustains real growth in persons who act, love, receive, and persevere.

### Historical pairing: what the ninth-century evidence does and does not show

The checked Gregorian edition complicates any simple claim of a stable ancient formulary. Wilson’s p. 172 note gives Ottobonianus 313’s four marginal incipits—*Respice*, *Respice*, *In te speravi*, and *Panem de caelo*—at Gregorian Sunday XII. The Collect, Secret, and Postcommunion stand at Sunday XIV on p. 173 with different chant cues. A later Gellone witness also assigns the three orations to Sunday XIV; a fourteenth-century Savona fragment has them under Sunday XIII. This proves transmission and calendar movement, not ninth-century co-location of all ten 1962 elements. The full formulary is therefore a received historical constellation whose theological relations must be argued, not presumed from a single ancient compiler.

## The Propers: Notable and Quotable

> These four cases passed a strict afterlife audit: the appointed wording, or a clearly identified biblical form behind it, enters a later non-exegetical register with a documentable change of function. Straight sermons, devotional reuse, musical settings, and bare mottoes were excluded. Dependence on the Missal is claimed only where it can be shown; textual convergence is named as such.

### A sung plea becomes a bull, a satire, and a bonfire (*Int., Grad.*)

Leo X opened the bull *Exsurge Domine*, dated 15 June 1520, with the psalmic cry *Exsurge, Domine, et iudica causam tuam*. The lament asking God to defend his own cause had become the juridical opening of proceedings against Martin Luther. Ulrich von Hutten then republished the bull with mocking annotations and sent the derisive edition to Luther; Luther publicly burned the bull with books of canon law in December 1520. One phrase therefore passes through prayer, papal juridical act, humanist satire, and symbolic destruction.

**Control.** The bull quotes the psalmic/liturgical form, not this Mass as a source. Its continuation follows the psalm rather than the Gradual’s inter-psalm ending. The incident is described, not recruited for either side of a confessional polemic.

### Bread from heaven in Joyce’s compromised Benediction (*Comm.*)

In the “Nausicaa” episode of James Joyce’s *Ulysses*, the Benediction versicle *Panem de coelo praestitisti eis* is read amid distracted talk, commercial detail, and Bloom’s voyeuristic attention. The language of bread bearing every delight becomes an ironized liturgical soundtrack to fractured and profane appetites. Joyce depends on the Benediction form heard after *Tantum ergo*, not on the Communion antiphon’s adapted *dedísti nobis*.

**Control.** The case concerns the wider liturgical life of Wisdom 16:20. The different pronoun and verb identify the route of dependence and prevent a false claim that Joyce quotes this Sunday’s proper.

### A Roman-Psalter phrase becomes a Nestroy family joke (*All.*)

The official program of the 32nd International Nestroy Conversations (Schwechat, 1–5 July 2006) announced Otmar Nestroy’s forum contribution *Familiäres oder: A generatione et progenie aus dem Hause Nestroy*. The rare Latin form sung in the Alleluia, followed by the German “from the house of Nestroy,” turns a scriptural span of generations into a bilingual genealogical joke at a scholarly family gathering.

**Control.** The surviving program supplies title, speaker, duration, and occasion but no abstract. No argument or intention beyond the title’s plain wordplay is inferred, and the title alone is reproduced under an unclear modern rights status.

### Werfel’s homeless times seek a Father’s hand (*Off.*)

Franz Werfel titled a 1935 poem *Tempora mea in manibus tuis*. The title reorders the same four words as the Offertory. Friedrich Buchmayr situates the poem in May 1935 under the pressure of homelessness: the speaker loosens claims of possession by kin, nation, and even self, and seeks the Father’s hand. The hunted psalmist’s trust thus becomes the title and governing address of a modern Jewish writer deeply engaged with Catholic Christianity while confronting dislocation.

**Control.** The biblical allusion is certain; dependence on this Missal proper is not. Werfel died in 1945 and the poem remains jurisdiction-sensitive, so only its Latin title is given and the modern poem is paraphrased.

## The Propers: Interpretive Possibilities

*These proposals arise from AI-assisted editorial comparison. They are attributed to none of the cited witnesses and make no claim about a compiler’s intention. Each gives its evidence class and controlling limit.*

**Unexplained desolation entrusted across time. (*Int., Ep., All., Off.*)** *Classification: near analogues located.* Psalm 73 asks why rejection continues and appeals to covenant; Paul marks 430 years without allowing time to void promise; Psalm 89 names refuge across generations; Psalm 30 entrusts “my times” to God. Augustine contrasts divine eternity with temporal mutability and depicts covenant-seekers awaiting what is promised, but no checked witness makes the four-way relation. The synthesis must not turn waiting into a chronology of the world’s end or treat distress as illusory.

**A petitioning voice becomes a thankful voice. (*Int., Gosp.*)** *Classification: not located in the checked corpus.* The Introit asks that God not forget the voices of those seeking him; the ten raise one voice for mercy; the one who sees his cleansing returns with a great voice to glorify God. The verbal movement can train prayer to return as thanksgiving. No located ancient, medieval, or liturgical commentator explicitly joins the chant’s voices to Luke’s two cries, and gratitude is not a price paid for cure.

**Manna becomes first-person thanksgiving. (*Gosp., Comm.*)** *Classification: near analogue located.* Wisdom speaks of bread given to Israel as “them”; the antiphon addresses God with *dedísti nobis, Dómine*. Heard after the stranger returns to give thanks, the grammatical adaptation can become the assembly’s answer: a people receives Israel’s manna memory as gift rather than possession. Rabanus and John Paul II establish Eucharistic reception of Wisdom, not this Gospel link. The move may never erase Israel, appropriate its history against the Jewish people, or imply that only Christians can give thanks.

**Obedience precedes sight and remains unfinished. (*Coll., Gosp., Postcomm.*)** *Classification: near analogue located.* The Collect asks love of what God commands; the lepers are cleansed while carrying out Jesus’ command; the Postcommunion asks continuing advance after sacramental reception. Bede distinguishes mere cleansing from faith’s grateful operation, but does not join the two orations. Obedience is not a technique that compels a miracle, and illness or delayed healing cannot be blamed upon insufficient faith.

**The Mediator’s hand and the worshipper’s times. (*Ep., Off.*)** *Classification: near analogue located.* The law is ordered *in manu mediatóris*; at the altar the singer confesses that his times are *in mánibus tuis*. Augustine says salvation is not in one’s own hand but in the Mediator’s, which supplies a genuine lexical analogue. He does not join the Epistle to this Offertory, and the relation neither identifies every contingency with God’s positive will nor removes human responsibility.

## Appendix: Scope and Qualifications

> **Edition and use.** This companion studies the recurring *Dominica Decima Tertia post Pentecosten*, II class, in the 1962 Vatican typical Roman Missal. It is not an official liturgical book, homily, civil-date assembly sheet, or ruling on present authorization. Calendar occurrence, precedence, commemorations, and particular calendars are outside this reusable formulary.
>
> **Text and translation.** All ten Latin elements, headings, boundaries, references, rank, Credo, and Trinity Preface were visually collated against the identified facsimile. Complete Scripture English is the registered public-domain Douay–Rheims/Challoner; oration English is Cummiskey’s anonymous 1861 witness. Witness-boundary notes expose places where those versions do not translate the appointed Latin form.
>
> **Research and rights.** Complete biblical contexts, principal Greek and Latin exegesis, representative medieval and later saintly reception, Catholic doctrinal controls, sacramentary transmission, and targeted cultural afterlives were checked within the matrix in `research/scope.md`. The survey is broad and claim-driven, not exhaustive. Protected modern works are paraphrased or quoted only in short, necessary compass; the repository does not relicense third-party texts.
>
> **Global limits.** The nine are neither identified as Jews nor described after they leave; *Nostra aetate* 4 excludes collective-rejection readings. Biblical *lepra* supplies no diagnosis, judgment on illness, or label for another person. No source located proves a single compiler’s cross-proper design or universal novelty for an editorial proposal.
>
> **Review.** The source leaf records completed textual, attribution, source-binding, originality, and profile audits. The full and synthesis candidates were built through settled references and inspected page by page; installation, catalog publication, web deployment, and live-route review remain separate integrating-session acts.
