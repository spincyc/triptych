# All Saints: desire, reconciliation and the sight of God

*Roman Missal Third Edition, United States: expansive study for 1 November 2026*

**All Saints**

Desire, reconciliation and the sight of God

Roman Missal, Third Edition — United States

1 November 2026; annual readings

God’s children already belong to him, yet they await a likeness that has not been fully disclosed. First John places those two assertions beside one another. The children are real; their life is unfinished. The promise of seeing God therefore concerns both their future and what they desire now. All Saints celebrates the fulfillment of that promise in the saints while the assembled Church still asks for mercy and growth in charity.

The Gospel describes people whose circumstances might seem to contradict their blessedness. They mourn, hunger for justice, make peace and suffer persecution. Revelation depicts a multitude that has passed through affliction and stands before God. Its members have washed their garments in the Lamb’s blood. The Mass sets this vision beside Jesus’ promises, and its prayers ask for help from the saints who have reached the end of the journey. Their glory and the assembly’s need belong to one communion.

Two questions deserve separate treatment. The first concerns purification: how does a person who already receives God’s love become capable of seeing him? Augustine explains the growth of holy desire and the believer’s willing dependence on grace. Thomas Aquinas distinguishes the virtues through which a person approaches happiness from the completed happiness of seeing God. The second concerns the people Christ gathers: how can they be one, and why may their work for peace bring opposition? Bede explains the numbered and innumerable assemblies in Revelation; John Chrysostom explains peacemaking through the reconciling work of the Son.

These questions give different accounts of the same Christian life. The first asks about desire and its fulfillment. The second asks about membership, reconciliation and perseverance. The difference becomes clear at the difficult words. John’s believer “sanctifieth himself,” yet receives sonship as a gift. Revelation gives a definite number, then shows a crowd beyond counting. Jesus blesses peacemakers, then blesses those persecuted for justice.

The prayers also speak of a difference between those already with God and those still seeking salvation. The saints continue to intercede. The proper Preface names the heavenly Jerusalem as mother and speaks of pilgrimage, help and example. At Communion, the appointed antiphon selects the promises of vision, sonship and kingdom. The Prayer after Communion asks about charity’s completion and the heavenly banquet. Thus the account of holiness must explain receiving help, belonging to others and hoping for a fulfillment still to come.

## The Propers: Detailed Commentary

### Festive joy and the request for mercy

The Entrance’s incipit, *Gaudeamus omnes in Domino*, means “Let us all rejoice in the Lord.” Its subject is common rejoicing in the saints’ celebration, with angelic praise of God’s Son. It is a composed antiphon. The chant-book directions sometimes printed beside it are separate musical provisions; they add no appointed psalm to these readings. The joy expressed at the entrance concerns a communion larger than the assembly now present.

The Collect asks for divine mercy with the saints’ intercession. Celebration and petition occur together: the congregation praises what God has done in the saints and still needs his favor. That combination becomes intelligible beside 1 John. Children may truly belong to the Father while still awaiting completion. The saints’ intercession addresses that continuing need. The incipit *Omnipotens sempiterne Deus* addresses almighty and eternal God; the complete prayer is located in the appointed-text section below rather than supplied in a new translation.

### Revelation: who can stand before God?

The First Reading occurs between the sixth and seventh seals. At the end of chapter 6, people hide from the wrath of the enthroned one and the Lamb, and ask who can stand. Chapter 7 depicts servants sealed before destruction and then a multitude standing before the throne. In chapter 5 the Lamb has appeared as slain and has received the book. These scenes explain why the multitude attributes salvation to God and the Lamb. Its security comes through the one whose death is already visible in the vision.[^1]

The appointment retains the number of the sealed but passes over the twelve tribal counts in verses 5–8. The hearer therefore encounters a definite total and then a crowd no one can number. Bede explains the number through twelve multiplied by twelve and by a thousand. He associates twelve with the apostles and with faith in the Trinity throughout the fourfold world. His conclusion is the fullness of the Church, descended from the patriarchs by natural lineage or imitation of their faith. The arithmetic is symbolic exegesis of an apocalyptic vision.[^2]

At verse 9 Bede permits two explanations of the transition. The vision may resume the account of victorious glory after an intervening recapitulation. It may also proceed from Israel, to whom the Gospel was first preached, to the salvation of the Gentiles. The second possibility distinguishes the groups at that point in the narrative. Both preserve the breadth of the vision; his discussion supplies no ceiling on the number who can be saved.[^3]

The elder’s question about the white-robed crowd makes their identity depend on what they have come through and how they were cleansed. Blood ordinarily stains a garment. Here the Lamb’s blood makes it white. Bede explicitly includes the whole Church in this cleansing, beyond those who died as martyrs. He connects their standing together in God’s service with faithful confession amid adversity. The received commentary of Victorinus similarly understands the robes through baptism in the Lamb’s blood and the preservation of grace received. Its transmitted text has a difficult recension history; that baptismal explanation is cited from the received commentary, without assigning every phrase to an unaltered ancient original.[^4]

### Psalm 24: the Creator and those who seek his face

The psalm begins with God’s ownership of the world and its inhabitants. It then asks who can ascend his mountain and remain in his holy place. The answer concerns hands, heart and the soul’s attachment to vanity. The worshipper receives blessing and mercy, and belongs to the generation seeking God’s face. The complete psalm continues with the entrance of the King of glory; the appointed strophes end before that dialogue.[^5]

Augustine reads the whole psalm in relation to Christ’s resurrection and glorification. The nations believing in the glorified Lord become his Church. He takes the mountain as the height of God’s righteousness, innocent hands as guiltlessness in deed, and the pure heart as purity in thought. Refusing vanity concerns a soul that longs for lasting eternity instead of treating itself as another passing possession. His account combines moral action with desire for an enduring good.[^6]

Aquinas distinguishes approaching the mountain from standing at the destination. He then distinguishes exterior innocence from interior purity and directly cites Matthew’s promise that the clean of heart will see God. His explanation of blessing and mercy has two forms. They can be the promised reward. They can also explain how a person becomes innocent enough to approach: God’s mercy makes such access possible. That second explanation matters when the psalm is read beside John’s command to purify oneself.[^7]

The response is appointed as an adaptation, “cf.” verse 6. The historical study Bible gives a third-person description of the seekers, and does not supply the approved adapted refrain. Its wording can explain the response’s scriptural basis; it cannot be recast as an invented text for the assembly to sing.

### First John: children now, likeness still to come

The epistle places the Father’s love before the believer’s work. The children already possess their identity; the world fails to recognize them because it failed to recognize him. Yet the author distinguishes that present identity from what will be manifested. Future likeness and the vision of God explain why hope has a purifying effect now. The next verses address sin and righteousness, and the wider chapter proceeds to love expressed in deeds. Adoption has moral consequences.[^8]

Augustine’s Tractate IV follows the passage from abiding in Christ into these assertions about sonship. He asks what remains to be hoped for if believers are already God’s children. His answer distinguishes seeing Christ’s humanity at judgment from the promised sight of the divine Word. The wicked too can see the judge in his human form; the promise to the pure concerns a vision beyond that encounter. He then explains desire as preparation for receiving what is promised. The actual reasoning about desire and willing purification is developed in the first interpretation below.[^9]

### Christ’s invitation before the Beatitudes

Matthew 11:28 follows Jesus’ thanksgiving for the revelation given to little ones and his assertion of the Son’s knowledge of the Father. Its invitation addresses the burdened. The following verses speak of learning meekness and humility and taking his yoke. Only verse 28 is appointed as the acclamation. The context explains what the offered rest involves.[^10]

Chrysostom asks how a yoke can offer rest when Jesus elsewhere demands a narrow way and costly obedience. He answers through meekness and humility: the disciple learns from Christ and ceases to bear the burdens of pride and sin in the same way. He retains both labor and gift. The invitation begins with the burden people already carry; Christ calls them toward salvation. Chrysostom explicitly relates this humility to the beginning of Jesus’ earlier teaching, the Beatitudes.[^11]

### The Gospel: blessing, conduct and promised fulfillment

Jesus sees the crowds, ascends a mountain, sits and teaches the disciples who approach him. Matthew has just described his Galilean teaching and healing. The discourse later extends through commands about reconciliation, prayer and obedience. Its concluding comparison of houses concerns hearing these words and doing them. Augustine uses that conclusion to explain why the Sermon describes the Christian life in its fullness.[^12]

The promises vary: kingdom, inheritance, consolation, satisfaction, mercy, vision and sonship. Augustine understands their variety through a course of conversion beginning with humility. Chrysostom stresses that the initial blessing concerns chosen humility. Low social position can coexist with pride, and disciples honored for their works will still need humility. His explanation makes the invitation available across social ranks while requiring a real disposition of the will.[^13]

Augustine understands the earth as a stable, eternal inheritance. Chrysostom includes a present, sensible reward as well as future gifts: violent striving can lose property and life, while meekness can preserve them. Aquinas reports Chrysostom and prefers a future interpretation.[^14]

The Gospel ends after the promise of heavenly reward in 12a. The following reference to earlier prophets belongs to the complete biblical context and Chrysostom’s commentary, rather than to the text appointed for proclamation. Likewise the study Bible and Augustine place meekness before mourning; the U.S. proclaimed Gospel places mourning first. Augustine’s explanation of successive stages follows the sequence of his own text.

### The saints’ concern at the Eucharist

The Prayer over the Offerings concerns the saints’ continuing concern for the salvation of those still on the way. Their completed life is thus compatible with help for others. The prayer is addressed to God, and their intercession is understood within that dependence. Its subject belongs beside the Collect’s request for mercy, although the two prayers occupy different ritual moments.

The proper Preface names Jerusalem as mother and speaks of the saints’ glory, the pilgrimage toward it, and their help and example. These are personal and communal descriptions. The living assembly can learn from those whose journey is complete and receive their intercession. The Preface’s Jerusalem language is part of a composed prayer; it does not appoint an additional reading from Revelation 21 or Hebrews 12. Its exact English remains available at the publisher locator below.

At Communion the antiphon selects Matthew 5:8–10: purity, peacemaking and persecution for justice. Vision, sonship and kingdom are consequently heard together at this point. Purity concerns the recipient’s heart, peacemaking the work done among persons, and persecution the cost fidelity may have. The selection keeps these demands beside one another. The two interpretations explain their relation in different ways.

The Prayer after Communion concerns charity’s perfection and arrival at the heavenly banquet. Receiving Communion is followed by asking for further grace. The future banquet is hoped for, while the sacrament is received now. The prayer therefore permits an account of real participation that still needs perseverance and growth. If the solemn blessing is selected, its petitions concern intercession, holy example, service and final joy together. Its application to service is pertinent to both interpretations, but it is an optional conclusion.[^15]

## Purified desire and the sight of God {#lane-purified-desire}

### A gift already received and a promise still awaited

John’s short passage makes the believer both a recipient and an agent. The Father’s love makes children of God. Those children then purify themselves because they hope to see him. The apparent difficulty is sharper than the familiar observation that people need to improve. If God gives the identity, why does its fulfillment still require purification? And if the believer purifies himself, what remains of the claim that the Father gives everything?

Augustine begins with the reality of sonship. A name without the thing named would be useless, like calling someone a physician who cannot heal. God’s children must be such in their life, faith, hope and charity. Yet Augustine does not infer that their present condition exhausts what John promises. The phrase “not yet” describes a future manifestation of the life they already have. Their sonship has begun truly while the promised sight exceeds their present experience.[^16]

His distinction between two ways of seeing Christ explains the difference. Christ’s humanity is visible, and those condemned at judgment will see the human judge. John’s promise cannot mean merely that encounter, since he connects the promised vision to likeness and purification. Augustine consequently speaks of seeing Christ as the divine Word, equal with the Father. The difference concerns what is seen and the beholder’s relation to him. A visual encounter with a human figure would not by itself account for the transforming likeness John announces.

The psalm asks who can ascend and stand in God’s holy place. Its question can be read beside this promise: approach has a destination, and that destination is communion with God. Aquinas’s distinction between ascent and remaining makes this precise. An intention to seek him begins a journey; standing concerns its completion. In this interpretation, the saints celebrated at the Entrance are those whose journey gives the congregation reason to rejoice, while the congregation’s Collect still asks for mercy along its own way. That connection follows from the difference between present adoption and completed sight.[^17]

### Why desire must grow

Augustine explains waiting by an ordinary container. A bag or skin intended to receive something large must be stretched. The promised gift does not change; the capacity of its recipient grows. He then changes the comparison from size to contents. A vessel full of vinegar has to be emptied and cleaned before honey can be poured in. The two comparisons answer different parts of the problem. Desire must expand, and desires that exclude the promised good must be relinquished.[^18]

His examples concern a good that words cannot adequately describe. Honey, gold and wine are comparisons, and he explicitly says they fall short of God. The purpose of desire is consequently more than increasing the intensity of an appetite for a familiar object. The person learns to seek the one from whom created beauty comes. Waiting can expose how readily a person identifies happiness with something smaller and already possessed.

The psalm’s refusal of vanity has a similar moral consequence. Augustine understands the soul as destined for an enduring life and therefore desiring an unchangeable good. In the psalm, clean hands keep this desire joined to deeds. A person cannot seek an eternal good only in imagination while treating neighbors unjustly. Aquinas likewise distinguishes purity from disordered interior appetites and warns, in his explanation of the verse, about pride arising even from an apparent purity. A purified heart can still be compromised by taking pride in its own condition.[^19]

The Prayer over the Offerings concerns the saints’ solicitude for people still seeking salvation. Beside Augustine’s account, that intercession means that completed desire for God is compatible with concern for others. A person perfected in charity does not become less capable of loving those who need help. The prayer’s subject therefore corrects an account of contemplation that would end in isolation. The proper Preface’s help and example similarly concern a pilgrimage undertaken in communion.

### Who performs the purification?

Augustine directly asks who purifies the believer. His answer is God. He then explains why John still attributes purification to the person. God does not purify an unwilling subject: the believer joins his will to God’s action. Consent is a real act, although the indwelling helper makes the purification possible. Augustine uses the request for help to show both sides. Asking for a helper acknowledges need, and the word “help” implies that the person asking also acts.[^20]

This account gives the Collect a clear relation to the epistle. Asking for mercy is an act of the needy will directed toward God. The saints’ intercession does not remove the assembly’s own petition or its need to consent. Likewise the Lamb’s blood in Revelation identifies the saving cause of the robes’ whiteness. Bede’s explanation extends the cleansing to the whole Church. The shared inference is that holiness received from Christ can require the recipient’s fidelity without making that person the independent source of holiness.[^21]

Aquinas reaches a compatible result through another text. In Psalm 23:5, blessing and mercy ordinarily appear after the description of innocence. Their position might suggest only a payment for prior achievement. Aquinas also permits the causal explanation: the person approaches because God’s blessing and mercy make innocence possible. The same verses therefore allow him to explain both reward and enabling grace. This is a separate exegetical argument from Augustine’s discussion of the will, and it answers the psalm’s own problem of access.[^22]

The acclamation’s invitation to the burdened belongs here because Christ calls people to receive rest from him. Chrysostom explains the yoke in the following context through learning humility and meekness. The willing person still labors, but learns from Christ rather than presuming to achieve the end by self-sufficiency. The invitation precedes the demand in Matthew 11:28–30. When heard before the Beatitudes, it allows their difficult demands to be understood within Christ’s call and assistance. Chrysostom himself relates that humility to the beginning of Jesus’ teaching.[^23]

### Why the pure heart is promised vision

Aquinas develops the difficulty in Matthew 5:8 by setting the promise beside texts that call God unseen and inaccessible. One solution would deny the sight of God’s essence and offer only a created reflection of his brightness. Aquinas rejects it because John promises seeing him as he is. He also argues from desire: someone who knows effects seeks their cause, and a final happiness that left the first cause unknown would leave that desire unfinished.[^24]

A second solution would make the divine essence visible to bodily eyes. Aquinas rejects that explanation through the Beatitude’s own wording. Jesus names the heart. The mode of seeing is intellectual, as the scriptural language of the eyes of the heart suggests. An increase in a bodily eye’s power would not turn the divine essence into a colored object. Aquinas can still speak of glorified creation manifesting God with extraordinary clarity, while distinguishing that manifestation from the intellectual sight of God himself.

These arguments clarify why purity concerns the heart and why the promise is future. Aquinas distinguishes acts of virtue that dispose a person toward happiness from the happiness they seek. Moral action orders the person toward purity and peace. Completed happiness involves the vision of God and the union of love. He therefore cites both John’s promise of sight and John’s statement about the Father’s love and sonship. Knowledge and loving communion occur together in his account.[^25]

This distinction does not empty present life of blessedness. Aquinas describes beginnings now and consummation in the heavenly homeland. Augustine likewise understands the promises as truly begun in the saints’ present life. The future is fuller than the present without making present grace fictitious. The Prayer after Communion can consequently ask for charity’s completion after the assembly has truly received the sacrament. Its hoped-for heavenly banquet concerns the fulfillment of communion, and does not make present reception an automatic assurance of perseverance.

### The Beatitudes as a course of conversion

Augustine begins the course with humility because pride resists God’s teaching. Meekness then concerns a teachable reception of Scripture. Mourning follows the discovery of one’s entanglement in lesser loves. Hunger for justice concerns the difficult effort of leaving those attachments. Mercy appears as the fitting conduct of someone who needs superior help and can still help someone weaker. Purity and peace concern a person whose desires are being ordered toward God.[^26]

The explanation of mercy is particularly concrete. A person asking help from God may be stronger than a neighbor in some matter. Augustine reasons that the one seeking help should give the help he can. Thus dependence on grace gives a reason for mercy instead of excusing inaction. His course also explains why purity cannot be detached from the preceding works of charity. The heart learns singleness through a conversion that includes conduct toward others.

His account of peace concerns the ordering of desires under reason and of reason under God. It describes the person in whom disordered appetite no longer rules. Persecution then reveals whether this interior order can endure an attack from outside. The promise of the kingdom at the first and eighth blessings lets Augustine relate the conclusion to the beginning. The eighth tests the life developed through the preceding blessings.

At Communion, the selection of purity, peace and persecution permits that course to be considered at its later stages. The congregation receives sacramentally while still seeking the completed vision; it seeks sonship’s likeness while already adopted. If the solemn blessing is chosen, its concern for example and service extends that hope into conduct. The earthly pilgrimage described in the Preface requires the active charity for which the final prayer asks.

### The four senses

Literal.

: John addresses actual children of God who await manifestation and purify themselves in hope. The psalm asks about admission to God’s holy place; Jesus promises the sight of God to the clean of heart; Revelation describes a multitude cleansed through the Lamb. Their different literary settings remain distinct.

Allegorical.

: Christ’s saving blood makes the Church the people seeking the Father’s face. Augustine reads the psalm through Christ’s resurrection and glorification. Aquinas brings Matthew’s promised vision together with John’s promise of likeness, as the life of those united to the Son.

Moral.

: The will consents to God’s purifying help. Clean hands, undivided desire, mercy toward those needing assistance and endurance belong to that consent. Augustine’s account of desire explains why relinquishing lesser attachments concerns preparation for communion with God.

Anagogical.

: The promised fulfillment is seeing God and sharing the heavenly banquet in perfected charity. The present beginnings of blessedness remain incomplete. Augustine distinguishes creaturely likeness from equality with God, even when that likeness is perfected.[^27]

## The Lamb gathers a people who make peace and endure {#lane-reconciled-multitude}

### A numbered people and a multitude beyond counting

The seer first hears the number of the sealed, then sees the immense multitude. Between those acts of hearing and seeing, the appointment passes over the distribution among the tribes. The remaining contrast is striking: an exact total and a crowd beyond any human count. Bede’s explanation of the twelvefold number concerns the whole Church. Its patriarchal descent includes imitation of faith, so membership is wider than natural lineage. His multiplication of the number relates that Church to apostolic preaching throughout the world.[^28]

His account of the following multitude retains a further possibility. After the tribes of Israel, first recipients of the preaching, the text may name the salvation of the Gentiles. This makes the succession historically intelligible as an order of preaching. Bede also gives recapitulation its place: the vision can return to the account of those victorious after persecution. These alternatives concern how the narrative unfolds. They do not compel the conclusion that the multitude outside the numbered group has a lesser share in the Lamb’s salvation.[^29]

The persons in the crowd have nations, peoples and languages. Their common worship does not require the narrative to deny those differences. They stand before the same throne and Lamb and attribute salvation to them. The unity described is consequently a shared relation to the Savior. In this interpretation, the Entrance’s common rejoicing with angelic praise is heard beside that worship. Bede explains the sevenfold praise of verse 12 as the Church’s confession that its members have received these gifts from God. The congregation’s festive praise can be understood in the same dependence.[^30]

The Collect’s many intercessors likewise belong to a communion whose members receive from God. Their intercession does not make independent sources of salvation alongside the Lamb. Their shared concern for people still in need becomes explicit again in the Prayer over the Offerings. The difference between the saints’ completed life and the living assembly’s petition permits assistance across that difference.

### Why the white robes do not exclude the unbloodied

The elder identifies the multitude through tribulation and washing in the Lamb’s blood. A narrow interpretation might restrict it to those who have shed their own blood in martyrdom. Bede directly addresses that possibility. Martyrs are washed in their own blood, he says, but Jesus’ blood cleanses the whole Church from sin. The cause of the multitude’s cleansing is thus the saving work of Christ, which extends beyond the manner of any individual’s death.[^31]

That breadth leaves tribulation in place. Bede speaks of entry into the kingdom through many tribulations and emphasizes the final persecution. He gives no date for it. His explanation of standing together concerns faithful confession in adverse circumstances. The robe consequently signifies a received cleansing joined to fidelity. His earlier comment on palms identifies the triumph of the Cross and the victory won in Christ. Neither the garment nor the palm makes suffering its own source of salvation.[^32]

The received Victorinus commentary reinforces the baptismal understanding of the garments and the need to preserve received grace. It differs from Bede at the eastern angel: the received Victorinus text identifies Elias, whereas Bede identifies the incarnate Lord, the messenger of the Father’s will. That difference survives their agreement about cleansing. The corporate argument here follows the account of the robes, rather than depending on an agreed identification of every figure.[^33]

The psalm begins with the world and everyone who inhabits it belonging to God. Augustine understands the nations believing in the glorified Christ as his Church. The generation of seekers then has a shared moral description: innocent action, a pure heart and desire for God’s face. Read beside Revelation, those features explain the life of a gathered people rather than a population assembled by origin alone. Aquinas similarly describes the many who seek God as one generation.[^34]

### Peacemaking and the Son’s work

Jesus promises that peacemakers will be called God’s children. Chrysostom explains the promised name by the work of the only-begotten Son: he unites the divided and reconciles the alienated. Peacemakers resemble him by doing a work of reconciliation. The inference moves from an activity to a family likeness. Sonship names a relationship whose character becomes visible in conduct.[^35]

He gives the command a wider reach than avoiding one’s own quarrels. The disciple must also act among others who are divided. That addition changes the practical meaning of peace. A person can refrain from initiating conflict while remaining indifferent to neighbors whose estrangement continues. Chrysostom requires the further work of helping them become reconciled. The blessing concerns something done, with other persons as its beneficiaries.

Augustine’s inward ordering and Chrysostom’s reconciliation therefore emphasize different objects. Augustine explains the peace of desires brought under God. Chrysostom requires action between people. Aquinas includes both, with an explicit further step from interior order and peace with others to making peace among those at odds. A person trying to reconcile others still needs his own desires ordered; personal tranquility by itself does not accomplish their reconciliation.[^36]

In this interpretation, the Lamb’s gathered people and the peacemakers can be considered together: people reconciled in Christ learn to undertake his reconciling work. The inference brings Bede’s explanation of the Church’s unity into relation with Chrysostom’s explanation of the Son’s activity. Each commentator supplies a distinct argument. Bede explains who the multitude is and how it is cleansed; Chrysostom explains the conduct that bears the Son’s likeness.

The epistle’s children already receive their identity from the Father’s love. Its wider context joins that identity to love in deeds, including assistance to a brother in need. The work of reconciliation therefore belongs to a life whose source precedes its actions. The saints’ intercession, described by the Mass’s prayers, gives another example of concern for those still needing help. Their perfected life is compatible with such concern.[^37]

### Why peace may bring opposition

Chrysostom immediately follows his explanation of peacemaking with the next blessing. He says Jesus adds persecution for justice lest every peace be thought blessed. Some agreements would require abandoning virtue or refusing help to others. Chrysostom understands justice broadly here, including virtue, godliness and assistance. Peace has to be tested by those goods. His explanation follows the actual adjacency of the two blessings.[^38]

The qualification also works in the other direction. Hostility received is insufficient to establish that the recipient acted justly. In the direct address that follows, Jesus specifies false accusations and suffering for his sake. Chrysostom retains both conditions. Aquinas likewise says the cause of persecution makes the difference, and requires falsehood and Christ as the reason in his treatment of verse 11. The moral question concerns what was done and why the person is opposed.[^39]

This explains why the Communion antiphon’s selection matters to the argument. Its three blessings include personal purity, reconciliation and persecution for justice. A social account of holiness has to retain the clean heart. An account of reconciliation has to retain the possibility of faithful opposition. Hearing these promises together allows the communicant to hope for sonship and kingdom without assuming that peacemaking always receives approval.

Chrysostom gives particular attention to reproach because injury often comes without public honor. A person facing danger can sometimes receive praise that encourages endurance; slander can remove even that comfort. His examples explain why Christ’s promise addresses the wounds inflicted by false reports as well as bodily harm. The later reference to the prophets belongs to the wider Gospel context; it explains the commentator’s reasoning without extending the appointed endpoint.[^40]

John’s statement that the world fails to recognize God’s children also concerns this difficulty. Augustine explains the failure through Christ’s own unrecognized lowliness and his reproof of sin. Recognition by the world therefore cannot decide whether sonship is real. This claim gives no permission to dismiss every criticism. The truth of the charge and the conduct producing it still require the tests Chrysostom derives from Jesus’ words.[^41]

### A community invited to rest and taught to serve

The acclamation addresses all who labor and are burdened. Chrysostom includes those in anxiety, sorrow and sin. Christ’s invitation is extensive in its addressees and personal in its source. Learning his humility concerns the life together of persons who otherwise might compete for honor or cling to their own resentments. The comparison with Revelation is that those gathered receive salvation from Christ; their unity begins in receiving his gift.

The Preface describes the heavenly mother-city and the pilgrims helped by the saints’ example and intercession. The people before God and those on pilgrimage can consequently be considered as one communion across different conditions. The mother-city describes belonging and destination. It prevents the multitude’s glory from being imagined merely as a crowd of unrelated individual successes. The Prayer after Communion asks concerning perfected charity and the heavenly banquet, so the hope of arrival is also a hope for charity’s fulfillment.

If the solemn blessing is selected, its petitions include holy example and service, along with intercession and final joy. Service gives a concrete form to the solidarity developed here. Those who celebrate the saints ask for help to live in a way compatible with their communion. The appointment remains optional; the argument’s concern for service already follows from the peacemaking Jesus blesses and the charity for which the Mass prays.

### The four senses

Literal.

: The vision depicts a multitude from the peoples worshipping before God. John names God’s children amid the world’s failure to recognize them. Jesus blesses those who make peace and those persecuted for justice, specifying false accusation for his sake in the direct address.

Allegorical.

: Bede reads the garments through baptism and the palms through Christ’s Cross. Chrysostom explains peacemakers through the reconciling Son. Together these accounts explain the identity and activity of a people united to Christ.

Moral.

: Christians reconcile divided neighbors, preserve purity and act justly when seeking peace. They test the cause of hostility instead of treating hostility itself as proof of righteousness. The saints’ continuing concern for others is compatible with the service asked of those still on pilgrimage.

Anagogical.

: The saints stand together before God, and the assembly hopes for a common heavenly banquet in perfected charity. Bede’s account includes the whole cleansed Church beyond martyrs. It gives neither an arithmetic population of heaven nor an assurance of each individual’s perseverance.

## Desire, reconciliation and the promised end

The first interpretation explains the change needed in the person who hopes to see God. Augustine’s growing capacity and Aquinas’s distinction between preparation and consummation answer John’s combination of present adoption, future likeness and self-purification. The second explains the identity and conduct of the people Christ gathers. Bede’s symbolic fullness and baptismal cleansing answer the vision’s question about the multitude. Chrysostom’s account of the reconciling Son explains why peacemaking belongs to sonship and why fidelity may still bring opposition.

The difference affects how purity and peace are understood. In the first interpretation, a heart learning to seek God without divided desire approaches the promised vision. In the second, that heart belongs to persons undertaking reconciliation among their neighbors. Augustine’s account of interior peace and Chrysostom’s account of active peacemaking are compatible, but one cannot replace the other without losing an object of Christian charity.

Augustine and Aquinas understand the promised earth as a future inheritance. Chrysostom includes present possession alongside future gifts.

Both accounts require grace, willing action and perseverance. Their common future is communion with God in perfected charity. The prayers ask for help on the way and describe the saints’ continued concern for those still seeking salvation. That communion lets the congregation rejoice in the saints while still asking to grow in the love whose completion it celebrates.

### Psalm 24: a sanctuary setting and a collection bound {#history-psalm}

The Douay–Rheims superscription names David and the first day of the week. Augustine reads that first day through the Lord’s resurrection and glorification. That is his Christian exposition of the superscription, and does not identify a dated occasion of the original psalm. The ascent, holy place and seekers describe worship at God’s sanctuary. An exact occasion and individual writing place remain unknown.[^42]

The collection boundary above derives from the official Psalms introduction’s account of the collection before the Maccabean period and its warning that individual psalms cannot be dated securely. It is a latest bound, not the year this psalm was composed. The traditional author label and that collection boundary therefore describe different facts. No reign-era date has been substituted for a missing occasion.[^43]

### Matthew: the mountain and the writing of the Gospel {#history-matthew}

The event row concerns Jesus’ teaching on a mountain. Matthew 4:23–5:2 places it in the Galilean ministry and names the disciples who approach him, with the crowds in context. It names no particular hill. The event’s exact year is unresolved in the chronology evidence. The scene is a narrated occurrence; it is separate from the question when the evangelist wrote the Gospel.

The composition alternatives preserve the differing ancient and modern reckonings reported by Jacquier. His discussion includes the tradition of a Palestinian Hebrew original and the disputed relation of the Greek Gospel to it. The earlier and later estimates cannot all be treated as agreed dates of one demonstrably identical textual event. No particular one is preferred here. The table retains their disagreement rather than silently assigning a proposed Hebrew date to the extant Greek work.[^44]

The acclamation from chapter 11 and Communion’s repetition of three blessings share the Gospel’s composition question. They create no independent composition occasions. Chrysostom’s exposition of the acclamation reads it with the Son’s revelation of the Father and the following invitation to learn his humility. That literary setting helps explain the verse, while adding no exact date to the event table.

### First John: probable Ephesian setting {#history-john}

The date displayed derives from Durand’s approximate span for the Johannine writings collectively. It is not a separately pinpointed occasion for this epistle. Drum considers an Ephesian association and the end of the first century probable, rather than certain. The immediate recipients’ town is not established. First John 3:1–3 is instruction about present sonship and future manifestation; it narrates no separately datable earthly event.[^45]

### Revelation: Patmos and the churches in Asia {#history-apocalypse}

Revelation 1:9 identifies Patmos in the seer’s account; the opening addresses seven churches in Asia. The throne in chapter 7 belongs to the vision. It is neither a recovered writing room nor a date for the saints’ arrival in heaven. The heavenly scene and the circumstances in which the book was written remain distinct.

Van den Biesen reports the traditional Domitianic window while discussing the difficulty raised by a Vespasian interpretation. Durand gives the approximate Johannine span displayed as another composition alternative. Their overlap is compatible with a late first-century setting, but the two sources have different scopes and do not provide one agreed exact year. The traditional attribution to John and historical questions about composition are preserved as the cited sources state them.[^46]
