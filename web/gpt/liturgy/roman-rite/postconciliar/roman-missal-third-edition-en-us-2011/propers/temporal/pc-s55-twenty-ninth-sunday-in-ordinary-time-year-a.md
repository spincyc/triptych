# Twenty-ninth Sunday in Ordinary Time, Year A: What belongs to God

*Roman Missal Third Edition, United States: expansive proper study for 18 October 2026*

**Twenty-ninth Sunday in Ordinary Time**

What belongs to God

Roman Missal, Third Edition — United States, Year A

18 October 2026

God names a king who does not know him. Jesus asks his opponents to name the image on a coin. Between those two acts of naming stands a question that reaches beyond taxation: how can a person receive the goods of this world without giving them the place that belongs to God? Cyrus has strength, a royal task and a place in Israel’s deliverance. None of these makes him the source of the salvation he serves. Caesar has a recognizable currency and real claims within civil life. Neither the image nor the inscription makes his claim unlimited.

The Twenty-ninth Sunday’s worship begins elsewhere than the court or the marketplace. It begins with a petitioner asking to be heard and sheltered. The Collect concerns the will that must serve; the prayers at the altar concern gifts, purification and a life moving toward eternal fulfilment. These prayers bring the argument home. The assembly is not merely deciding how powerful outsiders should behave. It must ask how its own powers, possessions, service and hope are to be returned to their giver.

Paul’s thanksgiving adds a second question. The Thessalonians have received a Gospel that bears fruit in work, labour and endurance. How can their action be real if the gift comes first? Their faith does not make them spectators of God’s power. Divine power is known in a community that believes, loves and perseveres. The short acclamation from Philippians holds together a received word and a visible light. The Eucharistic movement then carries this relation between gift and response into worship: those who offer still need cleansing, and those who receive still need to grow.

Two readings unfold these questions. The first follows Jerome’s distinction between Cyrus and the true Christ, and Chrysostom’s distinction between rightful dues and impious demands. It finds a freedom to serve the common life without worshipping its powers. The second follows Aquinas’s account of God’s action in willing and Chrysostom’s account of faith made active under pressure. It finds a freedom that grows through receiving and answering grace. Each reaches the whole formulary, but their answers are different: the first orders our allegiances; the second examines the transformation of the person who owes them.

The Communion alternatives give that distinction a particularly concrete setting. One uses a Psalm of mercy, deliverance and nourishment. The other selects the Son of Man’s self-gift as ransom. The former teaches dependence amid need; the latter names the saving deed on which Christian service rests. They are alternatives, not consecutive texts that every congregation hears. Either can deepen both readings, while changing the image through which their conclusion is received.

## The Propers: Detailed Commentary

### A prayer before the dispute

The Entrance takes its biblical basis from Psalm 16 in Vulgate numbering, Psalm 17 in Hebrew numbering. In the full Psalm the speaker appeals for a just hearing, asks that his steps remain firm and begs protection from enemies. Its closing hope is to behold God’s glory. The Entrance selects the appeal for hearing and the image of shelter; it does not rehearse the whole case for vindication. The worshipper therefore enters as someone who needs God, before hearing of either a victorious king or a disputed tax.

Augustine assigns the prayer to Christ together with the Church, his body. His explanation of verse 6 notices an unusual order: the speaker cries because God has heard. Augustine understands present confidence as the fruit of earlier help, when the prayer was weaker. Prayer is not a force that an autonomous speaker brings to bear on a reluctant God. The ability to pray strongly already bears the mark of mercy. That is a concrete reason why dependence and confidence need not oppose each other.[^1]

The pupil of the eye suggests something small, precious and carefully protected. Augustine gives it a Christological reading through Christ’s humanity; he reads the sheltering wings through merciful love. Aquinas, commenting directly on this Psalm, begins from the care involved in protection and offers several spiritual applications of the wings, including charity and the cross. These applications deepen the image rather than identifying an animal anatomy in God. Protection under the cross cannot mean that Christ’s members never suffer. It gives them a place from which to endure suffering without making their own strength their refuge.[^2]

### The will that the Collect brings before God

The Collect, identified by its Latin incipit in the text section, concerns devoted willing and sincere service of divine majesty. Its position matters: before the assembly hears a command about what to render, it asks for the disposition that can render rightly. The question is not only whether a duty can be named. A divided person may know the right action yet perform it for vanity, fear or advantage.

The petition thus gives an interior dimension to the Gospel’s public dilemma. Sincere service cannot be manufactured by flattering the one addressed, as Jesus’ questioners attempt. Nor does the prayer let the assembly treat good intentions as a private possession already secured. Asking God for readiness makes room for the change that the readings will demand.

### Cyrus: named, strengthened, and still unknowing

Isaiah 44 sets the prophecy against manufactured idols. Wood that becomes an object of worship cannot become the Creator who acts and calls by name. Near that chapter’s close Cyrus is associated with Jerusalem’s restoration. Isaiah 45 then addresses the Persian king as the Lord’s anointed, speaks of the strength given him, and places that strength at the service of Jacob and Israel. The appointed selection retains both the honour and the unsettling qualification: Cyrus does not know the one who equips him.

The title raises a real exegetical difficulty. If “anointed” points to Christ, should the passage be read directly as an address to Jesus? Jerome refuses that conclusion. He first insists on the proper name Cyrus, against readings that turn it into “Lord.” He then explains the royal designation by the insignia of kingship. Most decisively, the assertion that the addressee has not known God cannot simply be transferred to the Son through whom the world is reconciled. The negative clause controls the identification just as much as the title does.[^3]

Jerome nevertheless calls Cyrus a type and image of the true Christ. A type is a person or event whose real, limited action anticipates a fuller divine work. It need not share every characteristic of the fulfilment. Cyrus’s liberating office makes the comparison intelligible; his ignorance marks its limit. The result is stronger than either a flat political reading or a reading that erases the king. God works through this particular ruler, and the ruler is not thereby made the final saviour.

Aquinas likewise distinguishes the king’s dignity from a claim that he was actually anointed with sacred oil. In his exposition of Isaiah 45, the benefits of divine assistance have more than one intended fruit: liberation for the people, recognition of God, and the knowledge that reaches beyond Israel. He says that Cyrus remained in idolatry. The usefulness of an instrument and the conversion of its user cannot therefore be treated as the same event. A successful task is no proof that the person accomplishing it has rightly understood his life.[^4]

The selected text moves outward. God acts for Jacob and Israel so that knowledge of the one Lord reaches from sunrise to sunset. Israel’s particular election and the world’s knowledge do not cancel each other. The Lord’s care for a people is the means by which his identity becomes known more widely. Nothing in the passage makes the Persian king’s religion a second path of equal divine authority. The same oracle that gives him a role denies the existence of another God.

### All peoples give the Creator glory

Psalm 96 in Hebrew numbering, 95 in the Vulgate, answers the first reading with universal praise. Its selected verses call for a new song, proclaim divine glory among the nations, distinguish the Creator from idols, summon the families of peoples to worship and announce equitable government. The response from verse 7b concentrates this outward movement in a repeated act of giving God glory. The Lord who can employ a foreign ruler is not one tribal deity competing with other deities.

Augustine reads the new song through new love and the building of a house throughout the world. His account joins praise and mission. To tell the nations of God’s glory is also to refuse the preacher’s temptation to make himself the subject of the proclamation. Augustine’s inference starts with the object of the verb: whose glory is being declared? A splendid proclamation can become false in its direction when its servant receives the honour meant for God. The Psalm therefore tests the motives of religious speakers as well as the idols of those addressed.[^5]

His Christological reading also joins the Creator’s greatness to Christ’s humility. In §11 his received text includes “from the wood,” which he interprets of the cross. That phrase is not part of this Sunday’s appointed Psalm. The strophe also omits verse 10b, the clause concerning the world’s stability; Augustine’s discussion of that clause belongs to the full Psalm. The selected words proclaim divine kingship and equitable judgment. Christian reception then asks how that kingship is shown in Christ.

John Paul II makes that movement explicit in his audience on this Psalm. Sanctuary worship opens into the Lord’s universal rule, and prayer purifies faith from idols. He identifies the cross interpretation as a Christian rereading and ends by invoking Mark’s account of the Son of Man who serves. His conclusion relates kingship to self-gift; it does not turn the ancient song into a literal narrative of crucifixion. This becomes particularly fruitful if the Mark Communion option is used.[^6]

### A community whose faith has weight

The opening of 1 Thessalonians names Paul, Silvanus and Timothy and addresses a church “in God” the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. Thanksgiving follows before instruction. Paul remembers the work of faith, the labour of love and the endurance of hope. These are not three flattering labels placed over an otherwise ordinary association. Each virtue has a recognizable movement: faith acts, love spends itself, and hope lasts.

Chrysostom explains why thanksgiving to God and praise of the people belong together. Their conduct gives Paul something for which to thank God; thanksgiving teaches the hearers not to claim the whole achievement for themselves. Yet Chrysostom does not dissolve their conduct into an appearance. Faith’s work is seen in steadfastness under pressure; love involves actual labour; hope is tested by prolonged affliction. The surrounding chapter describes the reception of the word amid suffering, an explanation of the reading’s virtues even though that verse lies beyond the appointed endpoint.[^7]

Election enters this thanksgiving as something Paul recognizes through effects. Aquinas explicitly denies that God’s choice has been earned by antecedent human merit. He then explains the evidence in both hearer and preacher. He allows different distributions of the references to power, Spirit and fullness: signs and spiritual efficacy, or the preacher’s virtuous life, the Spirit and complete instruction. These alternatives keep the passage from becoming a slogan that identifies divine power with one spectacular manifestation alone.[^8]

The form of address also matters beside the Gospel. Caesar’s image identifies a coin; being “in God” identifies this community. Its distinction is relational before it is an achievement or a social rank. Paul does not praise the Thessalonians for commanding the city. He thanks God for a life capable of costly fidelity within it. The continuation in 1:9–10 speaks of turning from idols and waiting for the risen Son; that larger horizon explains how hope can endure without being measured by immediate success. It remains context, not an addition to the reading.

### A light held from another

The acclamation selects only Philippians 2:15d and 16a. Its image of light and its reference to the word of life stand within Paul’s exhortation to obedience after the account of Christ’s humility and exaltation. A star does not need to become the whole sky in order to be visible. Likewise a Christian community can shine within the world without identifying its own influence with God’s kingdom.

Aquinas distinguishes light by essence from light by participation. God is light in himself; the saints receive something of the light of the Word. Holding Christ’s word therefore explains rather than competes with their brightness. The moral consequence is that visibility cannot be detached from its source. A community may become highly visible through controversy or prestige while obscuring the word that gives life.[^9]

Chrysostom’s homily on the wider passage connects light with a life made blameless and with willing service instead of murmuring. His explanation of “word of life” stresses a life-giving principle or seed. The NPNF editor prefers an interpretation referring to the Gospel word and notes the difference. The editor’s comment is not a second statement by Chrysostom. Aquinas explicitly identifies Christ’s word; Chrysostom’s accent here falls upon the life visible in the saints. Both aid the connection between receiving and shining, but they reach it by distinguishable explanations.[^10]

### A trap answered through its own evidence

Matthew places the tribute question among the Jerusalem controversies following Jesus’ entry and temple teaching. The narrated alliance consists of the Pharisees’ disciples and the Herodians. Its flattery is exact enough to sound like confession: Jesus teaches truthfully and does not defer to appearances. Yet the speakers attempt to use that truthfulness to force a dangerous public answer. Their attitude shows why accurate religious language and sincere service are different things.

Chrysostom describes the double trap. An answer against tribute can be made into an accusation before Roman power; an answer in its favour can discredit Jesus before the people. Jesus first exposes the intention, then requests the money. The questioners identify its image and inscription themselves. Their answer becomes evidence against the terms in which they posed the dilemma. Jesus has not merely escaped a clever conversation. He has required people who tried to control him to acknowledge a fact already present in their common life.[^11]

The verb “render” is decisive for Chrysostom: the answer concerns repayment of what is due, rather than an arbitrary gift. At the same time, he limits the civil demand to what does not injure godliness. An impious requirement does not become righteous because Caesar demands it. This double affirmation rules out two easy evasions. Religious allegiance cannot excuse every refusal of civic duty; civic office cannot turn every demand into a moral obligation.

Aquinas first explains the literal civil exchange through the use of the coin. He then expressly turns to a mystical reading: the soul bears God’s image and must be rendered to God. The coin is an artefact marked by earthly authority; the person is not made by Caesar. This inference draws the whole self into the second half of Jesus’ answer. It is not a report that Jesus held up a second object, nor does it erase the money in his hand. The literal duty remains while the spiritual claim reaches the person who performs it.[^12]

### The offering and the servant’s purification

The Prayer over the Offerings relates the gifts through which worship is offered to the purification of those serving the mysteries. A person can possess something to offer and still need cleansing. The act of giving is therefore not a certificate of innocence. In the Gospel, the possession of a coin exposes a question; at the altar, the possession of gifts becomes an occasion to receive help.

The liturgical movement is especially searching for those who serve visibly. The act itself is good, yet the servant’s motives and habits remain open to healing. This is neither contempt for human work nor the claim that an imperfect person must cease every good act. It places work in a relation of dependence: service is offered through gifts received, and service itself is accompanied by a plea for purification.

### Communion through the Psalm of mercy

The Psalm option draws on Psalm 32:18–19 in Vulgate numbering, 33:18–19 in Hebrew numbering. The full hymn contrasts God’s counsel with the insufficiency of kings, armies and horses. Its movement toward the Lord’s merciful gaze thus answers a concrete temptation: confusing the instruments of protection with the power that can finally save. A great army may be an instrument within history; its greatness cannot abolish mortality.

Augustine understands the famine in the verse through deprivation of the Word and everlasting truth. Those who presume upon their own strength discover that even their strength fails when cut off from righteousness. His exposition then turns to patient waiting for incorruptible nourishment. The passage is not a promise that believers will always have abundant provisions. It exposes a hunger that provisions alone cannot satisfy.[^13]

Aquinas treats mercy’s effects as both rescue and strengthening. He distinguishes bodily rescue, rescue from sin and final rescue, and explains nourishment bodily, spiritually and sacramentally. His explicit sacramental reference gives the Communion setting a precise theological connection. He also insists that fear and hope belong together: fear without hope tends to despair, hope without fear to presumption. Mercy is neither a permission to disregard God nor a verdict that frailty makes return impossible.[^14]

### Communion through the Son’s ransom

The Mark option selects the ransom clause from 10:45. The preceding service clause and the dispute over rank belong to its Gospel context, not to the antiphon’s complete appointed wording. In that context Jesus is going toward Jerusalem; James and John seek places of honour, and the others become indignant. The Lord answers the competition by distinguishing the domination associated with rulers from the service his disciples must learn. His own self-gift supplies the measure.

Bede’s direct exposition of Mark makes humility, rather than power, the path to the summit of virtue. Christ gives his own action as an example for disciples who might disregard words. Bede then says that the Son of Man accepted the form of a servant to shed his blood for the world. In the same paragraph he explains “for many” as those who were willing to believe. The worldwide reach of the gift and its believing reception stand together in his account.[^15]

That conjunction prevents two reductions. The Son’s ransom is not merely a lesson in helpfulness: he gives the saving gift. Nor can the gift’s universal horizon be made to mean that every person already answers it in faith. Bede names the believer’s response without making faith a price paid in advance for redemption. His brief gloss does not by itself explain the origin of faith’s willing; the account of grace and willing in Aquinas’s Philippians exposition addresses that further question.

Chrysostom, commenting on the parallel in Matthew 20:28, draws the moral argument from the distance Christ descends. The Lord’s death for enemies overturns the apostles’ competition for status. The one who could claim the highest place takes the servant’s task. This argument is sharper than a general preference for courtesy: the saving act changes the disciples’ measure of greatness. Chrysostom here comments on Matthew’s parallel, while Bede comments directly on Mark.[^16]

“Ransom” also raises the question of release and payment. Gregory Nazianzen asks to whom Christ’s blood could be offered as a ransom. He rejects the devil’s entitlement to receive God and refuses an account in which the Father delights in or demands blood. He speaks instead of the Father’s acceptance, the Incarnation’s sanctifying work and liberation through the Son. Aquinas uses the language of superabundant satisfaction and a price paid to God, while denying that humanity ever ceased to be subject to God’s power. What was lost was union in charity.[^17]

These are different positive accounts. Their shared refusal of a devil entitled to payment does not make Gregory’s question and Aquinas’s answer interchangeable. Gregory prevents the image of ransom from implying a divine appetite for violence. Aquinas explains how the voluntary loving gift can restore the relation broken by sin. Both make Christ’s deed more than an example that human effort could reproduce on its own. The Communion excerpt names that saving gift before the worshipper can turn it into a programme of imitation.

### Present help and an eternal end

The Prayer after Communion places present participation on the way toward eternal gifts. Reception is therefore both real and unfinished. The assembly has not received an empty sign, but neither has its pilgrimage ended. Its hope reaches beyond the next political success, the next practical achievement or the next feeling of religious assurance.

This ending joins the two questions with which the readings began. Earthly goods can serve a true purpose without becoming ultimate goods. A human response can be truly active without becoming its own source. Present help matters because the goal is greater than the present; preparation matters because the gift calls a person onward. The concluding prayer sends the assembly into duties that remain real, with a hope those duties cannot exhaust.

## Receiving the world without worshipping its powers {#lane-rightly-ordered-allegiance}

An earthly good can be real without being ultimate. This resolves the apparent collision between Cyrus’s vocation and God’s sole lordship, between Caesar’s dues and God’s claim. The whole formulary teaches a person to receive created goods, discharge real obligations and seek protection, while refusing to make any of these goods the source or final measure of life. Jerome distinguishes the ruler’s liberating office from the true Christ; Chrysostom distinguishes rightful tribute from impious demands. Augustine’s universal song and Aquinas’s image of God extend their consequences into the life of the worshipping body.

### The instrument does not become the saviour

The difficulty in Isaiah is not simply that God works beyond Israel’s borders. The king receives a title of sacred resonance while the oracle twice identifies his ignorance. If usefulness were enough to establish holiness, the second assertion would make little sense. Jerome’s insistence on the actual Persian king lets both assertions stand. A ruler can serve a divine purpose without knowing it and without acquiring divine standing himself.

This changes how benefit is received. Israel’s restoration need not be denied because its human instrument is imperfect. Yet gratitude for restoration cannot turn into a judgment that everything about that instrument is good. The two mistakes are mirror images: refusing a real gift because its agent is not holy, or granting unlimited confidence to an agent because some good has come through him. The prophecy permits neither inference. It puts the act within a purpose named by God, for Jacob and for the knowledge of the nations.[^18]

Jerome’s typology carries this distinction into the Christian reading. Cyrus can prefigure liberation without becoming identical with the liberator who reconciles the world. The contrast between type and fulfilment prevents a religious vocabulary from flattening the difference between an office and the Son. The Church’s confession of Christ does not require the past to contain a series of lesser Christs whose every action is sacred. It recognizes genuine anticipations whose limits make the fulfilment more, not less, intelligible.

### The coin establishes a debt, not a deity

The Gospel makes the same question tangible through a coin. The questioners hope to make obedience to God and the payment of tribute mutually exclusive. Jesus’ answer refuses the forced choice. Chrysostom’s emphasis on “render” makes the civil obligation concrete: a return is due within the common life in which the currency is used. It is inadequate to spiritualize the exchange before allowing the coin to remain money and the tax to remain a real demand.

But the answer’s second clause prevents the first from becoming absolute. Chrysostom insists that a demand contrary to godliness has exceeded what can rightly be rendered to Caesar. The ruler’s office identifies the claimant; it does not automatically establish the moral character of every claim. This is why the answer is more demanding than a convenient division into a secular half and a sacred half. The person doing the rendering belongs to God while acting within civil life.[^19]

Aquinas’s expressly mystical interpretation brings the coin’s image into relation with the soul’s image. The analogy has a limit that gives it force. An inscription can mark an artefact as part of an earthly economy. God does not merely stamp an already independent creature: the creature receives being from him. Rendering the person therefore cannot be reduced to transferring a religious portion of one’s income while the rest of life becomes exempt. In Aquinas’s exposition, even those spiritually raised above the world must seek peace in the world where they live with others. Belonging wholly to God can generate duties within shared life rather than dissolving them.[^20]

The Gospel consequently offers a test rather than a ready-made modern constitution. Which real good does a claim serve, what is actually owed, and does compliance injure the allegiance due to God? Those questions require truthful discernment. They cannot be replaced by the assumption that every demand made by one’s preferred ruler is lawful, or every demand made by an opponent is idolatrous. The narrated questioners’ flattery warns that even true words about God can be recruited into a dishonest public argument.

### A people wider than an empire

The responsorial Psalm supplies the widest horizon. All families of peoples are called to honour the Creator; their rulers and their achievements stand within creation. Augustine’s new song is the song of a new love that builds a worldwide house. It crosses political and ethnic boundaries without making one earthly people the owner of divine glory. His warning to the preacher is essential here: the messenger who draws glory toward himself repeats within religion the appropriation that the Psalm exposes in idolatry.[^21]

Paul’s thanksgiving shows what such a people looks like locally. The Thessalonians are not praised as a rival imperial administration. They are named as a community in God whose faith acts, love labours and hope endures. The independent apostolic reading thus contributes a different measure of public fruitfulness. Visible weakness and costly perseverance can reveal divine power; visible dominance need not. Chrysostom’s account of their endurance prevents the universal Psalm from becoming only a splendid vision above the difficulties of actual neighbours.

The acclamation then gives that community an outward task. Holding the word of life and shining within the world are a form of witness, not a claim to own the world’s light. Aquinas’s participated light makes service possible without self-deification. The Christian who has something true to say must receive truth before bearing it. This is the religious counterpart to receiving a civic good without declaring its agent a saviour.

### From shelter to a salvation power cannot buy

The Entrance contributes a necessary vulnerability. A person who must answer worldly claims may need courage, but courage does not make him invulnerable. The petition for hearing and shelter keeps fidelity from turning into a performance of self-sufficiency. Augustine’s Christ-and-body reading places the assembly’s confidence in a received mercy. The Collect directs the will toward sincere service, so that resistance to an excessive earthly claim does not become an excuse for a self-willed life.

At the altar, the Prayer over the Offerings adds purification. One can render a debt outwardly while inwardly seeking superiority. Even religious giving can become a means of control, as though possessing something to offer made the giver the master of the encounter. A petition for cleansing denies that conclusion without denying the gift’s worth. Worship returns created goods to God while acknowledging that the worshipper needs more than the ability to give them.

The Psalm Communion option carries this movement through the failure of armies and horses to provide final deliverance. Augustine’s nourishment of truth and Aquinas’s sacramental food name a need beyond military security. The believer may seek ordinary protection and necessary food; he cannot make them guarantees against death. Fear and hope keep earthly prudence joined to dependence upon mercy. The prayer’s bodily setting matters precisely because bodily strength is not enough.

If the Mark alternative is used, the contrast becomes Christ’s saving self-gift. The Son does not save by making the disciples better competitors for domination. Bede first locates his bloodshed within a gift for the world, then names willing belief in his explanation of “for many.” Universal lordship does not eliminate the person’s owed response. The one who receives liberation owes himself to the liberator. That connection between Bede’s believing reception and Aquinas’s return of the divine image belongs to the theological argument here; Bede’s own passage explains Mark’s ransom, not Matthew’s coin.[^22]

The Prayer after Communion finally orders present help toward eternal gifts. This releases earthly goods from an impossible task. They can be good without being final; they can be sought without being worshipped. A person can work honestly for the common life and still wait for a fulfilment that no ruler, institution or personal achievement can deliver. Such hope is not indifference to the world. It makes faithful action possible without demanding that history provide the last judgment upon its worth.

### The four senses

Literal.

: Cyrus is a real king used for Israel’s restoration while remaining unknowing; Jesus answers an actual tribute trap; Paul thanks God for an actual community’s active fidelity. The Psalms ask for protection and give the Creator glory. The prayers place sincere service, purification and sacramental benefit before God.

Allegorical.

: Cyrus’s limited liberation anticipates the true Christ without transferring ignorance to him. Augustine’s worldwide new song describes the gathered Church, and Aquinas’s image reading places the whole person under God’s claim. Where the Mark Communion option is used, the liberator is named through his self-gift and believing reception.

Moral.

: Render genuine duties truthfully, refuse to make impiety righteous by appealing to office, and return the whole self to God. The person who serves must also accept purification. Civic responsibility and worship meet in one life whose powers are received.

Anagogical.

: Equitable divine government and eternal gifts free hope from dependence on transient success. The broader Psalm contexts promise a fulfilment beyond death and incorruptible nourishment; their final horizon explains the worshipper’s patience without promising present immunity from suffering.

## The received gift becomes willing service {#lane-grace-and-willing-service}

A further difficulty lies inside the worshipper’s action. If God gives strength, grace and even the capacity to will, what remains for a person to do? Conversely, if faith works and love labours, why should thanksgiving credit God? Cyrus’s unknowing reception of power, Paul’s grateful recognition of a fruitful community, and the Collect’s petition concerning the will make these questions concrete. Aquinas explains how divine action reaches willing itself; Chrysostom explains why the resulting human life must be active, costly and sincere.

### A gift can be used without its giver being known

Cyrus is not merely an example of someone else’s politics. His condition reveals a possibility within every gifted life: capacity can exceed acknowledgment. Aquinas distinguishes the benefit received by the people from the recognition sought in Cyrus. A good can really occur through a person who does not yet know its source. That distinction prevents a community from treating its own usefulness as sufficient evidence of conversion. It may accomplish something valuable while misunderstanding whom its accomplishment serves.[^23]

Jerome deliberately brings this problem into ecclesial life. At the end of his exposition he applies it to the teacher given speech and wisdom. Such a teacher must refer the glory to the giver rather than imagine that his power of speaking originates in himself. Otherwise the rebuke addressed to Cyrus can become his own. Jerome’s move is searching because the teacher’s task is religious. Knowing how to speak about God does not automatically mean acknowledging God through the manner of one’s life.[^24]

The Entrance and Collect answer this condition through petition. The Entrance’s confident cry grows, in Augustine’s explanation, from prior divine hearing. It begins with help rather than achievement. The Collect asks concerning the will’s devotion, placing the very readiness to serve within dependence upon God. These prayers do not deny the assembly’s responsibility. They name its deepest need: not only tools for good work, but a will capable of doing that work sincerely.

### God acts within willing

The acclamation’s two short phrases stand within Philippians 2, where exhortation to work out salvation is immediately joined to God’s action in willing and doing. Aquinas treats that wider context in lectio 3. He excludes the notion that free choice can save without divine help. He also excludes the denial of free choice through fate or providence: God’s action takes place inwardly in the will, moving the person to act. Divine help is not a second human agent pushing the person aside.[^25]

His next distinction reaches the beginning of action. It is insufficient to assign willing to ourselves and leave God only the completion of what we independently decide. The text says both willing and doing. Nor is this help payment for merit earned before grace. Aquinas’s account therefore does more than add divine assistance to a programme whose source remains wholly our own. The good decision itself can be truly ours and truly the fruit of grace.

This is why petition for the will is intelligible. If willing were wholly independent, prayer could ask only for favourable circumstances. If willing were unreal, exhortation and response would have no subject. Aquinas preserves both dependence and agency. The consequence is neither self-congratulation nor passivity, but a readiness to receive help at the point where a person chooses. The Collect’s concern is not completed when a person has identified a duty; it reaches the manner in which he becomes willing to perform it.

Lectio 4 brings this inward account to the acclamation’s outward image. The saints shine by participation in the Word’s light. Receiving does not extinguish their activity any more than receiving light makes a visible body disappear. Their distinct lives become the place where what was received is seen. Holding Christ’s life-giving word supplies the source and direction of that visibility. This keeps the acclamation from being reduced to the advice to become impressive.

### Thanksgiving recognizes action instead of replacing it

Paul’s opening thanksgiving provides the clearest human instance. He credits God for a people whose faith has work, whose love bears labour and whose hope sustains endurance. Chrysostom explains that their conduct gives grounds for thanksgiving; thanksgiving in turn teaches humility. The two acts are not competitors. To acknowledge that a gift made an action possible is not to say the action did not occur. To recognize the action is not to say the gift was unnecessary.[^26]

Chrysostom makes the words costly. Steadfastness has to survive what might turn it aside. Love is not exhausted by an affectionate name; it endures labour. Hope has to remain during affliction rather than wait for affliction to end before calling itself hope. This explains why the community’s good cannot be measured merely by ease or prosperity. The gift is visible in sustained fidelity where favourable conditions are lacking.

Aquinas’s account of election adds a necessary order. Fruits manifest God’s choice; they do not retroactively purchase it. His alternative explanations of the Gospel’s power and fullness allow attention to the preacher’s conduct and the completeness of instruction as well as to signs. An assembly seeking evidence of grace should therefore attend to the ordinary but demanding fruits Paul names: truthfulness under pressure, charity that continues when inconvenient, and hope that survives delay. These are applications of the text’s criteria, not a method for declaring the hidden destiny of particular people.[^27]

### From truthful words to a sincere offering

The Gospel’s questioners supply a contrary example. They speak truth about Jesus while trying to entrap him through it. Correct words can be detached from a truthful intention. That disjunction gives the Collect’s sincere service a practical edge: the mouth can render honour while the will attempts to secure control. Jesus’ exposure of the trap calls the speaker into the truth he has spoken.

Aquinas’s mystical return of God’s image asks more than an outward transfer. The person must answer the giver. Yet the literal tribute question remains: a claim to inward sincerity cannot excuse injustice in practical obligations. This is where the first reading of the formulary corrects a danger in the second. Grace does not make the tax disappear into an allegory of the soul. The person transformed inwardly must act truthfully toward neighbours and within the civil life in which they live.

The responsorial Psalm gives that transformed response a voice. Augustine’s preacher declares God’s glory instead of his own. The shift is small in grammar and large in life: a gift becomes praise when the giver receives the acknowledgment. At the altar, the Prayer over the Offerings adds that sincere service still needs purification. A willingness once awakened is not a permanent exemption from mixed motives. Serving the mysteries becomes an occasion for the servant’s renewal rather than a reward for having already become complete.

### Mercy nourishes; the Son first gives himself

The Psalm Communion option gathers fear and hope around the Lord’s mercy. Aquinas’s explanation is exact: fear alone can lead toward despair; hope severed from fear can become presumption. Together they describe a person who takes both sin and mercy seriously. Bodily food sustains bodily life; the Word sustains spiritual life; sacramental nourishment belongs within the same account of God’s strengthening help. The recipient does not need to pretend to be full before being fed.[^28]

Augustine’s patient soul is therefore a fitting companion to Paul’s enduring community. Need can persist while mercy is real. Neither author gives a warrant for blaming every sufferer for deficient faith. The hope in the Psalm reaches incorruptible nourishment; it cannot be tested simply by asking whether present circumstances have become comfortable. This makes perseverance a response to a promised good, rather than a denial of actual hunger.

The Mark option sharpens the order of gift, reception and imitation. Bede’s explanation begins with the Son taking the servant’s form and shedding his blood for the world. His gloss of “for many” then names those willing to believe. The recipient’s act has a specified object: the saving gift of Christ. Believing is not self-redemption, and Christian service does not supply a missing ransom equal to Christ’s own. Before the disciple gives himself, he receives the one who has given himself.[^29]

Bede’s reference to willing belief does not assign an independent origin to faith’s will. Aquinas’s account of God’s inward action supplies that distinct question’s answer. Chrysostom’s exposition of Matthew’s parallel then develops the consequence for conduct: Christ’s abasement unmakes the disciples’ ambition. A person can imitate the pattern of service without becoming another redeemer. The priority of Christ’s act is what gives imitation its Christian shape, rather than leaving it a contest in moral heroism.

The Prayer after Communion keeps this life open. Present participation prepares for eternal gifts; it does not convert progress into possession of the end. The assembly can leave with work to do because grace has not made work pointless. It can leave without claiming mastery because its work is not the source of salvation. Readiness, purification and growth belong to a single received life whose fulfilment still calls it onward.

### The four senses

Literal.

: Cyrus receives strength without corresponding knowledge; Paul recognizes an active congregation and thanks God for it; Philippians joins received life to visible conduct. The prayers ask for the will’s disposition, the servant’s purification and sacramental progress. The Gospel exposes truthful language used with an untruthful purpose.

Allegorical.

: Christ’s self-gift gives life to a body whose light comes from him. Augustine’s Christ-and-Church prayer, Aquinas’s participated light and sacramental nourishment, and Bede’s believing reception locate the community’s action in a prior gift. Cyrus remains a limited type, not a Christ who fails to know the Father.

Moral.

: Acknowledge the giver within one’s abilities, choose and carry out good work with received help, and let service expose what still needs purification. Hold fear together with hope. The virtues Paul names become strenuous love and sustained fidelity, without making effort a purchase of election.

Anagogical.

: Hope acts toward a good not yet fully possessed. Present nourishment sustains patient life on the way to eternal gifts; progress does not end the pilgrimage. Final fulfilment gives present work a direction beyond its immediate success or reward.

## One life returned to God

Both readings reject self-sufficiency, but they answer different questions. The first asks what earthly authority and created goods can rightly claim. Jerome’s unknowing Cyrus and Chrysostom’s limit upon tribute distinguish a genuine service from a claim to divine status. Its practical fruit is ordered allegiance: the freedom to fulfil civic duties without making them absolute.

The second asks how a person becomes capable of such fidelity. Aquinas’s inward divine action and Chrysostom’s labouring community show why gratitude and effort belong together. Its practical fruit is willing response: the freedom to act without claiming to be the origin of the good one does. Jerome’s warning to the gifted teacher brings Cyrus’s difficulty into the worshipper’s own life.

Either reading becomes incomplete when isolated. An account of obligations can leave the will unchanged; an account of inward renewal can lose sight of real public duties. The coin remains a coin, and the person who renders it remains God’s image. The same person needs shelter, a sincere will, purification, nourishment and hope. The Psalm Communion option emphasizes mercy in need; the Mark option emphasizes the saving gift received in faith. The final prayer gives both readings their horizon: present service tends toward a good beyond the power of the servant to produce.

## Appendix: Scriptural Date and Location {#historical-appendix}

Composition dates concern the writing of a work. Narrated events, received attributions, superscription settings and Christian prophetic referents answer different questions. The historical Catholic sources below preserve alternative judgments rather than a single agreed chronology. The modern NABRE introductions supply the identified critical horizons for the Psalms and Isaiah. Neither the date of the Sunday nor the date of a Missal is a date for its biblical passages.

### Psalms of shelter, mercy and universal praise {#history-psalms}

**Entrance: cf. Psalm 16:6,8 (Vulgate; Hebrew 17).** **Historical setting** – Occasion date unresolved. **Composition**: Before c. 165 B.C.

The Psalm’s received title names a prayer of David but identifies no particular occasion. Enemies and sheltering wings do not date a battle, flight or place of composition. The place and occasion of this prayer remain unresolved. Augustine’s Christ-and-body speaker is an interpretation of its voice, not a newly dated event in David’s biography.

**Communion option: cf. Psalm 32:18–19 (Vulgate; Hebrew 33).** **Traditional attribution** – disputed: David (reign in the usual chronology), B.C. 1055–1015. **Historical setting** – Occasion date unresolved. **Composition**: Before c. 165 B.C.

The Davidic heading in the Douay and Clementine traditions likewise names no event. The reign era in Corbett’s *King David* is explicitly his “usual chronology”; his opening account also acknowledges later datings influenced by Assyrian evidence. That disputed reference era dates the attributed author’s kingship, not this Psalm’s composition. Israel and Judah are its broad geographic frame, not a recovered room in which the poem was written.[^30]

For all three Psalms the common latest boundary comes from the NABRE Psalms introduction, which states the uncertainty of dating individual Psalms. It does not assert that they were written together or precisely at the displayed boundary. Their individual writing places are likewise uncertain.[^31]

**Responsorial Psalm: Hebrew 96 / Vulgate 95.** **Superscription setting**: The raising of the second Temple by Zorobabel, B.C. 537. **Composition**: Before c. 165 B.C. **Prophetic referent** – disputed: The Nativity of Our Lord, B.C. 3; The Nativity of Our Lord, c. B.C. 7.

The Greek and Vulgate superscription refers to a house built after captivity; the Hebrew title does not carry the same superscription. The historical association above is with Zorobabel’s raising of Jerusalem’s second Temple, as described at the opening of the encyclopedia’s Temple article. The superscription does not securely distinguish that raising from the later completion. Its associated date is therefore not a demonstrated composition date or an unambiguous date of dedication.[^32]

The Nativity alternatives concern the Psalm’s Christian prophetic referent. Gerard’s *General Chronology* reports a correction attributed to interpreters of Dionysius; James Howlett’s *Biblical Chronology* weighs Herod and infancy evidence and reaches a probable date. Both answers remain disputed. The Psalm itself gives neither a Nativity year nor a locality for the birth. Augustine’s Christian reading and John Paul II’s account of the cross therefore belong to reception, not to the dating of an ancient sanctuary scene.[^33]

### Isaiah’s address to Cyrus {#history-isaiah}

**Traditional attribution**: Isaias (ministry in Souvay’s traditional account), B.C. 740–701. **Prophecy given**: The prophetic activity behind Isaiah 40-55 in the NABRE introduction, toward the end of the Babylonian exile.

The traditional ministry horizon in Souvay’s *Isaias* concerns the prophet in Judah and Jerusalem and the attribution of the book to him. It is not a separately established date of an encounter between Isaiah and Cyrus. The NABRE introduction treats the activity behind chapters 40–55 within the late Babylonian exile. That is a different account of the prophetic horizon, not the same event shifted within one scheme. Nor does it imply that the final arrangement of every word of the book was completed at once.[^34]

Babylonian exile supplies the critical horizon; Jerusalem and its Temple are the destination of the promised restoration in Isaiah 44:26–28. Neither answer above dates Cyrus’s capture of Babylon or a decree. No such year is needed to identify the appointed oracle’s argument. The distinction between a named ruler and the Lord who calls him survives both source frameworks.

### Matthew’s temple controversy {#history-matthew}

**Event** – No narrated-event date in the chronology corpus. **Composition** – disputed: c. A.D. 38–45; c. A.D. 40–42; A.D. 40–45; c. A.D. 60–68; c. A.D. 64–67; c. A.D. 50.

Matthew 21:23 and the continuing controversies establish the Jerusalem temple setting of the tribute question. Its exact narrated date remains unresolved here. The composition alternatives date the Gospel, not this exchange. The temple is the scene of the narrative; it does not identify the writer’s place.

Jacquier’s Matthew article reports competing early testimony and the subsequent dates drawn from it. He notes uncertainty in interpreting Irenaeus’s account. The first five alternatives above belong to that discussion, which distinguishes early reported dates from a later proposed interval and another interpretation centred on the preaching and deaths of Peter and Paul. Durand’s *The New Testament* article assigns the sixth alternative, about A.D. 50, to an Aramaic original; he leaves the date of its Greek rendering undetermined. Overlap between intervals does not make them independent confirmations of one exact year.[^35]

### Mark’s journey toward Jerusalem {#history-mark}

**Composition** – disputed: A.D. 38–40; A.D. 40; A.D. 43; A.D. 50–67; A.D. 60–70; After Matthew, and not as late as the destruction of Jerusalem.

Mark 10:32–45 places the teaching on service and ransom on the ascent toward Jerusalem, after the Passion prediction and the request for seats of honour. Verse 46 reaches Jericho after the saying. The precise roadside locality of the teaching is not named. None of the composition dates above supplies an event date for that journey or for the Passion.

MacRory’s Mark article reports early subscriptions and chronicles, considers their possible derivation and weighs conflicting testimony toward a broader preferred interval. Its reported early dates and its preferred interval remain distinguishable answers. Durand’s *The New Testament* article gives the other numerical interval. The Pontifical Biblical Commission’s responsa V–VI of 26 June 1912 supply relative order and a limit before Jerusalem’s destruction or the beginning of its siege, not a numerical year. A relative answer must remain relative.[^36]

### Philippians: writer and recipients {#history-philippians}

**Composition** – disputed: A.D. 61; A.D. 62–64.

The two historical composition schemes give different dates within a Roman-captivity account. Prat’s schedule supplies the first; the encyclopedia’s separate Philippians article supplies the second and argues for Rome rather than Caesarea, while discussing the stage of captivity. Rome is the writer’s location in that account, Philippi the recipients’ city. The account is not a claim that every scholarly proposal places the writing at Rome. The short acclamation does not date its own delivery as an exhortation.[^37]

### Thessalonians: thanksgiving sent from Corinth {#history-thessalonians}

**Composition** – disputed: A.D. 52; A.D. 53–54.

Prat’s schedule supplies one traditional scheme; Drum’s article supplies the other. Drum argues for Corinth by joining the letter’s three senders, Acts 18:5 and the report brought by Timothy. Thessalonica is the receiving community’s city. The writing date does not automatically date every act of faith and love remembered in the thanksgiving. Drum also discusses a critical alternative outside the returned comparison; it is not silently made a third date here.[^38]

The Collect, Prayer over the Offerings and Prayer after Communion are composed prayers with no appointed biblical citation. They receive no biblical chronology from a resemblance to a verse. The Scripture references of the Missal antiphons identify adaptations or an excerpt; their dates concern the underlying passages, not the composition of those liturgical adaptations.
