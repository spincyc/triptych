# The Four Last Things: The Truth of a Life

## Before Jesus Christ

At death we shall stand before Jesus Christ. He will judge the life we have actually lived: what we loved, what we did, what we refused, and what we made of his gifts. The judgment will reach the hidden heart as surely as the public deed. No favorable account of ourselves can replace the truth; no forgotten act of fidelity will be lost. The Lord before whom we shall stand is already calling us to repentance. The time in which we can answer him is being given to us now.

The Creed identifies the Judge before we attempt to imagine the judgment. He is the Son who became man for our salvation, suffered under Pontius Pilate, was crucified, rose from the dead, and ascended to the Father. The one who will come in glory is the one who has already come in humility. His earthly life teaches us whom we shall meet. We have heard him summon sinners, receive the penitent, rebuke hypocrisy, feed the multitude, and give himself for his enemies. His judgment belongs to this same Lord, with this same holiness and love.

St. Cyril of Jerusalem begins his catechesis on Christ’s return by placing the two comings beside one another. At the first, the Lord was wrapped in swaddling clothes; at the second, he comes in glory. At the first, he endured the Cross; at the second, angels attend him. The Savior who submitted to judgment will himself judge (*Catechetical Lectures* XV.1–2). Cyril’s contrast directs Christian attention toward the full mystery of Christ. Bethlehem, Calvary, and the final judgment belong to one saving work. The gentleness with which the Lord entered the world and the majesty with which he will bring history to its end reveal the same divine fidelity.

We therefore contemplate judgment most truthfully in the presence of the crucifix. There we see what sin costs and how God loves sinners. The Crucified does not regard our betrayals as trifles. He bears our redemption through obedience unto death. Nor does he wait indifferently for our failure. He gives himself so that we may live. The thought of his judgment becomes fruitful when it brings us to this personal recognition: the one to whom I must give an account has shed his blood for me. Every evasion of conversion concerns a love already offered.

In John’s Gospel, Christ joins his authority to judge with his power to give life. The Father has entrusted judgment to the Son; the dead will hear his voice; those in the tombs will come forth to the resurrection of life or of judgment (Jn 5:21–29). The Judge possesses what he commands. He can raise the dead, and he can raise the sinner from spiritual death. Hearing his word now is already an encounter with the Lord whose voice will finally reach every grave. We prepare for that last summons by receiving the word which presently summons us to faith and obedience.

St. Thomas explains that Christ exercises judgment also in his humanity, as the head of the human family he redeems. Among the reasons Thomas gives is his kinship with us: God judges human beings through the man Christ, who shares our nature and knows our weakness without sharing our sin (*Summa theologiae* III, q. 59, a. 2). The Incarnation supplies a profound ground for confidence. Our Judge is not ignorant of hunger, weariness, sorrow, abandonment, or temptation. He understands the conditions under which we struggled to obey. His understanding is perfect, and therefore his judgment is perfectly true.

Thomas also relates Christ’s judicial authority to the merit of his Passion. The one who fought for divine justice and was unjustly condemned is fittingly the Judge of all (III, q. 59, a. 3). Before his tribunal, earthly prestige loses the power to settle a person’s worth. Those who once appeared to triumph over the innocent Lord will not supply the last word about him. Neither will the world’s judgments supply the last word about his servants. Christ’s exaltation assures us that truth is stronger than the verdict which violence, fear, or expediency can obtain.

This confidence cannot become familiarity without reverence. We cannot appeal to the mercy of Jesus while deliberately reserving a part of life in which he may not rule. His friendship seeks our conversion. The secret dishonesty, the cherished resentment, the impurity protected from correction, the refusal to fulfill a known duty: these are places where the coming judgment addresses the present will. To bring them before Christ is already to cease defending their concealment. We do not have to establish our innocence before approaching him. We must consent to his truth and ask to be made clean.

Annual meditation on judgment can begin here and remain here long enough to become personal. We are not merely reviewing an event which will happen to mankind. We shall meet Jesus Christ. The years already spent cannot be lived again, but they can be brought to him in repentance and thanksgiving. The coming year cannot be possessed in advance, but its first act of obedience can be made today. The Lord who will judge the whole life is present to the life still being lived. His word is already worthy of the submission that every creature will finally acknowledge.

## The Judgment at Death

Death ends the earthly time in which we can accept or reject the grace of Christ. At that moment each person receives eternal retribution in the immortal soul: heaven immediately, heaven through purification, or hell. This is the particular judgment (CCC 1021–1022). Its particularity means that this person, with this actual history of faith and works, is referred to Christ. We shall not disappear into the fate of a crowd. Neither a family’s piety nor an age’s corruption can supply another person’s answer in place of our own.

The Gospel gives this truth a piercing immediacy. The rich man and Lazarus die, and their condition beyond death is already different (Lk 16:19–31). The penitent thief receives Christ’s promise of paradise while the Cross still stands before the spectators (Lk 23:39–43). The outward manner of dying does not disclose the inward judgment. One man possessed comfort and recognition; another lay neglected; a third died under a criminal sentence. Christ sees the soul. The earthly observer sees fragments of the story and may mistake those fragments for the whole.

We should receive both the urgency and the consolation of this teaching. Urgency follows because the time for conversion has an end. Consolation follows because our life reaches the one competent to judge it. Our destiny does not depend upon whether survivors praise us adequately, understand us fully, or preserve our memory. A holy person may die unrecognized; a great wrong may remain concealed from everyone near the deathbed. Nothing is thereby concealed from Christ. He knows the final act of repentance and the last freely maintained refusal, the interior offering and the merely outward appearance of peace.

The soul which receives this judgment is our own continuing self. The Church teaches that a spiritual element endowed with consciousness and will survives death; she calls it the soul. Bodily resurrection remains future, and concerns the whole person (CDF, *Letter on Certain Questions Concerning Eschatology*, 1979, nos. 2–5). There is continuity between the person who lived, the soul judged at death, and the human being raised at the last day. Death does not cancel personal responsibility or turn the person into a memory belonging only to others.

The definitiveness of judgment gives weight to ordinary choices. During life we can repent of evil, fall from good, grow in charity, or become more deeply entangled in vice. At death this earthly probation ends. Thomas expressly joins the completion of a life to the possibility of judging it as a whole (III, q. 59, a. 5). Until its end, the history can still contain a conversion. A sinner need not continue as he has begun. A person who has served God faithfully must still persevere. The teaching directs the living toward the response that remains possible, without pretending that tomorrow belongs to us.

Postponement therefore concerns more than a date in the calendar. To say that one will repent later can be a way of choosing the present sin again. The promise of a future confession may quiet the conscience while leaving the will unchanged. Each repetition makes the familiar evil easier to protect, and each refusal of grace can make the next refusal less noticeable. God’s patience gives room for repentance; we misuse it when we make it part of a plan for delaying repentance. The uncertainty of our last day strips that plan of its imagined security.

Yet the finality of death does not authorize despair over years badly spent. The life still open before Christ remains open to his mercy. The thief could not return to the victims of every past wrong, begin a long course of penance, or demonstrate years of amendment. He turned toward the Savior beside him. His conversion teaches the greatness of mercy without making delay wise. We may trust the Lord who received him; we may not presume upon an opportunity which has not been promised to us. The grace available in the present is sufficient reason to turn now.

St. Alphonsus makes this practical turn in the twenty-fourth consideration of *Preparation for Death*. After contemplating the judgment, he urges the reader to settle the account of life while repentance is possible (consideration XXIV, point 3). His point is personal and immediate. The future judgment should not remain material for religious interest while an acknowledged sin is left untouched. The sinner can examine himself, confess, accept penance, and begin to amend. The fear awakened by judgment is meant to move toward these acts, where Christ’s mercy reaches an actual person with an actual need.

There is also a daily freedom in remembering the particular judgment. The opinions surrounding us can neither save nor finally condemn us. We may therefore refuse a dishonest advantage even when everyone expects us to take it. We may accept obscurity when obedience brings no praise. We may acknowledge a fault without defending an entire reputation. The measure of the life is being held by Christ. To live before him is to begin escaping the need to justify ourselves before every passing tribunal, while becoming more willing to give the honest account that charity and justice require.

## The Resurrection and the Whole of History

Christ will return in glory, the dead will rise, and all nations will stand before him. The last judgment manifests the truth of each life in the completed history of the world (Mt 25:31–46; CCC 1038–1040). The particular judgment has already fixed the soul’s destiny. At the last judgment that destiny reaches its bodily and public consummation. The one who loved or sinned through a body will stand before the Lord as a risen human being. The history in which the person acted will have reached its end.

Our bodies belong to the truth being judged. Through them we fed another person or withheld food, spoke courageously or lied, protected the weak or injured them. Knees bent in worship, hands which labored, mouths which confessed the faith, and bodies wounded by persecution belong to the lives Christ will raise. The resurrection brings the whole person into the fulfillment of God’s judgment. Salvation is personal enough to include the body which served, suffered, and died. The final judgment does not leave the material history of love behind as though only intentions had mattered.

Cyril presses this hope upon hearers whose social position might tempt them to imagine themselves overlooked. The poor laborer in the field, the person grinding at the mill, the servant, and the sick person in bed will all be found. Christ does not prefer the landowner to the laborer or the learned to the simple (XV.23). Cyril then names ordinary fidelities: prayer, almsgiving, fasting, chastity, and a marriage faithfully kept. These things are known to the Lord. The Church’s expectation of judgment gives eternal seriousness to lives which scarcely enter the public record.

Thomas explains the need for a final judgment by considering all that remains unfinished when an individual dies. A reputation may remain false. Children continue to live. Teaching continues to influence those who receive it. The body can be honored or dishonored. Projects to which a person devoted himself persist or collapse. These continuing relations belong to a life even after its earthly choosing has ended (III, q. 59, a. 5). God will judge them in the completed whole. The final disclosure reveals a truth no observer situated inside history could fully gather.

Thomas is equally explicit that the dead acquire no new merit or demerit through these later effects. Their state as regards the soul is already fixed. The continued history accompanies the reward or punishment; it does not begin another period of personal choice (a. 5, reply 1). This qualification protects the meaning of responsibility. A parent’s child remains a free person. A later reader may twist an author’s meaning. A good undertaking may be corrupted by successors. God knows precisely what belonged to the deceased’s intention, action, negligence, and foreseeable influence, and what belonged to the new choices of other people.

The doctrine consequently teaches care without demanding impossible control. A parent cannot guarantee a child’s holiness, but can teach faithfully and repent of harmful example. A teacher cannot govern every future reader, but can refuse to spread what he knows is false. A person who has injured a reputation cannot follow every retelling, but can correct the lie where he has power to do so. We are responsible for the fidelity given to us, including prudent attention to consequences. We are not the providence which governs every consequence. The last judgment belongs to God because only he knows the whole.

St. Augustine opens Book XX of *The City of God* by confessing that Christ will judge the living and the dead. He then dwells on the bewildering contrasts of present life: the innocent condemned, the guilty released, the useful person dying early, the wrongdoer prospering, the just person enduring illness (XX.1–2). These contrasts are familiar to anyone who has watched suffering closely. Augustine does not tell us that prosperity measures virtue or that affliction proves guilt. At the last judgment, the justice of God’s judgments will be manifest, including those whose meaning is now hidden from us.

This promise enters the Christian struggle to trust providence. We sometimes demand an explanation before we will consent to fidelity. We want to know why a prayer was not answered as expected, why a cruel person retained influence, or why a good work seemed to fail. Many such questions will remain with us until death. Faith can entrust them to the Lord without calling evil good or ceasing to resist it. His final judgment assures us that the truth is not being lost because we cannot yet see how it will be vindicated.

It also teaches us to distrust the triumph of an apparent success. A reputation maintained by fear may survive for decades. A household may preserve its respectable appearance by making one member bear everyone’s blame. A public achievement may depend upon dishonesty hidden from its beneficiaries. Duration and admiration cannot turn these things into justice. Conversely, work which appeared fruitless may have preserved another person’s courage, a concealed prayer may have sustained someone in temptation, and an apparently insignificant kindness may have borne abundant fruit. Christ judges the truth of these acts, including what remained hidden from those who performed them.

The last judgment will reveal God’s justice and the wisdom of his providence. That certainty gives the Church both patience and resolve. We may work for justice without believing that history must be forced into completion by our own strength. We may endure an unresolved wrong without consenting to it. We may die leaving tasks unfinished and trust that the Lord has not forgotten what they concerned. The world will reach its conclusion in the presence of Christ. Nothing good will need falsehood to defend it, and no evil will become innocent because its last earthly witness has died.

## The Heart Laid Open

The Lord will bring to light what is hidden and disclose the counsels of hearts. St. Paul says this while speaking of the stewardship entrusted to the apostles. He does not regard human approval as the final court, and he adds that an untroubled self-assessment does not justify him: the Lord judges (1 Cor 4:1–5). Conscience matters profoundly, but our present knowledge of ourselves is incomplete. We can miss motives, excuse habits, forget duties, or attach excessive guilt to something that was never freely chosen. Christ’s judgment reaches the truth beyond these distortions.

Augustine reads the opened books of Revelation 20 as the disclosure of God’s commands and of the deeds done or omitted in response. He understands the book of each life through the divine power which brings its acts before conscience (*City of God* XX.14). His account draws attention to the completeness of God’s knowledge. Forgetting does not undo an act, and remembering it with a convenient explanation does not determine its meaning. Before Christ we shall know ourselves without the evasions which now make repentance seem unnecessary.

Such truth concerns interior acts as well as exterior behavior. An outwardly generous gift can become an instrument of domination; an apparently gracious silence can conceal consent to slander; diligent service can be mingled with a hunger for recognition. These mixtures call for purification. They do not require us to deny every good in an imperfect work, but they ask us to surrender the part we keep defending. The Lord can distinguish the love that was real from the vanity that attached itself to it. Examination becomes more honest when we ask him to do that work within us now.

There are also hidden victories. A person restrained a cruel reply which no one knew he wanted to utter. Another fulfilled a duty through grief without making grief an excuse to abandon it. Someone prayed for an enemy while still feeling the wound. Someone resisted a temptation that had long mastered him. No spectator may know the effort involved. Cyril tells his hearers that their prayer, alms, and fidelity are recorded before God (XV.23). To remember this is to let service be service, even when gratitude and understanding never arrive.

God’s knowledge includes the unequal histories in which freedom is exercised. Ignorance, fear, compulsion, habit, and other psychological or social factors can diminish responsibility, sometimes even nullifying it (CCC 1735). The objective wrongness of an act and the personal culpability of its agent are distinct. The Judge knows every relevant circumstance without confusion. He knows the wound we did not choose, the light we truly lacked, the assistance we received, and the occasions on which we freely resisted what we knew to be good. No human estimate can achieve this completeness.

That truth should make our examination humble in two directions. We should not excuse a known sin by assembling a life story in which every wrong becomes inevitable. Nor should we claim a certainty of guilt which ignores involuntary suffering and the limits of freedom. A history can explain why a temptation is strong without making consent to it good. A strong emotion can accompany a holy act without becoming a sin. We bring the actual act, intention, circumstances, and degree of consent to a conscience formed by truth, receiving pastoral help where our judgment has become confused.

Conscience itself needs formation. It must be obeyed in its certain judgment, yet it can be mistaken, and negligence in seeking the truth can be culpable. Invincible ignorance has a different moral character from a refusal to learn what one should know (CCC 1790–1794). A person may carefully avoid a teaching because learning it would disturb a profitable arrangement. Another may sincerely struggle with a question and seek correction. Both can say that they followed conscience, but the phrase does not make the two histories identical. God sees the willingness or unwillingness to receive light.

Paul’s warning in Romans makes the danger sharper. A person condemns another while doing the same things, as though the ability to recognize evil gave immunity from its judgment (Rom 2:1–5). The apostle addresses the judge who has turned a true moral judgment into protection for an unrepentant self. We can fall into that position through indignation which never returns to our own conduct. Another person’s failure becomes absorbing; our own begins to seem small beside it. The truth we see outside ourselves should instead make us more willing to receive its searching light within.

Paul then asks what we have made of God’s patience. Its purpose is to lead us to repentance. We can misread the absence of immediate punishment as approval, or take repeated opportunities to return as evidence that returning can safely be postponed. The years in which a hidden fault has gone undiscovered are still years lived before God. His kindness has accompanied them with a call to conversion. When conscience awakens after a long evasion, we may receive that awakening as mercy, even though acknowledging the truth is painful.

The same chapter places conscience within God’s impartial judgment. Those who possess the revealed law are accountable for what they have received; those without that privilege still bear witness to a law written in their hearts. Their thoughts accuse or defend them, and God judges human secrets through Jesus Christ (Rom 2:12–16). Religious knowledge therefore gives us no warrant for imagining that the Lord has overlooked the interior life of someone beyond our acquaintance. He reaches the person we know only by a description. He also judges the person concealed beneath our own religious description.

For us, this demands a distinction between hearing the truth and doing it. A Catholic can have heard a commandment many times while continuing to arrange life around its evasion. The familiarity of the words may even make their demand harder to hear. Paul returns us to obedience: the law is given to govern conduct. We ought to be grateful when a Gospel already known suddenly becomes uncomfortable, because its discomfort may reveal the particular surrender we have resisted. The judgment of conscience can then become a threshold of repentance, through which we return to Christ’s judgment in the sacrament of mercy.

Luke places the demand for an account beside the gifts entrusted to the servant: more is required where more has been given (Lk 12:47–48). We should apply this first to ourselves. The faith we were taught, the sacraments available to us, the patient priest, the faithful parent, the friend who corrected us, the leisure for prayer, and the opportunities for service are gifts. They call for gratitude and a response. We cannot use them merely to compare ourselves favorably with those who received less help. Religious knowledge increases the obligation to let known truth govern life.

Cyril’s warning about the secret heart ends by directing his hearers toward faithful action. No absence of witnesses hides a deed from God; the conscience will bear witness; therefore baptismal cleansing calls for a life kept clean and a fruitful use of what God entrusts (XV.25–26). The movement is from disclosure to conversion. We do not need to reconstruct every hidden motive with perfect psychological clarity before obeying a known commandment. We can confess the sin we know, relinquish the excuse we recognize, and ask for light concerning what remains confused. The Lord’s perfect knowledge permits honest beginnings.

## The Words We Shall Answer For

Christ expressly includes our speech in the judgment. After speaking of the tree known by its fruit and the mouth speaking from the heart’s abundance, he warns that we shall render an account for every idle word (Mt 12:33–37). Words are acts by which the inward life enters the world. Through them we acknowledge God, instruct a child, bind ourselves by a promise, comfort the afflicted, accuse the innocent, or persuade another person to sin. The tongue’s work can pass quickly and leave consequences which last for years.

The warning occurs after Christ has delivered a man from demonic affliction and his opponents have attributed the work to the prince of demons. Their charge interprets a manifest good through a will set against its author. Chrysostom attends closely to this contradiction (*Homilies on Matthew* XLII.1). They do not deny that the sufferer has been healed, yet they malign the healer. The scene exposes a form of falsehood more deliberate than an error in information: a refusal to let another person’s good work disturb a settled hostility.

We can recognize a smaller version of that refusal in our own speech. Someone we dislike acts generously, and we immediately supply a selfish motive. A person we resent tells the truth, and we find a way to make the truth sound contemptible. We may possess no evidence for the explanation, but it preserves the judgment we prefer. Such habits teach the heart to resist gratitude. Before Christ, the question will include what we did with the good we saw, and whether envy made us unwilling to acknowledge it.

Chrysostom explains the idle word here through falsehood and unjust accusation; he also records the interpretation which includes empty, coarse, or immodest speech. His central point is the justice of the account: a person must answer for what he himself has said (XLII.1). The warning should make us attend to the moral act within an apparently passing remark. We chose to repeat the accusation, to exaggerate the failure, to make the insinuation, or to obtain laughter at someone else’s expense. The briefness of the act does not make its choice unreal.

James describes the disproportion between the tongue’s size and its power through a bit, a rudder, and a little fire which sets a great wood ablaze (Jas 3:1–6). A few words can direct a household’s judgment of an absent relative or establish the way a community speaks about a vulnerable person. When the story spreads, its first speaker may lose control of it. The consequences illustrate why judgment reaches the whole history of a deed. A statement we have ceased to remember may still be teaching others to despise someone whom God has commanded them to love.

This power also belongs to written speech. A letter, a message, or a public comment can preserve the moment in which anger outran truth and charity. Ease of transmission does not remove the speaker from the act. We can send an accusation to many people before taking the time to learn whether it is true, then treat the resulting injury as something caused by the audience. A sober preparation for judgment includes accepting authorship of our own words and the foreseeable harm we willingly set in motion.

James then turns from the tongue’s power to its contradiction. With it we bless God and curse human beings made in his likeness (Jas 3:9–12). The same mouth approaches worship and tears at the neighbor. The apostle makes that division intolerable to Christian conscience. Our praise of the Creator calls us to reverence for the creature. A person who has joined the Church’s prayer cannot regard contemptuous conversation afterward as belonging to a separate life, untouched by the worship just offered. God hears both, and grace seeks to bring them into one obedience.

The Church gives precise names to several ways in which speech violates this obedience. Rash judgment assumes another’s moral fault without sufficient foundation; detraction needlessly exposes real faults to people unaware of them; calumny injures reputation through falsehood (CCC 2477). These distinctions help us examine ourselves honestly. Saying that a story is true does not by itself justify passing it on. Saying that we merely repeated what we heard does not remove the responsibility to care whether our words injure someone unjustly. Truth and charity concern both the content and the purpose of communication.

There are occasions when truth must be spoken to protect someone or fulfill a duty of justice. A witness may need to report wrongdoing, a parent to confront a danger, or a person entrusted with care to seek help from someone able to intervene. Such speech has an end which idle accusation lacks: the good of persons and the right ordering of a real responsibility. The Catechism requires charity and respect for truth in deciding what should be communicated (CCC 2488–2489). Prudence seeks the appropriate person, manner, and extent; it does not turn concern for reputation into permission to conceal a continuing injury.

The temptation to silence can therefore be as searching as the temptation to gossip. We may dislike conflict, fear losing approval, or benefit from leaving an injustice undisturbed. If an actual duty requires us to speak, a pleasant appearance of peace can conceal a refusal of courage. Christ’s judgment includes omissions of truthful witness as well as malicious words. Preparation asks us to submit speech and silence alike to charity, accepting that the right word may cost something and that the satisfying word may need to remain unspoken.

Chrysostom gives a particular consolation to the slandered. They will not have to answer for the evil another person falsely says about them; the slanderer will answer for the wrong he has committed (XLII.2). An accusation cannot transfer its falsehood into the victim’s soul. This does not make the injury painless or forbid a truthful defense. It establishes where the final account rests. We may labor to correct the false report without making every hostile voice the master of our interior life. The Lord has never mistaken the accusation for the truth.

For the person who has harmed another by speech, repentance reaches the damage done. Forgiveness leaves a duty of reparation where justice requires it (CCC 2487). We may need to correct a false statement before those who heard it, withdraw an unfair judgment, or acknowledge that we spoke without knowing. The necessary repair may be embarrassing precisely because the original remark enhanced our standing. That cost belongs to the return to truth. We cannot always undo the whole effect, but we can cease lending our authority to the wrong and seek sound guidance about what further repair is possible.

Good speech also enters the account of a life. A patient explanation can spare someone from years of misunderstanding. An honest acknowledgment of fault can give a child permission to repent without despair. A faithful witness to Christ can remain in another person’s memory when the speaker has long forgotten it. The good treasure of the heart, of which Jesus speaks, bears fruit through such words. Vigilance over the tongue seeks this fruit: a mouth ready to bless, teach, encourage, confess, and defend the truth in charity.

Chrysostom follows his treatment of harmful speech by directing attention back to personal examination. He imagines conscience being summoned to consider its own acts instead of escaping into accusations against others (XLII.3). The practical wisdom is exact. We can spend an evening rehearsing another person’s failure and never notice the anger or vanity with which we rehearsed it. A quiet return to our own account breaks that diversion. What did we choose to say, what did we know, and what good or evil did we intend? These matters can be brought to confession while amendment remains possible.

The answer will sometimes require more than a resolution to speak carefully. If resentment continually supplies our conversation, the resentment itself needs conversion. If we must always appear knowledgeable, the vanity which makes us pretend knowledge needs surrender. If contempt wins us a place among friends, we may have to accept the loss of that approval. Christ’s image of the tree directs attention to the root. Grace can make the heart more truthful and charitable, so that restraint grows into a different manner of speaking.

Annual recollection gives us an opportunity to hear familiar speech anew. The expressions we excuse as our temperament, the stories we habitually tell, and the names we pronounce with contempt belong to the life which will be judged. So do the words of gratitude and reconciliation we have delayed. The Lord who knows every word can forgive sins of speech and teach us a better use of his gift. We may begin by letting the next necessary word be truthful, the next correction be charitable, and the next praise of God be joined to a sincere desire to honor his image in our neighbor.

## Faith Made Living by Charity

The life judged by Christ is a life summoned and sustained by grace. Ephesians describes sinners as dead in their offenses and God as rich in mercy. He makes them alive in Christ, gives salvation through faith, and creates them anew for good works (Eph 2:1–10). The order matters. We do not first produce a life worthy of God and then ask him to recognize it. He comes to us in our need, forgives, gives new life, and enables a response which was impossible without his gift. The works he judges belong within this history of grace.

The same passage which excludes boasting also excludes spiritual idleness. We are God’s workmanship, created in Christ for the good works in which he intends us to walk. The gift takes possession of our freedom by healing it for love. It teaches the dishonest person to tell the truth, the resentful person to forgive, the indifferent person to serve, and the fearful person to confess Christ. Grace reaches the will and makes obedience possible. A life touched by it is called to become different in its actual conduct.

St. James speaks with equal directness. A brother or sister lacks clothing and daily food. Someone offers peaceful words but withholds the necessities of life. James asks what such words accomplish and calls faith without works dead (Jas 2:14–26). His example exposes the distance between a profession and the love it requires. We can name a person’s need, feel religious concern about it, and remain unwilling to give what is ours to give. Living faith crosses that distance. It receives Christ’s command as something to be done.

Judgment according to works therefore concerns the reality of our response to God. Good intentions which never become obedience cannot indefinitely stand in place of the obedience they promise. Nor does a Christian life consist in an external record severed from faith and charity. The same act may be undertaken for vanity, fear, convenience, or the love of God. Christ knows its source and its circumstances. He judges both the act and the heart, the thing done and the freedom by which it was done.

The Church speaks of genuine merit in this life of grace. No one can merit the initial gift of forgiveness and justification. Once adopted in Christ, however, the faithful can act under the initiative of the Holy Spirit, and their graced acts have true merit before God. The merit is first attributable to grace and then to the free cooperation grace makes possible (CCC 2008–2011). God’s generosity gives our love an eternal significance. He does not treat the creature’s response as a fiction simply because he supplied everything by which it became possible.

A father can give a child both the material and the help for an act which really becomes the child’s offering. The comparison remains small beside adoption in Christ, yet it helps us receive both truths without rivalry. What we offer is truly ours, and what makes the offering possible is already God’s gift. Gratitude need not deny that obedience was costly. Humility need not deny that charity was real. We may thank God for a victory over temptation without claiming that we obtained it independently of him. We may ask for the promised reward while knowing that the promise itself is mercy.

Chrysostom sees this generosity in the final judgment of Matthew 25. Christ promises a kingdom in response to bread, water, clothing, shelter, and a visit. The good is real, but the gift bestowed far exceeds it. Chrysostom therefore says that those who receive the crown receive it by grace (*Homilies on Matthew* LXXIX.2). His reading prevents a cramped spiritual accounting. The Lord receives a little service offered in charity and brings his servant into a blessedness no earthly act could purchase. His promise dignifies the service without reducing heaven to wages measured by an earthly rate.

This is especially consoling when fidelity seems small. A sick person who can offer little outward activity may still love, pray, and accept help with gratitude. A parent repeatedly returns to the same unremarkable duty. A worker refuses one profitable dishonesty. An exhausted caregiver offers another patient answer. Such acts are neither substitutes for grace nor trivial beside it. They are places where grace becomes the free act of a child of God. Their worth cannot be measured by visibility, novelty, or the praise they attract.

The teaching also corrects the temptation to preserve a favored religious achievement as a substitute for present conversion. We may have undertaken a great sacrifice years ago, defended the faith courageously, or served the Church with unusual generosity. These goods deserve thanksgiving. They cannot authorize a present refusal of God’s command. The relationship remains living. The Lord calls the person who once obeyed to obey today, and the person who once fell to rise today. The account of a life includes perseverance, repentance, and the growth or decline of charity through time.

There is no need to choose between serious effort and reliance upon God. Reliance becomes prayer, and prayer asks for the grace to act. Effort becomes Christian when it answers that grace in humility. We bring our weakness to the Lord because the good commanded is beyond our unaided strength, and we undertake the next faithful act because his aid is real. Before judgment we will have no ground for boasting. We can have reason for hope: the Judge is the giver of the grace by which his servants live, and he is faithful to the reward he has promised.

## Christ in the Person Before Us

Matthew 25 gives judgment a face we can encounter before we die. The Son of Man comes in glory, gathers the nations, and speaks of hunger, thirst, nakedness, estrangement, sickness, and imprisonment. He identifies himself with the least of his brethren. What was done or refused to them concerns him (Mt 25:31–46). The revelation reaches ordinary human need with the authority of the eternal King. We cannot honor his majesty truthfully while treating those he claims as beneath our concern.

The corporal works of mercy bring love out of intention and into the body’s needs. A hungry person needs food; a sick person needs care and companionship; the stranger needs welcome; the imprisoned need not be forgotten. These needs can be inconvenient, repetitive, and resistant to a quick solution. They put limits upon the freedom to arrange life only for ourselves. Christ receives the service personally. To feed another person in charity is to enter a relation whose deepest meaning he will disclose at judgment.

Chrysostom asks his hearers to notice how accessible the commanded works are. Christ does not say that they cured the sick or broke open the prison. He says that they visited and came. The hungry one did not demand a costly banquet, but necessary food (LXXIX.1–2). The distinction frees charity from an excuse familiar in every age. We can decline a possible service because we cannot accomplish a complete rescue. Yet the inability to remove every suffering does not erase the duty to do the good within reach.

We can sit with the lonely person without curing loneliness for the rest of his life. We can bring a meal without solving the causes of poverty. We can help a family through one difficult week without possessing the means to carry it forever. Larger efforts may also be required, and charity can rightly sustain them. But the immediate person must not become invisible while we wait for a perfect remedy. Christ names acts which allow real mercy to begin within limited circumstances.

The condemnations in the passage include omission. The refusal to give food, clothing, welcome, or a visit is enough to show a terrible estrangement from Christ. Chrysostom joins this to the buried talent and the unprepared virgins: neglect of the neighbor can ruin a life which congratulates itself for avoiding more conspicuous wrongs (LXXVIII.1–3). We therefore examine more than the injuries we deliberately inflicted. We also examine the duties we evaded and the needs we freely chose to ignore.

This examination must remain concrete. The neighbor we have neglected may be a relative whose illness has become tiresome, a person whose need interrupts our preferred work, or someone from whom we expect no gratitude. We may have made generosity dependent upon being noticed. We may have confined it to people who share our tastes and opinions. We may support distant good causes while using impatience to keep a dependent person from approaching us. Christ’s identification with the least reaches these arrangements and asks what kind of love they contain.

James begins his account of living faith by rebuking partiality toward the well-dressed rich man and contempt toward the poor man (Jas 2:1–13). The person who can benefit us receives a place of honor, while the inconvenient person is made to stand aside. Such preference can survive under courteous language and respectable habits. An annual examination before judgment can uncover the social calculations by which we decide whose suffering deserves our attention. The Lord’s presence is not proportioned to a person’s usefulness to us.

Chrysostom’s own exposition of Christ’s least brethren emphasizes the poor and neglected believer, including the lay Christian and not only the monk (LXXIX.1). The claim gives the baptized neighbor a searching place within the Church’s life. Baptism does not make another member’s deprivation someone else’s problem. At the same time, Christ commands love of enemies and shows mercy beyond the boundaries by which we ordinarily recognize friends (Mt 5:43–48; Lk 10:25–37). Our charity must be as wide as the Lord’s command, beginning with the actual persons he places before us.

Mercy also requires truth. A person in need is not helped by being used as a stage for our virtue. Listening may reveal that the assistance we enjoy offering differs from the assistance needed. Prudence governs the form of a gift, responsibilities to dependents remain real, and some requests cannot rightly be fulfilled. Yet prudence must serve charity. We should examine whether an apparently careful judgment is actually a practiced way of keeping our comfort untouched. The test includes our willingness to seek a good we can responsibly do when a particular request cannot be granted.

The good done in secret belongs here as fully as the mercy made visible. A person can serve for years without receiving the affection hoped for. The Lord does not forget the repeated visit, the patient washing, the money quietly given, or the interrupted sleep. Cyril’s assurance that no faithful act is lost before God guards these works from bitterness (XV.23). We may desire natural gratitude, but we need not make gratitude a condition of obedience. Christ will know the whole service, including the effort by which love endured its obscurity.

The judgment scene ends in eternal punishment and eternal life. Its gravity does not permit us to reduce mercy to a pleasant supplement to religion. Christ has made our treatment of the neighbor a place of response to himself. Before that truth, it is possible to stop discussing charity and begin an act of it. A call can be made, a debt of care acknowledged, money set aside, a visit arranged, a pattern of neglect confessed. The person before us is not merely an occasion for our improvement. He or she is someone Christ loves and has entrusted to our love.

## Oil for the Lamps, Fruit from the Talents

The two parables preceding the final judgment in Matthew 25 teach readiness through the passing of time. The bridegroom delays; the master returns after a long absence. Waiting exposes what the servants and virgins have become. The decisive hour finds some prepared and others unprepared. The delay was not empty. It was the time in which fidelity could be practiced, gifts could bear fruit, and love could be kept alive.

All ten virgins fall asleep. The difference lies in their provision of oil. When the summons comes, the wise are ready to enter; the foolish have left the necessary preparation undone (Mt 25:1–13). Vigilance therefore cannot mean a state of uninterrupted emotional intensity. Human beings must sleep, work, eat, and fulfill ordinary responsibilities. The readiness Christ commands can inhabit these things. It consists in a life furnished for his coming, one in which the inward substance of love accompanies the outward profession of expectation.

Chrysostom reads the lamps through the honor of virginity and the oil through mercy, especially almsgiving. The great labor of chastity does not dispense the virgins from love of the needy (LXXVIII.1). His reading searches every form of religious excellence. A difficult penance, exact observance, intellectual accomplishment, or conspicuous work can become the thing upon which we rest while some ordinary duty of charity remains refused. A gift from God is corrupted when we use it to declare ourselves exempt from another command of God.

The foolish virgins discover their lack when the opportunity to remedy it has passed. In Chrysostom’s exposition, the request for borrowed oil shows that another person’s goodness cannot replace the response which should have been ours (LXXVIII.1–2). The communion of saints truly helps us, and their intercession is a gift; it does not turn our refusal to love into their obedience. We cannot borrow a parent’s fidelity while rejecting the faith the parent taught, or rest upon the holiness of a community while refusing its discipline. Grace reaches us through others so that we too may respond.

The parable of the talents deepens the same summons. The master entrusts different amounts according to the servants’ capacities. The servants who receive five and two both put the gift to work, and both receive the same commendation for fidelity. The servant who receives one buries it (Mt 25:14–30). Christ asks for a faithful answer to what has actually been entrusted. A smaller capacity does not prevent that answer. Envy of another person’s gifts can become an excuse for leaving our own unused.

Chrysostom names a wide range of talents: money, teaching, assistance, protection, and whatever ability a person can use for the neighbor’s good (LXXVIII.2–3). This includes gifts so familiar that we scarcely recognize them as gifts. We can read a difficult letter for someone, teach a child patiently, speak a word of correction, defend an absent person from slander, give practical help, or make room for a lonely guest. The field of stewardship is as particular as the life God has given us. Faithfulness begins within it.

The buried talent is especially sobering because the servant returns what he received. He can point to its preservation, yet he has refused the purpose of the gift. His account of the master as harsh becomes part of the excuse. Fear has protected inactivity. This can happen in spiritual life when we use our imperfection as a reason never to begin serving, or the possibility of failure as a reason never to attempt a known good. We may be unable to guarantee success; we remain able to obey within our responsibility.

Chrysostom applies this distinction to instruction and correction. The servant can give the counsel that is his to give and leave the outcome to the Lord; another person’s refusal does not make the faithful effort worthless (LXXVIII.2). Here vigilance acquires patience. We are not commanded to manufacture another person’s conversion. We are commanded to love truthfully, speak when duty calls for speech, and persevere without trying to possess the result. The Lord can distinguish failure in outcome from failure in fidelity.

The warning against delay continues in the preceding servant parable. A servant decides that his master is long in returning, begins to beat his fellow servants, and indulges himself. The delay becomes permission for abuse (Mt 24:45–51). In contrast, the faithful servant gives the household its food at the appointed time. Readiness is shown in fulfilling the duty for which authority was given. Parents, employers, teachers, and those entrusted with any responsibility prepare for judgment by serving those under their care, especially when no superior is watching.

The passing years can make this vigilance either more settled or more fragile. A habit of prayer can become a living return to God or a performance which leaves the will untouched. Repeated responsibility can mature into patient love or harden into resentment. Success can deepen gratitude or persuade us that correction is unnecessary. Annual meditation on judgment interrupts the assumption that yesterday’s fidelity guarantees today’s. The lamp needs oil; the talent must still be used; the household must still be fed. Christ will find us in the life we are actually living.

## The People Entrusted to Our Care

A talent can be an ability; it can also be a responsibility for persons. In Luke’s account of the vigilant servants, Peter asks whether the parable is addressed especially to the disciples or to everyone. Christ answers with the faithful steward appointed over a household to give its members their portion in due season (Lk 12:41–44). Authority appears as an obligation to feed. The steward receives power because others need something which he has been charged to provide. His readiness for the master’s return is measured by the faithful discharge of that charge.

The unfaithful steward reverses this order. Deciding that the master delays, he beats the servants and abandons himself to appetite (Lk 12:45–46). Those whose care justified his authority become objects of his convenience or anger. He treats the household as though the master’s absence had made it his possession. The sudden return reveals the falsehood. An entrusted office cannot be transformed into a private right to use others, however long the misuse has continued or however little resistance its victims have been able to offer.

The Lord’s warning reaches familiar forms of responsibility. A parent, teacher, employer, superior, or public official makes decisions that affect people who may have less freedom to resist. The responsibility may be modest in the world’s eyes and still be decisive for someone under it. We should therefore examine the place from which our choices reach another person’s daily life. The relevant question is the good entrusted to us: whether those within our care received justice, truthful guidance, patient attention, and the protection which our position made possible.

Authority can hide faults from the person exercising it. Those who depend upon us may remain silent because correcting us feels dangerous or useless. Their compliance can then be mistaken for agreement, and our freedom from criticism for proof of wisdom. The final judgment will not accept that mistake. Christ knows whether peace came from just conduct or from another person’s fear of speaking. Remembering his judgment should make a person in authority more willing to hear an unwelcome account, seek counsel, and permit the correction which pride would prefer to exclude.

This willingness has a spiritual cost. To admit a fault before someone entrusted to us can seem to diminish the authority required for our work. Yet defending an error because we hold office confuses the office with our personal innocence. A parent can acknowledge an unjust outburst while continuing to guide a child. A teacher can correct an explanation without ceasing to teach. A superior can repair an unfair decision while fulfilling a real duty of governance. Humility allows responsibility to serve its purpose instead of becoming a monument to the person who holds it.

James makes the account of teachers especially serious. He warns against becoming teachers in great numbers because they receive a stricter judgment, and immediately speaks of the failings of the tongue (Jas 3:1–2). Religious instruction brings this warning close to anyone who speaks publicly about God. The capacity to explain doctrine is a gift for the hearer’s good. It calls for accuracy, preparation, and submission to the truth taught. A teacher should fear misleading another person through negligence, making conjecture sound certain, or using sacred teaching to magnify himself.

The warning also requires a life willing to receive the command it explains. We can teach patience while excusing our own cruelty, speak of poverty while indulging greed, or exhort repentance while refusing correction. The discrepancy does more than expose personal weakness. It can make the hearer distrust the truth because its messenger appears to use it only against others. Paul’s examination of the religious teacher in Romans returns the command to the one who teaches it: the person who instructs another must also receive instruction himself (Rom 2:17–24).

This should lead those entrusted with teaching toward prayer and fidelity, rather than toward concealment of the truth. Fear of being imperfect cannot justify abandoning a duty to instruct. The apostles’ warning asks us to teach as people under judgment, dependent upon grace and answerable for our care. We can study what we do not understand, distinguish the Church’s doctrine from our own opinion, and correct an error we have spread. We can ask that the word first make a claim upon our own life. Such humility gives seriousness to instruction without requiring a performance of flawless sanctity.

Other forms of authority concern goods and labor. James speaks with dreadful directness about wages withheld from those who reaped the fields: the workers’ cry has reached the Lord (Jas 5:1–6). Here judgment enters the apparent distance between prosperity and the lives that helped produce it. A person may enjoy abundance while concealing from himself what was denied to someone with less power. The apostle refuses that concealment. God hears the claim which the prosperous person has found ways not to hear.

This passage asks us to bring our practical arrangements before conscience. An employer’s religious devotion cannot make dishonest withholding just. A person administering another’s goods cannot treat those goods as available for private advantage. A household cannot purchase its comfort through a deliberate refusal of obligations owed to someone dependent upon it. Particular questions can require competent advice, but the need for advice must not become a permanent shelter from an obligation already clear. Christ’s judgment reaches the decision by which we preferred our advantage to another person’s due.

Works of generosity do not erase such duties. Giving alms can be a true good, yet a gift offered publicly does not authorize the retention of what belongs to an unpaid worker. The person praised as a benefactor may still need to restore what he wrongfully kept. Annual meditation can uncover this divided account, in which selected acts of generosity have made us unwilling to examine the way our resources are obtained or administered. Repentance makes the whole relation to goods answerable to justice and charity, including the parts from which we derive little admiration.

An entrusted duty can also be neglected through the desire to avoid discomfort. The steward might provide pleasant company while failing to supply the household’s food. Likewise, a person responsible for guidance may decline to correct a dangerous habit because correction risks resentment. Kindness requires attention to the actual good of the person, including goods which cannot be secured by constant approval. The task should be carried out with patience and proportion, but it remains a task. The returning Lord will know when apparent gentleness concealed the preference for an easier life.

The opposite temptation is to make ourselves responsible for what belongs to another person’s freedom. Parents can teach and give example; they cannot compel an adult child’s interior assent. Teachers can explain; they cannot guarantee that knowledge will become obedience. Chrysostom’s treatment of the talents helps here: the servant must offer the counsel entrusted to him, while the result is left to the master (LXXVIII.2). The distinction guards authority from both negligence and domination. We must do our part faithfully without claiming the power to possess another person’s response.

Some people carry deep grief over failures within those entrusted to them. Remembering judgment may awaken a truthful need for repentance, but it can also tempt them to assume every later evil as their own deliberate act. Christ will distinguish the care given, the omission freely chosen, the limitation honestly encountered, and the independent choice of another person. We should acknowledge an actual failure and repair it where possible. We should also entrust to his knowledge the outcome which we could not control. The Judge’s completeness is a ground for honest responsibility and for relief from a false omnipotence.

James places patient endurance beside his warning to the unjust. The wronged are to strengthen their hearts for the Lord’s coming; they are also warned against resentful complaint against one another, because the Judge is near (Jas 5:7–9). Those who have suffered through another person’s misuse of power remain called to charity. They may seek protection and a just remedy without allowing injury to become permission for cruelty toward someone else. Awaiting Christ means entrusting the full account to him while remaining obedient in the part of life still placed in our own hands.

The faithful stewardship which judgment discloses may have attracted very little notice. Someone administered a difficult trust honestly, gave a weaker person a fair hearing, resisted a convenient injustice, or persevered in teaching when progress seemed small. These goods belong to Christ’s knowledge. Authority received from him can become a work of humble service, and the account we owe can keep recalling us to its purpose. The master will return to his household. Blessed is the servant whom he finds caring for the people he entrusted to him.

## Justice for the Wronged, Mercy for the Penitent

The Lord’s judgment belongs to those who have suffered wrong as well as to those who must repent of doing it. Augustine’s account of the innocent person condemned and the guilty person released remains painfully recognizable (*City of God* XX.2). Human evidence can be hidden, institutions can fail, and a victim can die without public vindication. The last judgment promises that God’s justice will be manifest. No failure of an earthly court gives evil the power to establish its own innocence forever.

This promise honors the truth of suffering. A Christian should never be required to call a lie true, an abuse harmless, or a betrayal good in order to express trust in God. Providence can bring good from what creatures do wickedly; the creature’s wickedness remains wickedness. The Judge knows what was taken and who was injured. He knows the effects which could not be put into words and the losses which no earthly reparation could wholly restore. Hope in his judgment allows the wounded person to entrust the unfinished claim to someone who understands it completely.

That entrusting does not remove the duties of the present. The vulnerable must be protected, truthful testimony given where required, and just remedies pursued. Forgiveness does not require leaving a person exposed to further injury or pretending that trust has already been restored. Nor does recourse to legitimate justice itself mean a refusal to forgive. We can renounce personal vengeance while seeking protection and accountability. The eternal judgment belongs to Christ; the responsibilities already placed in our hands remain ours to fulfill.

The one who has done wrong must hear the same doctrine from the other side. A hidden injury does not cease to matter because its victim is silent. A person who has been forgiven cannot treat forgiveness as a reason to refuse restitution. Christ’s mercy reaches the actual sinner and therefore reaches the actual wrong. It gives courage to name what happened without changing its description to preserve self-esteem. A truthful confession can be the beginning of a more demanding love than the evasive peace we had maintained before it.

St. Thomas locates mercy at the root of divine justice. Whatever is due to a creature rests upon a prior gift: the nature, existence, and capacities which God freely bestowed. Tracing these gifts to their source reaches the goodness of the divine will (ST I, q. 21, a. 4). Justice and mercy are therefore united in God’s works. He does not become merciful by abandoning the truth, nor just by ceasing to be good. The very creature who owes him an account exists because divine generosity has already acted.

The Cross makes this unity personal. Jesus Christ gives himself for sinners while revealing the seriousness of the sin from which he saves. Mercy does not pronounce the injury unreal. It opens the sinner’s return, heals the will, and brings the penitent into a restored relation with God and neighbor. The victim’s hope and the penitent’s hope can both rest in the same Savior. His knowledge is not divided between pity for one and truth for the other. He can vindicate justice without limiting the power of forgiveness.

The final disclosure also concerns the grace which delivered sinners. In the account transmitted in the posthumously compiled Supplement to the *Summa*, Thomas holds that forgiven sins will be made known, together with the repentance and mercy by which the saints were saved (Supplement, q. 87, a. 2). He calls this the more probable and more common opinion and records Peter Lombard’s contrary view that sins erased by repentance will not be disclosed to others. Thomas adds that such disclosure would bring the blessed no shame. The general doctrine is certain: God’s justice and providence will be manifest. This particular explanation retains its theological status.

Whatever the manner of that manifestation, forgiveness will not be exposed as a deception. A sin truly remitted is not waiting to return as an unforgiven charge. The glory of a redeemed life includes the reality of redemption. We already know this in a limited way when the Church remembers Peter’s denial together with his repentance and faithful witness. Knowledge of the fall can magnify gratitude for grace. At the last judgment the truth will be governed wholly by God, beyond the cruelty and appetite for scandal with which we sometimes receive another person’s faults.

Meditation on that day can therefore purify our use of information now. We may need to know an accusation to protect someone or discharge a duty. We do not need to enjoy another person’s disgrace. We may need to correct a false account without making a life reducible to its worst act. The Lord’s judgment frees us to be exact about wrong while humble about the person. We too depend upon mercy, and we too have a history whose full meaning exceeds what our neighbors can see.

The final word will belong to Christ, who knows the wounded and the guilty more deeply than either knows himself. We can ask for the conversion of an enemy without abandoning the truth of the harm. We can accept forgiveness without claiming that repair is unnecessary. We can confess our guilt without pronouncing ourselves beyond redemption. Justice is strong enough to name the wrong, and mercy is strong enough to save the sinner who turns to God. The time for that turning is now.

## Coming into the Light in Confession

The sacrament of Penance gives the prospect of judgment a present place of encounter with Christ. We examine conscience, confess our sins, receive absolution, and undertake the penance given. In this sacrament the sinner comes before God’s merciful judgment while conversion is still possible. The Catechism calls it, in a certain way, an anticipation of the judgment at the end of life (CCC 1470). What we fear bringing into the light can be brought to the Savior now. He has instituted a sacrament in which repentance meets his forgiveness.

Examination begins by asking for truth before asking for a favorable account. The commandments, the Gospel, and the duties of our state of life give conscience something firmer than the impression we happen to have of ourselves. We consider thoughts, words, deeds, and omissions, the wrong we chose and the good we owed. We also remember the grace which accompanied us. Gratitude helps us recognize where God has acted; it prevents examination from becoming a story in which sin seems more powerful than the mercy that has already sustained us.

A useful examination moves from general characterizations to particular acts. It is easy to say that we have been selfish and difficult to acknowledge the money withheld, the responsibility abandoned, or the harsh word deliberately chosen. General remorse can leave the protected fault intact. Naming the act allows us to see what repentance requires. The purpose is neither a polished narrative nor a complete psychological autobiography. We bring our sins honestly, with the circumstances necessary to understand them, and receive the priest’s help where we are uncertain.

Francis de Sales’s meditation on judgment turns in precisely this direction. After contemplating the separation of the good and evil, he considers the opening of conscience: the malice of sin is disclosed, but so are repentance and the effects of grace. His practical resolution is to examine conscience, confess faults, and follow the counsel received (*Introduction to the Devout Life* I.14, considerations 5–6 and affections 2). The future disclosure becomes a reason for present sincerity. God has given both the means of return and the time in which to use them.

Contrition is sorrow for sin with the resolution to turn from it. It can begin in fear of judgment and punishment; such imperfect contrition is itself a prompting of grace and disposes us for sacramental forgiveness. Perfect contrition arises from love of God above all things and includes the firm resolve to seek sacramental confession as soon as possible (CCC 1451–1453). We need not reject the first movement of fear because our love is still weak. We can let it bring us to Christ, asking that sorrow deepen into grief for having offended the One who loves us.

The sincerity of this sorrow is not measured by tears or by the intensity of emotion. We may feel profoundly and remain unwilling to change; we may feel little and make a costly, deliberate act of repentance. The will’s return matters. A firm purpose of amendment means that we intend to reject the sin and take the steps required to resist it. It does not mean that we can guarantee, by our own strength, that no weakness will ever reappear. We ask for grace and choose the means of fidelity which are available to us.

This choice often has a practical shape. We can remove an occasion we have willingly maintained, change a dishonest practice, stop a conversation when it becomes detraction, or place a neglected duty at a definite time. A purpose of amendment which refuses every practical consequence remains doubtful. Conversely, one honest and concrete change can make repentance more serious than many sweeping promises. The Lord does not require an eloquent declaration. He calls the person who has been lying to tell the truth and the person who has been withholding love to begin loving.

Confession requires honesty about grave sin, including what was secret. The shame that tempts us to conceal a fault can be offered as part of the return. Yet sincere confession is not an achievement of perfect memory. We examine diligently and confess what we know, without deliberately withholding what should be brought before God’s mercy (CCC 1454–1456). Forgetfulness and concealment are different. The penitent’s task is to be truthful and receptive, accepting guidance when a doubt needs resolution rather than attempting to manufacture certainty by endless recollection.

Absolution restores communion with God and reconciles the sinner with the Church (CCC 1468–1469). We should receive this as an action of Christ. The priest’s ministry brings an objective gift which does not wait upon a corresponding feeling of relief. A person may leave confession still emotionally troubled and nevertheless truly reconciled. Scrupulous distress calls for patient pastoral care and, where appropriate, professional help. Its insistence is not proof that forgiveness failed. Fidelity can include the humble acceptance of sound counsel when private anxiety demands that an honestly confessed matter be reopened without end.

Forgiveness also leads toward satisfaction and reparation. Stolen goods should be returned, harm to a reputation repaired where possible, and injuries addressed according to justice. Absolution removes sin; the disorder caused by sin may still require healing (CCC 1459–1460). The penance assigned is part of that return, and the wider duty to repair harm belongs to love of the neighbor. We should not seek a sacramental peace which depends upon leaving someone else to bear an avoidable loss caused by our wrongdoing.

Reparation can require prudence. A clumsy attempt to disclose everything to everyone may cause further injury. Some restitution is straightforward; other cases require confidential counsel about what is owed and how to fulfill it. The difficulty is a reason to seek good guidance, not to declare the obligation nonexistent. Repentance becomes mature when it accepts that forgiveness and responsibility can stand together. The forgiven person is being restored to the freedom to do what justice and charity require.

The sacrament thus prepares us for judgment by bringing us repeatedly into truth and grace. We cease treating Christ’s knowledge as an enemy of our peace. We learn to accuse our sin without hating the creature whom God has made, to receive mercy without diminishing the wrong, and to begin amendment without pretending to possess our own salvation. The Lord before whom we will finally stand is already receiving the penitent through his Church. We can meet him here, confess what must be confessed, and leave with work to do in the freedom of forgiveness.

## Purified for the Vision of God

Those who die in God’s grace and friendship but remain imperfectly purified are assured of heaven. They undergo the final purification which the Church calls purgatory (CCC 1030–1032). Their judgment belongs wholly on the side of salvation. The friendship of God has not been lost, and their destiny will not change into damnation. Yet the holiness required for the vision of God must be complete. The Lord who forgives also purifies; nothing unworthy of his presence will remain as a permanent compromise within the life of heaven.

This teaches us to desire more than escape from condemnation. Sin can leave attachments which survive a real return to God. We may sincerely repent of pride while still craving the attention which nourished it. We may stop an injustice while retaining the habit of treating another person as inferior. We may give up a pleasure forbidden by God’s law while continuing to resent the law. These attachments need healing. Grace calls us toward an undivided love in which obedience is no longer accompanied by a protected wish to be free of God.

The Church distinguishes the guilt of sin, eternal punishment, and temporal punishment. Forgiveness and the restoration of communion with God remit the eternal punishment of sin; temporal punishment can remain. Purification from sinful attachments takes place here or, for those who die in grace still needing it, after death. A conversion of fervent charity can be so complete that no punishment remains (CCC 1472–1473). The language of punishment expresses the seriousness of sin and the restoration of order. The language of healing expresses the transformation of the person. Both concern the same saving work of God.

An earthly analogy can help without measuring the mystery. Someone who has lied can be truly forgiven while the habit of lying still needs to be overcome and the truth restored to those deceived. The forgiveness is real from the beginning; its reality makes a new course possible. It does not make the falsehood good or remove the need for amendment. Purgatory concerns a far deeper purification, accomplished by God in those already destined for him. The soul’s assured salvation and the painful removal of what remains disordered belong together.

St. Paul speaks of work tested by fire, with some work enduring and other work consumed, while the person is saved through fire (1 Cor 3:10–15). The passage first concerns building upon the foundation which is Jesus Christ, and the Church receives it among the scriptural witnesses to purification (CCC 1031). Its image teaches the gravity of what we build. Much that appears substantial can prove unable to endure the Lord’s scrutiny. Fidelity must be founded upon Christ and formed by charity, rather than upon the impressive appearance which our work presents to others.

Benedict XVI considers the theological proposal that the purifying fire is the encounter with Christ himself, Judge and Savior (*Spe salvi* 47). In this account his love reveals and burns away the falsehood which cannot endure communion with him. The proposal helps us contemplate the personal character of purification while remaining an explanation of the mystery, rather than a defined description of its mechanism. Salvation reaches the person completely. The Savior’s gaze does not leave a soul permanently divided between the desire for God and the attachments that hinder its fulfillment.

We can cooperate with purification now. Patient acceptance of unavoidable trials, prayer, works of mercy, and fitting penance can deepen charity and detach us from sin (CCC 1473). This does not justify seeking harm or treating another person’s suffering casually. The crosses already present in faithful duty are often sufficient to expose our impatience, vanity, and need for control. We can receive them in union with Christ, asking that the burden become an occasion of love. What is hard need not be spiritually barren.

The small renunciation can be especially revealing. We surrender the final word in an argument, the pleasure of repeating a humiliating story, the comfort purchased at another person’s expense, or the demand to be thanked. These acts can seem slight, but they touch habits by which the self keeps returning to its own advantage. Their worth lies in charity. We undertake them because we want to love God more wholly and our neighbor more truthfully, accepting the loss that this purification requires.

The communion of saints extends our love to the faithful departed. The Church offers prayers in suffrage for them, above all the Eucharistic sacrifice, and commends works of charity and penance on their behalf (CCC 1032). These acts truly assist those being purified. They do more than preserve our memory of the dead. They belong to the communion in Christ through which his members can help one another even beyond death. Their destiny is already settled in grace; our suffrages serve their purification within that assured salvation.

Benedict relates this practice to the real interdependence of persons. Our lives enter one another’s lives, and love in Christ does not become external to another person when death occurs (*Spe salvi* 48). We should let that truth shape remembrance. We can have Mass offered for those we have loved, pray for people whose names are disappearing from earthly memory, and persevere after the first intensity of grief has passed. We can also pray for someone toward whom we still bear resentment, asking God to bring his saving work to fulfillment.

All Souls brings these duties before the Church each year. The dead are entrusted to divine mercy; they are not ours to declare saved without purification, still less to consign to loss. Our task is faithful suffrage and hope. As we remember them, we also learn how we would wish to be remembered: with truthful love, prayer, and confidence in Christ. The judgment at death does not make love useless. For the souls being purified, it places them securely within the saving purpose to which the Church’s intercession belongs.

## Holy Fear and Persevering Hope

The thought of judgment awakens fear because our acts matter eternally and because we have sinned. It awakens hope because the Lord is just, mercy has been offered, and grace can make us ready. The Catechism joins conversion, holy fear, commitment to justice, and the blessed hope of Christ’s return (CCC 1041). We should allow these truths to remain together in prayer. Fear can shake us out of indifference; hope gives courage to obey the summons we have heard.

Holy fear directs us toward God. It acknowledges his majesty, grieves over offense against him, and takes his warnings seriously. It becomes fruitful in repentance and fidelity. A fear which merely circles our own imagined catastrophe can consume attention without making the next act of love easier. When this occurs, we need to return to what Christ has actually given: his commandments, his promises, his sacraments, and the ordinary duties through which grace calls us to live. The future is entrusted to him; the response available now is entrusted to us.

Francis de Sales places the two sentences of Matthew 25 beside one another. He dwells upon the terror of being sent away and then upon the blessed welcome by which Christ draws his servants to himself (I.14, considerations 5–6). The meditation does not end in the contemplation of terror. It ends in repentance and gratitude for the time and means of salvation. Christian fear finds its proper direction when it makes us more willing to receive the Savior, and Christian hope finds its proper seriousness when it makes us willing to change.

Hope must therefore be distinguished from an assumption that our present course will end well regardless of what we choose. We ask for perseverance because it is a gift, and we cooperate because the gift calls for a real response. Prayer, sacramental life, amendment, works of mercy, and faithful duty belong to that cooperation. A person who knows a grave sin needs confession and conversion, not an argument for postponement. A person who has honestly repented needs trust in mercy and renewed fidelity, not a private sentence of hopelessness.

Christ’s delay is a mercy in which fidelity can grow. Another year may allow a wound to be repaired, a habit resisted, a duty embraced, and love made more generous. It may also reveal that a fault we hoped to leave behind still requires patient struggle. Repeated weakness should make us more dependent upon grace and more honest about the means we need. The Lord calls us to persevere in returning and obeying. We should not make discouragement another reason to abandon the good.

Nor is vigilance confined to moments when we feel close to eternity. Much of life proceeds through familiar work. We keep promises, earn an honest living, care for those entrusted to us, worship, and pray. The servant is blessed when the returning master finds him doing the task assigned (Mt 24:45–46). This gives spiritual weight to steadiness. Readiness can be quiet, practical, and unnoticed, because its measure is the will of the Lord rather than the intensity of the servant’s feelings.

The desire to meet Christ can grow through such fidelity. We begin by fearing the account we must render; we learn to desire the holiness that would make the meeting joyful. We become less interested in appearing innocent and more willing to be purified. We become less eager to pronounce upon others and more attentive to the charity owed them. We bring our sins to confession, our dead to the Church’s intercession, and our unfinished questions to providence. The same judgment which humbles self-confidence strengthens hope in the Lord.

## The Year Still Given

At the year’s end the Church’s expectation of Christ’s return brings the whole life back into view. The months have contained duties fulfilled and neglected, graces welcomed and resisted, losses endured, and opportunities that will not return. Some matters call for thanksgiving; some for confession; some for patient amendment. Meditation on judgment becomes fruitful when it allows these realities to be named before God without either evasion or despair.

We need not devise an extraordinary life before we can become ready. The neglected person may already be known to us. The sin that needs confession may already have been recognized. The restitution delayed, the prayer habit abandoned, and the word of forgiveness withheld may have occupied conscience for a long time. The Lord’s coming gives these familiar things their true urgency. We can begin with the next act of fidelity rather than waiting for a moment in which conversion will cost less.

Christ will judge the truth of the life, and Christ offers the grace by which the life can become holy. We shall come before him with nothing concealed and nothing misunderstood. The mercy now received will not have been imaginary; the charity now practiced will not have been wasted. What remains for us is to answer him while this day is ours: to repent, receive forgiveness, repair what can be repaired, and love those he has given us to love.

## Scope, Method, and Qualifications

This essay is Catholic spiritual reading for annual preparation for death. Scripture and authoritative Catholic teaching govern its treatment of particular and final judgment, grace, works, conscience, Penance, purification, and suffrages. The Fathers and saints are read at the identified loci; their interpretations, Aquinas’s theological explanations, and Benedict XVI’s proposed account of purifying encounter retain their respective status. Practical applications are source-grounded synthesis. The essay does not determine individual culpability or anyone’s eternal state, settle the schools on predestination, or describe a perceptual or chronological mechanism of judgment and purification. English Scripture follows the registered Douay–Rheims/Challoner witness. No text is offered for vocal prayer. Historical translations and official texts retain their separate rights; the study is not an ecclesiastically approved catechism or a substitute for personal pastoral care.
