# The Four Last Things: The End for Which We Were Made

## The Life for Which We Were Made

Heaven is eternal life with God: the perfect communion of the blessed with the Father, through the Son, in the Holy Spirit. Those who die in God’s grace and friendship, once wholly purified, see him face to face and live with Christ forever (CCC 1023–1029). God himself is their happiness. The good for which the Christian hopes is therefore greater than relief from suffering, reunion with the beloved dead, or the continuation of a life already known. All these desires find their proper place within the promise that we shall belong wholly to God and possess him without end.

Before his Passion, Jesus speaks of the Father’s house and promises to receive his disciples to himself (Jn 14:1–6). The destination has the character of a home because the Father receives his children there through the Son. Christ is the way. He goes before us through his death and Resurrection and desires that where he is, we also may be. Preparation for heaven begins by receiving that desire of Christ as the truth about our future, even when our own desire remains hesitant or divided.

The same farewell discourse joins the future dwelling to God’s present indwelling. The Father and the Son make their home with the one who loves Christ and keeps his word; the Spirit is promised as the abiding Paraclete (Jn 14:15–26). Heaven brings to fullness the communion that grace begins. God already draws the baptized person into friendship, teaches him to pray, forgives him, and gives him the charity by which he can love. Yet this beginning is lived in faith, amid weakness and struggle. The fullness remains a gift to be received.

In the prayer of John 17, Jesus names eternal life as knowing the Father and the Son whom he has sent. This knowledge involves the whole person in the love in which the Son glorifies the Father. Jesus asks that his disciples be with him to behold his glory, and he speaks of the Father’s love for him before the foundation of the world (Jn 17:1–5, 24–26). Our salvation opens into a love that did not begin with us and will never be exhausted by us.

The Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are the one God whose own life is freely shared with the redeemed. We receive this communion by grace and remain the creatures God has made and loves. The Son who became man brings us to his Father; the Spirit makes us living members of Christ. Every creaturely joy in heaven is received within that communion, whose source and measure remain God himself.

In the light of Christ’s promise, we can examine whether the projects death will interrupt are serving the life he offers. Some responsibilities become more urgent: fidelity to those entrusted to us, repentance for our sins, the work of reconciliation. Other ambitions lose their claim to rule the heart. We begin to distinguish what can be offered to God from what we have been trying to possess apart from him.

Such preparation has room for affection, gratitude, and sorrow. The thought of heaven does not make the death of someone loved painless. Nor must we pretend that leaving our present work and companionship costs nothing. Christ promises a good that exceeds our present knowledge, and we approach it with a heart that still knows separation from within. Hope enables that heart to entrust itself to him. We follow the Savior who knows us, who died for us, and who wills to gather his people into his glory.

The Four Last Things lead toward this fulfillment. Death makes our pilgrimage finite; judgment discloses its truth; hell reveals the terrible finality of refusing God. Heaven names the end for which mercy calls us, the good toward which conversion moves. A soul can become so accustomed to considering only its faults that it forgets what forgiveness is restoring. God heals us for friendship with himself. Remembering the last things each year gives us occasion to consider what we have allowed to weaken that friendship and to seek its renewal.

## Desire and the Gift of God

The heart learns something of its capacity for God through the goods it receives. Friendship frees us from loneliness, and understanding a truth opens possibilities we had not seen. These real goods can awaken a desire that reaches beyond them. We want the person we love to live, and we want to understand more fully what we have begun to know. We can receive these goods gratefully while discovering how much still exceeds our grasp.

Aquinas asks whether any created good can constitute perfect human happiness. His answer turns upon the range of our desire: a finite good offers a real perfection but cannot contain the fullness of good toward which the will reaches (ST I–II, q. 2, a. 8). Wealth, honor, power, and even the whole created universe remain finite. However much we receive from them, our desire still reaches beyond what they can give. Its completion lies in the source from whom they receive their goodness. We can therefore give thanks for a created good while acknowledging that it leaves something to be desired.

We often expect a created good to satisfy that further desire. We may ask another person’s affection to secure us against every fear. When affection cannot do this, we begin to blame the person who loves us or to demand ever more assurance. A gift that should bring joy becomes an occasion of anxiety for both people. Hope in God releases the other person from this demand for absolute security. We can attend more freely to his own good when we are less occupied with asking him to answer every need of ours.

The intellect has its own movement of desire. Aquinas describes the wonder that arises when we know an effect but have not reached its cause. An answer can produce further inquiry because knowing that something happens leaves us wanting to understand why. He brings this movement to its highest fulfillment in the vision of God (ST I–II, q. 3, a. 8). While our knowledge stops at created effects, we still desire to know their first cause. Aquinas therefore places perfect happiness in knowing God himself. The desire to understand an effect is finally satisfied when its divine cause is directly known.

Yet desire does not give us a power proportionate to its promised fulfillment. Aquinas teaches that no created intellect, human or angelic, can see the divine essence by its own natural strength (ST I, q. 12, a. 4). We cannot make ourselves capable of heaven by becoming more clever, more refined, or more intense in our longing. The supernatural end is freely given, and God supplies the grace by which it is attained. Our dependence upon grace also gives grounds for confidence. A person whose earthly abilities are modest is not thereby placed farther from the heart of God than a person of extraordinary talent.

Grace makes a saving response possible. When God awakens faith, moves us to repent, or enables an act of charity, he is drawing a real person into a real friendship. He enables our willing and acting, so that obedience can be our answer to his love. Prayer, sacramental life, and works of mercy are acts through which we make that answer. As we act, his grace continues to sustain us and to deepen the charity with which we respond.

A sober desire for heaven therefore includes the desire to be changed. We may long to be free from pain while guarding the resentment that makes another person’s happiness unwelcome. We may wish to see God while refusing the truth he has already shown us about an injustice. These contradictions do not require us to abandon hope; they identify places where hope must become prayer and repentance. The heavenly good is God in his holiness. To desire him sincerely is gradually to consent to the purification of what prevents us from loving him and the people he gives us to love.

The discipline of receiving ordinary goods gratefully helps this consent. We can enjoy a meal without making comfort the measure of the day, accept praise without feeding upon it, and grieve an ending without declaring that all good has ended. In each case we receive a finite good without demanding that it secure our whole happiness. The freedom involved is sometimes costly because an attachment has become familiar and seems part of our identity. As those attachments loosen, we can discover how much of our conduct they had determined. The change can feel like loss even when it makes us freer to love.

## To See God Face to Face

Benedict XII defines in *Benedictus Deus* (1336) that the souls of the just, once purified, enjoy the immediate vision of the divine essence before the resurrection of their bodies and the general judgment. Their blessedness is already real, and the vision continues without interruption. They are with Christ and with the holy angels. Their bodies await resurrection while their souls already possess the promised vision.

The vision is immediate: God himself is its object. The blessed do not merely contemplate a more excellent created representation in place of God. No creature stands between them and him as a substitute object of vision. We now learn through words, images, signs, and effects. These can truly lead us to God, but the promised end exceeds their manner of mediation. God makes himself directly known. The knowledge that comes through faith is fulfilled in sight, and the hope that awaited possession gives way to possession. Charity endures, for the one whom we have begun to love is present in the fullness of the gift.

This vision is not the sight of a bodily shape. God is spirit, and the divine essence is not an object made visible by moving nearer to it in space. Aquinas distinguishes the intellectual vision of God from the operations of bodily sight and imagination (ST I, q. 12, a. 3). Even in our present life, bodily sight is only part of knowing a person. Truth, fidelity, and love engage the mind and heart through the visible world. God will be known more intimately than anything we now know, although the manner of that knowledge exceeds our present experience.

Aquinas calls the God-given perfection that enables this knowledge the *light of glory*. The light strengthens the creature’s intellect; it is not another object replacing God (ST I, q. 12, aa. 2, 5). The intellect needs this strengthening because its own power is finite, while the God it will know is infinite. God enables it to know him directly while preserving the difference between his nature and ours.

The blessed see God without comprehending him exhaustively. To comprehend in this strict sense would mean knowing the infinite divine reality with an infinite adequacy. No created mind possesses such a measure (ST I, q. 12, a. 7). Nevertheless, God is truly present to the blessed; they are not receiving a fragment of him, as though his being could be divided into portions. The limitation belongs to the finite knower. God remains inexhaustible even when the blessed know him directly and are wholly happy in that knowledge.

Nor does the vision make every blessed person omniscient. Aquinas explicitly denies that seeing God means knowing everything that God knows or everything that his power could bring into being (a. 8). The fullness of happiness lies in possessing God, not in acquiring divine sovereignty over all possible knowledge. Some of our present curiosity is generous; some is an anxious desire to be beyond surprise or dependence. Heaven fulfills the hunger for truth while healing the impulse to secure ourselves through control. The limit Aquinas describes belongs to the creature’s knowledge, while the happiness comes from God himself.

The Catechism also speaks of finding one’s true identity in Christ (CCC 1025). The person who has prayed, repented, served, and suffered is the person whom Christ receives. Grace heals what sin has injured in that person and frees him from the vanity and fear that have distorted his conduct. We shall belong to God as ourselves, made whole by the charity for which he created us.

For a person accustomed to prayer in darkness, these truths give patience. The silence of prayer can seem empty because faith has not become sight. Distraction can make even a short act of recollection difficult. Yet the worth of prayer is not measured by how closely its present feeling resembles glory. To remain with God in faith, return after distraction, and entrust oneself to his word are real acts of communion. Perseverance in this prayer depends upon the God who promises to make himself known and who already gives grace to seek him.

Contemplation of the beatific vision also teaches reverence in theological study. We can understand true distinctions about God without possessing the reality as the blessed possess it. Study becomes spiritually fruitful when clearer knowledge nourishes adoration and obedience. For example, knowing that the vision depends upon God’s gift gives us a reason to ask for his help while studying and to give thanks for what we understand. We study a gift we cannot yet experience as the blessed do, trusting the God who promises it.

## Love, Freedom, and Joy Without End

The vision of God is inseparable from loving him and rejoicing in him. We can know a created good and remain divided about it, fearing the cost of receiving it or preferring some competing satisfaction. In the blessed, charity is whole. God is known as the supreme good and loved without reserve. Their joy is the joy of possessing him, and their security belongs to the same gift. The fear that makes earthly happiness tremble is gone. They do not watch the good recede, calculate how long it may last, or brace themselves against the next separation.

Augustine ends *City of God* by considering this secure happiness (XXII.30). He describes the blessed resting, seeing, loving, and praising. The blessed are free from the weariness and need that burden our action. Their praise expresses the goodness they behold and the gratitude of those who have received everything. God himself is the reward. Augustine gathers the rightful objects of human longing—life, health, peace, honor, abundance—into the possession of the giver. Their fulfillment rests in him, and their many forms of goodness no longer pull the heart apart.

Eternity cannot be understood simply by extending the duration of an earthly pleasure. Our pleasures can become tedious because they are limited, because our powers tire, or because desire seeks a good they cannot supply. These conditions do not define the blessed enjoyment of God. Augustine speaks of a love without satiety in the sense of weariness, and praise without exhaustion. The blessed continue to delight in the God whom they possess. We cannot describe the entire manner of a creature’s eternal experience, but we can entrust ourselves to the promise that the good possessed will never become a burden to those who possess it.

The freedom of the blessed belongs within this fullness. Augustine says that the final freedom is greater than the first: the blessed cannot sin, and their inability is God’s gift. Their will has been healed so completely that delight in evil has no place in it. To choose sin is to damage the freedom for which we were made. The power to turn against our good is therefore no perfection whose absence would impoverish heaven. God himself cannot sin; the creature shares, by grace, an unfailing adherence to goodness without becoming divine by nature.

This teaching reaches the weariness of repeated moral struggle. We know the divided experience of wanting to be generous and resenting the request, desiring purity and returning to what harms us, intending to forgive and rehearsing the offense. Grace already works within those conflicts, but the conflict is not our final condition. Heaven promises a will no longer at war with itself. The good will be loved with the whole person. Perseverance gains strength from knowing that the present battle is directed toward peace, and that peace will involve a more complete exercise of love than we have yet known.

The desire for such freedom changes how we practice virtue. A reluctant act of patience may be a true beginning, yet we hope to become patient enough to delight in another person’s good. We resist a temptation, but we also seek the healing of the desire that makes it powerful. We ask forgiveness for an outburst, then ask to love the person whose need exposed our impatience. These acts seek an inward conversion that restraint alone cannot supply. We ask God to heal the desires behind our conduct, until we can love the good willingly and without division.

Paul’s account of charity places this fulfillment before us with great sobriety (1 Cor 13). Prophetic gifts, eloquence, knowledge, and sacrifice can dazzle observers while the heart remains without love. Charity is patient, kind, free from envy, and joined to truth. Its permanence gives the passing duties of our lives their eternal seriousness. We shall not carry every earthly capacity into glory in its present use, but love formed by grace belongs to the life that endures. Paul gives us reason to examine an admired achievement by the same measure as a hidden act of service: the charity with which it was done.

The annual remembrance of heaven can therefore become a renewal of courage. God is drawing us toward a happiness in which we can love without the shame of divided intention, rejoice without the fear of theft or loss, and praise without turning praise back upon ourselves. A habit that repeatedly defeats us can still be brought to him for healing. We continue asking because he promises a freedom more complete than the self-restraint we have so far attained.

## The Worship of Heaven

In John’s vision the door is opened, the throne is revealed, and the life surrounding it is gathered into adoration (Rev 4). The imagery is rich with light, living creatures, elders, vesture, and crowns. Its center is the one seated upon the throne. The living creatures acclaim God’s holiness, and the elders fall before him. We can allow its many figures to awaken wonder without assigning each a physical arrangement or claiming to know every detail of the life they signify. What is clear is the joyful acknowledgment of God’s holiness and of the dependence of every created thing upon his will.

The elders cast their crowns before the throne. A crown ordinarily signifies distinction, authority, or a victory already attained; here it becomes an offering. They honor God with the crowns they have received. They acknowledge that their glory comes from him and offer it in thanksgiving. An earthly achievement can teach us a little of this movement when success awakens thankfulness for the people, opportunities, and powers through which it became possible. Remembering how much we have received can correct the wish to regard an achievement as wholly our own.

The elders praise God because all things exist through his will. Before we consider what a creature has done for us, it already has its place within his generosity. This can enlarge our prayer beyond the circle of immediate benefit. We can give thanks for a truth we may never master, a beauty far from our own country, or a person whose service will never become known to us. The world contains goods that are real without becoming our possessions. To worship the Creator is to rejoice that he gives being so abundantly. Such thanksgiving can continue even when our own needs remain pressing, because God’s goodness extends beyond what we have received ourselves.

John’s vision then concentrates upon a sealed book and the grief that no one can open it (Rev 5). He is told of the victory of the lion of the tribe of Judah, and he sees a Lamb standing as though slain. The worship of heaven is inseparable from the saving death of Christ. The Lamb receives the book, and the new canticle praises him for redeeming a people by his blood. His self-offering remains the subject of their praise in glory.

We can desire freedom from suffering while resisting the costly love through which Christ draws us to himself. The promise is indeed freedom from suffering, but the life that reaches fulfillment is the life given to us through Christ’s self-offering. We shall rejoice in a love whose earthly form included obedience, patience, and the gift of himself for those he came to save. We cannot prepare for that joy by treating every costly demand of charity as foreign to happiness. In offering something costly for another’s good, we seek to share the charity for which the Lamb is praised.

The new canticle praises the Lamb for redeeming a people by his blood from every tribe, tongue, people, and nation. The breadth of the communion is part of its glory. Each of us enters the Church within particular languages, customs, and relationships; none of these beginnings encloses the whole company of the redeemed. Heavenly hope teaches us to receive that company with gratitude. The faith of Christians formed in unfamiliar circumstances is a gift to the same body. Their sufferings matter to us, and their holiness belongs to a communion we desire to share. Concern confined to Christians whose circumstances resemble our own falls short of the company the canticle describes.

The redeemed are described as a kingdom and priests. Worship is thus bound to the dignity Christ gives his people. They participate in the praise offered through Christ and receive their dignity from him. Their life under his sovereignty exceeds what present political arrangements can describe. Whatever differences of service and glory belong to the saints, all are joined in acknowledging him. Each contributes to their common praise as someone whom Christ has redeemed.

John sees bowls of incense identified with the prayers of the saints. Within the vision’s splendor there is room for prayer: the cry that rises to God is received within the worship of heaven. This image can sustain the Christian who has little outward evidence that his intercession matters. A prayer offered in a crowded household, from a prison, or during an ordinary journey may be hidden from every observer. Its value is known to God. John identifies the incense with their prayers without describing how individual saints know particular requests. The petition hidden on earth is received by the God to whom the saints offer praise.

The praise widens through myriads of angels and finally through every creature in heaven, on earth, and in the sea. Glory is given to the one on the throne and to the Lamb. We shall be fulfilled within a good in which others also rejoice. Their worship will belong to our happiness because the God they praise is the God we love. Even now we can learn something of this when another person’s thanksgiving enlarges our own, or when the Church’s common prayer gives words to a gratitude we could not have expressed alone.

The contemplation of heavenly worship prepares us for the worship entrusted to us now. It can teach us to enter the Church’s praise with attention, to receive silence as room for adoration, and to offer our voices as members of a body. The Father is worthy of love before we have obtained an answer to each particular petition. Christ is worthy of thanksgiving because he has redeemed us. We can adore while a petition remains unanswered, joining the praise offered for God’s holiness and Christ’s accomplished redemption. Our worship in faith joins that acknowledgment and awakens the desire to take our place, wholly and forever, in its fulfillment.

## The Communion of the Blessed

The life of heaven is shared. The Father gathers a people in his Son; each person is loved with a love that opens him to the others. *Lumen gentium* describes the disciples still journeying on earth, those undergoing purification, and those already in glory as members of one Church, united in the charity of God and neighbor (49). Death does not sever the communion established in Christ. The blessed are more deeply united to him and therefore more fully concerned for his members. Their perfection in love does not draw them away from our need.

Their intercession belongs to Christ’s mediation. The saints pray through him, with him, and in him; the grace by which they became holy is his gift. When we ask their prayers, we enter more consciously into the fellowship of his body. The charity of the saints comes from God and expresses his generosity toward us. The help they give witnesses to the abundance of a redemption that makes creatures able to share in Christ’s care for his people.

Cyprian gives this communion the warmth of homecoming. At the end of *On Mortality*, he asks Christians to consider their true country and the company awaiting them there: patriarchs, apostles, prophets, martyrs, virgins, and the merciful who served the poor (26). He also names parents, brothers, and children. His exhortation, given amid the trials of an epidemic, moves from the particular loves of a family to the great family of the redeemed. The holy dead are secure in their own salvation and solicitous for ours. Their joy invites the pilgrim’s desire to grow beyond mere attachment to the familiar world.

In Cyprian’s exhortation, the company of our own dear ones opens into a household larger than we knew on earth. The patriarchs become our fathers, and the apostles are members of the family to which we belong in Christ. The grace that unites each blessed person to God unites the whole company in charity. To learn the lives of the saints is already to become acquainted with this larger home and to recognize the many ways Christ forms his members.

Mary has a singular place in this communion. Taken body and soul into heavenly glory, she is the image and beginning of the Church’s promised perfection and a sign of hope to the pilgrim people (*Lumen gentium* 59, 68). Her Assumption makes bodily glorification present in a redeemed creature in a manner that anticipates our common consummation. Her maternal care continues through her intercession, always dependent upon the one mediation of her Son (62). The Christian who turns to Mary for help in persevering is turning toward the life that her Son has already brought to fullness in her.

The holy angels also belong to the communion of heaven. They adore the same Lord and share the happiness of his presence. Their life reminds us that the breadth of creation exceeds our own kind, while the admission of human beings to their company displays God’s generosity toward our weakness. Human beings remain human in sharing their worship. The communion includes these distinct creatures, all receiving their happiness from the same Lord.

Aquinas relates differing degrees of the beatific vision to the light of glory and to charity (ST I, q. 12, a. 6). Augustine considers how such difference can exist without envy. He compares the members of the body: the finger does not resent the eye, for both share one harmonious life (*City of God* XXII.30). Another’s excellence is a good within the communion to which each belongs. The blessed do not measure their happiness against an advantage withheld from them. Each is fulfilled by God’s gift; each loves the good God gives to the others.

Here the hope of heaven uncovers a wound in our present communion. We may sincerely admire holiness until it appears in someone who receives the attention we wanted. A neighbor’s gifts can seem to diminish our place; another’s success can expose a secret claim to distinction. Augustine’s account shows why envy is incompatible with the communion we hope to share. We can ask for a charity that thanks God for another’s gifts before calculating our own share of recognition. To praise another person’s fidelity, accept help, or serve unseen is to consent to a communion whose final joy will include the splendor of everyone God has sanctified.

## Learning to Desire What Heaven Gives

Holiness prepares us for the actual happiness of heaven. The blessed love God without reserve; they love one another in him; they rejoice in truth. A life that deliberately shelters hatred, dishonesty, or impurity is therefore moving against the good it professes to seek. Hatred prevents us from sharing another’s joy, and dishonesty makes truth unwelcome. Our capacity to receive God’s gift needs healing. The Lord who forgives also makes holy, and the cleansing he completes in those who die in his friendship belongs to the generosity with which he brings them into his presence.

In the sermon “Holiness Necessary for Future Blessedness,” preached before his reception into the Catholic Church, John Henry Newman considers this relation between the heart’s character and its happiness (*Parochial and Plain Sermons* I.1). A person does not become able to delight in God’s holiness merely by entering a different place. Heaven is life in God’s presence; the heart must be made capable of loving that presence. Newman’s imagined case of an unholy person admitted unchanged is a spiritual warning about the need for conversion, not an account of someone actually unhappy within the beatific vision. The blessed are holy because grace has made them so.

Newman compares this preparation to learning to take part in worship. In prayer we give attention to God, confess our need, praise him, and seek his will. These acts have their proper end in a heart turned toward him. Prayer can expose how much of our attention we are willing to give to God. We may find it easy to spend ourselves on an ambition and difficult to give God a few attentive minutes. The discovery should bring us to him honestly. It need not be converted into a verdict that every distracted or dry prayer proves a lack of love. Fidelity in dryness can itself be an act of love.

Prayer also exposes the conditions we set upon our obedience. We can practice an outward duty while preserving a hidden condition: we will serve so long as we are appreciated, forgive so long as the other person remains beneath us, or pray so long as prayer offers consolation. God patiently brings these conditions into the light. Their surrender enlarges our capacity for charity. A person who accepts correction and returns to a duty without the reassurance he wanted may be growing toward heaven in a way that remains invisible to him. He may recognize the growth only later, in a changed response to the same difficulty.

Newman’s sermon ends by directing this demanding work toward the help of the Holy Spirit. God is at work within us, and his help makes the change possible. Without that assurance, a more truthful knowledge of our attachments can become discouragement. With it, recognizing an attachment gives us something definite to bring to God for healing. We can ask for a desire we do not yet fully possess, and we can act upon the grace given today without first feeling the completion that belongs to glory.

St. Francis de Sales makes the same movement practical in his meditation on paradise (*Introduction to the Devout Life* I.16). He begins with beauty, draws the reader toward the company of the saints, and brings the consideration to God’s presence. The affections of admiration and desire then become a resolution to relinquish particular hindrances and undertake particular helps. The resolution answers the desire awakened by the meditation. A particular attachment or neglected help can now be considered in relation to the good Francis has asked the reader to contemplate.

A renewed desire for heaven may therefore take a very ordinary form. It may mean putting aside a source of vanity, being faithful to a neglected prayer, or ending the deliberate rehearsal of resentment. A resolution can be kept when it names something we can actually do and a hindrance we can recognize. We do not know how much purification remains. We do know that every consent to grace belongs to the journey he has opened. We welcome that purification because we hope to see him face to face.

## The Blessedness Christ Teaches

The Beatitudes give the desire for heaven a recognizable form. At the beginning of the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus pronounces blessed those whose condition can appear least secure: the poor in spirit, the meek, those who mourn, those who hunger for justice, the merciful, the clean of heart, the peacemakers, and those persecuted for righteousness (Mt 5:1–12). His promises include the kingdom of heaven, consolation, divine sonship, and the sight of God. The people he names must still wait for consolation, justice, and peace. Jesus calls them blessed in their present need because he promises their fulfillment in God. They can persevere in seeking those goods before they possess them.

Poverty of spirit places us before God as receivers. We possess nothing by which we could make him our debtor or secure his kingdom as an acquisition. Even a long life of fidelity remains sustained by gifts received. This poverty can be lived amid different outward circumstances, but it cannot remain indifferent to the use of material goods. The heart that knows its dependence upon God must learn to hold possessions in a way that serves his purposes. Wealth loses its power to confer final security; deprivation loses its power to prove that a person is forgotten. Receiving the kingdom requires trust in the Father who gives it. Examining the use of our possessions can show where we are seeking a security they cannot provide.

The promise to the meek has a similar depth. Meekness is strength brought under the government of charity. We need it whenever a good could be pursued through domination, humiliation, or revenge. The meek can defend the vulnerable and speak firmly because gentleness does not require the surrender of truth. What they relinquish is the claim to possess others by force of personality or power. Christ’s promise of an inheritance exposes the insecurity beneath our grasping. We receive our lasting good from the Father. In preparation for that inheritance, we can learn to exercise authority as a service, to leave room for another person’s response, and to endure a limit without making someone else pay for it.

Those who mourn are also called blessed. The promise does not require us to regard every sorrow as a sign of virtue; it directs sorrow toward the consolation of God. We mourn death, repent of sin, and grieve the injuries inflicted upon the innocent. Such grief can deepen our knowledge that the world needs a redemption greater than our own capacity to repair it. The God who consoles will bring his work to completion. Meanwhile, we can remain beside someone whose sorrow we cannot relieve, allowing him to grieve and trusting the consolation God promises.

Hunger and thirst for justice turn expectation into a sustained appetite for the good. We know what bodily hunger does: it gathers attention, makes the need concrete, and refuses to treat nourishment as an optional ornament. Christ uses that urgency to describe the desire for righteousness. We are to want the right ordering of our own lives and the good of those whom injustice harms. Satisfaction will come from God; the hunger he blesses nevertheless asks for our present action. A person who longs for heaven cannot be content that a weaker person is cheated, a family abandoned, or the truth traded for advantage. Our powers are limited, but their limitation does not cancel the work they can do. We can continue working where a wrong remains, asking God for the perseverance and help the task requires.

The merciful are promised mercy. In their conduct the gift received begins to bear fruit: another person’s misery becomes a summons to help. Mercy can take the form of food, protection, instruction, pardon, or time freely given. Its manner must fit the actual need. Sometimes kindness requires a patient explanation; sometimes it requires protecting a person from the consequences of another’s wrongdoing. The heavenly promise draws these acts into the generosity of God without turning them into a purchase price. We need mercy ourselves, and grace makes us capable of becoming its servants. Each genuine work of mercy prepares us for a communion in which another’s good is welcomed as our own joy.

The clean of heart shall see God. Here Christ joins the highest promise to an inward transformation. Purity includes chastity, but the heart also needs cleansing from deceit, divided allegiance, and the wish to appear devoted while keeping its real treasure elsewhere. To desire the vision of God is to desire truth in the place from which our choices arise. We can let the Gospel uncover the intention beneath an outwardly admirable act. We can acknowledge that a service has become a means of influence or that religious knowledge has become a possession we use to rank people. Such discoveries are invitations to cleansing. God prepares the heart to receive himself by freeing it from the falsehoods with which it tries to secure a lesser good.

Peacemakers bear the family likeness of the God whose children they will be called. The peace they seek has room for truth, repentance, and the restoration of right relations. An appearance of calm sustained by intimidation cannot satisfy that desire. Nor can peace be built by endlessly preserving the quarrels through which we defend an injured importance. To make peace may require bringing a concealed harm into the light, asking pardon, or accepting the slow labor of rebuilding trust. The final communion gives this work its direction. We learn to want people reconciled in the good, even when reconciliation gives us no public credit and requires others to receive something we cannot supply.

The last Beatitude joins the promise of the kingdom to persecution for righteousness and fidelity to Christ. Opposition by itself does not prove that we are faithful; we can provoke it through our own injustice or folly. The Lord speaks of suffering borne for his sake. When fidelity truly costs us acceptance, livelihood, or safety, the promised kingdom declares that the cost cannot measure the worth of what is being given. Our life is held within a belonging deeper than the verdict of those who reject us. This is a hope the martyrs lived with their whole bodies. It can also sustain the quieter courage required to remain honest, protect an endangered person, or confess the faith when doing so carries a real loss.

The Beatitudes describe related changes in a person’s life. Recognizing our own need for mercy prepares us to show it to another person; seeking truth in our own heart helps us work more honestly for peace. Their fulfillment is the Lord’s gift, and their present beginnings depend upon his grace. Jesus follows them with the calling to be salt and light, so that human works may lead others to glorify the Father (Mt 5:13–16). Heavenly hope becomes visible through a life whose good points beyond itself. In an annual meditation, we can dwell upon the Beatitude that most closely touches our present responsibility and ask to live it more fully. The responsibility remains when we finish the meditation and calls for the grace we have sought in prayer.

## The Same Person, Raised in Glory

The soul’s immediate blessedness does not exhaust the human destiny. God created us as embodied persons, and the salvation accomplished by Christ reaches the body. The resurrection of the dead belongs to the Creed because the body belongs to the person whom God saves. Our final hope is the restoration and glorification of the whole human being. The separated soul truly lives with Christ; nevertheless, the common consummation still awaits his return, the raising of the dead, and the manifestation of his victory throughout creation. Heaven’s present reality and the future resurrection are parts of one promise.

Paul grounds this promise in an event: Christ has risen from the dead, the first fruits of those who have fallen asleep (1 Cor 15:20–28). The harvest takes its character from him. He has entered the condition toward which his members are being led, and death itself will be destroyed. The Christian therefore looks to the risen Lord when considering his own body. The body’s future is not determined by the weakness visible in it now. Its final destiny rests upon the power of Christ, who will conform our lowly body to his glorious body (Phil 3:20–21).

Paul compares the body to a seed sown in the earth (1 Cor 15:35–44). The seed dies, and what grows differs greatly from what was sown. God gives it a new manner of life, and the contrast is immense: corruption yields to incorruption, dishonor to glory, weakness to power. The risen body is our own body, transformed by God. Its identity does not require the continuation of every present limitation. Resurrection means that the Creator is faithful to his creature while bringing that creature into a condition beyond its present powers. The likeness to a seed illustrates the change without explaining how God will raise the dead.

A “spiritual body” remains a body. Paul is naming the life-giving rule of the Spirit, not the replacement of human flesh by a disembodied existence. The bodily humanity of the risen Christ is the pattern of our hope (CCC 999–1000). In our present condition the body is vulnerable to exhaustion, disease, injury, and death; its desires can also resist the good known by reason. In glory bodily life will participate in the peace of the whole person. The body will express the soul’s communion with God without the corruption and division that now burden it.

Irenaeus presses the reality of this hope through the Eucharist. In *Against Heresies* V.2.2–3, he answers those who deny that flesh can receive salvation. The Lord shed real blood for us, and he gives his body and blood as food. The flesh nourished by this gift belongs to him and cannot be dismissed as intrinsically incapable of eternal life. Irenaeus joins the Creator’s gifts of bread and wine to the Redeemer’s gift of himself. The same divine generosity sustains our present bodies, gives the sacrament, and will grant resurrection to the bodies committed to the earth.

Irenaeus emphasizes that the body’s incorruptibility comes from God, whose strength brings life out of our weakness. Immortality is received; the creature does not become the independent source of its own duration. The body’s return to dust reveals how completely we depend upon the Creator. Resurrection displays what that Creator can give. Our dependence will endure in glory, acknowledged with gratitude by creatures who live through his generosity.

The traditional language of the glorified body’s freedom from suffering and corruption, its glory, and its complete obedience to the life of the Spirit gathers the substance of this promise. We can consider this promise while caring for someone whose body now appears frail or unattractive. We cannot estimate his heavenly dignity by his present strength, intelligence, or independence. A body that requires washing, feeding, or patient assistance belongs to someone called to resurrection. Care for that body is therefore an act of reverence toward God’s handiwork. The promise of glory deepens the seriousness of bodily mercy while illness remains.

This also changes how we regard our own vulnerability. Age or disability can take away a form of usefulness around which we built our identity. The loss is real, but the call to communion with God does not depend upon preserving that usefulness. We remain persons capable of receiving and giving love, even when the manner of that exchange becomes hidden from others. The final restoration is Christ’s work. Our present bodily life, whether vigorous or greatly limited, can be entrusted to him and offered within the communion of his members. The body he will raise is the body he already claims as his own.

Paul concludes his teaching on resurrection by urging steadfast labor in the Lord, because such labor is not vain (1 Cor 15:58). That exhortation follows his account of the body’s victory over death. The bodily needs of the people with whom we now live belong within that service. We are free to seek healing, relieve pain, honor the dead, and patiently bear what cannot yet be remedied. Beneath these differing actions stands one hope: Christ’s victory will reach the whole person, and death will not retain what God has made for life.

## Creation Awaiting Its Freedom

The redemption of the body belongs to a promise wider than the human person considered alone. Paul describes creation groaning as it awaits freedom from corruption, and he joins its expectation to our own waiting for bodily redemption (Rom 8:18–25). The world God made is involved in the destiny of his children. The beauty of created things is real, their suffering and decay are real, and the final word about them belongs to the Creator. We cannot determine creation’s consummation by extrapolating from the processes that now wear it away.

The new Jerusalem descends from God in Revelation 21. God dwells with his people, wipes away their tears, and makes all things new. Its center is his presence. The city has no need of a temple distinct from the Lord and the Lamb, for divine communion fills its life. The nations appear within this renewed world, bringing their glory and honor. Their common life is described through a dwelling received from God and through the goods the nations bring into it.

The same chapter excludes everything defiled. Renewal brings judgment upon evil as well as fulfillment of good. We cannot simply identify the future city with the continuation of whatever civilization happens to admire. Human works contain generosity and exploitation, beauty and vanity, truthful service and lies. Our work can serve the good promised by God, but this mixture needs his judgment and purification. God knows what in our labor belongs to charity and what must pass away. We serve best when we surrender both our work and our claim to control its ultimate form.

*Gaudium et spes* 39 states that the manner and time of creation’s transformation are unknown to us. It distinguishes earthly progress from the growth of Christ’s kingdom, while teaching that hope for the new earth should strengthen our care for this one. The good fruits of human dignity, fraternity, and freedom, fostered in the Spirit, will be found purified and transfigured. We can therefore undertake responsible work for human dignity while entrusting its lasting fruit to God’s transformation. Our labor is real and valuable; the final transfiguration is God’s act.

That distinction sustains patience when good work appears fragile. A person can work for justice and still die before a wrong is repaired. The good already done for someone who was harmed retains its value, even while much remains unfinished. We are responsible for the fidelity of our service, and its final fruit belongs to God. The promise that charity endures lets us undertake imperfect work without either demanding immediate perfection or giving ourselves permission to do nothing.

Paul describes creation’s expectation as groaning. We receive its goods amid real affliction. Bodies suffer, people injure one another, and places can be damaged by carelessness or greed. These injuries remain to be resisted and relieved while we wait for the redemption Paul promises. The final newness cannot be produced by multiplying our present arrangements; nevertheless, the love with which we tend a person or a place can already belong to the kingdom that is coming.

Waiting for creation’s renewal gives us both work to do and something to entrust to God. Gratitude for its beauty need not become possessiveness, and sorrow over its wounds need not become despair. The Father who gives the world through his Son has not forgotten it. Our earthly home is passing in its present form, but its Maker is faithful. We can love what he has made while awaiting what he alone will make of it. When our part in a task ends, its unfinished good can still be entrusted to him.

## Love and Memory Made Whole

Our desire for heaven often begins with particular people. The death of a parent, a spouse, a child, or a friend makes eternal life cease to be merely a distant subject. We want to know whether the love shared in time will be lost. In the communion of saints and the resurrection, the same persons are saved and their love is perfected in God. The fulfillment he promises exceeds our ability to preserve the familiar terms on which we first learned to love.

Christ speaks plainly about one such transformation: the risen neither marry nor are given in marriage (Mt 22:30). The Sadducees’ question imagines resurrection as the continuation of present marital claims under impossible circumstances. Jesus directs them to God’s power and to the life he gives. Marriage is an earthly vocation with its own holiness, fidelity, and sacramental meaning; it is not an institution perpetuated in the resurrection. The love formed through it is entrusted to the fullness of charity. A husband and wife can hope to love one another perfectly in God without imagining their earthly marriage continuing in another form.

Trusting this promise requires us to surrender some present expectations about affection. Our present love is intertwined with exclusivity, need, habit, and sometimes possession. Even a generous love can be afraid that the beloved’s wider happiness will reduce its own place. Heavenly charity heals that fear. The good of another person is loved in God, whose fullness does not compete with our love but makes it truthful. We do not know the exact texture of every future relationship. We do know that perfected charity will contain no jealousy, no manipulation, and no fear that God’s gift to someone else deprives us of the good he wills for us.

Cyprian’s hope of reunion has strength within this larger charity. He names the embrace of dear ones and the gladness shared between them, then places that reunion among the apostles, martyrs, and all the friends of God (*On Mortality* 26). Cyprian’s exhortation concerns the reunion of the saved; the final state of a particular departed person remains in God’s judgment. We may pray and hope for the salvation of those we love. Their presence in God would perfect the love we have begun, placing it within a communion greater than we can now receive.

The promise also concerns memory. Much of our present self-understanding is bound to what has hurt us or to what we have done wrong. We can fear that happiness would require the erasure of our history, while remembering seems inseparable from renewed grief or shame. Augustine considers this tension in *City of God* XXII.30. He distinguishes intellectual knowledge of an evil from continued experience of its suffering. The blessed know their deliverance and give thanks to the Redeemer, while the misery from which they were delivered no longer afflicts them. Their gratitude is truthful, and their healing is complete.

Augustine illustrates the distinction through the knowledge of a physician who understands diseases he has not personally suffered. His purpose is to show that knowledge need not reproduce affliction within the knower. This is his theological explanation of blessed memory; revelation does not give us a detailed account of every remembered event. The divine promise is that tears, mourning, and death will pass away (Rev 21:4). We can therefore entrust our history to God without deciding beforehand which particular mode of memory must constitute its healing. The Savior who knows our lives more truly than we do can bring them to peace.

The hope of healed memory has consequences now. We can repent without treating shame as an eternal identity. We can tell the truth about an injury without making bitterness the proof that we have taken it seriously. Forgiveness does not require the denial of justice, and grief does not have to be forced into an appearance of ease. But neither our guilt nor our wounds can define the limits of Christ’s saving power. To place the past before him is to consent to a future in which it is known within mercy and truth. We ask to be healed in the truth of our lives, trusting that the misery we have known need not remain our condition.

## The Blessed and the Justice of God

The question of those who may finally refuse God touches the hope of heaven with particular pain. It becomes most difficult when we think of someone we love. The blessed possess perfect happiness and are wholly united to God’s will; eternal loss remains a real possibility for the creature who dies in unrepented mortal sin. We cannot resolve this gravity by imagining that charity becomes indifference, or that God’s justice becomes an evil from which the blessed must be protected. His goodness is the ground of their happiness, and their love is perfected in agreement with him.

Aquinas addresses the question in the *Supplement* to the *Summa* (q. 94, a. 3). He says that the blessed rejoice in divine justice and in their own deliverance. He distinguishes this from rejoicing directly in another’s suffering as suffering, which would belong to hatred. The punishment enters his account indirectly, under the good of justice; cruelty is excluded. His explanation is demanding, and its meaning is lost if it is made either a permission for malice or a promise that judgment will cease to matter. The blessed love the holy order of God with a love entirely free from sin.

This account does not give us a description of the precise experience of a mother, husband, or friend faced with another person’s loss. Our present emotional imagination cannot supply what has not been revealed. We know that the blessed will be neither deceived nor miserable, and that their charity will contain no hatred. We also know that we are not presently entrusted with declaring the final destiny of particular people. The proper response to someone whose life troubles us is prayer, truthful witness, patient love, and such help as charity allows. The time given to us is a time in which we seek salvation together.

Concern for another’s salvation can become anxious when we assume responsibility for choices we cannot make for him. His life matters immeasurably, but we cannot believe, repent, or persevere in his place. We can be faithful to our own responsibility and entrust him to a mercy wiser than our fear. That entrustment does not end intercession. It purifies it, freeing prayer from the demand that God disclose beforehand what only he can judge. We ask for his salvation because we desire his good, and we continue to love him while leaving his freedom and final judgment in God’s hands.

The consideration of divine justice should make our desire for heaven more charitable now. A person who enjoys imagining another’s condemnation is consenting to something from which heaven would have to heal him. The saints’ joy is the joy of God’s goodness, and our preparation must move toward that goodness. We can oppose evil, seek protection for those harmed, and still pray for the conversion of the wrongdoer. Seeking protection and seeking conversion can place difficult demands upon us at the same time. We need God’s help to fulfill both without excusing the wrong or wishing evil to its author.

## The Eucharistic Foretaste

Christ gives the pilgrim Church a pledge of the life she awaits. In the Eucharist he gives himself, and the communion promised in glory is already begun sacramentally. The Catechism calls the Eucharist a pledge of future glory and directs its meaning toward the Lord’s return (CCC 1402–1405). The same Savior who will be our eternal joy comes to us under the sacramental signs. His presence is real while its manner remains veiled. Faith receives what sight does not yet disclose, and the heart is nourished for a fulfillment it cannot attain by its own strength.

In receiving the Eucharist, we receive the same Christ whom we hope to see in glory. His death and Resurrection have opened heaven, and the Eucharistic sacrifice makes present his saving self-offering. The sacrament therefore joins remembrance and expectation. The Church remembers the Paschal mystery while awaiting its consummation in the kingdom. She awaits the fulfillment of what the Lord has accomplished, while the risen Christ continues to give the fruits of his sacrifice to his members.

Irenaeus reasons from the Eucharist to the resurrection of the body. Created bread and wine belong within the sacrament by which Christ nourishes his people; the bodies that receive him are destined for resurrection (*Against Heresies* V.2.2–3). Our dependence upon material food and our dependence upon divine life meet within God’s saving generosity. We receive this gift as embodied members of Christ, in the flesh whose salvation Irenaeus defends. The Creator’s handiwork is taken into the service of a redemption that will reach its bodily fullness.

The communion is ecclesial as well. *Lumen gentium* 50 places our closest sacramental union with the heavenly Church in the Eucharistic sacrifice. The saints whom we commemorate are joined with us in the praise of the triune God. Mary, Joseph, the apostles, martyrs, and all the blessed belong to the communion in which the pilgrim Church worships. The Mass can be celebrated in a poor or nearly empty church, amid distraction and human weakness, and still possess this heavenly breadth. What faith knows of its communion far exceeds what the eye can count in the room.

Eucharistic communion also makes a claim upon our conduct toward one another. We receive the Lord whose will is the unity of his people in charity. It is possible to desire spiritual consolation while remaining unwilling to bear the inconvenience of a fellow member. The sacrament calls that divided desire toward healing. A person who needs our forgiveness, our material help, or simply our patient attention cannot be excluded from the meaning of communion. We can bring our reluctance to Christ and seek the help we need to fulfill that inconvenient duty.

The foretaste does not always feel like one. A distracted reception, a long period of dryness, or the grief of a difficult season may leave little sensible joy. The sacrament’s truth is not established by our experience of consolation. The word *pledge* directs attention to the fidelity of the giver. Christ commits himself to his people, nourishes their life of grace, and draws them toward their end. We can give thanks for his presence even when thanksgiving is quiet and laborious. Such faith honors the difference between the gift itself and the feelings by which we sometimes recognize its sweetness.

Adoration teaches the same patience. To remain before Christ is to consent to a presence whose depth we do not command. We bring our needs, but we also learn to be with him for his own sake. The familiar pressure to make every interval productive can loosen in that act. A creature’s highest good is communion with God; time spent attending to him is already ordered toward its end. This does not relieve us of duties outside the church. It restores their order by joining them to the Lord from whom they receive their meaning and in whom their charity will endure.

At the threshold of death the Eucharistic promise takes on the intimacy of food for the journey. The Christ whom the Christian has received throughout life remains the one toward whom he goes. Even when illness has made bodily reception impossible, desire for him can remain within the Church’s prayer and care. The final hope rests in his mercy and fidelity. The sacramental life has been preparing the person to receive him wholly: the God hidden under the signs is the God who will make himself known face to face, and the communion begun in faith is ordered to an unending life of love.

## Prayer and the Enlargement of Love

We can speak rightly about our final end and still live with our attention almost entirely elsewhere. Prayer turns what we know of God into personal address and consent. We attend to the one who knows and loves us, bringing him the life we hope he will perfect. The act may be simple: listening to the Gospel, recollecting ourselves before a task, remaining quietly with Christ, or giving thanks for a received good. Through such acts we learn to seek God during a day otherwise occupied with his gifts.

Bernard begins *On the Love of God* by saying that God himself is the reason for loving God (I). He unfolds the claim through the prior generosity of divine love. God has no need of what we can supply; he has loved sinners and given his Son for them. Our love answers his prior gift. This prevents the desire for heaven from becoming a negotiation in which God is valued only as a means to some other reward. He gives himself. To desire that gift rightly is to desire him, and the growth of charity makes our desire more truthful.

This growth does not require us to stop wanting happiness. God made us for blessedness, and the hope of receiving it is good. What needs purification is the separation we sometimes make between our happiness and God’s goodness. We can treat prayer as a means of obtaining control, comfort, or exemption from the claims of love. Bernard directs us to the God whose own love is free and generous. As we come to know him, we can desire our good within his will and learn to rejoice in what honors him. The promised reward and the divine giver are united because the reward is communion with him.

In his account of the highest degree of love, Bernard speaks of loving oneself for God’s sake (X). He describes consent to God’s will extending even to the love we bear ourselves. His images of water mingled with wine, iron glowing in fire, and air filled with light suggest a creature thoroughly changed by participation in divine love. He then states that human nature is preserved and receives a greater beauty, power, and glory. We remain the persons God created while learning to love ourselves in relation to him.

To love ourselves in God is also to receive our limitations without hatred. Some forms of apparent humility conceal resentment that we are not more impressive, less needy, or less dependent upon mercy. True humility can ask for growth while giving thanks for existence itself. God does not wait for us to become self-sufficient before he can love us. Prayer gradually brings this truth into the heart. We can acknowledge a fault without turning against the whole person who needs forgiveness. We can accept help, submit a talent to service, and allow God to choose forms of fruitfulness that do not flatter our ambitions.

The same purification reaches prayer’s consolations. A period of sweetness can awaken gratitude and strengthen perseverance, yet the heart may begin to seek that experience as though it were the end. When the feeling passes, we can discover whether we are willing to remain with the Lord. Faithful prayer in such a season does not prove that love has become perfect, but it gives love room to become less possessive. We return to the received words of Scripture and the Church, offer our attention as we can, and ask for the charity that makes prayer true even when it is poor.

Francis de Sales’s meditation on paradise allows the imagination to serve this desire. He takes the beauty of a starlit night and a brilliant summer day, then points beyond their combined splendor to the blessed country (I.16). He moves from beauty to the saints and from the saints to God’s presence, where their joy is fulfilled. Our own meditation can begin with something beautiful we have seen and received gratefully. In giving thanks, we turn to its Creator and can come to desire the uncreated good from whom its loveliness comes.

The person who desires God’s presence must also receive God’s will in the place where he has been set. A request that interrupts our recollection may call for the patience we have just sought in prayer. Our reluctance shows how much we still need the grace for which we have asked. Answering the request gives us an occasion to act upon that grace and to love the neighbor whose need has become apparent.

## The Growth of Christian Hope

Christian hope is a theological virtue: through it we desire eternal life and trust the promises of Christ, relying upon the help of the Holy Spirit. The Catechism presents it as God’s answer to our aspiration for happiness, directing that aspiration toward his kingdom (CCC 1817–1821). Hope can therefore remain alive when we cannot predict a favorable earthly outcome. Its assurance rests upon the Lord’s fidelity and power to save. We may be unable to see how a particular difficulty will end, yet still place our lives before the God who calls us to communion. The virtue enables a real movement toward him within that uncertainty.

Hope receives the demands of perseverance together with the grace by which we can answer them. The Catechism joins the expectation of heaven to Christ’s merits, prayer, and the patience needed during trial. A difficult day need not become a verdict upon the whole pilgrimage. We can ask for the help required to remain faithful within it. Prayer draws expectation into the present: the kingdom we await becomes the good for which we ask, and the Father’s will becomes the order to which we consent. The promised end sustains the next act of obedience without making the entire course of our future visible in advance.

Bernard’s account of growing love helps us understand how this hope can become more generous. In *On the Love of God* he begins with the human person who naturally attends to his own needs (VIII). Bodily weakness and dependence are facts of our condition. Hunger requires food; danger calls for protection; trouble brings us to seek help. Bernard does not expect a creature to begin as though it had no need to be sustained. He asks how the love that begins with ourselves can be drawn into a larger order. The command to love our neighbor as ourselves checks the impulse to make our own advantage the measure of every good.

Dependence can bring a person to seek God. The person who needs help can learn that he is neither his own creator nor his own sufficient keeper. He can turn to God because a need has become urgent and gradually recognize the goodness of the one to whom he turns. An imperfect beginning need not be despised. Many prayers first take shape through fear, illness, or the need of someone loved. What matters is whether we allow the encounter to deepen. We can receive an answer gratefully, acknowledge the giver, and remain open to his will beyond the particular relief we sought. Need becomes a place where the knowledge of God may begin to grow.

Bernard describes a further movement from loving God for the help he gives to loving him for his own goodness (IX). Frequent recourse to the divine helper brings a fuller acquaintance with the one who helps. His account is personal: the gift opens a relationship in which the giver becomes loved. A friendship among creatures can offer a limited analogy. We may first seek someone because he can teach us a skill, then come to value his wisdom and finally his person. In the life of grace the movement reaches an altogether greater object. God draws us to discover that his goodness deserves love beyond the occasions on which it has relieved an immediate need.

The distinction helps us examine our expectation of heaven. We rightly desire safety from sin, deliverance from death, and the joy of a life made whole. Yet these promises can remain loosely connected to the living God in our imagination. Bernard invites us to let the desire deepen until we love the promised communion because it is communion with him. The goodness revealed in Christ becomes the reason we want to be with Christ. Heaven is then increasingly desired as the fulfillment of a friendship already begun. We welcome the gifts through which that friendship is sustained, while learning to seek its giver in everything he allows us to receive.

Bernard’s account also enlarges love of neighbor. When God is loved for himself, the people who belong to him enter more deeply into our concern. Charity is freed from the narrow circle of those whose companionship directly benefits us. We can seek another person’s good because he is God’s creature and is called to God’s friendship. This gives hope a communal direction. We desire salvation with others and become willing to serve their journey. A household’s hospitality can express that desire by receiving someone whose need brings no immediate advantage to its members. They serve a person called to God’s friendship and begin to welcome his good as their own concern.

In the following chapter Bernard considers loving oneself for God’s sake (X). His sequence helps us see why self-forgetfulness need not mean self-contempt. The creature’s own good is brought within the goodness he loves in God. We may ask for our salvation wholeheartedly because God wills to give himself to us, and we may welcome the transformation through which we become capable of receiving that gift. Bernard’s account of degrees supplies a direction for desire. We need not turn it into a method for assigning ourselves a spiritual rank. Our motives remain mixed, and God can lead us through many returns to elementary dependence. The useful question is whether love is becoming more truthful, grateful, and ready to consent to him.

Bernard has already considered the relation of love to reward (VII). A love directed toward something other than its beloved would be valuing the beloved chiefly as a means. The reward proper to loving God is God himself. This keeps the desire for eternal happiness within charity: the person who hopes for God hopes to receive the one whom he loves and to love him more perfectly. Our good is fulfilled in belonging to him. As charity grows, we come to desire his presence for the goodness we have learned to love, and the reward is the fuller possession of that same good.

When we return to this meditation, we may discover that our longing for heaven still depends heavily upon escape from a particular burden. Bernard’s account allows us to recognize this mixed beginning and ask to know and love God more fully within it. We may also discover a new readiness to give thanks, or a freedom to serve without demanding an immediate return. These are gifts to receive gratefully, rather than measures of our whole spiritual condition. As need recurs, we can return to God with a clearer knowledge of his goodness and a greater readiness to receive his will.

## A Hope to Renew Each Year

An annual return to heaven can gather the scattered desires of a life. During the year many immediate concerns rightly claim attention, while others acquire an urgency they do not deserve. A persistent worry can occupy our attention so completely that the promised end seems remote. To consider heaven again is to recover proportion. The question is no longer merely whether the year has gone as we wished. It is whether we have allowed its goods and trials to draw us toward the communion for which we were made, and whether we are ready to receive the grace still offered.

The Church’s remembrance of the saints and the faithful departed gives this reflection a human company. The saints show what grace can accomplish in persons who once faced weakness, uncertainty, and the demands of their own time. The dead whom we commend to God remind us that the pilgrimage has a real end. We can thank him for their gifts without making ourselves judges of their final state. We can pray for their purification and eternal rest, and renew our own willingness to be purified. The communion for which we hope is already teaching us to love across the boundary of death.

Francis de Sales brings his meditation to a definite resolution because a desire left wholly indefinite can be displaced by the next attraction. The return to heaven may disclose one attachment that repeatedly rules us or one neglected good through which charity could grow. We need not produce an elaborate account of the coming year. A faithful response can begin with a concrete consent that is possible today. Keeping that resolution gives the desire a place in the decisions of the coming day. The Lord forms persons through the ordinary matter of their lives, and a repeated fidelity can become the place where a divided heart learns to love.

Paul’s desire to depart and be with Christ stands beside his readiness to remain for the good of the Philippians (Phil 1:20–26). His longing does not make their need an interruption of his true vocation. Christ is his life, whether that life is spent in further service or brought to the fulfillment he desires. This is a mature form of heavenly hope. We may long to be with the Lord while receiving the present day as the day in which he asks us to love. The timing of our summons belongs to him; the charity of our response belongs to the grace he gives now.

This hope can also remain when spiritual feeling is quiet. We may believe sincerely and still feel more apprehension than longing at the thought of death. Affection for those who depend upon us, bodily fear, and sorrow over unfinished responsibilities can coexist with trust. We can place these movements before God without pretending they have already been resolved. The desire to desire heaven is itself something we can bring to prayer. Christ’s promise is firmer than the changing strength of our response, and his patient work can deepen our readiness in ways that are hidden even from us.

God promises to make love whole. The blessed will know him directly, love him without sin, rejoice with his saints, and share the risen life of Christ in a creation made new. We await the fulfillment of these promises while receiving the grace to persevere today. We are learning to receive the one who has already given himself for us and who desires that we should be with him.

The time still given to us can be spent answering Christ’s promise: returning to him after sin, receiving his gifts, and serving those entrusted to us. We cannot know when that time will end. We can ask to remain faithful within it and to desire more fully the life he offers. The God who already draws us into friendship is preparing us to rest in him, see him, love him, and praise him without end.

## Scope, Method, and Qualifications

This essay is Catholic spiritual reading for an annual return to the hope of heaven. It treats Trinitarian communion, the beatific vision, personal identity, heavenly worship, the Beatitudes, the saints, bodily resurrection, renewed creation, present sacramental life, and growth in hope. Benedict XII’s definition and authoritative Catholic teaching are distinguished from the particular explanations of Augustine and Aquinas. Irenaeus is cited at one passage joining Eucharist and resurrection. Cyprian, Bernard, Francis de Sales, and Newman contribute particular spiritual arguments; Newman’s cited sermon predates his reception into the Catholic Church. The conditions of blessed memory, knowledge, and creaturely experience of eternity remain incompletely specified. Practical applications are synthesis. Private revelations and a complete survey of theological schools lie outside its scope. Historical translations retain their own rights status; no prayer composed or adapted for recitation is included. Source and theological checks do not constitute ecclesiastical approval.
