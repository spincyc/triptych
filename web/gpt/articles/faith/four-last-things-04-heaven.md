# The Four Last Things: The End for Which We Were Made

*Heaven: a Catholic study of the beatific vision, communion of saints, resurrection, and the new creation*

[Death](four-last-things-01-death.html) · [Judgment](four-last-things-02-judgment.html) · [Hell](four-last-things-03-hell.html) · [Heaven](four-last-things-04-heaven.html)

## The Life for Which We Were Made

Bad pictures of heaven make salvation sound like survival after everything worth loving has ended: bodiless minds, clouds, repetition, a service prolonged beyond endurance. These pictures make God one more object among others and eternal life an extension of our present routines. The Christian promise begins elsewhere: the crucified Jesus has risen, and he brings those who belong to him into his life.

In his prayer before the Passion, Jesus names eternal life as knowledge of the Father and of Jesus Christ whom the Father sent (Jn 17:1–5). Later in the same prayer he asks that his disciples be with him and see his glory, within the love that precedes the world’s foundation (17:24–26). Knowledge here belongs to communion. Salvation brings us through the Son, in the Holy Spirit, to the Father; it does not leave Christ’s humanity behind on the way to an impersonal absolute.

The Church calls this fulfillment heaven: perfect communion with the Trinity, with Mary, the angels, and all the blessed (CCC 1023–1029). Its center is the beatific vision, the immediate knowledge of God himself. Its fullness includes the resurrection of the body and renewed creation. These are dimensions of one salvation, because the God who gives himself is also the Creator who redeems his creatures.

## Desire and a Gift Beyond Our Power

Finite goods both satisfy and leave room. Beauty awakens further longing; knowledge opens new questions; love desires a communion it cannot secure against death. These goods are not fraudulent. The mistake is to demand that one creature supply the complete happiness for which we long.

Aquinas argues that no created good can be our perfect happiness, and that its fulfillment lies in the vision of God (ST I–II, q. 2, a. 8; q. 3, a. 8). Yet desire is not a claim upon God. No created intellect, human or angelic, can attain the divine essence by its natural power (ST I, q. 12, a. 4). Philosophy’s questions about our final good do not amount to a promise that God must grant supernatural communion. Christian hope receives that promise in Christ.

The promise also judges desire. A wish to dominate, possess, or avenge oneself cannot be carried unchanged into perfect charity. Other loves have good objects but need healing in the way they love. Heaven fulfills the person; it does not simply ratify the person’s present catalogue of wants. Grace makes us capable of receiving the good as gift and of rejoicing when others receive it too.

## Seeing God Without Becoming God

Benedict XII’s constitution *Benedictus Deus* (1336) teaches that the souls of the just, once purified, see the divine essence immediately, before the resurrection of their bodies and the general judgment. The vision continues without interruption. The definition guards both the reality of communion after death and its directness: no creature serves as a substitute object in place of God.

“Vision” does not mean looking at a bodily shape. Aquinas distinguishes the God who is known from the creature’s God-given capacity to know him. What he calls the light of glory perfects the intellect; it is not a picture interposed between the blessed and God (ST I, q. 12, aa. 2, 5). Grace enables a created knower to receive what exceeds every natural power.

The distinction remains forever. To know God immediately is not to comprehend him exhaustively, as though a finite mind could contain the infinite (a. 7). The blessed do not become additional divine persons, dissolve into God, or acquire God’s omniscience. Communion fulfills personal identity: the one whom Christ saves remains himself, liberated from sin’s distortion (CCC 1025).

This also explains why knowledge and love are not competitors. The blessed know God as truth and delight in him as good. The language of intellectual vision identifies the immediacy of knowing; it does not describe a cold spectator. God gives himself, and the whole person is brought to fulfillment in that gift.

## A Communion Without Rivalry

The saints are not decorative figures around an essentially private happiness. *Lumen gentium* 49 teaches that those journeying on earth, those being purified, and those in glory belong to one Church in Christ. The blessed intercede through him and in union with him. Their prayer participates in his mediation; it does not supply something his saving work lacks.

Death therefore does not make those united to Christ useless to one another. To ask a saint’s intercession is to seek help from a fellow member of his body. Mary and the saints direct attention toward the Lord whose grace made them holy. Their communion encourages our prayer for the dead and our care for the living; charity is already the bond of the life we hope to share.

Difference survives without envy. Aquinas relates different degrees of vision to the capacity perfected by grace and charity, not to natural cleverness (ST I, q. 12, a. 6). Augustine supplies an illuminating image: the finger does not resent the eye, for both belong to one body (*City of God* XXII.30). Another’s excellence becomes a shared good rather than a threat. Each blessed person is fulfilled, and none purchases happiness through another’s deprivation.

That is difficult to imagine because our present loves are mixed with comparison. We often ask whether we matter most rather than whether the beloved is happy. Heaven promises the healing of that rivalry. Its communion is personal enough for each to be known and generous enough for another’s joy to increase our own.

## The Same Person, Raised in Glory

The vision enjoyed by the purified soul is already true blessedness. Yet a human being’s destiny is not permanent separation of soul and body. The same God who receives the soul will raise and glorify the body; history awaits its public consummation. The doctrine of immediate vision and the promise of resurrection protect different aspects of the same hope.

Paul begins his great account with Christ’s Resurrection as the first fruits, then speaks of the transformation of the dead (1 Cor 15:20–28, 35–54). His seed image holds continuity and change together: what is sown in corruption is raised incorruptible. A “spiritual body” is still a body, now vivified and governed by the Spirit. It is neither a ghost nor an exchange of the person for an unrelated successor. Christ will conform our lowly bodies to his glorious body (Phil 3:20–21; CCC 999–1000).

Irenaeus gives this teaching a strikingly concrete foundation. In *Against Heresies* V.2.2–3, he argues against those who deny salvation to flesh. The body nourished by Christ’s body and blood in the Eucharist cannot be dismissed as incapable of God’s gift. He links the created bread and wine, Christ’s real flesh, our bodily nourishment, and the resurrection God grants. The argument binds sacrament and destiny together: the Creator does not save us by repudiating his handiwork.

Resurrection does not mean that every earthly arrangement continues unchanged. Christ says that the risen neither marry nor are given in marriage (Mt 22:30). The Church promises the identity and transfiguration of the person, not a duplicate of present society. We can trust that grace fulfills love without pretending to know every form its fulfillment will take.

## The World Is Also Awaiting Redemption

Romans 8:18–25 joins creation’s groaning to our expectation of bodily redemption. Revelation’s final city descends from God; God dwells with his people, death passes away, and the nations appear within the vision of renewed life (Rev 21:1–5, 24–27). Salvation does not end with souls escaping a discarded world. Creation itself awaits freedom from corruption.

The transformation remains God’s act, and its manner exceeds our knowledge. *Gaudium et spes* 39 distinguishes earthly progress from the growth of Christ’s kingdom while insisting that hope for the new earth should intensify care for this one. The fruits of dignity, fraternity, and freedom, fostered in the Spirit, will be found purified and transfigured. That gives lasting significance to work in charity without declaring every achievement or institution eternal.

The practical consequence is exacting. We may not make politics bear the promise of resurrection; we may not use resurrection to excuse neglect of justice, suffering, or the earth. Feeding a child and caring for a damaged place do not manufacture the new creation. They honor the creatures whom God calls us to love now. What is done in charity is not meaningless merely because its final form lies beyond our control.

## A Joy That Does Not Wear Out

Boredom makes sense when a finite good has been exhausted or when we are unable to attend to what is good. Neither condition describes perfected communion with God. Still, calling God infinite does not give us an itinerary of heavenly experiences. Eternal life is secure participation in his life, not a schedule we can extend indefinitely on a calendar.

Augustine’s last chapter of *City of God* brings rest, vision, love, and praise together (XXII.30). Rest means freedom from evil and frustrated desire, not inertness. He also argues that inability to sin perfects freedom: the blessed receive from God an unfailing delight in the good. Losing the capacity for self-destruction does not mean losing personal agency. Love no longer needs the possibility of betrayal in order to be free.

What becomes of painful memory? Augustine distinguishes knowing a past evil from continuing to suffer it. The healed can remember deliverance gratefully without being imprisoned in the wound. This is a theological account of the promise that God wipes away tears, not a revealed psychology of memory. We know that communion will be truthful and sorrow overcome; we do not know every mode of recognition or remembrance.

## The Painful Question of Another’s Loss

Could anyone be happy while someone loved is lost? The question exposes the limits of our imagination. An answer that makes heaven depend on cruelty would contradict charity. An answer that infers a guarantee of universal salvation would exceed the promise we have received.

The classical account begins with the blessed’s conformity to God’s just judgment. Aquinas distinguishes approval of justice from enjoyment of suffering as such (Supplement, q. 94, a. 3). His language about rejoicing in the punishment of the wicked can jar; his distinction matters precisely because it rules out pain as the good in which the blessed delight. This is a theological explanation, not an invitation to rehearse satisfaction at another’s ruin.

It does not remove every emotional difficulty. We cannot now reproduce the knowledge and healed love of the blessed. What we can do is refuse to anticipate another’s condemnation, pray for salvation, and trust that perfected charity will contain no malice. Hope for heaven should deepen our concern for people whose final destiny we cannot see.

## The Foretaste Given Now

Heaven begins in grace, although faith is not yet sight and our charity remains imperfect. The Eucharist gives this hope a bodily and ecclesial form. The risen Christ gives himself as food and unites his members; the sacrament is a pledge of future glory because of who is present and what he promises, not because it produces a particular feeling (CCC 1402–1405).

*Lumen gentium* 50 describes our closest sacramental union with the heavenly Church in the Eucharistic sacrifice. Prayer with the saints, prayer for the departed, and service of a neighbor belong within one communion. We need not wait for extraordinary experiences to begin living toward its fulfillment. Receiving Christ calls us to receive the person who needs our patience, forgiveness, or material help.

The final hope is neither private escape nor the endless preservation of our present circumstances. It is the Father bringing his children, through the Son and in the Spirit, into the fullness of his life: persons healed, bodies raised, creation renewed. Because that gift is promised, the next act of charity can be offered without demanding that this passing world supply the whole reward.

## Scope, Method, and Qualifications

This article treats Trinitarian communion, the beatific vision, personal identity, the communion of saints, bodily resurrection, renewed creation, and their present sacramental and moral consequences. Benedict XII’s definition and the Church’s authoritative teaching are distinguished from the particular explanations of Augustine and Aquinas. Irenaeus is read at one identified passage linking Eucharist and resurrection; no account of his entire eschatology is claimed. Questions of memory, creaturely experience of eternity, the blessed’s knowledge, and sensible conditions remain incompletely specified. The ethical applications are explanatory synthesis. No private revelation, complete survey of theological schools, or independent ecclesiastical or specialist approval is claimed.
