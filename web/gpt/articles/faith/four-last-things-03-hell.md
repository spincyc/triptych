# The Four Last Things: Hell

## Hell Is Eternal Punishment

Hell is the everlasting punishment of those who die in mortal sin without repenting and accepting God’s mercy. Its chief punishment is the loss of God: exclusion forever from the blessed vision and communion for which we were created. The damned also suffer the punishment Christ describes as everlasting fire. The souls of the damned suffer immediately after death; at the resurrection, their bodies too will share their punishment (CCC 1033–1038).

Christ himself declares the sentence: “Depart from me, you cursed, into everlasting fire” (Mt 25:41). These words belong to the Gospel of our salvation. The Lord who speaks them became man for us, sought sinners, forgave sins, and gave his life upon the Cross. His warning proceeds from his knowledge of the ruin from which he came to deliver us. Faith receives the warning from the same Lord whose promises it trusts.

The sentence is a judgment. God truly judges and punishes sin. The sinner’s free rejection of grace and the Judge’s just sentence belong to the same reality. Damnation does not result from a divine decision to create someone for misery: God predestines no one to hell. It requires a willful turning away from him and persistence in that rejection until death (CCC 1037). Neither does it require the sinner to desire punishment for its own sake. A person may know that a choice gravely offends God and still choose it for the satisfaction, revenge, power, or advantage it promises. The chosen evil can be loved while its eternal consequence is pushed from thought.

God created the whole person for communion with himself. The body shares in our response to his grace: we act through it, and he will raise it and judge what we have done. Everlasting blessedness is the fulfillment of that created life. Its final loss therefore reaches beyond the deprivation of any particular pleasure or possession. A good which sustains us during earthly life can never replace the end for which we exist.

Hell also differs utterly from purgatory. Those undergoing purification belong to Christ and will enter heaven. The damned are deprived of salvation; their punishment does not prepare them for the blessed vision. Nor do they cease to exist. At the end of the judgment scene Christ sets everlasting punishment beside life everlasting (Mt 25:46). Both are final destinies. The earthly interval in which a person can turn from sin and accept grace comes to an end at death (CCC 1021, 1030–1032).

We meditate upon this truth while repentance remains possible. The sinner who has spent years refusing a known duty can still turn to Christ; the passage of those years has not made his present refusal necessary. This possibility is itself mercy. Another day has been given, the Gospel remains before us, and the Church continues to minister Christ’s forgiveness. Yet the time received calls for a response now. Continuing to postpone conversion uses the very gift that makes conversion possible to prolong the sin from which we need deliverance.

As the year draws toward its close, an examination of conscience can expose how little a defended fault has changed despite everything else that happened. The occasions were different, but the excuse remained available. We may have become quicker at explaining why a duty could wait. We may also discover that grace has weakened an attachment which once seemed immovable. Such a review concerns the state of the soul before God. Outward activity alone cannot tell us whether we have grown in charity or become more practiced in avoiding its demands.

The saints considered hell in order to live and die in God’s friendship. Their meditations examine how sin estranges us from him and how his grace restores us. They also compare the good of remaining faithful with the suffering fidelity may require. Christ’s warning gives a reason to bear that suffering: the threatened loss is greater than the temporal good we are tempted to preserve by sin.

## Hear the Warning from Christ

In the Sermon on the Mount, the warning about hell enters the ordinary relations of human life. Christ speaks of anger against a brother and contemptuous speech, then commands reconciliation before the offering is brought to the altar (Mt 5:21–24). Worship and conduct toward another person cannot be sealed off from one another. A mouth that calls upon God must also answer for the words by which it humiliates someone God has made. An injury carefully preserved in memory can become the excuse for further injuries, until cruelty seems like justice to the person practicing it.

Christ addresses the heart from which such conduct proceeds. The commandment against killing reaches our treatment of the brother; the commandment against adultery reaches the deliberate lust of the heart (Mt 5:27–30). The outward act matters, and the interior consent matters. The Lord sees what can remain hidden beneath a respectable life. There are sins for which no public accusation will ever come, but concealment does not make them harmless or remove them from his judgment.

His words about the eye and the hand make the cost of fidelity unmistakable. Something as dear as a bodily member cannot be preferred to eternal life. The command calls for the decisive rejection of what leads into sin. St. Augustine, reading the parallel warning in Mark, applies the members to people held as close and useful as parts of one’s body: even such an attachment must be relinquished when it draws one away from God (*City of God* XXI.9). The renunciation can be painful precisely because the attachment is strong.

Christ’s command concerns the rejection of sin and its occasions; bodily mutilation would injure the person called to salvation. Augustine’s example makes the severity concrete. An attachment may give companionship, practical help, and the comfort of being understood while also drawing a person repeatedly into grave sin. Those real benefits explain why relinquishing it hurts. They do not remove the obligation to end the sinful conduct. Honest changes may preserve the relationship’s good; where the relationship itself remains an occasion of grave sin, ending it may be required. Obedience then involves accepting a real loss and seeking God’s help to endure it.

The Sermon ends by bringing religious profession under the same light. The narrow gate leads to life; the broad road leads to destruction (Mt 7:13–14). The breadth of a road says nothing about the goodness of its destination. Common practice does not absolve a conscience. If a dishonesty has become ordinary within a profession, or a contempt has become normal within a household, the number of people sharing it cannot make it faithful to Christ.

The following verses speak of fruit, of calling Jesus Lord, and of doing the Father’s will. Christ even describes people appealing to extraordinary works performed in his name, yet hearing that they must depart because they have worked iniquity (Mt 7:15–23). Religious language and religious gifts do not excuse an unrepented evil life. We may know the prayers, defend the faith, or be admired for a visible service and still need to obey the command we have evaded.

The two houses complete the passage. Both builders hear; one puts the words into practice, while the other does not (Mt 7:24–27). The difference appears when the houses are tried. A meditation likewise can leave the hearer convinced while a known duty remains undone. Someone who has withheld a debt may understand repentance more clearly after prayer and still avoid the creditor. Paying what is owed requires an act beyond understanding its necessity. The Lord compares the security of the house to that obedience, so the hearer must consider where his conduct still refuses what his mind acknowledges.

In Matthew’s parable of the wheat and the cockle, the master permits both to grow until the harvest (Mt 13:24–30). The present mingling of good and evil will not last forever. The servants do not determine the hour, and their eagerness to uproot everything is restrained by concern for the wheat. When Christ explains the parable, he identifies the harvest with the end of the world and the reapers with the angels. The Son of man sends them; they gather the workers of iniquity and cast them into the furnace of fire, while the just shine in their Father’s kingdom (13:36–43).

God restrains the servants while the wheat and cockle grow together, and Christ assigns the final separation to his angels. His patience gives sinners time to repent. It also forbids us to treat our own anger as the execution of his sentence. St. Alphonsus begins his consideration on the abuse of mercy with this parable: the Lord who waits for the sinner also judges persistent refusal (*Preparation for Death*, XVII.1). The sinner misuses that patience when he takes continued prosperity as permission to continue in sin.

Matthew places the parables of the treasure and the pearl between this explanation and the parable of the net (13:44–50). The kingdom is worth surrendering everything to possess. The net then presents the final separation and repeats the warning about the furnace. Desire for the kingdom and fear of its loss thus stand close together in Christ’s teaching. We cannot understand what it means to lose heaven while treating heaven as one modest good among many. The good offered is so great that every other possession must be judged in relation to it.

In Mark, the warning follows Christ’s care for the little ones and his promise concerning a cup of water given to those who belong to him (Mk 9:40–47, in the Douay–Rheims numbering). The good done to another is precious; leading another into sin is grave. The hand, foot, and eye recur, together with the worm that does not die and the unquenchable fire. Christ impresses the truth by repetition. Love must refuse to make another person’s fall the price of our pleasure, approval, or profit. We answer for the influence we willingly exert as well as for the acts we perform alone.

Lazarus lies at the gate while the rich man feasts daily. The need is nearby, embodied, and persistent. Death comes to both, and their conditions are reversed: Lazarus is comforted, while the rich man suffers torment and begs for relief (Lk 16:19–26). The story follows the teaching that one cannot serve God and mammon, and Christ’s rebuke to those who justify themselves before others while God knows their hearts (16:13–15).

The great chasm in the parable cannot be crossed. The rich man then asks that his brothers be warned, but Abraham directs them to Moses and the prophets. Their duty is to hear the word already given; an extraordinary sign would not supply the obedience they refuse to that word (16:27–31). The living reader has received the warning the brothers need. There is no need to wait for an unusual experience before attending to a neighbor or repenting of a known sin.

Matthew’s final judgment scene makes the encounter with Christ still more direct. The King names food, drink, welcome, clothing, care, and a visit. Those condemned answer as though they had never met the Lord in need, but he identifies their omission toward the least with their omission toward himself (Mt 25:31–46). The Catechism receives this warning as a summons to meet the serious needs of the poor and the little ones (CCC 1033). Charity has bodily tasks. A person can refuse it through settled indifference as well as through active hostility.

Christ knows the life that public reputation can conceal. His words about the builders and those who call him Lord give religious hearers particular cause to examine themselves. Familiarity with his teaching can make its language easy to repeat while obedience remains costly. The warning reaches us as people who have heard, and who may therefore mistake hearing for fidelity. In annual recollection we must allow a command we already know to question the conduct we have continued to defend. The Judge who will expose that refusal is speaking while it can still be confessed and forsaken.

## The Loss of God

The chief punishment of hell is the loss of God. The expression can seem brief beside images of fire, but it names the deepest deprivation a creature can suffer. God is the source of every good and the end for which we were made. The blessed behold him and live in his friendship, with every holy desire fulfilled in him. To be excluded forever from that communion is to lose the good to which the whole person is ordered. Nothing left within the created order can make up for it.

Separation from God does not mean that the condemned become independent of their Creator. They remain creatures sustained by his power. What is lost is the blessed possession of him, the communion of love and glory. This makes the loss more searching than exile from a beloved earthly place. A traveler can find another home; a person deprived of an earthly good may discover consolation in another. No other final end waits beside God to receive the soul and make it blessed.

St. John Chrysostom develops this truth in his twenty-third homily on Matthew. The homily has followed Christ’s teaching about the narrow gate, false prophets, and the tree that is cut down and cast into the fire. Chrysostom observes that the condemned person suffers both the burning and exclusion from the kingdom. He calls the latter the more grievous punishment. He also recognizes why his hearers find it difficult to grasp: they do not adequately know the greatness of the blessedness they stand to lose (*Homilies on Matthew* 23.9).

That difficulty is itself a subject for meditation. We readily understand the loss of health, money, work, companionship, or honor, because we have tasted those goods and organized our lives around them. The loss of God can seem distant if prayer is distant, if the Eucharist is received without attention, or if heaven is seldom considered. Our weak apprehension does not measure the good’s smallness. It reveals how much the heart still needs to learn to desire its true end.

Chrysostom makes the deprivation personal by placing Christ before his hearers. He asks them to think of a father who would bear any loss rather than be parted from a beloved child. Even that strong attachment cannot equal the good of being with Christ. The damned lose the blessed company of the Savior. Chrysostom dwells on the face of the Lord, on hearing oneself rejected by him, and on the love with which that Lord had first sought the sinner.

Chrysostom recalls how Christ has created and sustained us, suffered for us, given his Spirit, and offered his kingdom. These gifts establish the sinner’s debt of gratitude. The Lord who pronounces the sentence has already sought the person he judges; the face lost forever is the face of his benefactor. A Christian considering that deprivation also remembers his own baptism and forgiveness. Repeated invitations to return make continued resistance a refusal of love already received.

At this point Chrysostom turns to the hungry Christ. The Savior has given himself for the sinner, and the sinner will not give him bread. The Lord’s identification with the poor in Matthew 25 brings eternal loss into the present relation between giver and beggar. The hearer cannot safely admire the doctrine and remain unconcerned about the hungry person. The Christ whose glorious face we hope to see has entrusted a present claim upon our charity to the needy (23.9).

Chrysostom then compares the pursuit of wealth with the relief of suffering. One man spends his efforts acquiring fields and possessions; another uses his goods to relieve need, heal sickness, and release people from distress. The second becomes a refuge to others. He compares worldly power with patience under injury as well. The powerful person may be swollen with pride, while the person who bears an insult meekly possesses a dignity that death cannot strip away (23.10–12). Wealth and influence can command admiration now, but the use of them reveals whether their possessor is preparing for the kingdom.

This is a demanding account of charity because it concerns what we love. We may prefer to be known as benefactors while avoiding the person whose need is inconvenient. We may desire the respect accorded to virtue while refusing the humiliation that would let a quarrel end. Chrysostom’s treatment of the loss of Christ presses beneath those arrangements. The heart must be prepared for the Lord himself. Possessions, influence, and public esteem are to serve that end, and become dangerous when we protect them at its expense.

St. Alphonsus begins his consideration of hell with the two movements of sin: turning away from God and turning toward a creature. The created good is sought against the order established by its Creator. Accordingly he distinguishes the pain of loss from the other punishments, placing the loss of God first in gravity (*Preparation for Death*, XXVI.1). His distinction explains what the sinner is choosing. We seek a particular satisfaction, while the grace and friendship we surrender can receive scarcely a moment’s serious attention.

In the third point, Alphonsus considers how grief over a loss grows with our estimate of what has been lost. His example is a precious jewel: its value makes its loss more keenly felt. But God exceeds every scale of exchange. The soul’s final deprivation cannot be understood as losing one valuable object while retaining enough others to be content. The good lost is infinite. Alphonsus brings the consideration back to Christ’s sentence of departure and to the terrible permanence of never seeing him in glory (XXVI.3).

The saint then turns his meditation toward the grace still available. His prayers recognize that the living sinner can yet love God, repent, and recover friendship with him. The possibility of loving him now is already a great mercy. We can share that gratitude without pretending that we have measured the depths of eternal loss. To recognize God as our supreme good is enough to make a beginning: the attachment which costs his friendship must be renounced, and the love he commands must be sought from him.

We can dwell upon this loss without continually seeking a more alarming image. Consider the good of belonging to Christ and the attachment for which we endanger that friendship. The attraction of the attachment is familiar; we have rehearsed its advantages whenever temptation returns. Prayer gives time to consider what we have left out of that calculation. A satisfaction which passes cannot compensate for the everlasting deprivation of God. The choice becomes clearer when the will considers both what it wants immediately and what it risks losing.

## The Fire and the Resurrection of the Body

Christ warns of punishment as well as exclusion. The fire, the weeping, the gnashing of teeth, and the worm that does not die describe the affliction of the condemned under divine judgment. They suffer the loss of blessedness and the punishment pronounced by the Judge. A person considering his own sin must therefore attend to more than the happiness he has failed to seek. He has offended God, and he is answerable for that offense. The mercy offered now delivers him from a just sentence which Christ describes in these terrible terms.

The Fourth Lateran Council confesses that all will rise in their own bodies and receive according to their deeds: the reprobate perpetual punishment with the devil, the elect everlasting glory with Christ (*Firmiter credimus*, DS 801). The contrast is between two ends of the same bodily resurrection, not between persons who rise and persons who disappear. Christ likewise teaches a resurrection of life and a resurrection of judgment (Jn 5:28–29). The body which shares the labor and choices of earthly life will share the person’s final condition.

St. Cyril of Jerusalem unfolds this teaching while instructing those preparing for baptism. In his eighteenth catechetical lecture, he insists upon the identity of the body raised: the very body which is now mortal will be raised, though transformed. The Lord who made it can bring about what exceeds our present experience. Cyril distinguishes the glory of the righteous from the enduring punishment of the sinner; the latter’s body endures suffering without being consumed (*Catechetical Lecture* 18.18–19).

Cyril explains why bodily conduct belongs to this account. The mouth can pray or blaspheme. The body can preserve chastity or commit fornication. The hand can steal or give alms. The same body has served the person in these actions and will share in their final outcome. His examples identify powers which can be used either well or badly. Bodily resurrection therefore bears directly on the catechumen’s preparation for a changed life: the body that God will raise is already involved in the obedience or disobedience for which the person must answer.

His next exhortation is to care for the body and not misuse it as though it were not our own (18.20). The Creator witnesses what no human observer sees. Secrecy changes the social circumstances of an act; it does not remove the act from the life God knows. A private cruelty, a concealed theft, or an impure use of another person remains the deed of a bodily creature whose whole existence belongs to the Lord. Reverence for the body includes reverence for its destiny and for the bodies of other people.

Cyril speaks to those approaching baptism of the cleansing God gives, and then urges vigilance against future wounds. The body which needs cleansing is the body God receives into the life of grace. His exhortation to guard it follows from that gift. A baptized Christian returning through Penance can apply the same concern to an established bodily habit. Repeated actions have made one response familiar and easy; repentance may require patient changes before temptation becomes acute. God’s forgiveness restores the penitent, who must then cease using his body to renew the fault he has confessed.

St. Augustine considers the scriptural imagery in the twenty-first book of the *City of God*. He receives Christ’s repeated warning about unquenchable fire and affirms that the body itself will suffer by fire. He records different interpretations of the worm: some understand bodily affliction, while others understand the anguish of the soul (XXI.9). The interpretations differ over what that particular image describes. Both concern the enduring misery of the condemned.

In the following chapter, Augustine asks how material fire can afflict spirits. He affirms that it can do so by God’s power while acknowledging a mode of connection beyond our comprehension (XXI.10). Ordinary experience of fire cannot explain how a spirit suffers from it. Augustine reasons from the Creator’s power over both spiritual and bodily creatures. He can affirm the punishment Christ reveals while admitting that he cannot describe the connection which makes it possible.

Augustine also distinguishes the rich man’s experience in Luke 16, before the final resurrection, from the bodily punishment at the end. The parable uses eyes, tongue, flame, and a finger carrying water to speak of an experience occurring while the dead remain without their risen bodies. Its reality does not depend upon turning every feature into a diagram.

The fallen angels are punished too. Christ calls the fire prepared for the devil and his angels; they are subjects of divine judgment. Their rebellion ends in punishment under the Creator whose authority they rejected. The sinner condemned with them shares that punishment. In Matthew’s sentence, the Judge commands departure into the fire he has prepared, and those he condemns must undergo his sentence. The spirits who tempted people away from obedience also suffer the final defeat of their own disobedience.

The resurrection requires us to consider what we are doing with the body that will be raised. Cyril’s examples make amendment possible to identify. A person who has used his hand to steal must cease taking what belongs to another and restore what he can. The same hand can then serve another’s need. These are successive acts of the person God calls to salvation, who remains responsible for his bodily life. Care for the body includes directing its powers toward holiness while grace and time permit amendment.

## A Punishment That Does Not End

Every earthly affliction is endured within a life that passes. We may not know when relief will come, but time can bring a change, and death itself puts an end to temporal suffering. Hell has no last day. The punishment does not exhaust itself, finish a purification, or reach a point at which the condemned can begin another earthly trial. Christ calls it everlasting.

St. Augustine attends closely to the final sentence of Matthew 25. Everlasting punishment and life everlasting stand within the same declaration, describing the two outcomes of judgment. He argues that the endless life of the saints and the endless punishment of the condemned must both be received from that sentence (*City of God* XXI.23). The ground of his certainty is the truthfulness of the divine word. He will not set a conjectured eventual release above what Christ has said.

The chapter first considers the devil and his angels, to whom the same fire is appointed. Augustine then observes that the sentence joins the punishment of the wicked to theirs. His concern is the consistency of faith before a single Lord and Judge. The word which secures our hope of an everlasting heaven also teaches the permanence of hell.

Augustine also urges obedience while opportunity remains. The permanence of the revealed sentence gives the hearer a reason to attend to God’s commands now. The argument has a practical consequence for someone who continues to dispute a duty he already recognizes. Further delay does not resolve the difficulty of obeying; it adds another refusal to those already made. Recollection can expose that repetition. The person needs to ask for grace to do what is required, even when he has not ceased to feel the attraction which first led him away.

The second death names this final ruin. In the thirteenth book, Augustine distinguishes bodily death, in which the soul leaves the body, from the death of the soul deprived of its good life with God. In final damnation, soul and body are reunited and the person continues to suffer. The soul animates the body, yet the whole person lacks blessed life with God. The condition is called death because of its penal misery, not because the creature ceases to exist (XIII.2).

This distinction changes the measure of our fear. Bodily death is a real enemy and a severe trial. Yet to avoid it by grave sin would place temporal survival above the life of grace. A person may lose earthly life in fidelity to Christ and gain everlasting life. Another may preserve health, security, and reputation while making choices that lead toward the second death.

The martyrdom of St. Polycarp gives this ordering of fear a living form. The Church of Smyrna recounts his confession before the magistrate. When pressed to reproach Christ, he remembers the Lord he has served and refuses to blaspheme his King and Savior. Threats of beasts follow, then the threat of fire. Polycarp answers by distinguishing a fire that burns for a little time from the fire of coming judgment and eternal punishment (*Martyrdom of Polycarp* 9–11).

Polycarp remembers Christ’s faithful goodness when the magistrate demands blasphemy. His refusal answers a particular demand to betray his King and Savior. The threats do not change the character of that act, though they make obedience more painful. Smyrna’s narrative then describes his confidence and joy (12). His answer about eternal fire explains why a threat of burning cannot compel his consent. He can lose bodily life while remaining faithful to the Lord who gives everlasting life.

The martyr’s decision also clarifies choices in which the threatened loss is smaller. A profitable deception may seem difficult to abandon because others depend upon the income and the arrangement is longstanding. Those circumstances call for an honest way of meeting real obligations; they cannot make continued fraud right. The loss required by amendment belongs to this passing life. Polycarp’s confession helps us judge that cost in relation to the good of remaining faithful.

St. Alphonsus dwells on precisely this difference in his consideration on the eternity of hell. Even a limited pain becomes harder to bear as it continues, yet hell goes beyond every duration we can count. His argument rests on the scriptural teaching of everlasting punishment, then measures the fleeting pleasure of sin against that unending loss (*Preparation for Death*, XXVII.1). Any number of years would still have an end; the revealed punishment has none.

The brevity of sinful pleasure often becomes evident even within this life. Anticipation magnifies it; possession soon passes. What remains may be guilt, damage to another, and the need to construct a further excuse. Eternity exposes the poverty of consenting to a grave evil for so little. It asks us to judge the promise of temptation by the end to which it leads, before its immediate attraction has taken command of the will.

St. Cyprian brings finality and present mercy together in his address to Demetrianus. He describes enduring punishment of soul and body, with sorrow that can no longer bring fruitful repentance. Then he turns to the person he is addressing and urges him to seek salvation while life remains (*Ad Demetrianum* 24–25). The opportunity will not remain forever; it has not yet ended. Neither age nor the number of past sins should prevent a living person from returning to God.

Cyprian addresses hostility toward Christians, yet offers his persecutors the way to rejoice with Christians eternally. He grounds this offered life in Christ’s Cross, blood, and reconciliation of humanity to the Father. Their hostility has injured the people with whom he invites them to share everlasting joy. His appeal asks them to cease that hostility and receive the reconciliation Christ gives.

Cyprian’s invitation addresses someone whose sins are already numerous and whose remaining life may be short. Neither fact prevents a present return to God. The years spent cannot be recovered, but their misuse can be repented of; the hostility practiced can cease. This is why the permanent sentence must be considered while we are still living. A sinner can now receive the mercy which Cyprian offers his persecutors. If shame over the past keeps him from returning, he is refusing the very reconciliation by which Christ heals that past.

## Mortal Sin and the Life of Grace

The danger of hell concerns our relation to God now. Mortal sin destroys charity in the heart and deprives the soul of sanctifying grace. It turns the person away from God as his last end. Venial sin wounds charity while leaving that friendship intact. The difference concerns the life of the soul, and an honest examination must preserve it (CCC 1854–1856).

St. Thomas Aquinas explains the gravity of mortal sin by considering the principle which orders our will to God. That principle is adherence to our last end through charity. When sin destroys it, the sinner cannot repair the supernatural loss by his own power. The damage is irreparable from the resources of the fallen person himself, though God can restore what sin has destroyed (ST I–II, q. 87, a. 3).

The last end is the good for whose sake all other goods must be ordered. A Christian may affirm that God is his end while treating a particular desire as exempt from that ordering. A person who will knowingly commit a grave injustice in order to retain wealth has, in that choice, preferred the lesser good to God’s law and friendship. The whole life need not be outwardly disorderly for such a choice to be serious. A single deliberate refusal in grave matter can contradict the charity by which the soul belongs to God.

Nor does the fact that the sinful act has finished mean that the relation has healed itself. A lie can be over as a spoken event while its harm remains. A betrayal can cease as an outward act while the betrayer still refuses repentance. Mortal sin demands a return of the will to God and the forgiveness he alone gives. Time can make the memory less vivid without making the sinner reconciled. We therefore need to examine what has been repented of, as well as what has ceased to trouble us.

Three conditions must be present together for a personal sin to be mortal: grave matter, full knowledge, and deliberate consent (CCC 1857). Grave matter concerns the act’s seriousness; knowledge and consent concern the person’s responsible choice. This protects the examination from both carelessness and confusion. We must consider the real evil of an act and the freedom with which we chose it. An alarming feeling by itself settles neither question.

Temptation is not consent. A thought can present itself against our will; an attraction can be felt while the will refuses it; fear or agitation can persist after a faithful refusal. The presence of struggle is therefore no proof that grace has been lost. The question is what we knowingly and freely did with the temptation. In the same way, having little emotional disturbance after a grave choice does not turn that choice into innocence. Conscience must be formed by truth, not by the intensity or absence of feeling.

Ignorance, duress, fear, habit, and other impediments can diminish responsibility; deliberate cultivation of ignorance and hardness of heart do not excuse it (CCC 1735, 1859–1860). God knows the whole act and the person who performs it. An examination of conscience should be candid about circumstances without making them a shelter for dishonesty. Where the conscience remains troubled and uncertain, the counsel of a steady confessor helps it attend to what is real. Repeatedly reopening settled doubts can distract from the acts of trust and obedience which are presently required.

Venial sin also deserves repentance. It leaves charity alive but weakens its exercise, nourishes disordered attachment, and can dispose the soul toward more serious consent (CCC 1862–1863). Its danger is often the ease with which it becomes cherished. We begin to regard a particular unkindness, vanity, indulgence, or evasion as something we are entitled to keep. The annual examination can uncover this consent to remain unchanged, even where no grave sin has occurred.

The distinction between mortal and venial sin gives proportion to that examination. It does not make venial sin desirable, nor does it turn every imperfection into eternal catastrophe. A person who loves God wants to be rid of whatever offends him. The motive can mature from avoiding destruction to refusing even a smaller injury to friendship.

An examination becomes more truthful when it follows one defended fault through its excuses and effects. We may have admitted impatience in general while refusing to acknowledge the particular person we repeatedly humiliate. Naming the conduct makes repentance less evasive. It also helps us recognize grace at work when the same provocation no longer draws the same willing cruelty. The annual review can then include thanksgiving for a change we did not produce unaided. Sorrow and gratitude both require attention to what actually happened, rather than a general judgment that we have been either good or bad.

The soul that discovers mortal sin is being called to return. Aquinas’s account of the lost principle does not close the door upon the living sinner; it tells him where help must come from. We cannot bargain ourselves back into charity or erase the past by becoming impressive in another part of life. We can receive Christ’s mercy, consent to the conversion he gives, and submit the wound to the sacrament he has established.

## Do Not Become Another Person’s Tempter

Christ places the warning about the little ones immediately before the warning about the hand, foot, and eye. He speaks with terrible severity of the person who causes one of his believing little ones to fall (Mk 9:41). Renunciation must therefore include conduct by which we endanger someone else. A person may be concerned about resisting his own temptations and still persuade another to do what he knows is wrong. The other’s salvation also places a claim upon his conduct. Pleasure, convenience, or standing among others cannot justify drawing a soul away from God.

The Church calls this evil scandal. The word here concerns leading someone into sin. Public embarrassment or the discovery of a fault may accompany it, but its deepest harm is spiritual. A person can cause scandal quietly, in a conversation no one else hears, by making evil attractive or by teaching another to dismiss the voice of conscience. The Catechism describes the scandal-giver as the neighbor’s tempter and teaches the gravity of deliberately leading another into a grave offense (CCC 2284).

St. Thomas considers scandal among the sins opposed to charity. He begins with the image of an obstacle placed in a traveler’s path: a word or deed can become a stumbling block on the spiritual way. Commands, inducements, and examples can all draw another toward sin (*Summa theologiae* II–II, q. 43, a. 1). Each possibility asks something different of the conscience. We may have ordered what was wrong, persuaded someone to join us, or made our own conduct appear a safe pattern for imitation.

Aquinas also preserves the distinction between our influence and another person’s will. Our word can provide an occasion for his sin; it does not replace his consent (a. 1, reply 3). This keeps responsibility truthful on both sides. We cannot excuse our deliberate encouragement by saying that the other person chose freely. Neither should we imagine that every later fault in someone we have known was secretly caused by us. The examination concerns the real help we gave to evil, including what our position or example reasonably communicated.

Authority makes this responsibility more searching. A child receives more than instructions from a parent; the child also observes which instructions the parent treats as binding. A younger worker learns what integrity costs by seeing whether a superior protects it under pressure. The Catechism therefore attends to the authority of the scandal-giver, the weakness of the person influenced, and the special duties of those who teach or educate (CCC 2285–2287). Using such trust to undermine a conscience betrays the person who relies upon us.

The temptation can be concealed beneath an offer of belonging. A friend hesitates to join a dishonest practice, and we work upon his hesitation until he is ashamed of resisting. We may tell ourselves that we are relieving an unnecessary scruple, though we know why the act is wrong. His refusal also exposes our own conduct, and persuading him would make that exposure easier to dismiss. The invitation thus seeks two advantages for us at his expense: companionship in the act and relief from its accusation. Concern for his salvation requires us to respect the conscience we were trying to silence.

The influence of example can outlast the act. An excuse taught in a household may be carried into another household by someone who learned it there. We cannot calculate every later consequence, but we can examine the conduct we taught people to admire or dismiss. A younger person may have learned that fidelity is foolish because we regularly mocked it. Repentance then requires attention to the lesson as well as the original act. If our example made resistance harder, a candid acknowledgment of its wrongness may help the person we influenced recover confidence in what his conscience told him.

Care for another’s soul also requires discernment. Aquinas distinguishes giving scandal from another person’s taking occasion to sin even from a good act (a. 1, reply 4). Someone may resent a just refusal or envy another’s virtue. That reaction does not make obedience an injury. We are not required to surrender God’s command whenever it displeases someone. Otherwise concern for scandal could itself become a way of compelling sin, with the most unreasonable person determining the conduct of everyone nearby.

In treating spiritual goods, Thomas refuses the proposal that one should commit sin in order to prevent someone else’s sin. He also asks for patience with the weak. An action which can prudently be deferred, or a matter which needs explanation, should be handled with attention to the person’s capacity and need. The truth must be taught faithfully, with judgment concerning persons and occasions (a. 7). This leaves room for gentleness, a quieter manner, and a better time. It leaves no room for using charity as the name of a lie or for presenting an evil as good.

Repentance for scandal accordingly seeks the neighbor’s good as well as our own pardon. A harmful instruction may need to be withdrawn. A person whom we pressured may need to hear that his original hesitation was right. An arrangement which rewards dishonesty may need to be changed. Such correction is to be proportionate and prudent; it need not spread a hidden sin to people who had no knowledge of it. The aim is to stop providing help to evil and, where possible, remove the false lesson our conduct has given.

A good example can also assist another person’s freedom. When a superior accepts a loss rather than demand wrongdoing, those beneath him see that integrity can survive its cost. He removes the pressure his authority might have added to temptation. A parent who admits a fault likewise makes it easier for a child to acknowledge the truth about his own. Christ promises a reward for the cup of water given because someone belongs to him. Such assistance serves a person whom he calls to eternal life, and belongs beside the refusal to obstruct that person’s way to God.

## Mercy Received and Mercy Abused

St. Alphonsus describes three acts of divine mercy: God waits for the sinner, calls the sinner, and receives the sinner who returns (*Preparation for Death*, XVI.1–3). Each act concerns a different need. The sinner needs time in which to repent, an awakening from his refusal, and reconciliation when he returns. His conversion depends upon a goodness already at work during his estrangement. Alphonsus asks him to consider that prior mercy so that he can recognize why a present return is possible and give thanks for the help he has received.

The first act is God’s patience. Alphonsus asks the sinner to consider the life preserved and the benefits received even during a time of infidelity. The ordinary continuance of life can conceal this gift. We wake, eat, work, make plans, and assume another day will follow. But the time in which a person can repent is not self-created or guaranteed. To find oneself still able to turn toward God is a reason for gratitude.

The second act is the call. Alphonsus names inspirations, the reproach of conscience, preaching, trials, and the deaths of friends among the ways a sinner is awakened. Their common purpose in his meditation is reconciliation. We may have resisted such a summons because it disturbed an arrangement we wanted to preserve. A serious recollection asks whether the same truth has been reaching us repeatedly while we have kept assigning obedience to a later time.

The third act is reception. Alphonsus dwells on Christ’s parables of the lost sheep and the returning son. In Luke 15 the father sees the son coming, runs, embraces him, and restores him to the household (Lk 15:11–24). The son has acknowledged his sin and turned homeward. The father’s generous reception answers the fear that this return can bring only rejection. The son comes with a truthful confession, and he receives more than he had dared to request. A sinner who has resolved to return can approach Christ’s mercy with that confidence.

Shame can become an obstacle at precisely this point. A person may be willing to consider hell in the abstract yet unwilling to say a humiliating truth in confession. St. Francis de Sales addresses that difficulty after his early meditations on the last things. He urges a courageous, humble confession and distinguishes the shame of committing sin from the goodness of confessing it. He places the penitent, in imagination, at the feet of the crucified Savior, receiving the fruit of his Passion (*Introduction to the Devout Life*, I.19).

Francis places self-accusation before the Savior who suffered for the penitent. Confession brings the sin to Christ for healing through the ministry of the priest. The penitent must therefore speak truthfully even when the admission is humiliating. Concealment would preserve the fault at the moment he has come to seek forgiveness for it. He can accept that humiliation because Christ’s Passion has already shown how much his reconciliation matters to the Lord. The counsel and penance he receives then concern the amendment of a life restored by mercy.

Contrition includes sorrow for sin and a purpose of amendment. Fear of eternal punishment can give rise to imperfect contrition, itself a gift of grace, which disposes the sinner for forgiveness in Penance but does not alone remit grave sin. Perfect contrition arises from loving God above all things and obtains forgiveness of mortal sin when joined to the firm resolution of sacramental confession as soon as possible (CCC 1451–1453).

A purpose of amendment is a present willingness to forsake the sin. It does not mean possessing a private certainty that no weakness will ever recur. The penitent can know his frailty while sincerely resolving to resist, use the necessary helps, and return promptly if he falls. But he cannot preserve a deliberate intention to continue the grave evil and call that intention repentance.

Alphonsus addresses the opposite distortion in the next consideration, on the abuse of divine mercy. A person proposes to commit the sin now and confess it afterward. The future act of repentance is used to excuse the present act of rebellion (XVII.1). This is presumption. It treats the mercy which calls us out of sin as though it had promised permission to remain in it until we choose a convenient moment to stop.

The disorder lies in the present choice even before uncertainty about the future is considered. A good intention for tomorrow cannot turn today’s deliberate grave evil into obedience. Moreover, tomorrow’s repentance is itself a grace; it cannot be put under our control as an item already secured. Presumption claims the benefit of conversion while rejecting conversion’s demand. The sinner who has made this bargain can repent of it now, including the misuse of mercy among the sins brought to Christ.

Repentance also reaches what the sin has done to another person. Forgiveness does not make a stolen possession rightfully ours or restore a damaged reputation without our cooperation. We must repair harm as far as possible: return what was taken, correct the false account, make compensation where it is due, and accept the penance given (CCC 1459–1460). Such acts give bodily expression to the change of heart.

The forgiven person must accept that his restored life is a gift. Repeated self-accusation cannot take the place of the amendment and reparation now required. If he has damaged a reputation, continuing to dwell on his guilt while leaving the false account uncorrected prolongs the injury. Receiving mercy should make him more willing to perform the difficult act that benefits his neighbor. The remembrance of hell then includes gratitude for deliverance: he can act as someone whom Christ has received again, using the grace restored to practice the charity his sin contradicted.

## The Divided Will and the Grace to Return

The need to repent can be clear while the will remains divided. St. Augustine describes this condition with painful candor in the eighth book of his *Confessions*. By this stage of his account he can no longer shelter behind uncertainty about the truth. He admires the surrender made by others and knows that he ought to give himself to God. Yet he remains attached to the life from which he longs to be freed. His account helps us recognize the interior resistance which can survive even an earnest meditation on eternal punishment.

Augustine traces a chain from disordered willing through indulgence to habit. Repeated indulgence has made the old desire strong, while his newer desire to serve God has not yet overcome it (VIII.5.10). The chain is his own history of consent. It has not appeared from nowhere, and he does not describe himself as merely an innocent spectator of what his desires have become. At the same time, he knows their present force from within. The past has formed a difficulty which a bare announcement of good intentions has failed to remove.

Augustine’s account explains why the formation of a habit deserves attention before it has become deeply established. Each consent makes another consent easier, and a repeated excuse becomes readily available when conscience protests. His narrative also gives hope to the person who already knows that difficulty. Augustine writes after his deliverance and remembers how little his own resolutions had accomplished. The same history which exposes his responsibility records what grace has healed. A person recognizing himself in the chain can therefore ask for help without denying either his own past consent or God’s power to free him.

He compares his condition to someone struggling to wake from a heavy sleep. He knows that waking is better, but the comfort of remaining asleep keeps defeating the movement to rise. The promise of getting up soon becomes a way of continuing to lie still (VIII.5.12). This image reaches a form of delay more evasive than open denial. We can agree with every warning, intend some future amendment, and continue the same refusal. Agreement then gives us the appearance of conversion while leaving its decisive act indefinitely postponed.

The danger is particularly clear when we think of final impenitence. We need not announce an intention to reject God forever in order to keep rejecting a present call. We can instead reserve today for the attachment and imagine tomorrow as the day in which everything will change. Augustine’s recollection lets us hear the weakness of that bargain from inside a life. Time by itself had not supplied the surrender he was withholding. He needed grace to consent now, in the actual struggle, with the desires that made consent difficult.

In the garden with Alypius, the conflict becomes acute. Augustine can command bodily movements and perform them, yet cannot simply command himself into the complete willing he desires. He discovers that part of him wills and part resists (VIII.8.19–9.21). In the next chapter he insists that both movements belong to the same person. He is not two beings, one innocent and one guilty. The divided will is his, and the healing must become his.

His candor can free an examination from vague language. We may say that circumstances prevent a return when we mean that we fear its cost. We may say that we cannot give something up when the truth is that we have not yet consented to the loss. Other impediments may be real and deserve patient help; Augustine is describing his own responsible resistance. The reader should bring the same honesty to his particular case.

Augustine personifies his old pleasures as voices tugging at him while he turns away. They ask whether he can live without them. Their claim is persuasive because they are familiar, while the freedom being offered remains untried (VIII.11.25–26). Temptation thus presents conversion as an empty future deprived of its most necessary goods. The sinner can fear deliverance almost as though it were destruction. A life without the cherished wrong may feel unimaginable precisely because the wrong has occupied the imagination for so long.

Against those voices Augustine sets the figure of Continence, accompanied by the examples of people of different ages and conditions who have received her gift. They have not achieved fidelity by possessing a nature altogether unlike his. God has given them what they could not securely give themselves. The invitation is to stop attempting to stand upon his own strength and entrust himself to the Lord who can receive and heal him (VIII.11.27). The good life appears as fruitful and gracious, and the heart is invited to believe that obedience can be lived.

At the turning point, Augustine hears the repeated summons to take up and read, opens the apostolic book, and receives the command to put on Christ and stop providing for sinful desire. He recounts the inward assurance that followed. Alypius joins him, and Monica receives the news with thanksgiving (VIII.12.28–30). The command addresses the provision Augustine had been making for the desire that divided him. He must cease arranging his life for its continuance. His narrative brings the long argument with himself to a consent which he receives the grace to make.

Augustine has described a struggle in which the danger is known and surrender remains difficult. An annual meditation can expose the same distance between conviction and consent. Its urgency calls the person to stop postponing the duty he acknowledges; Augustine’s thanksgiving gives him reason to ask for the grace he needs. The old attachment may still appear indispensable. That judgment has been shaped by repeated indulgence, and Augustine’s deliverance shows why it need not determine the future. He who once feared to live without it can remember it afterward as a bondage from which God freed him.

## Fear Brought Before the Savior

Fear of hell can interrupt the movement toward sin. When a desired pleasure fills the imagination and love has become faint, remembrance of the end can give the will a reason to refuse. St. Ignatius of Loyola expressly seeks this help in the First Week of the *Spiritual Exercises*. The request introducing his meditation on hell asks for an interior sense of its pains so that, if faults cause the exercitant to forget the love of the eternal Lord, fear may help keep him from sin (nos. 65–71).

Ignatius asks for fear’s assistance when the love of the Lord has been forgotten through one’s faults. The exercitant knows the evil of sin, but its attraction may occupy his imagination more powerfully than its consequences. He needs to recognize inwardly what his judgment already acknowledges. Remembering punishment can interrupt his willing pursuit of a pleasure that has seemed harmless because its end remained distant. The request seeks help for that moment of weakness, when a truth confessed in general must become a reason for refusing a particular act.

His method engages the imagination through the five senses. The exercitant considers the fire, the cries, the bitterness, and the other pains presented in the exercise. Sight is not left to wander after the object of temptation; hearing, taste, smell, and touch are drawn into the consideration of its end. The method presses against the habit of imagining sin’s attraction vividly while keeping its consequences abstract.

These are images used in meditation, not a new revelation of the physical arrangement of hell. Ignatius asks the exercitant to consider the horror of condemnation before the Savior. A person who can profitably use the senses in this way should keep the exercise directed toward its intended fruit. Another may be held more deeply by Christ’s words of departure, by the permanence of the loss, or by remembrance of mercy. In each case the will is to become more ready to refuse sin and belong to God.

Ignatius ends with a colloquy addressed to Christ. The exercitant recalls those who did not believe and those who believed but did not obey, then gives thanks that his own life has been preserved. He remembers how the Lord has continued to show him pity and mercy. The remembrance of punishment thus brings him to a personal acknowledgment of what he has received. He has been given time to return to Christ, despite faults for which he can claim no excuse. Gratitude makes his continued refusal still harder to defend.

The colloquy also gives the frightened sinner someone to whom he can turn. Recognizing the danger does not itself supply the strength to resist temptation. In prayer he brings his weakness and past resistance to the Lord who bore the Passion for sinners. A resolution dependent only upon his present feeling will weaken when the feeling passes. Recourse to Christ asks for help during the temptation itself, when the attraction returns and the warning again becomes easy to dismiss. Ignatius’s meditation ends in that dependence upon the Savior.

Fear also needs truth about its own object. The evil to be feared is sin and final separation from God. God himself is good, and his goodness makes that separation dreadful. A fear that drives a person to conceal sin or flee the sacrament has lost its proper direction.

As charity grows, sorrow becomes more deeply concerned with God himself. The soul grieves that it has offended such goodness and refused such love. Fear need not be disowned for this to happen. It can continue to guard fidelity while love gives a fuller motive for it. Polycarp’s confession shows the union: he knows the eternal danger, and he refuses to betray his King and Savior.

The fruit of this meditation can be considered in the act which follows it. A quiet refusal of a familiar temptation may require more fidelity than a strong emotion during prayer. The will has to obey when the object again appears attractive and the imagined consequences seem remote. Gratitude to Christ can help sustain that obedience, because the penitent remembers whose mercy he is receiving. Intense feeling may come or remain absent. What needs attention afterward is the willingness to use the help offered when the occasion for sin returns.

Ignatius’s surrounding instruction on general confession also preserves proportion. He proposes it to those who choose it freely, explains its possible benefits, and states that one who confesses annually is not thereby obliged to make a general confession (no. 44). An annual preparation for death should therefore be undertaken with sound counsel. Its purpose is a sincere return and a more faithful life.

## The Fear That Grows with Love

Fear of punishment can begin to loosen the hold of sin, while fear of offending God deepens as love becomes stronger. St. Thomas distinguishes these concerns in his treatment of the gift of fear. A person may first refrain because he dreads the penalty and then become more attentive to the offense against the One he loves. The concerns can remain together while charity grows. His account explains how an imperfect beginning can prepare the will for a fuller response to God, and why increasing love need not make the Christian careless about salvation.

Thomas begins by asking in what sense God is to be feared. God is goodness itself; we do not fear him as though he were an evil. We rightly fear his just punishment, and we fear being separated from him through our fault. His justice gives reason for fear, while his mercy gives reason for hope (*Summa theologiae* II–II, q. 19, a. 1). The two movements can therefore belong to the same soul before the same Lord.

The fear called servile regards punishment. The fear called filial regards offending God, as a child fears to offend a beloved father (a. 2). The names identify different objects of concern. In the first, the evil that arrests attention is what sin will bring upon the sinner. In the second, the heart has become attentive to the wrong of turning against the One it loves. Filial fear thus includes a personal relation to God, received through charity, and a desire to remain faithfully within that relation.

Thomas teaches that fear of punishment is good in itself, while distinguishing it from the servility which resists love. A person may refrain from a crime because he dreads its penalty, yet still cherish the injustice and wish he could commit it safely. The defect lies in the refusal to love justice, not in recognizing that punishment is dreadful (a. 4). A meditation on hell should welcome the warning’s power to restrain sin and ask that the heart itself be changed. Mere outward restraint leaves work for grace to accomplish within.

Nor must a person prove that he loves God by becoming indifferent to his own salvation. Thomas explains that concern for one’s own good can be ordered within charity. Fear of punishment can remain alongside love when the person’s own comfort has not become his supreme end. Indeed, charity intensely rejects the loss of God, which is itself the chief punishment (a. 6). We were made for communion with him. To desire that communion and dread its loss is consistent with loving him above all things.

This distinction brings clarity to a conscience troubled by mixed motives. A penitent can be sorry that he offended God and also afraid of the punishment he deserved. The presence of the latter concern does not by itself make the former unreal. He should turn toward God with the contrition he has, seek the sacrament, and ask for a more generous love. An endless effort to separate every movement of the heart can become another way of delaying obedience.

Thomas calls fear a beginning of wisdom in more than one way. Fear of punishment can first restrain the sin that obstructs a wise life. Filial fear belongs to wisdom already beginning its work: the person reveres God and submits to being ruled by him (a. 7). The difference becomes visible in ordinary conduct. Someone may initially stop a dishonest act because judgment has become frightening. As love matures, he also wants the truth to govern his dealings because they are offered to God.

The gift of fear belongs to this filial disposition. Thomas describes a soul becoming responsive to the Holy Spirit through reverence and through refusal to separate itself from God (a. 9). This has a quiet firmness. The person becomes more willing to be corrected, less determined to reserve one protected area against God’s command, and more ready to acknowledge dependence. The Lord’s nearness does not make him ordinary or place him at our disposal. Familiarity in prayer can deepen awe when the soul remembers whose friendship it has received.

Filial fear and hope strengthen one another. Hope relies upon God’s help; filial fear guards against withdrawing from that help (a. 9, reply 1). The soul does not become more hopeful by pretending that its own refusal is harmless. It becomes more hopeful by trusting the giver of grace and more watchful because it does not want to resist him. This union corrects both the arrogance which presumes upon pardon and the discouragement which treats amendment as impossible.

Thomas teaches that filial fear grows as charity grows, even while the active fear of punishment diminishes. Greater love makes offense and separation more hateful; closer adherence to God gives greater confidence (a. 10). This growth may appear in a quicker willingness to accept correction. A fault once defended has become grievous because the person more clearly recognizes whom it offends. He also has greater reason to seek help promptly: his hope rests upon the God whose friendship he values. Thomas’s account explains how reverence and confidence can increase together in the same person.

A person beginning with fear of punishment can therefore pray for charity without pretending that he has ceased to dread the punishment. He should use the motive which restrains him and ask to love God more fully. Thomas’s distinction prevents him from either despising the help already received or treating outward restraint as the whole of conversion. In the next temptation, the good first required may be simply to refuse consent. As the heart is healed, that refusal can become more willing because the person increasingly desires the friendship which sin would destroy.

## Renew the Heart with St. Francis de Sales

St. Francis de Sales gives the meditation on hell a place within the beginning of the devout life. His sequence leads the soul through the things that disclose its end and the choices which follow from it. In the meditation itself he considers the sufferings of the senses, the greater pain of losing the vision of God’s glory, and the eternity of the deprivation. He then asks for definite resolutions by which sin is to be avoided (*Introduction to the Devout Life*, I.15).

Francis moves from the pains of hell to resolutions against sin. An impression of suffering can fade while an attachment remains protected. He asks the person meditating to consider what conduct must change and to resolve upon the means of changing it. This requires honesty about an evil which can seem small when considered only in its immediate attraction. The resolution is strengthened by the thought of what that evil endangers. A person who has considered the everlasting loss of God has reason to accept the inconvenience of removing an occasion of grave sin.

His fifth part turns to the renewal needed after good resolutions have already been made. He compares the heart’s devotion to a clock. It requires regular winding and, at intervals, a more careful examination of its workings. Rust must be cleared, what is bent put right, and what is worn attended to. At least once a year, the devout person should examine the affections and passions and renew the purpose of serving God (V.1).

The image recognizes gradual deterioration. A soul may not have made a dramatic decision to abandon prayer. Prayer was shortened, delayed, displaced by other business, and finally omitted. A habit of generosity may have narrowed as comforts multiplied. A fault once resisted may have acquired a convenient explanation. The annual recollection gives time to see developments that are hard to notice in the middle of daily activity.

Francis joins the renewal to Confession and the Eucharist. The examination exposes what needs healing, and the sacraments bring the soul again to the mercy and life of Christ. His counsel is practical about time: choose a suitable occasion, seek greater recollection, and make the meditations with the advice of one’s spiritual father. The end of the liturgical year can provide such an occasion, especially when the remembrance of death has already made the soul attentive.

He begins the renewal with the benefits of God’s call. The person remembers the renunciation of mortal sin, the dedication of the whole self to divine service, and the resolve to rise again through grace if a fall should occur (V.2). He considers the helps by which God drew him: prayer, sacraments, spiritual reading, and the growth of love. Conversion is remembered as mercy, and progress is received with thanksgiving.

Francis also attends to those called late in life. The years lost do not make the present call worthless. Mercy has reached the sinner before death and has opened a way of service. That remembrance places regret within gratitude. We can grieve over time wasted while giving thanks that the time now offered can be used differently.

The examination that follows is to be made quietly and patiently. Francis permits its parts to be distributed across an appropriate period; he does not require the whole to be performed in one strained sitting. He directs attention toward conduct in relation to God, oneself, one’s neighbor, and the passions. Progress is to produce thanksgiving without self-congratulation. Little progress, or even decline, is to produce renewed humility and effort with God’s help, rather than discouragement (V.3).

At the close, Francis names the means that must support the renewed purpose: prayer, sacraments, good works, correction of faults already discovered, avoidance of occasions, and fidelity to counsel (V.15). The annual examination can show where one of those means has been allowed to lapse. If prayer has repeatedly been displaced, merely resolving to pray more leaves the old difficulty untouched. A suitable time must be set aside and kept when competing demands return. The resolution then addresses the way devotion actually weakened, instead of depending upon the recollection’s strong impression to continue.

Francis’s comparison with a clock concerns continuing attention to devotion. A yearly examination gives time to discover a fault which ordinary haste has concealed; the means he names help the person correct it afterward. The resolution must fit the duty and the weakness discovered. An indiscriminate increase of practices can leave the neglected obligation as neglected as before. The person should use counsel to choose a definite amendment he can faithfully undertake, remembering that the time for serving God remains limited. Renewal asks him to begin that amendment within his present vocation.

## Compassion for Souls

St. Teresa of Avila recounts a private mystical experience of hell in chapter 32 of her *Life*. She describes its terror and anguish, then tells how the remembrance affected her over the following years. She understood it as a mercy which made her more grateful for her deliverance, more resolute under suffering, and more concerned for souls. The account develops those consequences in her religious life.

She understood what she had been shown as a great mercy, a disclosure of the danger from which the Lord had delivered her (32.6). Her remembrance therefore includes gratitude from the beginning. The suffering described does not turn her attention away from Christ’s compassion. It makes that compassion more deeply felt. The Lord had loved and preserved her when her own response had been weak and divided.

Teresa says that earlier reading about hell had not greatly moved her, and that her soul had not usually advanced by the way of fear. She describes the continuing force of what she had undergone (32.6–8). Earlier knowledge and this vivid apprehension affected her differently. The distinction helps explain why she dwells upon its lasting consequences. She had already known the teaching; now earthly suffering appeared less formidable, and she felt more deeply what it would mean for another soul to be lost.

One fruit was courage in suffering and opposition. Teresa says the remembrance made earthly trials seem endurable and weakened her fear of the world’s contradiction (32.7–8). Her account resembles the martyr’s ordering of fear: when the eternal danger is apprehended, temporal opposition loses its claim to govern everything. Fidelity may still hurt, but it no longer seems reasonable to surrender the greater good merely to avoid that hurt.

Another fruit was compassion. Teresa turns from her own deliverance toward souls in danger and says that she would willingly endure many deaths to save even one (32.9). The thought of endless misery enlarges concern for another person. We feel compassion at a temporary illness or grief; the danger of eternal loss should move love still more deeply.

This charity extends beyond the people whose companionship is easy. Cyprian’s appeal to persecutors has the same breadth. He offers those who trouble Christians the possibility of eternal joy with them, because Christians are not permitted to repay hatred with hatred (*Ad Demetrianum* 25). Hell’s reality makes the desire for an enemy’s salvation urgent. We may need protection from his conduct or justice concerning a wrong, while still desiring that he repent and live.

St. Paul gives intercession a revealed foundation: prayers, supplications, and thanksgivings are to be offered for everyone, including those in authority, because God wills all to be saved and to know the truth. Christ is the one mediator who gave himself for all (1 Tim 2:1–6). The concern for souls which the saints express enters this prayer of the Church. We ask for conversion, the helps of grace, and perseverance for people we can name and for those whose struggles we do not know.

The Church also continues her suffrages for the departed undergoing purification. Their salvation is assured; they still need purification before entering the blessed vision. Prayer for them follows from the distinction between purgatory and hell. Our charity can assist those being purified while the warning of eternal loss calls the living to repentance.

Intercession also prepares us to help a person whose return may require a patient conversation. If we have prayed for his conversion, we should be willing to hear the difficulty he has been avoiding, without treating his disclosure as an opportunity for humiliation. A candid account of our own repentance may help him stop defending his sin. Prudence must judge what the relationship permits and what counsel is appropriate. Our concern should make it easier for him to seek Christ’s forgiveness. Grace must win the consent which pressure alone cannot produce.

Teresa asks what she can do for God and resolves to avoid occasions of mortal sin. Her first answer is to live the religious vocation already given her with greater fidelity, keeping her rule as perfectly as she can (32.10–11). The memory of hell has intensified her desire to serve; her rule identifies duties through which she can act upon it. She does not need to invent another vocation before answering the mercy she has received. She begins with the obedience which already places demands upon her time and will.

A Catholic reader can ask the same question within the duties of his own state. Someone concerned about the conversion of others may need first to repair a neglect which has made his faith appear empty to the people closest to him. Praying for their salvation cannot excuse refusing the patience or justice he owes them. Teresa’s response directs zeal toward duties whose claims can be known. Greater fidelity there gives the desire to serve souls a form which can be practiced even when no unusual work is possible.

The misery Teresa describes moved her to desire another’s salvation even at great cost to herself. Remembering that response can correct the resentment with which we sometimes regard a sinner. His need of conversion is a reason to pray and act for his good. We may still have to oppose his conduct or seek justice for an injury. Those duties remain compatible with desiring that he repent and live. The contemplation of eternal loss gives us reason to resist the satisfaction of imagining a person we dislike beyond the reach of mercy.

## Persevere in the Mercy You Have Received

St. Alphonsus addresses the person who has renounced sin and hopes to have received pardon, then reminds him that the race is not yet over. Perseverance is needed until the end (*Preparation for Death*, XXXI.1). The mercy received should become the manner of life in which the Lord will find us. The resolution formed during recollection has to survive the ordinary return of fatigue, temptation, irritation, and desire.

Alphonsus names avoidance of occasions, frequent recourse to the sacraments, daily meditation, and prayer. He warns against placing confidence simply in one’s purposes. We need God’s assistance in the conflict, and the discovery of our weakness should make recourse to him more prompt. A person may sincerely resolve to change while still relying upon the same unguarded arrangements in which he repeatedly fell.

An occasion of sin must be judged by its effect upon this person’s conduct. An arrangement may repeatedly place him where he is likely to consent to grave evil. He may already know from several falls that a general resolution has left the danger undisturbed. Amendment then requires changing the arrangement, with the inconvenience or loss that follows. The relevant question is what would actually interrupt the familiar course toward consent. Christ’s teaching about the hand and the eye gives a reason to accept a painful renunciation for the good of eternal life.

The safeguard should address the temptation before it becomes acute. If a recurring exchange reliably ends in cruel speech, the person can decide beforehand how to leave it when its direction becomes clear. Waiting until anger has taken command makes the refusal harder. This precaution acknowledges what experience has shown about his weakness. It also gives his purpose of amendment a definite act to perform. He must still ask for grace when the provocation comes, but he has ceased deliberately placing himself where he repeatedly abandons the restraint he intended to keep.

Prayer accompanies the safeguard. It acknowledges that the soul cannot produce supernatural fidelity from its own resources. Alphonsus urges immediate recourse to Jesus and to the intercession of his Mother in temptation. The person who has learned this dependence does not need to wait until a fall to remember God. The first movement of danger can become a summons to ask for help, and the help received can become an occasion of gratitude.

Daily examination helps reveal whether the annual resolution has survived. A familiar excuse may have returned before we notice that the old conduct is again becoming ordinary. A brief review of the day can identify the occasion on which we yielded and the help we failed to use. It can also recognize fidelity which discouragement overlooks. The review should lead to sorrow for the fault, thanksgiving for grace, and an act of amendment appropriate to what happened. Its value lies in returning promptly to God, before delay acquires another week’s force.

At the yearly recollection, set aside enough quiet to consider one of Christ’s warnings. The rich man and Lazarus, for example, require attention to a need which had remained near the rich man’s gate. We can ask whether a known claim upon our charity has likewise become easy to pass by. In prayer we can consider how indifference became settled and what responding would now cost. That examination must remain honest about our responsibilities and means. It should also disturb an excuse which has allowed a real duty to remain indefinitely outside our concern.

Consider the finality Christ describes before deciding upon the response. The rich man’s request for relief cannot undo the separation, and his concern for his brothers turns toward those who can still hear Moses and the prophets. We are among the living who have received the warning. A duty postponed during recollection will soon face the same competing attractions as before. The thought of the end helps us judge whether those attractions deserve the obedience we have given them. Friendship with God is endangered by a grave refusal, even when the immediate inconvenience of obeying fills our attention.

Bring what has become clear into repentance. If conscious of mortal sin, turn to God in contrition and seek sacramental confession promptly. Let the examination be honest and proportionate; let the accusation be candid; let the purpose of amendment include the needed change. Where restitution or reconciliation is due, determine the next just act and undertake it. If a matter is genuinely difficult, bring it to the confessor’s counsel with a willingness to follow sound direction.

Remember the mercy by which a return is possible. A previous confession may have ended a refusal which once seemed fixed. Recalling it gives reason to seek the same Savior’s help in the fault now exposed. This memory must include what made the return possible: grace received, the truth spoken, and a willingness to surrender the sin. It can strengthen hope without making the present act of repentance unnecessary. The Lord who received us then is the Lord to whom we must now bring what we have continued to withhold.

Take one definite resolution from the recollection into the coming day and ask for God’s help to keep it. Choose the act which the examination has shown to be required, then provide for the circumstances in which it must be done. A resolution to repair a wrong needs a first step and a time; an occasion to be removed needs an actual change. Return to the purpose when its cost becomes apparent. Alphonsus’s counsel on perseverance concerns that continuance, when the initial impression has faded and the ordinary duty still asks for consent.

In the prayer following his consideration on abused mercy, St. Alphonsus asks for the love and perseverance needed to remain faithful. We may make our own this complete sentence from that received prayer:

> “My God, for the love of Jesus Christ, give me holy perseverance till death, along with your holy love.”

*Preparation for Death*, XVII.1, prayer excerpt; anonymous English translation, Dublin, 1843, p. 209.

## Scope, Method, and Qualifications

This essay is Catholic spiritual reading for annual preparation for death. Scripture and the Church’s teaching establish its account of hell; selected Fathers and saints deepen the contemplation and guide its practice. The Church’s doctrine, attributed theological explanations, imaginative meditation, and Teresa’s private testimony retain their respective authority. The selection spans martyrdom, patristic catechesis and preaching, scholastic theology, and spiritual masters; it is not an exhaustive survey. It neither estimates the number of the damned nor determines particular persons’ final destiny. The companion essays consider death, judgment, and heaven. Practical applications to annual recollection are source-grounded synthesis. Scripture quotations follow the Douay–Rheims, Challoner revision; the citation to Mark 9 follows that witness’s numbering. The closing prayer reproduces an identified historical English sentence without adaptation. This spiritual reading does not replace personal sacramental counsel.
