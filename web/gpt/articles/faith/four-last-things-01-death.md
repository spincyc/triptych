# The Four Last Things: The Hour That Cannot Be Delegated

*Death: a Catholic study of mortality, Christian preparation, and resurrection*

## To Die in Christ

We shall die, and the hour of death will end the time given us for repentance. The Church teaches us to keep that hour before us and to prepare for it in Christ. Through Baptism we have already died with him sacramentally; to die in his grace is to complete that dying with him and enter his life (CCC 1010–1014). We seek a good death by living faithfully in that grace and asking Christ to preserve us in it to the end.

Someone else can stand beside our deathbed, but no one can undergo our dying for us. A priest can absolve and anoint; friends can forgive and keep watch; the Church can place her prayer around our weakness. These are real gifts, and our need for them can be very great. Yet the life being surrendered is ours. The accumulated provisions by which we have made ourselves secure will cease to be available in their familiar way. Possessions remain behind. Offices pass to successors. Unfinished plans fall into other hands. Even the body through which we have known the world will be taken from our keeping.

We can begin by considering the good we have postponed, the sin we have excused, and the forgiveness we have withheld. There is still time to seek pardon and amend our conduct. We need not wait until death becomes imminent before examining how we are living. The same Lord who will receive our last breath is calling us now, through his word, the sacraments, and the duties nearest at hand.

The Four Last Things—death, judgment, hell, and heaven—concern the eternal destiny of this life. Death leads to judgment; heaven and hell are the opposed definitive outcomes. Purgatory is the final purification of those already assured of heaven. The person who acts today will stand before God, with the good received from his grace and the sins for which mercy is needed. Our daily choices matter because we are choosing how to respond to the Lord with whom we are called to live forever.

The Church’s year gives this contemplation a familiar setting. At All Saints we honor those who have attained holiness; at All Souls we pray for the departed who still need our prayers. In Advent we await Christ’s coming, the resurrection of the dead, and the completion of history. Another year of our life has been spent when these seasons return. Someone who once prayed beside us may now be among those for whom we pray. A burden once remote may have become our daily responsibility. Remembering death again allows us to consider these changes before God and examine how we have responded to them.

We cannot command another year, another day, or even another opportunity to do what conscience already requires. Christ has died and risen, and he offers us the grace to turn to him now. These truths give us reason both to repent promptly and to hope. We can spend much time imagining our last hours while avoiding that present response. Prayer about death bears fruit when it leads us to seek the Lord’s mercy and follow him in the life still given to us.

We can prepare without knowing what our death will look like. It may come after a long illness or with little warning; it may leave time for speech or take away that power. We cannot arrange the entire event as a final expression of our character. We can ask for perseverance, seek reconciliation, learn charity, and consent today to belong to Christ in circumstances we cannot yet foresee. When we cannot imagine how we would endure them, we can ask for the help he gives to the weak. The Church teaches us to entrust the unknown hour to him and fulfill the duties already entrusted to us.

## The Wound of Death

Death separates the soul from the body. It tears apart the unity of the human being and ends the familiar presence through which we have known one another. The hand can no longer answer our hand. A voice whose smallest inflections were familiar has become silent. The body is still the body of the person we love, but we cannot restore the living exchange by which our love was ordinarily expressed. The Church honors that body and prays for that person because she knows both the reality of the wound and the promise of its healing.

At Lazarus’s tomb the Lord meets two sisters grieving for their brother. Martha and Mary both tell Jesus that, had he been there, Lazarus would not have died. Martha professes her faith in the resurrection at the last day, and Jesus draws that faith toward his own person: he is the resurrection and the life. Then he goes to the tomb, weeps, and calls Lazarus forth (Jn 11:17–44). His tears show his love for Lazarus and his sorrow among the bereaved, even as he comes to restore their brother to them.

Our sorrow may therefore be brought to him without first being made eloquent or serene. An empty place at table, the absence of a companion, or a recollection that suddenly interrupts an ordinary day can become the occasion of prayer. We may be unable to explain our grief even to ourselves. Christ knows it before we can explain it. Martha and Mary approached him with their loss; we too may speak to him through tears and trust his promise of resurrection.

Death also belongs to the history of sin. Genesis presents the return to dust within the catastrophe of humanity’s disobedience (Gen 2:17; 3:19). Paul contrasts Adam’s trespass with Christ’s obedience and names death the wages of sin (Rom 5:12–21; 6:23). The Catechism receives this teaching in its distinction between the natural mortality of the human body and the gift by which God had destined humanity not to die (CCC 1006–1008). Our bodily nature is corruptible; immunity from death belonged to the original divine gift. Death’s familiarity within our present condition does not make it the fulfillment for which the Creator made us.

This teaching concerns the condition of the human family. It does not authorize us to measure an individual’s guilt by the timing or painfulness of that person’s death. When Christ is told of violent deaths and a fallen tower, he refuses to identify the victims as greater sinners than their neighbors. He directs his hearers toward their own repentance (Lk 13:1–5). His answer requires us to examine our sins. Those who suffer need our help, and their calamity gives us no knowledge of their guilt.

The death of a child, an unexpected death, or a death after long suffering can expose how little we control. We need not describe such a loss as though its good were immediately apparent. The promise of resurrection concerns something God will do. It does not demand that the bereaved discover, within every detail of their present pain, a reason that would make the pain welcome. We can trust the crucified and risen Lord while still asking why this person died and why those who loved him must suffer the separation.

The Second Vatican Council speaks of the human person’s recoil before death and the seed of eternity within us (*Gaudium et spes* 18). Christ conquers death and calls the whole person to communion with God. Those who survive us may remember us with love, but our life before God continues even when they can no longer remember. We may therefore pray with confidence for the dead whose names and histories have been lost to us: God still knows each one.

## An Enemy Made to Serve Life

Augustine asks why the baptized still die when their sins have been forgiven. The question bears directly on Christian hope. Baptism is real regeneration; death is still before the regenerate. The believer receives a new life without receiving immediate exemption from the body’s mortality. In *The City of God* XIII.4–5, Augustine explains this remaining passage through the testing of faith and the extraordinary work of grace. Faith waits for what God has promised and what is not yet seen. Meanwhile, the punishment of sin is turned to the service of righteousness.

His sharpest example is martyrdom. The first disobedience brought death into human life. The martyr is threatened with death unless he disobeys, and accepts dying in order to remain faithful. The enemy is made to serve the obedience it once seemed able to destroy. Augustine is exact about the distinction: death has not become good in itself. God has granted the faithful a good use of what is evil. The wicked can misuse a good thing, as when the law becomes an occasion for transgression; the righteous, helped by grace, can use even death well.

Augustine’s distinction explains how a martyr’s death can be holy while the killing remains evil. The persecutor threatens the loss of bodily life to compel apostasy. By accepting death rather than denying Christ, the martyr keeps faith with the Lord who gave him that life and promises to raise his body. His constancy is sustained by grace. We honor the martyr because of his love and obedience, while condemning the violence inflicted on him.

Most Christians will not die by a persecutor’s sentence. Yet Augustine’s account helps us understand how death can become an obedient offering in an ordinary sickroom. The grace of Christ may meet us where independence gives way to dependence, familiar work becomes impossible, or a treasured plan must be relinquished. None of these losses becomes delightful merely because it can be offered. Their sorrow may remain. But sorrow need not determine the whole meaning of what we do with them. We can receive help, forgive, thank God for another’s kindness, and consent to place unfinished work in his hands.

These acts are sustained by the same Lord who strengthens the martyrs. A person who has always managed the household may find being washed or fed more humiliating than an outsider can understand. A person who has found purpose in competent work may find enforced inactivity a severe trial. A parent may be troubled less by physical pain than by the prospect of leaving a child. We can bring the particular loss before God, ask for the help it requires, and seek to continue loving within it. The offering may need to be renewed each day as the same difficulty returns.

Approaching death, we may become unable to pray or act in the ways we had hoped. We can ask for the grace to accept help in that weakness. The person who can say little, follow only part of a familiar prayer, or simply consent to be prayed for remains within the Church’s charity. Dependence on God has always been the truth of that life, including its strongest and most accomplished years. The help now received from others allows the weakened person to participate as he can, while the Church continues praying on his behalf.

We can practice this surrender before illness requires it. In confession we acknowledge sins we have tried to justify. In restitution we give up something we have wrongly treated as ours. In forgiveness we renounce revenge. In willing service we allow another person’s need to interrupt our arrangements. These acts cannot guarantee a peaceful death; they help us become accustomed to obeying God when obedience costs us something. We will still need his grace when a loss is imposed on us and we have less freedom to choose its circumstances.

The baptized still die because the bodily fulfillment of Christ’s promise remains future. Augustine explains that faith trusts God for this unseen reward. Death can therefore test that trust without defeating the grace received in Baptism. We rely on Christ to preserve us through the trial and to raise the body whose mortality he has allowed to remain.

## The Lord Who Entered Death

Christ’s victory begins with a death that is fully real. The eternal Son assumed a complete human nature, with a human soul and body. On Calvary it is the divine Son who dies, and the death he undergoes is human: his soul is separated from his body. His divinity remains immortal. During that separation both soul and body remain personally united to the Word (CCC 624–626). The Incarnation is not undone between Good Friday and Easter. The Lord who lies in the tomb is the same Lord who has spoken to the disciples and who will stand among them risen.

Aquinas explains the continuing union by attending to the person of Christ (ST III, q. 50, aa. 2–3). Death divides his human soul from his human body; it does not divide the one person of the Word into two, nor remove either soul or body from that person. The Creed therefore attributes both burial and descent among the dead to the Son himself. We can trust that the Son has truly entered the condition of the dead and remained united to the humanity he assumed for our salvation. Neither his body in the tomb nor his soul among the dead has been abandoned by the Word.

His death is freely accepted. Jesus speaks of laying down his life and taking it up again (Jn 10:17–18). That freedom does not make the sufferings unreal. In Gethsemane he is sorrowful and distressed; he asks that the chalice pass, and freely submits to the Father’s will (Mk 14:32–42). The Letter to the Hebrews remembers his cries and tears, and teaches that the Son learned obedience through what he suffered (Heb 5:7–9). He who is without sin knows the human shrinking before suffering and death, and his human obedience remains entire within it.

We can therefore distinguish fear from refusal. An involuntary recoil before dying does not by itself mean that the will has abandoned God. The Lord’s own agony prevents us from requiring ourselves or another person to feel no anguish as a condition of faithful surrender. Faithfulness may take the form of returning, within anguish, to the Father’s will. The desire for relief, the request for companionship, and the effort to remain obedient can coexist. Christ himself asks the disciples to remain and watch.

On the cross the Lord continues to care for others. He receives the penitent thief and gives his mother and the beloved disciple to one another (Lk 23:39–43; Jn 19:25–30). While his enemies mock him, he promises paradise to the man beside him and provides for those who remain. His final surrender to the Father completes this obedient love (Lk 23:44–46). The dying Christian can turn to a Savior who has known bodily helplessness and continued to love within it.

When Christ rises, the body that bore the wounds is glorified beyond death’s reach. He shows his hands and his side to the disciples (Jn 20:19–29). Their Lord’s identity continues through death and resurrection, while his manner of bodily life is transformed. He has done more than return to the mortal condition from which Lazarus was recalled. Paul names him the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep and joins his Resurrection to the resurrection of those who belong to him (1 Cor 15:20–28).

This is why we can offer our own death in union with his obedience and love (CCC 1011). The offering begins with what Christ has accomplished. We do not make our death redemptive by sufficient intensity of feeling. He draws our frailty into his own saving act. While we are able, we can deliberately entrust the manner, time, and circumstances of dying to the Father, asking for the grace to remain faithful through what we cannot control. Such an intention need not be accompanied by certainty that we will feel brave when the hour arrives.

We entrust our future to this crucified and risen Son. He saves those who die in his grace and brings them into his life. Asking for a good death therefore includes asking for the faith to rely on him when pain or weakness makes our own resources fail.

## Freedom from the Fear of Death

The Letter to the Hebrews teaches that the Son saves us by sharing our humanity. Those whom he sanctifies are his brothers; he is not ashamed to call them so. Because the children share flesh and blood, he has shared the same human condition, entering death to break the devil’s power and release those whose fear of death has held them in lifelong servitude (Heb 2:11–18). He takes the flesh in which we suffer and offers the obedience we could not supply for ourselves. His victory concerns us because he has made himself our brother and undergone the death we fear.

Earlier, Hebrews distinguishes what we see around us from what has been accomplished in Christ. We do not yet see all things subjected to humanity, but we see Jesus, who suffered death and is crowned with glory and honor (Heb 2:8–10). Violence continues, bodies fail, and the faithful remain exposed to loss. We take courage from the Lord’s exaltation while awaiting the completion of his victory in his members. The suffering still before us must be endured with his help, in hope of the glory he has already attained.

Fear enslaves us when it leads us to act against conscience. Bodily survival is a great created good, and the care of life ordinarily serves love. Yet a person can begin to treat continued safety as a good for which every other good must be surrendered. We may conceal the truth because speaking it carries a cost, refuse another person’s need because helping would disturb our security, or practice the faith only while it leaves our arrangements untouched. Such choices train us to avoid the sacrifices fidelity requires. The fear of death can influence our conduct long before our life is threatened, through a growing refusal to risk anything that sustains its comfort or security.

Christ addresses that captivity when he sends the disciples to bear witness. He teaches them whom to fear and what to trust: those who can kill the body cannot determine the soul’s destiny; the Father knows the sparrows and has numbered the hairs of their heads (Mt 10:26–33). Divine care and the possibility of persecution appear together in his teaching. The disciples are precious to the Father within the danger of their mission. They can confess the Son because they are already known and loved by the Father, whose knowledge reaches beyond every power that may threaten them.

This confidence helps us act courageously in ordinary duties. We may be asked to speak an unwelcome truth, protect someone with little influence, or bear a loss that honest conduct makes unavoidable. The immediate calculation can be discouraging. A useful friendship may cool; advancement may become less likely; someone whose approval we value may misunderstand us. Remembering death helps us consider how much these losses should weigh against our duty to God. The people whose displeasure we fear are mortal too. Their power over our circumstances will end, while we will still answer to God for the choices made under their influence.

Courage also requires prudence. We remain responsible for dependents, for careful speech, and for discerning the duties of our state. The courage we seek enables us to do the good required of us when doing it is costly. A parent may endure exhausting work for a child’s sake; a physician may remain faithful to a vulnerable patient; a worker may refuse the dishonest act expected of everyone. Each must consider the actual duty and seek sound counsel where needed. We can ask Christ for the wisdom to recognize that duty and the strength to fulfill it despite the risks.

Hebrews also explains how Christ helps us in weakness. The Son became like his brothers so that he might be a merciful and faithful high priest; having suffered when tempted, he can help those undergoing trial (Heb 2:17–18). We therefore approach one who knows human vulnerability from within his sinless human life. The letter later invites us to draw near with confidence to the throne of grace, where mercy and timely help are given (Heb 4:14–16). We can bring our fear to him as soon as it arises and ask for strength to fulfill the duty before us.

Peter’s failure shows how much we need such help. Before the Passion is complete, the disciple who followed Jesus into the high priest’s courtyard denies knowing him. When the Lord turns and looks at him, Peter remembers and weeps (Lk 22:54–62). His collapse reveals how quickly a sincere attachment can yield under pressure. At the Lord’s look he remembers the warning he had received and recognizes what he has done. When we discover cowardice in ourselves, we too can acknowledge the deed and turn to Christ for mercy. Remembering Peter can help us seek that mercy promptly instead of dwelling on how brave we once expected to be.

Remembering Peter can also help us examine how we respond to fear now. We cannot establish today exactly how we will feel under the pressure of a final illness. We can learn where we habitually seek refuge when frightened. We may grasp for control, withdraw, accuse someone, or make promises merely to escape discomfort. Bringing these habits into prayer and confession allows us to ask for help with them. Over time we can learn to pray when fear first arises, acknowledge what frightens us, and consider what faithfulness requires before acting to escape the discomfort.

Paul confesses the security of Christ’s love amid suffering at the close of Romans 8. He names tribulation, distress, hunger, danger, persecution, and the sword; he even recalls the condition of those treated as sheep for slaughter. Then he confesses that neither death nor life nor any created power can separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus (Rom 8:31–39). His confidence rests upon the Father who has given his Son, and upon Christ who died, rose, and intercedes. We can ask the interceding Christ to sustain our fidelity through the trials Paul names, including those we fear most.

We may therefore ask for the grace to obey Christ while still afraid. The annual remembrance of death gives us time to examine the occasions when we have preferred security to fidelity. We can acknowledge a particular failure, seek pardon, and ask how to fulfill the duty we avoided. We can also become more patient with someone whose courage is hard won. The Lord who knows our weakness gives us reason to persevere in this work without pretending that we have already overcome every fear.

## The Dying Begun in Baptism

Paul speaks to the baptized as people whose relation to death has already changed. They have been buried with Christ through Baptism so that they may walk in newness of life. Their union with his death includes the promise of union with his Resurrection (Rom 6:3–11). Through the sacrament we really participate in his saving mystery. We receive a life that begins now and will be fulfilled when he raises us from the dead.

Paul immediately joins this gift to conduct. The baptized are to refuse sin’s reign in their mortal bodies and offer themselves to God as people brought from death to life (Rom 6:12–13). The body that will one day die is presently an instrument of obedience. Its hands, voice, eyes, strength, and time can be offered to charity. Preparation for death therefore includes how we inhabit the body now: the uses we make of it, the restraints we accept, the persons we serve, and the worship in which we place ourselves before God.

Baptism establishes our life in Christ before we have done anything to earn it. Many of us were carried to the font before we could form an intention of our own. The Church confessed the faith over us, and the Lord received us through her ministry. At the other end of life we may again need to be carried, spoken for, and surrounded by prayer. Receiving this help can remind us how much of our Christian life has depended on others: those who brought us to Baptism, taught us to pray, corrected us, or helped us return after we had fallen away.

We must freely respond to the grace we have received. We belong to Christ, and our choices should accord with that gift. Dying to self takes place in the ordinary duties through which we learn to obey him. We keep a promise when inconvenience has made it costly. We accept a correction without immediately preparing a defense. We undertake a duty that brings no admiration. We refuse an indulgence because it is injuring our freedom to love. In each case, the sacrifice concerns something we have wanted to keep at the expense of fidelity.

None of this requires contempt for created goods. Food, friendship, marriage, work, and rest are gifts through which we can give thanks to God. Baptismal renunciation opposes their disorder, the possessiveness that makes a gift rival its Giver. We can learn to enjoy a good thing without demanding that it last forever in its present form. We can work carefully without claiming final control of the result. We can love a person deeply without making that person responsible for providing the security that belongs to God alone.

This education in freedom becomes especially visible when a familiar good is withdrawn. A change in health may reveal how much self-respect depended on strength. Retirement may expose a need to be necessary. A child’s departure may show where parental love has become a demand to direct every outcome. These revelations can be painful, but they offer places for grace to work. The difficulty is no proof that earlier love or work was worthless. It reveals an attachment within something good that the Lord is teaching us to surrender more freely.

Fasting, almsgiving, obedient service, and patient prayer help us submit to Christ in the use of these goods. Fasting disciplines an appetite; almsgiving makes possessions available to someone in need; service requires time we might have kept for ourselves. Their Christian purpose is fulfilled as they help us love God and our neighbor more freely. When death removes the bodily powers by which we have practiced them, we still rely on the life received in Baptism. The Lord with whom we have died sacramentally has promised to raise us with him.

## The Same Self, Awaiting Wholeness

The spiritual soul survives the separation from the body and receives judgment. The Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith’s 1979 letter teaches that consciousness and will remain, so that the human self subsists (no. 3). The one who has loved, sinned, repented, and been forgiven is the one who stands before God. We hope for our own salvation and pray for the people we have known, confident that death has not made them cease to exist or erased the lives for which they will answer.

This continuity gives moral seriousness to the present and solidity to our hope. The acts of charity that appear to vanish into ordinary days belong to a life that endures. So do the choices for which we must ask mercy. God knows the person in the fullness of that history, without the forgetfulness that limits our own account of it. We do not carry ourselves beyond death by the strength of memory. We are held in existence by the God who created us and whose judgment is perfectly true.

Yet a surviving soul is not the whole embodied human being. The blessed truly see God before the general resurrection, while awaiting reunion with their bodies (CCC 1023, citing Benedict XII’s *Benedictus Deus*). Their blessedness is real, and humanity’s bodily consummation remains future. Thus the Church invokes the saints now and awaits the resurrection of the dead. Our communion with the saints includes hope for what God has still promised to accomplish in them and in us.

Paul’s account of the seed and the harvest teaches us to receive continuity together with transformation (1 Cor 15:35–49). The body is sown in weakness and raised in power. What is corruptible is made incorruptible by God. The risen body is our own, yet it will no longer be subject to the conditions under which we now suffer, decline, and die. The language of a spiritual body does not mean that the body has ceased to be bodily. It names embodied life brought fully under the life-giving power of the Spirit, as the risen Christ already reveals.

We can remember this promise when disease, age, or injury has changed a body almost beyond recognition. God will raise the person whose mortal condition we can no longer remedy. Christian reverence therefore remains when beauty, responsiveness, and usefulness have disappeared. The body through which the person has lived deserves care in illness and honor after death. Its decay gives us reason to long for the resurrection God has promised, while the surviving soul remains before him.

The CDF letter warns against arbitrary pictures of the interval between death and resurrection (nos. 2–5 and concluding doctrinal paragraphs). It affirms the truths on which the Church’s prayer depends: the self endures, judgment is real, the saints live in God, the departed can be helped by intercession, and bodily resurrection is still to come. These are definite grounds for hope even when we cannot answer further questions about the manner of life after death.

Beside a bed or grave we can commend the person by name, ask mercy, honor the body, and await the fulfillment of God’s promise. We need not know the details of the departed person’s condition in order to pray. The Church continues to intercede for the dead because Christ makes that prayer effective; we can participate in it even when grief leaves us little else to say.

## The Hour We Have Not Been Told

The certainty of death and the uncertainty of its hour belong together. We can assent to the first while living as though the second guaranteed delay. Yet an unknown date supplies no promise of more time. In the parable of the rich man, the barns are enlarged and the years of enjoyment confidently anticipated before God announces that the man’s life will be required that night (Lk 12:15–21). The error lies in treating stored goods as security for a future the owner cannot command. His preparations have left the most necessary relation unattended: he is not rich toward God.

Prudent provision remains a duty. Dependents need to be supported; responsibilities may require saving, planning, and patient work. But we can make provision as though the continuation of our life were assured. We may consider every foreseeable interruption to our plans while neglecting our dependence on God for life itself. We can examine this danger by asking what our preparations serve: the needs of those entrusted to us, or a confidence in our arrangements that has allowed prayer and repentance to be put off.

St. Francis de Sales considers the uncertain hour in the fifth meditation of the *Introduction to the Devout Life* (I.13). He asks the reader to consider that death may come in one season or another, in one place or another, with warning or suddenly, with a confessor at hand or without that opportunity. Each possibility makes it harder to assume that we will recognize a safe time to begin preparing. A reader who now has access to confession and the ability to amend a fault can consider how much depends on circumstances that may later be withdrawn.

He then considers the farewells death will require: to possessions and pleasures, to companions and family, and finally to one’s own body. He asks the reader to consider how these attachments will appear at death and to choose holy friendship, a love capable of enduring in God. This choice requires attention to the way we love now. Francis directs the reader to examine conscience, put what is disordered into order, and seek the help of the Blessed Virgin and the saints. His meditation ends in resolutions for the life the reader can still amend.

Alphonsus Liguori presses the same point in *Preparation for Death*, especially considerations IV.1 and V.1–2. A person may know perfectly well that everyone dies and still place his own death at a convenient distance. Alphonsus warns especially against intending to sin now and confess tomorrow. Such a decision presumes a future opportunity to repent. It also presumes that we will later be willing to relinquish the sin we are deliberately choosing today. We can guarantee neither. The time God gives for repentance is being misused when we take it as permission to continue refusing him.

We can apply this warning to a decision already before us. We may be postponing confession because the sin is embarrassing, delaying restitution because it will cost money, or putting off a difficult reconciliation until the other person becomes easier to approach. Embarrassment, cost, and difficulty will not necessarily diminish with time. Continuing to avoid them can strengthen the habit of refusal. Acknowledging the cost of repentance allows us to ask for the grace to bear it and take the first step, while the opportunity is still available.

In Luke’s account, the servants keep their lamps burning and wait for their master. When he finds them ready, he seats them at table and serves them (Lk 12:35–40). Christ commands watchfulness with this promise of welcome. The servants have reason to desire their master’s arrival, because he will receive them with a generosity beyond the service they have rendered. We too can await the Lord with desire as we seek to be ready for him, asking that our duties be fulfilled in hope of that welcome.

An annual meditation can expose a pattern that a single earnest resolution misses. The same postponed confession, entrenched resentment, or neglected obligation may return before us year after year. Time has been passing while the excuse has remained almost unchanged. Recognition need not lead to despair. It gives a specific place to begin. Instead of making another broad promise to become serious, we can perform the next concrete act that seriousness already requires. A priest can be contacted, a debt acknowledged, an occasion of sin removed, or a neglected person visited.

We cannot recover the years already spent. We can seek mercy for our sins and use the time that remains to amend our life. Alphonsus addresses a reader who is still able to respond. If his warning makes us afraid, we can bring that fear to confession and prayer, asking Christ for help with the particular sin we have been unwilling to leave. The opportunity to repent is itself a reason for gratitude.

## The Mercy Beside the Cross

Luke recounts a conversion in the last hours of a man’s life. Two criminals are led out with Jesus and crucified on either side of him. The rulers and soldiers mock the Savior, challenging him to prove his identity by saving himself. One of the condemned joins that demand. The other turns toward Jesus with a different appeal (Lk 23:32–43). They suffer the same sentence and see the same crucified Lord, yet respond differently to him. The penitent’s response gives us reason to hope for those who turn late and to consider how we are responding to grace now.

The penitent begins by addressing his fellow sufferer. He recalls the fear of God, acknowledges that their own sentence follows their deeds, and bears witness that Jesus has done nothing wrong. Luke leaves the detailed history of their offenses untold. He records the penitent’s acknowledgment of responsibility and his testimony to Christ’s innocence. The man answers his companion’s mockery by admitting the guilt they share and distinguishing Jesus from them. His suffering has not prevented this act of repentance and faith.

We can practice this honesty before we are near our own death. We may be occupied with the unfairness of particular circumstances while avoiding the wrong for which we really are responsible. An accusation may be exaggerated; the accuser may have faults; the consequences may hurt more than we expected. Those questions may matter, yet none removes the need to acknowledge the sin itself. The thief admits that his deeds deserve punishment while bearing witness to Jesus’ innocence. We too can acknowledge what we have done without waiting for everyone else to acknowledge their faults or for the consequences to become easier to bear.

He then addresses Jesus as a king, asking to be remembered when Jesus comes into his kingdom. What stands before his bodily eyes is a man sharing the shame of execution, mocked beneath a royal inscription. His appeal reaches beyond that visible humiliation. He entrusts his future to the sufferer beside him, believing that the crucified Jesus has a kingdom in which he can remember another. The request does not explain how this will be accomplished. Faith can turn toward the person of the Savior before it understands the manner in which his promise will be fulfilled.

Jesus promises that the penitent will be with him in paradise that very day (Lk 23:43). The man who faces execution will live in communion with Christ. He has little time left for action, yet can entrust himself to the Lord who receives him. His hope depends on the authority and mercy of Jesus. The mockery continues around the cross, but Jesus answers the man’s appeal as the King he has confessed, promising what only the Savior can give.

We should let this scene give its full consolation to those who have turned late. A life may contain grave wrongs and long resistance. Its repair may be incomplete; the time remaining may be short. The penitent does not have to persuade the Savior that the past was better than it was. He can acknowledge it and turn. The Church’s urgency in bringing Penance to the seriously ill belongs to this confidence in Christ’s mercy. No human calculation of years wasted gives us authority to tell a contrite person that it is now too late to seek forgiveness.

Deliberately delaying conversion differs from the thief’s response. He turns to Jesus in the opportunity given to him. His repentance is reason for us to turn too; we have no promise that we shall control the conditions of our own last hours. Admiration for his trust becomes fruitful when we act on the grace already offered. Whoever can confess, make restitution, or cease a wrong has a present opportunity to repent. We can ask Christ to help us use it, with the same confidence in his mercy that the thief expressed beside him.

When time and ability remain, a sincere return also seeks to repair the injury that can still be repaired. A dying person may be able to acknowledge a neglected child, correct a false accusation, disclose a debt, or ask pardon from someone long wounded. Such acts can be costly even within weakness. They express contrition through attention to the person who has been injured. Where possible, those helping the dying can assist with the conversation or practical arrangement required. When circumstances make a desired reparation impossible, the person can place both the sorrow and the unfulfilled duty before God, who knows what can and cannot still be done.

The Church and the family can help this turning by making room for honest speech. A familiar attempt to reassure the dying is to insist that they have never done anything requiring serious repentance. Affection may intend comfort, yet the sufferer’s conscience may be asking for help to acknowledge a real fault. We can listen without shock, help obtain a priest, and allow a person to ask forgiveness without being hurried back into a flattering account of life. Christ answers the thief’s acknowledgment of guilt with a promise of paradise. That promise gives us reason to help the contrite person seek forgiveness confidently.

At the same time, we cannot prescribe another person’s final words. Some will be unable to speak; some will lack the strength to explain what is happening inwardly; some will die without the opportunity for a recognizable farewell. The Gospel scene directs us toward the Lord who receives the penitent, whose knowledge is greater than our observation. We offer every help within our power and pray for the grace of repentance and perseverance. The absence of a scene we can understand does not entitle us to describe the secret encounter between God and a soul.

Luke then recounts the Lord’s own final surrender. After the promise to the thief, darkness falls, the veil of the temple is torn, and Jesus commends his spirit to the Father (Lk 23:44–46). The penitent has entrusted himself to Jesus; Jesus freely surrenders his human life to the Father. One is a sinner’s appeal for mercy, the other the Son’s saving obedience. In considering them together, we can recognize our dependence on what Christ accomplishes for us. The man who asks to be remembered receives the promise of salvation from the Lord who is giving his life to save him.

When we read this account again, we can acknowledge a particular wrong before Christ and ask for his mercy. The past may be painful to confess, and a desired reparation may be difficult. The thief’s appeal shows that we can approach the Savior with that need plainly admitted. We can also pray for someone who appears far from repentance, asking that grace reach him and that we be ready to help if he seeks a way back.

## What We Must Learn to Release

St. Benedict commands daily remembrance of death among the tools of good works in chapter 4 of his Rule. Around it stand fear of judgment, desire for eternal life, vigilance over one’s conduct, prayer, confession of faults, reconciliation, and the command never to despair of God’s mercy. Elsewhere in that same chapter are care for the poor, visiting the sick, burial of the dead, and consolation of the sorrowful. The monk who remembers his own death is also commanded to attend to the suffering and death of others.

Benedict ends his list by forbidding despair of God’s mercy. The reader who examines his conduct is therefore also commanded to hope. An unacknowledged sin still needs to be confessed, and death may come before the occasion we have imagined for doing so. We can remember that danger and seek forgiveness without concluding that our repeated failures have put us beyond mercy. Benedict places remembrance of death among practices to be undertaken daily, as part of a life in which repentance continues.

One of the hardest attachments to release is our preferred account of ourselves. We can admit general imperfection while defending every particular instance. We excuse impatience because the other person is difficult, concealment because honesty would threaten our position, or disobedience because we believe we know better. These explanations can make faults familiar without making us willing to amend them. In confession we must name what we have done, accepting that the truth may disturb the account we have grown used to giving. A confessor’s questions can help us recognize what our excuses have concealed.

Another attachment is the claim to possess the outcome of good work. A task may really have been entrusted to us, and we may have served it faithfully. Yet its future belongs to providence, which includes other workers with different gifts. A parent cannot control the entire life of a child; a teacher cannot complete every student’s education; a pastor cannot see the full harvest of his ministry. To prepare for death is partly to learn the limits of stewardship. We labor with care, make responsible provision, and surrender the claim that love requires us to govern everything forever.

Forgiveness has a similar relation to relinquishment. Some injuries continue to demand recognition, protection, or the repair of justice. Forgiving does not make those claims unreal. It renounces the desire to make another person’s suffering the final settlement of our own. Where reconciliation is possible, charity moves toward it truthfully. Where contact would renew harm or where the other person refuses peace, the will can still surrender revenge and seek that person’s conversion. A dying person should not be forced into an unsafe encounter in order to satisfy someone else’s picture of a perfect farewell.

Possessions also teach us where our hearts have settled. Christ joins treasure and heart (Mt 6:19–21). What we cannot willingly share may reveal a deeper dependence than the amount itself suggests. Almsgiving is an act of worshipful freedom: something under our control becomes available for another’s need. Responsible care for dependents remains a duty, and generosity must be ordered by justice. But the duty of provision should not become an indefinite excuse for refusing every gift. The distinction becomes clearer when we remember that death will eventually remove even what we now decline to share.

There is also the attachment to being understood. We may leave a conversation unresolved, our intentions misread, or a work imperfectly appreciated. Charity permits us to explain what should be explained, but it cannot promise that every person will finally see us as we wish. Christ knows the truth. Learning to rest in his knowledge loosens the need to compel agreement from every witness. This can make the last years gentler: there are things we may clarify, things we must repair, and things we may leave with God.

These relinquishments are learned through repeated acts, and repetition should not discourage us. A single generous decision does not abolish possessiveness, nor does one patient response remove the desire to control. Grace forms habits through the return to what is good. Each renewed act places a little more of life under charity. When age or illness narrows the field of action, the same charity can continue within a smaller field. A grateful word, a patient silence, or the acceptance of needed help may then become the full work of a day.

## Cyprian’s School of Mortal Life

St. Cyprian addresses a community experiencing widespread death in his treatise *On Mortality*. Disease has reached believers as well as their neighbors. Some are unsettled that faith has not protected them from the common affliction. Cyprian answers that Christians share the bodily condition of the human race while awaiting incorruption (no. 8). Believers and unbelievers alike become sick, and the faithful must endure bodily suffering until the promised transformation. He directs their hope toward the life to come while examining how they respond to the epidemic before them.

He recalls the struggle with avarice, lust, anger, ambition, pride, and envy (nos. 4–5). The longing to be with Christ includes the longing to be free of this warfare, to love without the instability that wounds love here. His language is ardent: the Christian is a traveler whose home lies with God. At the close of the treatise he gathers the apostles, prophets, martyrs, virgins, and merciful into the expectation of a heavenly homeland (no. 26). The end of life is contemplated through the company into which the faithful hope to be received.

This desire has an apostolic pattern. Paul longs to depart and be with Christ, yet recognizes that continuing to live serves the needs of the faithful (Phil 1:20–26). Charity holds together the desire for the Lord and the willingness to remain at work. We may ask for healing and more time, especially for the sake of responsibilities entrusted to us. We may also consent to the Lord’s summons when it comes. The desire for heaven does not give us authority to hasten death; it orders the heart toward God while leaving our time in his hands.

Cyprian’s most searching account of the epidemic concerns what it reveals about the living. Do the healthy tend the sick? Do relatives love their own? Do physicians remain with their patients? Do the powerful show pity, and do the wealthy become generous (no. 16)? Mortality tests more than the composure of the person who dies. It tests the charity of everyone near the suffering. The Christian who remembers death must be ready to spend life in service. An eloquent desire for heaven is hollow if it leaves the sick neighbor without help.

He also addresses believers who fear that an ordinary death has deprived them of the martyrdom for which they had hoped (no. 17). Martyrdom, he answers, is not under their control. God knows the heart’s readiness and judges it truly. This passage releases us from trying to choose a spiritually impressive ending. Our task is fidelity to the circumstances God actually permits. A hidden death after long frailty can receive the same wholehearted consent that had imagined itself answering a public trial. The Lord sees the will that illness prevents from displaying its strength.

Cyprian rebukes grief severely, particularly where he fears that Christians mourn as though the departed had been extinguished (nos. 20–21). He urges them to express their hope in the way they mourn. The Gospel also recounts Christ’s tears at Lazarus’s tomb, and Augustine describes his grief for Monica. These accounts show how deeply the separation can hurt even those who hope in resurrection. We can acknowledge the pain and continue praying for the departed, trusting that death has not destroyed the people whose absence we feel.

Cyprian’s questions require us to examine our conduct toward those who are suffering. As we ask God to free us from avarice and ambition, we can make time or possessions available to someone who needs them. A visit, a meal, relief for an exhausted caregiver, or faithful professional care responds to the needs he names. Such service may interrupt the plans we had made for prayer or reflection. It is itself part of Christian preparation, because we are practicing the charity in which we hope to persevere until death.

## Polycarp’s Long Fidelity

The Church of Smyrna recounts how its bishop, St. Polycarp, endured martyrdom after a long life of service to Christ. Writing to fellow Christians, it commends his conduct as faithful to the Gospel. At the opening, the community emphasizes that he waited to be delivered up and that Christian love seeks the salvation of the brethren as well as one’s own (*Martyrdom of Polycarp* 1). He faces the threat of death with concern for others, accepts their help, and continues his ministry of prayer. His conduct shows how a death endured faithfully can complete the charity practiced through a lifetime.

Polycarp receives his friends’ help before he is brought to trial, and the community preserves the memory of his fidelity after his death. He must answer personally before the magistrate, yet he has lived and served among people who love him. Our own preparation likewise depends on the Church. We have received the faith through others and are sustained by their prayer. What they have taught us can help us remain faithful when they can no longer assist us directly. Remembering their care also gives us reason to ask how our conduct will affect those who survive us.

The account gives a sharp warning against trying to manufacture the occasion. It recalls a man named Quintus who volunteered and encouraged others to do the same, then yielded when frightened by the beasts. The author declines to commend such voluntary self-surrender to persecution, saying that the Gospel does not teach it (ch. 4). Soon afterward, Polycarp accepts his friends’ persuasion to withdraw from the city (ch. 5). The narrative permits him to receive prudent help and avoid immediate capture. Readiness for martyrdom can coexist with care for the life that remains available for service.

We too can become attached to an imagined way of dying. The thought of a public confession may seem easier to bear than years of growing dependence. We may imagine generous sufferings while resisting an ordinary inconvenience already before us. Polycarp accepts counsel and withdraws when his friends urge it, then remains faithful when he is captured. We can learn from him to receive prudent help while asking for grace to endure what cannot be avoided. This requires attention to our actual circumstances and duties, including those less appealing than the trials we have imagined.

When the armed party finally reaches him, the narrative lingers over his treatment of the men who have come to arrest him. He orders food and drink for them and asks for time to pray. In the prayer that follows he remembers those who have come into his life, the great and the small, the distinguished and the obscure, together with the whole Catholic Church throughout the world (chs. 7–8). The approach of death gathers his concern for others into prayer. The hospitality and remembrance show a heart still occupied with persons beyond the immediate threat to itself.

A final illness may leave us little of the strength or freedom of action Polycarp had at his arrest. Pain and exhaustion can make it difficult to attend to anyone else. We can still ask for the charity expressed in his hospitality and intercession. It may be possible to thank a caregiver, ask that an exhausted relative receive help, or remember a person in prayer. Practicing intercession while we are well can help us remember others when illness makes sustained thought difficult. When we can no longer form words, the Church continues to pray for us and for those we love.

Brought before the proconsul, Polycarp is urged to consider his age, swear by Caesar’s fortune, and revile Christ. His reply recalls eighty-six years of service to the Lord and the goodness he has received from him; he cannot now blaspheme his King and Savior (ch. 9). He names years of service, leaving his precise age uncertain. His reason for refusing is personal and explicit: the Lord has done him good throughout that service. When the proconsul demands that he revile Christ, Polycarp remembers the Savior’s goodness and confesses his allegiance to him.

Memory can serve our preparation in this way. We naturally remember our failures when contemplating judgment, and conscience has reason to bring them before mercy. We can also remember the Lord’s fidelity: the pardon received, the strength supplied for a duty, the person through whom help arrived, the good preserved through a season of confusion. Such remembrance gives substance to thanksgiving. We are entrusting our final hour to the same Savior who has already borne with us. His past gifts do not promise that the manner of our death will be easy; they teach us whose goodness remains present when ease is taken away.

The governor changes his threats, moving from beasts to fire, while Polycarp remains firm in his confession (chs. 10–12). Each threat demands that he choose between preserving his bodily life and remaining faithful to Christ. Polycarp answers with the eternal consequences of that choice before him. His endurance shows what baptismal fidelity may require under persecution. The life given to Christ must be entrusted to him even when keeping it would require apostasy. Polycarp’s earlier willingness to withdraw from danger makes his constancy at trial especially instructive: he had sought to preserve his life, yet refuses to preserve it at this cost.

Before the execution, Polycarp prays in thanksgiving (ch. 14). He thanks the Father, through the Son, for the day and hour in which he is counted among the martyrs and shares the cup of Christ. His thanksgiving includes hope of resurrection and the eternal life of both soul and body through the Holy Spirit. He thus commends to God the body about to be killed, trusting that God will raise it. This hope explains how he can give thanks at such an hour: through his suffering he expects to share in Christ’s life. His gratitude concerns the salvation he receives through the Passion and Resurrection of the Lord.

Polycarp’s gratitude concerns God’s gift; his persecutors remain responsible for killing him. His death exemplifies the good use of death Augustine later explains. Grace enables the martyr to remain faithful under violence, while those inflicting it act wrongly. We can apply this distinction to suffering imposed on us in less extreme circumstances. We may oppose the wrong, seek relief, and ask for protection. Where suffering remains despite these efforts, we can ask God to help us endure it faithfully and continue to love those entrusted to us.

After Polycarp’s death, the community defends its love for him by distinguishing the adoration owed to Christ, the Son of God, from the affection given to the martyrs as the Lord’s disciples and imitators (ch. 17). Its love for Polycarp follows from his faithful service of Christ. The community gathers his remains and describes its intended annual remembrance: thanksgiving for those who have completed their course, and preparation for those who may yet follow them (ch. 18). The Christians who remember his death expect to face trials of their own. They give thanks for his fidelity and seek strength to persevere as he did.

We can remember the saints with the same desire for help in our own trials. Polycarp’s fidelity gives us reason to thank God for the grace that sustained him and to ask for perseverance in our less dramatic duties. We can also remember the faithful departed who taught us to pray or helped us remain faithful, asking mercy for their sins. Their example may disclose a neglected duty, an old kindness we can imitate, or a good work we are now able to continue. Annual remembrance allows gratitude for them to lead to a definite act of charity.

## From Penance to Viaticum

The Church accompanies the dying through the sacramental life in which she has nourished them from the beginning. In Penance, Christ reconciles the sinner. In Anointing of the Sick, he strengthens the person who is seriously ill or weakened by old age. In the Eucharist as Viaticum, he gives himself as food for the final journey. Through these sacraments the dying receive his forgiveness, strength, and presence as they approach the end of earthly life (CCC 1524–1525).

Preparation therefore includes learning to receive forgiveness before our last illness. An honest confession requires us to speak without arranging an excuse at the center of every admission. We name the sin because we desire freedom from it. The habit of approaching the sacrament can gradually overcome the fear that being fully known means being rejected. Christ already knows the truth, and his mercy calls us to bring the truth willingly before him. Shame need not be allowed to reserve a part of our life from absolution.

The thought of death can uncover something long withheld. A person may have carried a grave wrong for years, hoping that silence would diminish its reality. Another may have become distant from the sacraments through a combination of shame, neglect, and the assumption that returning would be impossibly difficult. The present invitation is simple in its direction, even when the circumstances need patient help: seek a priest and begin to speak truthfully. There is no need to settle every anxiety about the conversation before requesting it. The desire to return is already something to bring.

The sacrament of Anointing need not be reserved for the last few moments. The fitting time has arrived when a member of the faithful begins to be in danger of death through sickness or old age. The Catechism also identifies serious operations and increasing frailty as occasions for seeking it, and permits its repetition when a new grave illness arises or the condition becomes more serious (CCC 1514–1516). The sick person and those caring for him should contact a priest early enough for the Church’s ministry to be received with whatever attention and freedom the condition allows. Waiting for certainty about the final hour can deprive a person of that opportunity.

James directs the sick to call for the priests of the Church, who pray and anoint in the Lord’s name (Jas 5:14–15). The Catechism describes the sacrament’s gifts as strengthening, peace, and courage, renewed trust in God, and closer union with Christ’s Passion (CCC 1520–1523). Bodily healing is included within its purpose if God wills it. Where illness continues, the sick person still receives grace to endure it in union with Christ.

Anointing also places suffering within the Church’s communion. The sick person receives her intercession and, through union with Christ, contributes to her sanctification (CCC 1522). A life whose outward activities have diminished can still bear fruit for others. This truth should be offered gently, with respect for the sufferer’s freedom and strength. We cannot demand that another person make visible spiritual use of every pain. The sacrament gives what the weakened person needs; it is not a test designed to prove how well he is coping.

Viaticum brings the Eucharist to the threshold of death. The Lord who once gave his life now gives his risen body as the pledge of resurrection. The promise that he will raise those who eat his flesh and drink his blood appears at John 6:55 in the Douay–Rheims, corresponding to John 6:54 as cited by CCC 1524. The dying Christian receives the same Lord who has nourished the Church throughout the pilgrimage. The one who can no longer come to the assembly remains a member of Christ’s body, visited and fed by its Head.

In receiving Communion through life, we have been receiving the Lord whom we hope to receive at death. Each reception calls for faith in his presence, gratitude for his gift, and a willingness to live according to his charity. We can prepare for Viaticum by attending to those dispositions now. If illness later makes concentration difficult, the habits formed in ordinary worship may help us turn toward him with the attention still possible. We can also make our desire for Communion known while we are able to express it clearly.

Practical preparation serves this sacramental readiness. We can make our wish for a priest known to family and caregivers, keep parish contact information accessible, and speak plainly about the importance of confession, anointing, and Communion when serious illness comes. The particulars will depend on the person’s condition and the priest’s pastoral judgment. These arrangements are acts of love toward those who may otherwise have to guess our wishes while distressed. We are providing room for the Church to do what she has received from Christ, and for ourselves to receive it as fully as possible.

## Keeping Faith with the Dying

A dying person remains our neighbor, entitled to the patience and reverence charity gives to a person. The changing body can dominate the room: breathing, discomfort, medication, and the labor of ordinary care demand attention. Within those necessities there is still someone to address by name, someone whose preferences matter, whose history deserves respect, and whose weakness does not cancel dignity. The body needs care precisely because it belongs to this person, not because it can still perform the actions that once made the person useful to others.

Accompaniment often asks us to accept a slower pace than our anxieties would prefer. A conversation may need to stop before we feel it is finished. The dying may want silence when visitors have prepared words, or may return repeatedly to a worry the family believed was already settled. We should listen for the person rather than insist upon a scene that reassures us. A confession of fear may be an act of trust toward the listener. It need not be immediately corrected into a statement of confidence.

The Lord asked his disciples to watch with him in his agony. We can remember his request when remaining present seems a small service beside the suffering we cannot relieve. Watching may include prayer, but also water, a changed position, help with a practical concern, or relief for another exhausted watcher. Caregivers need care as well. The relative who has remained attentive for weeks can become weary, irritable, and ashamed of weariness. Taking a turn at the bedside or helping with other duties allows that person to rest without leaving the dying alone.

Christian care protects life without treating every possible prolongation as obligatory. Ordinary care remains owed even when death is imminent. Medical procedures that are burdensome, dangerous, extraordinary, or disproportionate to their expected outcome may legitimately be discontinued: one accepts the inability to prevent death without choosing to cause it. Pain relief may also be legitimate when a risk of shortening life is foreseen, provided death is not intended as either means or end. The Catechism distinguishes these decisions from euthanasia and assigns them to the competent patient or those legitimately entitled to act, respecting the patient’s reasonable will and interests (CCC 2276–2279). Applying the principles requires the actual prognosis, burdens, alternatives, and competent medical and pastoral counsel. Inability to cure, by itself, does not make all treatment or care dispensable.

These distinctions serve a person whom love must neither abandon nor claim as its possession. A family may find it hard to accept that further intervention cannot secure the return it longs for. Another may be tempted to treat dependence as the disappearance of meaningful life. In both situations, attentive care remains a duty. We are called to seek the person’s good within the truth of the condition, while acknowledging that our power to heal has limits. Hope in resurrection permits us to recognize those limits without making death our chosen solution.

The dying person may still be able to offer gratitude, forgive, ask pardon, or entrust a concern. These possibilities deserve room, but they should not become demands for a visibly edifying death. Pain, delirium, exhaustion, or unconsciousness can take away the power to speak coherently. The final words we hear are not a complete judgment upon a lifetime. We continue to pray, seek the Church’s ministry where possible, and commend the person to the God who knows the heart more deeply than any witness beside the bed.

Even a solitary or sudden death is not outside that knowledge. Those who were absent may suffer over what they were unable to say or do. There may be real faults to acknowledge, but absence itself need not be turned into a claim that the loved one was therefore abandoned by God. The Lord’s presence does not depend upon our success in being present. We can entrust the unspoken words, repent of what needs repentance, and continue the charity that remains possible through prayer and care for those who survive.

## Monica’s Farewell and Augustine’s Tears

In the ninth book of his *Confessions*, Augustine remembers a conversation with his mother shortly before her death. They stand together at a window in Ostia and speak of the eternal life of the saints (IX.10.23–26). Their minds ascend through created things toward the Wisdom by whom all things are made. Then they return to ordinary speech. The account gives a moment of shared contemplation within a particular mother and son’s history, after the long years in which Monica had prayed for his conversion.

Monica tells him that her great earthly hope has been fulfilled: she has seen him become a Catholic Christian and a servant of God. Soon afterward she falls ill. When Augustine’s brother expresses the wish that she might die in her own country, she sets aside anxiety about the place of burial. Her request is to be remembered at the Lord’s altar. Augustine marvels at the change, because she had formerly cared deeply about being buried beside her husband (IX.11.27–28). Grace has loosened a cherished attachment without diminishing the love beneath it.

Monica trusts that God will know where to raise her body. She can therefore accept burial far from home and ask for what she most desires: remembrance at the altar. Her changed wish shows how faith in resurrection can loosen anxiety about circumstances we cannot control. We can make appropriate arrangements and honor family bonds, yet still entrust to God what illness, distance, or a sudden death prevents us from arranging. Those who survive us can pray for us wherever we are buried, as Monica asks her sons to do.

Augustine’s account of the death itself is painfully exact. He closes her eyes. Grief rises within him, and he attempts to restrain it. Those who hear him speak with others may suppose he is not sorrowful, while he knows the inward wound of their life together having been torn apart (IX.12.29–32). His theological understanding has not prevented the loss from reaching the habits of love. The familiar presence, built from years of shared life, has been taken away.

He also finds himself sorrowing over his sorrow. He is troubled that ordinary human feeling has such power over him. Later, after rest, he recalls Monica’s tenderness and service and allows himself to weep before God (IX.12.33). The tears he had restrained become a place of relief. He does not invite the reader to mock them. He asks for the charity that recognizes a fellow human being grieving for the mother who had once wept for his salvation.

St. Ambrose likewise begins the second book of *On the Death of His Brother Satyrus* by remembering the fresh wound to which the first address had given expression. He says that remedies applied too sharply may increase pain, and allows a place to natural affection and tears before turning toward a wider consolation in resurrection (II.1–3). Later he asks that memory become grateful rather than something we must avoid because it only hurts (II.8). The bishop speaks as a brother. His exhortation toward hope comes from within the bond whose severance he feels.

The apostolic command is that we not grieve as those without hope (1 Thess 4:12–17 in the Douay–Rheims). Hope changes the meaning and destination of grief. It does not require the bereaved to demonstrate, at once, that every ache has subsided. Tears can accompany confidence in Christ. Remembering a gift can remain painful while gratitude slowly grows. The Church gives us words and actions in which both sorrow and hope can be carried when our private strength is insufficient.

Augustine also prays for Monica’s sins. He praises the grace visible in her life, yet acknowledges her need for forgiveness after Baptism (IX.13.34–36). Her good works are God’s gifts; she depends on the mercy of the Christ who redeemed her. He trusts the innocent blood shed by the Savior and asks that his mother remain under that protection. The son who remembers her holiness still intercedes for her, relying on the same mercy she had sought for him.

At the close of the book he asks its readers to remember Monica and Patricius at the altar (IX.13.37). He seeks the prayers of people who will know his parents through his account of them. We can follow him in asking others to pray for our own dead. Affection alone cannot tell us their eternal state. It gives us reason to desire their salvation and to continue asking mercy for them, through the intercession the Church teaches and especially at the altar where Monica wished to be remembered.

At a Christian funeral the Church honors the body, proclaims resurrection, and commends the deceased to God. When the Eucharistic sacrifice is celebrated, she asks the Father to purify the departed and admit them to the fullness of his kingdom (CCC 1684–1690). The funeral therefore helps the dead as well as consoling the living. Our desire to express admiration and remember the person’s gifts should leave room for this intercession. Even when a life has given much reason for gratitude, the person still needs the mercy sought in the Church’s prayer.

This prayer continues after the funeral. The Church’s suffrages, especially the Eucharistic sacrifice, help those undergoing the final purification of the saved (CCC 1030–1032). We can remember the dead at Mass, pray for them, and perform works of charity in their memory. A visit to a grave may renew sorrow, but it can also become an occasion to commend the person to God again. As time passes, anniversaries and familiar prayers help us continue an intercession we might otherwise neglect when the first intensity of bereavement has subsided.

## The Mother of God and the Coming Year

The Church teaches us to ask the Mother of God’s intercession for the hour of death and to entrust ourselves to St. Joseph, patron of a happy death (CCC 1014). These familiar devotions place the unknown hour within the communion of saints. We are asking for help at an event that will really come, for perseverance in grace when our own resources may be least available. The habitual petition becomes more deliberate as we allow its meaning to reach the life we are living.

Mary stood by the cross of her Son, where he gave her to the beloved disciple as his mother (Jn 19:25–27). She remained with him through his suffering. When we ask her help for our last hour, we seek her intercession with the Son whose death she witnessed. We ask to remain faithful to him, to receive his mercy, and to endure the trial with her help. Her presence at Calvary gives us reason to turn to her when the suffering of someone we love leaves us able only to remain beside him and pray.

We also ask St. Joseph to help us persevere faithfully. The Church commends him as patron of a happy death; the Gospels recount his care for Jesus and Mary while leaving his own death unrecorded. We can turn to his intercession as we put our affairs in order and seek the grace to surrender them. The responsibilities we have received remain duties to fulfill, and we can ask his help in providing for those entrusted to us before our work passes to others.

In asking these saints’ help, we seek the grace to repent, forgive, repair, and persevere. Their intercession assists the sacramental life and charity in which we are called to live. We can receive the prompting to confess a concealed fault, follow a priest’s counsel, or undertake a resisted duty with gratitude for the help we have sought. A familiar devotion becomes more deliberate when we remember what we have asked for and respond to the opportunities for amendment that follow.

An annual return to the Four Last Things gives us time to examine the year that has passed and consider the year we may still receive. We can give thanks for the graces received in the sacraments, for protection and reconciliation, and for the people through whom we have been loved. We can acknowledge the sins that require confession and the duties still awaiting attention. Prayer can then address these needs specifically, asking for help with the work and relationships in which faithfulness will be required of us.

A quiet period for this reflection can begin with Christ at Lazarus’s tomb, his prayer in Gethsemane, or his mercy toward the dying thief. Attending to what he does and says helps us bring our needs to him in faith. We can then examine the year. Has a recurring fault become easier to excuse? Has prayer been steadily crowded out? Have those who depend on us received patience and attention? Are we allowing an old injury to determine how we treat someone? We can consider a particular instance and ask what repentance or greater charity now requires.

The next step should be proportionate and definite. A confession can be arranged. A needed restitution can be begun. A conversation with a priest can address a matter conscience cannot resolve alone. One neglected duty can receive a place in the week. A resolution becomes more trustworthy when it takes bodily form in time, money, words, or service. The annual return then has a fruit that persists after the emotional force of the meditation has faded.

Practical affairs also belong within charity. The people who survive us may need to know where essential records are kept, what responsibilities remain, whom to contact, and what we desire for sacramental care and a Catholic funeral. Appropriate legal and medical arrangements require competent advice and attention to actual circumstances. Their spiritual purpose is plain: we are relieving others of avoidable confusion and making provision for those entrusted to us. Arranging these matters can be an act of humility, an acknowledgment that our work will pass into other hands.

The remembrance of our dead should have a place as well. Their names can be brought to Mass and prayer; an anniversary can renew intercession; the needs of someone they once cared for can be attended to. We may discover that grief has become closed around a single painful scene. The Church’s prayer opens it again toward the whole person and toward the mercy of Christ. We can remember the gifts with gratitude, acknowledge what was difficult, and ask for the purification and fulfillment that only God can give.

The coming year remains uncertain. If it is given, there will be work to do and pleasures for which to give thanks. We can return to them with a clearer sense of the time available to love the people still with us. A visit we have postponed can be made; an apology can be offered; a kindness can be acknowledged. Some circumstances will remain unresolved despite our efforts. We can place them before God and continue doing the good within our power, grateful for the life in which he still permits us to serve.

## We Are the Lord’s

Our preparation will remain imperfect. We may return to the same weaknesses and need the same mercy long after we hoped to have outgrown them. The years may not unfold according to the plan made in a quiet hour. Yet the foundation remains Christ’s death and Resurrection, his call to repentance, and the grace by which we can answer. A good death is finally a gift to be sought with a faithful life and a humble hope.

The Church teaches us to confess, feeds us with the Eucharist, strengthens us in illness, and commends us to God when our earthly speech has ended. We can receive that ministry now, make our desire for it known to those who may care for us, and ask the saints to help us persevere. The same charity requires us to help the dying receive these gifts and to continue praying for them after death.

“Whether we live, or whether we die, we are the Lord’s” (Rom 14:8). We prepare to die by living out that belonging: receiving mercy, making peace where we can, keeping faith with the vulnerable, and offering the time entrusted to us. When that time is complete, we ask to surrender it to the same Lord who has sustained every faithful act within it. He has entered death, he lives forever, and he will raise his people.

## Scope, Method, and Qualifications

This essay is Catholic spiritual reading for an annual return to the Four Last Things, especially around November and Advent. It contemplates death through Christ’s Paschal mystery, freedom from fear, the penitent thief, baptismal life, personal continuity and resurrection, martyrdom, preparation, sacramental care, accompaniment, grief, and suffrages. Scripture and authoritative Catholic teaching govern; the Fathers and saints supply identified theological and spiritual witness. Practical applications are original pastoral synthesis. The account of original justice is not a biological theory. No particular person’s eternal state, local lectionary, funeral formulary, or medical case is determined. Sacramental and medical principles are general and require competent application. The work has received source and editorial checks, not ecclesiastical approval. Historical translations retain their own rights; biblical wording follows the Douay–Rheims American edition of 1899. The references specify material numbering differences and source editions.
