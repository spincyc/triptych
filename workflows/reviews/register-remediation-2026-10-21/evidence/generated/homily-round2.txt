Early on the morning before the Pasch, the Passover, a prisoner is brought to the Roman governor's hall. His accusers will not go inside, so Pilate goes out to them and comes back in to the prisoner. He has been told that this man calls himself a king, and his first question is the one we have just heard read: "Art thou the king of the Jews?" The man in front of him is bound. His own nation and the chief priests have handed him over. In Saint John's account, what follows this conversation is the scourging, the crown of thorns, the purple, and the handing over to be crucified.

This Mass has been singing of his kingship since it began. At the Introit we heard the song of the angels round the throne in the Apocalypse, that "The Lamb that was slain is worthy to receive power." The Collect prayed for all the families of the nations. Just before the Gospel, in the chant called the Gradual, the choir sang from a psalm about a king who shall rule "from sea to sea, and from the river unto the ends of the earth," and "all kings of the earth shall adore him: all nations shall serve him." Then the prisoner answers Pilate: "My kingdom is not of this world. If my kingdom were of this world, my servants would certainly strive that I should not be delivered to the Jews: but now my kingdom is not from hence." How can a kingdom that is not of this world claim the ends of the earth and the homage of every king?

Two answers come easily, and both are mistaken. The first is Pilate's fear: that this man wants Caesar's throne. The second is the opposite mistake. It hears "not of this world" as meaning that Christ reigns in heaven and in the privacy of the soul, and has no claim on the world of laws, offices and governments. Pope Pius XI, when he gave the Church this feast, called it a grave error to say that Christ has no authority whatever in civil affairs. Nor can that meaning stand beside the Collect and the Gradual we have just heard, which pray for the nations and sing of the kings.

Saint Augustine read the Lord's words closely. Jesus puts the same thing three ways: his kingdom is not of this world; if it were, his servants would fight; it is not from hence. "From hence" is the old English for "from here." Augustine points out what the Lord did not say. He did not say that his kingdom is not in this world, only that it is not of it; and he did not say that it is not here, only that it is not from here. "For His kingdom is here until the end of the world, having tares intermingled therewith until the harvest." It is here the way a stranger is in a foreign country: present, but taking its origin from somewhere else. And when Augustine asks who makes up this kingdom, his answer is short: "Those who believe in Him." They once belonged to the world, and they have been made his kingdom by being born again in Christ.

Augustine explains that change from today's Epistle. Saint Paul gives thanks to the Father "who hath delivered us from the power of darkness and hath translated us into the kingdom of the Son of his love." To translate, in Paul's sense, is to carry across: to lift someone out of one place and set him down in another. Saint John Chrysostom presses the word. God did more than rescue us from darkness; he carried us into a kingdom, and called it the kingdom of the Son he loves, so giving us the same honour as the Son. The people of this kingdom have been moved into it, and they go on living in the world while they belong to it.

Chrysostom also asks what the denial means. Christ does rule here, he says; the kingdom is called "not from hence" because its power comes from above and is not a human power. The proof is the servants who do not fight. "Here He shows the weakness of kingship among us, that its strength lies in servants; but that which is above is sufficient for itself, needing nothing." No one is fighting for the man who stands bound before Pilate, and he is bound, Chrysostom says, "by consenting, and giving Himself up."

Saint Thomas Aquinas puts the two halves together in a single phrase: the kingdom is not from here, and yet it is here, because it is everywhere. A kingdom that begins in one place in the world is bounded by that place and must defend its borders. A kingdom whose beginning is above has no border on earth to defend, and so it can claim the whole earth without competing with any power in it. Augustine, on the same verse, has the Lord say to every kingdom of the earth: "I interfere not with your government in this world."

The Gradual still sings that all kings shall adore him. Augustine, explaining that psalm, says the kings adore him because he delivered the poor from the mighty, and the poor man of the psalm is the whole people of those who believe in him. "In this people are also kings adoring Him." They come in the way everyone comes in: they do not think it beneath them to be poor, and they humbly confess their sins. Saint Robert Bellarmine reads the second psalm, from which our Offertory is taken, as the prophet urging the kings of this world to subject themselves freely to Christ, the supreme King of all kings. The Gradual's verse is fulfilled in part, Bellarmine says, when a great many princes and nations are converted, and to the letter at the day of judgment, when every knee shall bend. Augustine tells those same kings not to be sad, as if their dignity were being taken from them, because it is good for them to be under him.

That is the subjection the Collect asks for. It names the trouble first. The families of the nations are scattered, and what scattered them is a wound, the wound of sin. Then it asks that they be brought under his rule, and it calls that rule most gentle, most loving. Pope Pius XI taught, in the letter that gave us this feast, that every person, every family and every state is under the rule of Christ, and that rulers owe him public honour and obedience; and he wrote there too that Christ in his earthly life did not interfere with those who held earthly goods, and does not now. The subjection is real. It is asked of every station in life, rulers included, and it is the subjection of people who have heard the truth.

For the Lord tells Pilate what his kingship is for: "For this was I born, and for this came I into the world; that I should give testimony to the truth. Every one that is of the truth heareth my voice." Augustine takes hearing here to mean obeying and believing, and he says that in these words the Lord speaks of the grace by which he calls. A kingdom whose servants do not fight gathers its people another way. The King speaks the truth, and grace gives ears to hear it. The Gospel stops at that sentence, before Pilate asks, "What is truth?" Later in this Mass the Preface names the kingdom by its goods, and truth comes first: "a kingdom of truth and life; a kingdom of holiness and grace, a kingdom of justice, love and peace."

The King who gave himself up in Pilate's hall is the one offered on this altar. In a few minutes, in the prayer the priest says quietly over the gifts, called the Secret, he will say, "O Lord, we offer Thee this host for the reconciliation of humanity," and ask that Jesus Christ, whom we offer in this sacrifice, "will bestow on all Gentiles the gifts of unity and peace." The scattering the Collect named at the beginning of Mass is answered here by a gift asked of the King who is himself the victim. The Preface sets out the order: the Father anointed his Son as eternal Priest and King of all, so that he might offer himself on the altar of the cross, and then, with every creature made subject to him, hand over to the Father an eternal and universal kingdom.

After Communion the priest will pray for us as those "who glory to fight under the standard of Christ the King." That comes not long after a Gospel in which the King says his servants do not fight. If his kingdom needs no one to fight for it, as Chrysostom says, then the fighting this prayer speaks of cannot be his defence. It is our own struggle. Saint Hilary, writing on the second psalm, says the kingdom of God is where sin is overcome and death destroyed, and that those who become heirs with the eternal King must themselves become kings. Blessed Ildefonso Schuster, writing on this prayer, says that the King's standard is the Cross.

Augustine's kings came into the kingdom with the poor, confessing their sins. The way is open to every one of us here. If you have stayed away from confession, go soon, and bring to it whatever has kept you from hearing his voice.

The prisoner Pilate questioned is the King this whole Mass acclaims. His kingdom does not come from this world, and it is present in it: in you, whom the Father has carried out of darkness; in every nation where his voice is heard; and on this altar, where he who consented to be bound is offered for the reconciliation of mankind. He asks the kings of the earth, and each of us, to come under his rule freely now, before every knee bends at the judgment. To those who come, he gives what the Communion antiphon promises: "the Lord will bless his people with peace."
