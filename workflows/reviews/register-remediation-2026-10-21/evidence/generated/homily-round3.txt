Early on the morning before the Pasch, the men who had brought Jesus to Pilate would not go into the governor's hall. So Pilate went back and forth, out to the accusers and in to the prisoner. The Gospel we have just heard is his first questioning inside. Jesus stands there bound, handed over by the leaders of his own nation. Pilate asks him whether he is the king of the Jews, and Jesus, before answering, asks Pilate where the question came from.

The Mass around that scene makes very large claims for this prisoner. Before the Gospel, the Gradual said of this King that "he shall rule from sea to sea, and from the river unto the ends of the earth," and that "all kings of the earth shall adore him: all nations shall serve him." After the Creed, the Offertory gives the Father's word to his Son in the second psalm: "Ask of me, and I will give thee the Gentiles for thy inheritance, and the utmost parts of the earth for thy possession." At the beginning of Mass the Collect asked that all the families of the nations be brought under his rule. And in the middle of it all the prisoner says, "My kingdom is not of this world." How can a kingdom that is not of this world claim the whole earth, and every king in it?

Two quick answers are on offer, and neither will do. The first hears "not of this world" as meaning that Christ's kingship belongs to the soul and to the next life, and has no claim on how the world is run. The Pope who gave the Church this feast called it a grave error to say that Christ has no authority whatever in civil affairs. The second answer is the suspicion behind Pilate's question: that this king wants Caesar's throne. Jesus answers that one himself. If his kingdom were of this world, he says, his servants would be fighting to keep him from being handed over.

St Augustine reads the Lord's words closely. Jesus did not say that his kingdom is not in this world. He said that it is not of this world. He did not say that it is not here. He said that it is "not from hence." Those words say where the kingdom comes from. They do not say that it is absent. Augustine puts it plainly: "For His kingdom is here until the end of the world, having tares intermingled therewith until the harvest." It is here, and it lives here as a stranger lives in a foreign land. He also says what the kingdom is: those who believe in Christ. They were of the world while they belonged to its prince, and by being born again in Christ they have become a kingdom that is no longer of the world, though they are still in it.

Augustine explains this change with the words of today's Epistle. St Paul gives thanks to the Father, "who hath delivered us from the power of darkness and hath translated us into the kingdom of the Son of his love." To translate, in that old sense of the word, is to carry across. Those whom the Father carries across are not taken out of the world. They are carried, while they live in it, out from under the power of darkness.

St John Chrysostom points to the proof Jesus himself gives, the servants who do not fight. "Here He shows the weakness of kingship among us," he says, "that its strength lies in servants; but that which is above is sufficient for itself, needing nothing." That is why this King can stand bound before the governor and still answer him as a king.

St Thomas Aquinas draws from this the answer to our question. The kingdom is not from here, he says, and yet it is here, because it is everywhere. A kingdom that takes its beginning from one place in the world is bounded by that place. A kingdom whose beginning is above is bounded by no place. So it can claim every nation without competing with any throne.

How, then, does it claim them? Jesus tells Pilate: "For this was I born, and for this came I into the world; that I should give testimony to the truth. Every one that is of the truth heareth my voice." The subjects of this King are those who hear him. Augustine says that to hear, in this verse, is to believe and to obey, and that the hearing is itself a grace, because it is Christ who calls. This kingdom gathers its people by the truth spoken to them and by the grace that lets them hear it. The Collect said what is wrong with the nations: they are scattered, the scattering is a wound, and sin made the wound. The rule it asked for them it called most loving. The Preface of this feast names the kingdom by what it holds: "a kingdom of truth and life; a kingdom of holiness and grace, a kingdom of justice, love and peace." The first thing it names is truth, the truth this prisoner was born to bear witness to.

There is a further reason the servants do not fight. Chrysostom says that Christ was made captive "by consenting, and giving Himself up." The trial before Pilate is the beginning of the Passion, and the prisoner has accepted it. That is how he wins the nations the Offertory promises. The Father says to the Son, "Ask of me." St Thomas observes that the nations were already his by right, because he is the Son. Yet the Father willed that he should ask for what was already his, and he asked in two ways: by his prayer, and by his passion. So the King who stands before Pilate is not losing his kingdom. He is asking for it, in the way the Father willed, by giving himself up. The Introit at the start of this Mass sang of him in just this way: "The Lamb that was slain is worthy to receive power."

That same sacrifice is offered on this altar. In the Secret the priest offers "this host for the reconciliation of humanity." Host there means victim: what is offered is Christ himself. And the prayer asks that he "will bestow on all Gentiles the gifts of unity and peace." The nations that the Collect called scattered by sin are to be gathered as a gift, and the gift is asked for here.

What, then, of the kings in the Gradual, and of anyone who holds power? Augustine speaks to them directly when he explains the second psalm: "be ye not sad, kings of the earth, as if your excellency were taken from you." It is good for them, he says, to be under Christ. And when he explains the Gradual's psalm, he says who the kings are who adore. They belong to the poor people who believe in Christ, and they come in as the poor come in, humbly confessing their sins. St Robert Bellarmine reads the psalm's promise as a call to kings to subject themselves to Christ freely. That happens in part whenever rulers and their peoples are converted, and it will happen to the letter at the judgment, when every knee bends. The Pope who instituted this feast taught both halves of this. Rulers owe Christ public honour and obedience. And Christ did not, and does not, interfere with those who possess the goods of this world.

After Communion we will pray that "we who glory to fight under the standard of Christ the King" may reign with him for ever. It can seem strange to pray that, a little while after hearing the King say that his servants do not fight. Chrysostom's argument rules out one meaning: Christ needs no soldiers to defend him, so our fighting is not his defence. St Hilary of Poitiers says what the kingdom of God is: the place where sin is overcome and death destroyed. Those who become heirs with the eternal King, he adds, must themselves become kings. The war of that prayer, then, is the war against sin, and each of us begins it in himself.

Since this kingdom is everywhere, it includes every place where any of us has charge of other people. The Introit gave us a prayer for such a charge: "Give to the king thy judgment, O God, and to the king's son thy justice." Bellarmine reads it, in its first sense, as David's prayer for his son Solomon, newly anointed, who asked not for riches but for the grace of judging rightly, "for kings were created for the people, not the people for kings." The psalm goes on to ask that the king do justice to the poor. Whatever charge you hold, ask God for that judgment and that justice, and use the charge for the people who are under it.

In a moment we will stand and say the Creed, which is how the subjects of this King answer when they have heard his voice. Then the Son who gave himself up before Pilate will be offered on this altar for the reconciliation of the nations, and he will come to you in Communion. The Communion antiphon sings that "the Lord shall sit king for ever." Augustine reads that verse of the saints. The Lord dwells in them first, he says, in the flood of this world, kept safe in the Church as in the ark, and afterward he will sit reigning in them for ever. That is where the King whom Pilate questioned has his throne: in you who hear his voice, in this world now, and afterward for ever.
