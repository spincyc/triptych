## Interpretation followed

The controlling interpretation is the study's "Not from hence, and therefore
everywhere" (`kingdom-not-from-hence`, sec. 30; interpretations.md §3). One
strand of "The Lamb receives the kingdom" (`lamb-receives-the-kingdom`,
sec. 20; §2) is joined to it: the prisoner's consent, and the asking of
Ps 2:8 made by prayer and passion and offered now in the Secret. The research
(interpretations.md §6, last note) suggested this combination, and the
comparison (sec. 50) treats the two as complementary. At the close, one locus
from "The kingdom handed over" (sec. 40) is used: Augustine on the Communion
verse. It extends the argument's "here" into "for ever" and does not bring in
that interpretation's handing-over argument. Augustine's *Letter* 185 and
Schuster's p. 203 are not used.

## What the text changes, and the detail that makes it possible

A listener commonly hears "My kingdom is not of this world" as meaning that
Christ's kingship is private, inward or deferred, and has no claim on how the
world is run. *Quas primas* names this view as an error. The homily shows from
the wording of Jn 18:36 that the denial concerns where the kingdom comes from
and by what means it is defended, not where it is. The listener then sees the
kingdom as present everywhere, claiming every nation and every person who has
charge of others, while rivalling no throne.

Decisive textual detail: the Gospel's last clause, "but now my kingdom is not
from hence" (*non est hinc*), read with Augustine's distinction of "of" from
"in" and of "from hence" from "here" (*Tract. in Io.* 115.2), and the servants
who do not fight (Jn 18:36; Chrysostom, *Hom. in Io.* 83.4). Aquinas's *non
est hinc … est tamen hic, quia ubique est* (*Super Io.* 18 lect. 6) draws the
conclusion. The argument depends on this Gospel's wording and on the
Gradual's and Offertory's claims, so it would not survive a change of
readings.

## Points given in plain words, not quoted

- Jn 18:28–29 (accusers outside the praetorium; Pilate goes in and out).
- *Quas primas* nn. 17, 32: the grave error of denying Christ authority in
  civil affairs; rulers owe him public honour and obedience; he did not and
  does not interfere with those who possess earthly goods. Described only, as
  the rights route requires.
- Augustine, *Tract. in Io.* 115.2 (of/in, from hence/here; the kingdom
  sojourns as a stranger; the kingdom is those who believe, reborn; Col 1:13
  as his own gloss) and 115.4 (hearing is believing and obeying; the grace
  that calls).
- Aquinas, *Super Io.* 18 lect. 6 (not from here, yet here, because
  everywhere); *Postilla super Psalmos* 2 nn. 5–6 (the nations his by right
  of inheritance; willed to ask; asks by prayer, Jn 17:20, and passion).
- Augustine, *Enarrationes in Psalmos* 2.9 (it is expedient for kings to be
  under him) and 71.14 (kings adoring belong to the poor people who confess
  their sins).
- Bellarmine, *Explanatio*, Ps 2 (kings exhorted to subject themselves
  freely) and Ps 71 (fulfilled to a certain extent in converted princes and
  nations, to the letter at the judgment; literal sense of v. 1, David's
  prayer for Solomon "just anointed," asking not riches but the grace of
  judging rightly).
- Hilary, *Tractatus super Psalmos* 2.42–43 (the kingdom of God is where sin
  is overcome and death destroyed; co-heirs must become kings).
- Augustine, *Enarrationes in Psalmos* 28.10 (the Lord dwells first in his
  saints in the flood of this world, kept in the Church as in the ark, and
  afterward sits reigning in them for ever).
- The Collect (scattered by the wound of sin; most loving rule). It is
  paraphrased because the Lasance English carries the misprint "familes."
- Ps 71's request for justice to the poor (study sec. 10, Introit).

## The homily's own links (preacher's inferences, not credited to a text)

- A kingdom whose beginning is in one place is bounded by it, and one from
  above is bounded by none (the study's inference after Aquinas, sec. 30).
- The trial is the asking of Ps 2:8, and the Secret's sacrifice is that asking
  offered now. The nations' unity is a gift asked for at this altar (study,
  secs. 20 and 30, editorial synthesis).
- The Postcommunion's "fight" is understood through Chrysostom's argument (not
  his defence) and Hilary's kingdom where sin is overcome (the war against
  sin). Neither Father comments on the prayer. The research labels this
  synthesis editorial (interpretations.md §3, Postcommunion).
- The Creed as the answer of those who hear his voice; the Introit verse as a
  prayer for any charge over others; the closing "throne … in you who hear his
  voice," which joins Augustine's kingdom-as-believers (115.2) to his reading
  of Ps 28:10.

## Response

The homily makes one response, once: whatever charge over others a listener
holds, ask God for the judgment and justice of the Introit verse and use the
charge for those under it. It follows from the argument that a kingdom from
above reaches every place, rulers included, as rulers.
