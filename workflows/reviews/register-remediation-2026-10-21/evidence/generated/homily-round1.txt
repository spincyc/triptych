Early on the morning before the Pasch, the men who had brought Jesus to the Roman governor would not go into his hall, and so Pilate went in and out between them and the prisoner. The Gospel we have just heard is his first questioning of Jesus inside. "Art thou the king of the Jews?" Jesus stands there bound, handed over by his own nation and its chief priests. Before the Gospel the choir sang of a very different king: "he shall rule from sea to sea, and from the river unto the ends of the earth," and "all kings of the earth shall adore him: all nations shall serve him." The Collect at the beginning of Mass asked that all the families of the nations be subjected to his rule. In a few minutes the Offertory will give the Father's word to his Son: "Ask of me, and I will give thee the Gentiles for thy inheritance, and the utmost parts of the earth for thy possession." The one to whom all this is promised says to the governor, "My kingdom is not of this world."

Pilate asks a second time, "Art thou a king then?" Set beside the chants of this Mass, his question becomes harder: how can a kingdom that is not of this world claim the whole earth, the nations for an inheritance, and the service of every king? Two answers come easily. One hears "not of this world" and concludes that Christ rules hearts and has nothing to say to anyone who holds authority over others. Pius XI, who gave the Church this feast in 1925, called it a grave error to say that Christ has no authority whatever in civil affairs. The other hears the Gradual and makes Christ a rival of Caesar for his throne. The Fathers and Doctors who expound these texts refuse both answers, and they do it by attending to the exact words the Lord used.

Jesus speaks of his kingdom three times in two sentences. In the Latin of the Gospel it is not de hoc mundo, of this world; if it were ex hoc mundo, his servants would strive for him; and now it is not hinc, from hence, from here. St Augustine, expounding this Gospel, gives the Lord's answer to the whole world: "Hear then, ye Jews and Gentiles; hear, O circumcision; hear, O uncircumcision; hear, all ye kingdoms of the earth: I interfere not with your government in this world." And he points to what the Lord did not say. He did not say that his kingdom is not in this world, but that it is not of this world; he did not say that it is not here, but that it is not from hence. "For His kingdom is here until the end of the world, having tares intermingled therewith until the harvest." Yet it is not from here, "for it only sojourns as a stranger in the world."

Augustine says plainly what this kingdom is: "Those who believe in Him." They were of the world while they belonged to its prince, and they have been made a kingdom no longer of the world by being reborn in Christ. For that change Augustine goes to the Epistle of this Mass, where St Paul thanks the Father "who hath delivered us from the power of darkness and hath translated us into the kingdom of the Son of his love." To be translated is to be carried over. Those who believe have been carried out of one dominion into another while they go on living in the world and under its governments.

St John Chrysostom reads the same sentence as a statement about the King's power. He asks whether the kingdom is then not of this world also, and answers: "Certainly it is. How then says He it 'is not'? Not because He does not rule here, but because He has his empire from above, and because it is not human, but far greater than this and more splendid." His proof is the servants who do not fight. "Here He shows the weakness of kingship among us, that its strength lies in servants; but that which is above is sufficient for itself, needing nothing." This King could have had servants strive for him, and he did not. Chrysostom says he was made captive "by consenting, and giving Himself up."

The Fathers read the Offertory's psalm of what he is doing in that hall. There the Father says to the Son, "Ask of me." Cassiodorus observes that the almighty Word, having all that is the Father's, needs nothing to be given him. St Augustine reads the asking of the manhood the Son took on himself, the Son "who offered up Himself as a Sacrifice in the stead of all sacrifices." St Thomas Aquinas adds that Christ asks by his prayer and by his passion. God wills that we receive his gifts by asking, and he willed to show this in Christ, that his Son should ask for what already belonged to him by right. The man standing bound before Pilate is asking for the nations, and the asking is the passion he has consented to.

From the Lord's words St Thomas draws a conclusion: his kingdom is not from here, and yet it is here, because it is everywhere. A kingdom that took its beginning from one place in the world would be bounded by that place. A kingdom whose origin is above is bounded by no place, and so it can claim the whole earth without competing with any power in it.

The kings of the Gradual remain, and here the Fathers speak concretely. Augustine, on the Offertory's psalm, speaks to the kings directly: "now that I am set as King, be ye not sad, kings of the earth, as if your excellency were taken from you, but rather understand and be instructed. For it is expedient for you, that ye should be under Him." St Robert Bellarmine hears the prophet exhorting the kings "to freely subject themselves to him, the true and supreme king of all kings." On the Gradual's own psalm, Augustine says where such kings stand. The poor man whom the king delivers from the mighty is "the people of men believing in Him. In this people are also kings adoring Him," kings who do not disdain to be poor and who humbly confess their sins. Blessed Ildefonso Schuster, writing on this Mass in 1930, says that "Jesus does not come to dethrone the sovereigns of the earth," and that he gives to rulers and subjects alike the laws of truth and justice. Pius XI held both things together: Christ does not interfere with those who possess earthly goods, and yet individuals, families and states are under his rule, and those who govern owe him public honour and obedience.

The Fathers also place that homage in time. Theodoret of Cyrus says that some kings adore him willingly in this present life and all of them after the resurrection, since "now we see not as yet all things subject to him," but then every knee shall bow. The Alleluia sang of the same King: "his power is an everlasting power that shall not be taken away: and his kingdom that shall not be destroyed."

The Gospel ends on the words that say who the King's subjects are: "Every one that is of the truth heareth my voice." Augustine takes hearing here as obeying and believing, and he says that in this word Christ "commendeth the grace whereby He calleth according to His own purpose." The kingdom gathers its people by the truth they hear and by the grace that calls them, not by the strength of servants. When the Preface of this Mass names the goods of the kingdom, the first it names is the truth to which the King bore witness before Pilate: "a kingdom of truth and life; a kingdom of holiness and grace, a kingdom of justice, love and peace."

The prayers of the Mass ask for the nations in the same way. The Collect calls them "separated by the wound of sin," and it asks that they be subjected to "His most loving dominion." The Latin word is suavissimo, most gentle. The Secret, said over the gifts, asks that Jesus Christ, "Whom we immolate in this sacrifice, will bestow on all Gentiles the gifts of unity and peace." To immolate is to offer as a victim. The nations scattered by sin are to receive their unity and their peace from the one who stood bound before Pilate and is offered on this altar.

One prayer of this Mass seems to pull the other way. After Communion the priest will ask that "we who glory to fight under the standard of Christ the King, may forever reign with Him on the heavenly throne." The King of the Gospel has just said that his servants do not fight. Chrysostom's reasoning shows what that prayer cannot mean: Christ's kingdom does not stand on servants who fight, and our fighting is not his defence. St Hilary of Poitiers says that the kingdom of God is where sin is overcome and death destroyed. The war under this standard is our share in his victory over sin. Schuster says what the standard is: "The Royal Standard of Jesus Christ is the Cross," "for the kingdom of Christ is that of obedience, humility and sacrifice."

When the choir sings the Father's "Ask of me" at the Offertory, the Secret that follows will offer the same sacrifice by which the Son asked. Ask with him then, by name, for the people over whom you hold any charge, that they may hear his voice and be carried over into his kingdom.

The Communion takes two half-verses of a psalm: "the Lord shall sit king for ever," and "the Lord will bless his people with peace." Augustine, expounding that psalm, says that the Lord gives strength to his people fighting the storms of this world, "for peace in this world He hath not promised them"; the peace he blesses them with is peace in himself. The verse the Communion shortens begins, "The Lord maketh the flood to dwell," and Augustine reads the flood as this world: "The Lord therefore first inhabiteth the deluge of this world in His Saints, kept safely in the Church, as in the ark." He takes up the next words, "And the Lord shall sit a King for ever," and goes on: "And afterward He will sit reigning in them for ever."
