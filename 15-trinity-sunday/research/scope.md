# Trinity Sunday — Research Scope

**Last updated:** 2026-07-10

This record preserves the source roles, historical alternatives, and claim boundaries compressed in the reader-facing guide. Complete appointed texts, checksums, image locations, witness differences, and line-by-line collation results remain in [the verified propers](../propers/verified.md).

## Scriptural and Liturgical Scope

- Appointed and immediate contexts studied: Tobit 11–13; Psalm 8 in full; Daniel 3, including the Prayer of Azariah and Song of the Three Young Men; Matthew 28; and Romans 9–11, with Romans 15–16 for the letter’s circumstances.
- The Collect, Secret, and Postcommunion were analyzed in their complete verified Latin forms. The PDF reproduces only the clauses needed for its argument because it is designed for use beside a hand missal.
- Six distinct directly appointed passages were inventoried in Catholic canonical order: Tobit 12:6 (Introit, Offertory, and Communion), Psalm 8:2 (Introit psalm verse), Daniel 3:52 (Alleluia), Daniel 3:55–56 (Gradual), Matthew 28:18–20 (Gospel), and Romans 11:33–36 (Epistle). The three Tobit adaptations are consolidated because they draw from the same verse; the composed orations and their biblical resonances are excluded.
- The recurring movement from received mercy through wonder and baptismal confession to self-offering is an editorial map of the assembled formulary, not a claim about the compositional intent of the biblical authors.

## Historical Coordinates of the Direct Scriptural Propers

Each proper row reports inherited composition metadata under the neutral headings `Location` and `Date`; the spanning prose distinguishes that placement from current historical judgment. The Gospel alone adds a `Narrated event` row so the risen Christ’s Galilean setting is not confused with the Evangelist’s writing place.

- **Tobit 12:6:** the older reconstruction summarized by the *Catholic Encyclopedia* attributes the autobiographical opening to Tobit and most of the remaining narrative to Tobit and Tobiah after Raphael commands them to record God’s deeds. It accordingly locates the writing within the family’s Assyrian-diaspora world at Nineveh and dates it to the early seventh century BC. The article’s phrase “during the Babylonian Exile” does not sit cleanly with that early date or the narrative’s Assyrian setting; the table therefore says Nineveh and marks the placement as traditional rather than harmonizing the difficulty. The intended horizon is Israel in dispersion, and the verse occurs after the son’s return, the father’s healing, and Raphael’s disclosure. The *NABRE* instead treats the work as an anonymous Aramaic religious novella, probably written early in the second century BC at an unknown place for Jewish instruction. The verse’s public blessing of providential mercy gathers fidelity, almsgiving, family restoration, and angelic service into Israel’s wisdom tradition.
- **Psalm 8:2:** the Hebrew and Greek/Latin title name David, so the table uses a broad `c. 1000 BC` Davidic orientation in the kingdom of Israel. No title or narrative fixes a site or moment in David’s life. The inherited first audience is worshipping Israel; the hymn moves from the Lord’s world-filling name to humanity’s smallness and God-given royal vocation within creation, later received christologically in Hebrews 2. Modern historical authorship, date, and place remain unrecoverable from the text.
- **Daniel 3:52, 55–56:** the traditional position assigns the book to Daniel in Babylon during the exile, broadly 570–536 BC. On that account, a Judahite captive who had passed from deported youth into court service preserved for fellow exiles the praise spoken by the three young men under Nebuchadnezzar. Both chants belong to the deuterocanonical Prayer and Song preserved in Greek. Even the older *Catholic Encyclopedia* distinguishes this addition from the question of the book as a whole: many Catholic writers associated it with Daniel’s time, while the article judges an unknown later Jewish author more likely. The *NABRE* dates the book’s final form to the persecution of Antiochus IV, 167–164 BC, for suffering Jews, and identifies the prayer and song as a Greek addition. The addition’s particular place and date are not securely known. Its salvation-historical force is clear despite the literary uncertainty: faithful Israel blesses the God of the fathers and summons creation to praise while living under pagan imperial power.
- **Matthew 28:18–20:** ancient ecclesial tradition identifies Matthew the Apostle and former tax collector as author, writing in the native language of Palestinian Jews before leaving Palestine and addressing Jewish converts and other Jews. Traditional chronologies vary from about AD 38 to 68; the table uses `c. AD 40–50` as a broad representative orientation, not a precise autograph date. In that inherited biography, Matthew writes as a mature apostolic witness after the Resurrection and Pentecost, interpreting Israel’s Scriptures around Jesus the Messiah and the nations’ inclusion. Current *NABRE* judgment dates the canonical Greek Gospel after AD 70, probably around AD 80–90, regards Antioch in Syria as plausible rather than certain, and understands its church as moving from a strongly Jewish-Christian to a predominantly Gentile membership. The narrated commission occurs on an unnamed mountain in Galilee after the Resurrection, traditionally c. AD 30–33; no source identifies the mountain securely.
- **Romans 11:33–36:** Pauline authorship is both traditional and the mainstream historical judgment; Romans 16:22 names Tertius as the scribe. The *NABRE* locates composition in Greece, likely Corinth, in AD 56–58, represented compactly as `c. AD 57`. Paul writes to Jewish and Gentile house churches in Rome that he had not founded. His eastern mission was nearly complete, he was preparing to take the Gentile collection to Jerusalem, and he hoped to visit Rome on the way to Spain. The doxology closes Romans 9–11: Israel’s election and stumbling, Gentile inclusion, the warning against boasting, and divine mercy lead the apostle to praise rather than a detached metaphysical maxim.

Geographic equivalents remain deliberately broad. Ancient Nineveh lies by present-day Mosul in Iraq; the kingdom of Israel occupied the southern Levant; Babylon lies in present-day Iraq; Palestine and Galilee belong to the southern Levant, with Galilee in present-day northern Israel; Antioch is Antakya in present-day Türkiye; Corinth is in present-day Greece; and Rome is in present-day Italy. These equivalents orient the reader without turning a narrative, recipient, or plausible modern location into a proved writing site.

## Direct Reception Retained

- St. John Chrysostom, *Homily 19 on Romans*, directly comments on Romans 11:33–36: Paul recognizes the depth without measuring it, follows the reversals involving Israel and the nations, and confesses God as first benefactor rather than debtor: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/210219.htm).
- St. Hilary of Poitiers, *De Trinitate* II.1–2, directly treats Matthew 28:19 as a saving baptismal rule joining Creator, Only-begotten, and Gift while acknowledging the limits of theological speech: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/330202.htm).
- St. Basil the Great, *On the Holy Spirit* 10.24; 12.26, 28; 27.68, directly uses Matthew 28:19 to defend the Spirit’s inseparable coordination with Father and Son in baptism and doxology: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3203.htm).
- St. Gregory Nazianzen, *Oration 40* §§41, 45, directly joins the one Godhead and power of the three with Christ’s command to disciple and baptize, and refuses to oppose dogma to conduct: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/310240.htm).
- St. Augustine, *Exposition on Psalm 8* §§4–8, directly reads the admirable name throughout the earth christologically and places praise in the mouths of the little ones before mature comprehension: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/1801008.htm).

No direct patristic exposition of the appointed Tobit 12:6 or Daniel 3 verses was needed for the claims retained in the compact revision; those chants are handled through their canonical contexts and clearly labeled editorial relations. This is a limit of the retained guide, not a claim that no ancient commentary exists.

## Related Doctrinal and Sacramental Illumination Retained

- St. Gregory Nazianzen, *Oration 40* §25, on offering oneself and clothing oneself with Christ after baptism, illuminates the Secret but does not comment on it directly: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/310240.htm).
- St. Augustine, *City of God* X.5–6, 20, on visible sacrifice, interior self-offering, the redeemed city, and Christ as priest and sacrifice, supplies doctrinal illumination for the Secret: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120110.htm).
- St. Augustine, *De Trinitate* VIII.7.10–VIII.10.14, on brotherly charity, God as love, and lover–beloved–love, is retained as a disciplined analogical beginning, not a proof or exhaustive model of the Trinity: [text](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/130108.htm).
- The feast’s appointed Preface confesses distinction of Persons, unity of essence, and equality of majesty. It is liturgical context, not an eleventh proper and not evidence that the cited Fathers authored the formulary.

## Biblical-Historical Sources and Controlling Distinctions

- *NABRE* introductions and notes used: [Tobit](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/tobit/0), [Psalms](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/psalms/0) and [Psalm 8](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/psalms/8), [Daniel](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/daniel/0), [Matthew](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/matthew/0) and [Matthew 28](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/matthew/28), and [Romans](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/romans/0).
- Traditional orientation used: *Catholic Encyclopedia*, [“Book of Tobias”](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/14749c.htm), [“Daniel”](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04620a.htm), [“Book of Daniel”](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04621b.htm), and [“Gospel of St. Matthew”](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/10057a.htm).
- Tobit 12:6 is the scriptural source of three chant adaptations, but its Vulgate wording does not itself name the three divine Persons. The explicit Trinitarian language belongs to the liturgical adaptations and is not projected back into the human author’s compositional intent.
- Daniel’s narrated furnace, the traditional writer’s Babylonian location, and a later addition’s unknown composition site are distinct questions even though the compact table can show only the traditional composition metadata and then qualify it in prose.
- Romans 11:36 is a doxology about God in its immediate grammar. The guide does not mechanically distribute its prepositions among Father, Son, and Holy Ghost; Matthew’s baptismal name and the Church’s rule of faith govern the explicit Trinitarian confession.
- Matthew’s singular “name” names three Persons, but grammar alone is not a complete treatise on consubstantiality. The guide relies on the cited baptismal reception and the feast’s liturgical confession for its doctrinal development.

## Survey and Material Limits

- Surveyed the public 1962 missal facsimile and OCR, the secondary color image witness, the complete appointed biblical contexts in the *NABRE*, relevant New Advent Catholic Encyclopedia articles, and the patristic works already retained in the guide.
- The historical table’s dates are deliberately approximate traditional orientations. Tobit’s inherited reconstruction, the specific origin of Daniel’s Greek addition, Psalm 8’s historical setting, Matthew’s traditional range, the exact Galilean mountain, and the canonical Gospel’s modern composition city remain materially uncertain.
- The public survey does not exhaust manuscripts, untranslated works, subscription databases, or current specialist monographs. Older public-domain translations are working texts.
- Sources were retained because they establish or materially qualify claims made in the guide; no numerical or source-category quota was applied.
